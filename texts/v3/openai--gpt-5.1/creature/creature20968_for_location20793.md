They live as if the bowl is older kin, and they are its careful cartilage — joint-creatures in a body of stone and water.

They are not a people of a single outline; they are what the basin can sustain without bruising.

---

### Their bodies

They are built for edges and for cold. Slender, but not fragile; dense in the way of things that grow slowly.

Height: from child to elder, they rarely exceed the span from the basin floor to your hip, though a few are taller. The tallest are not imposing; they’re attenuated, all leverage and reach. Most adults stand somewhere between your chest and your shoulder.

Limbs: long, with elongated feet and hands. Their feet are broad, fan-like when spread, with four main toes and a stub of a fifth that never quite leaves the ground. Each toe ends not in a claw but in a dense, keratinous pad with fine, concentric ridges — the friction ridges of a species that walks mostly on wet rock. Their gait is always spring-loaded: knees bent, ankles loose. When they walk quickly they seem to pour forward, silently, like water finding a slope.

Hands: five-fingered, but the ring and little fingers are webbed halfway down, good for pinching flat slabs and cradling thin, fragile things. The thumb can fold further back than yours, given to precise pressure rather than brute grip. Skin of palms and fingertips is pearly, thickened and almost translucent, with pale whorls where old cuts have healed.

Skin: exposed skin takes the color of the basin. It’s not simply gray but a variable, muted palette of cold hues: stony umber along shins, a dusting of lichen-olive at shoulders, bluish cast where veins thread close to the surface. Under the constant, directionless light their skin has a particular sheen, not glossy, not dull — more like wet river pebbles that have dried but still remember.

Across their bodies run faint, branching tracings of slightly different tone, as if their flesh has grown along stress lines like slowly flowing ice. On some, especially the elders, those tracings gather into nodes at joints, where the skin is thickened and rougher. These nodes respond to the environment: in sudden drops of pressure they rise slightly, becoming more pronounced, like gooseflesh with intent.

Face: the face is longish, features compressed toward the center. Cheekbones are high and flat, giving more surface for arteries to warm the air they breathe back in. Noses are narrow with flared upper bridges where sinuses widen — they are connoisseurs of inhaled temperature. Nostrils can pinch almost shut, leaving only slit-thin openings.

Eyes: large without being owlish. The irises range from stone-gray to muted amber to the deep, dark green of wet moss. The pupil is not perfectly round; it forms a slightly vertical oval that can narrow into a hairline in intense light, admitting only a tight column. Around the iris, the sclera carries a blurred ring of pigment — something between a shadow and a halo. The whole eye glows a little in the basin’s hard luminosity, catching stray photons greedily. In moments when the vertical streaks appear in the sky, fine, hidden membranes slide across the eyes from the inner corner, a second eyelid with microscopic reflective crystals; they see those sky-scripts more clearly than you ever could.

Ears: modest, tucked close to the head, but with elaborately folded cartilage inside. Their hearing isn’t about distance or volume; it’s about nuance. They can distinguish one droplet’s landing from another, the particular timbre of a distant fracture in ice from the usual restlessness of the mountains. Preauricular skin bears tiny, almost invisible vibrissae — whisker-like hairs — that tremble at pressure changes too small to register as sound.

Body hair: sparse and fine on the face and hands, denser along the spine, chest, and the backs of limbs. It lies close and flat, more like the underfur of cold-adapted mammals. Its color mirrors their skin but can show subtle patterns: swirling darker whorls at the shoulders, pale streaks down the thighs, as if reflecting the flow paths of water.

Under the hair, in the larger muscles, are minuscule, scattered pigment organs, no bigger than the head of a pin. They are not for show but for calibration: in strong shifts of ambient brightness they darken or pale, allowing the body to re-balance how much light it absorbs. To touch them, you’d feel nothing, but under the omnidirectional glare they adjust like the iris of the whole body.

### How they move here

They are so thoroughly tuned to the basin’s frictions that their clumsiness only appears elsewhere.

On stone: they walk almost never flat-footed. The pads at the balls of their feet are richly innervated; they sense micro-tilts and water-film thickness through them. A human would test an icy rock with sight; they test it with weight. Children learn to stand on sloping, slick planes long before they learn to run. Standing still, they often rock subtly, distributing force like a tripod on soft ground. To an outsider it looks like a constant, nervous shifting; to them, stillness is this dynamic balance.

On water: they do not wade except where they must. Instead, they skim the edges of streams, toes kissing only the highest, driest asperities of stone. Where stepping stones are arranged, they place their feet on the lateral edges rather than the center, trusting the bite of their pads more than the flat surface. They can cross a path of half-submerged stones with no more disturbance than the passage of one of their transparent fish.

Climbing: their hands are not built for powerful dangling, but for planar grip. Climbing a wall, they press as much surface of palm and finger as possible against the rock, smoothing, not clawing. They exploit every wet ledge and moss-mat as if the basin itself had offered them rungs. Their center of gravity stays close, knees always bent; their movements are slow and nearly tremorless. Where you would pant and grope, they appear to stroll vertically.

They rarely fall, and when they do, they do not shout.

### Clothing and second skin

Clothing is less aesthetic expression than dynamic membrane between them and the bowl.

Inner layers: soft, tight-wrapped hides from the shimmering-furred animals they keep. These hides have been worked until they are thin but not fragile, oiled with a resinous secretion gleaned from one of the cliff-hugging plants. The oil does not bead water away in big droplets; it makes moisture crawl, gathering into lines that can be directed.

Between hide and skin, in colder cycles, they pack thin mats of that same moss they use as sealant. These mats are combed into layers: living moss against the hide, dead, fibrous matter toward the skin. The living side holds a thin, constant cool-damp; the dead side buffers heat loss. It is a portable wetland, assisting their own sweating and chilling, making them part of the basin’s moisture-circuit.

Outer membranes: the glossy sheets you saw are not simply leather. They are stretched and re-stretched from the membrane that lines the lungs of the antlered animals — harvested only at death, scraped until nearly translucent, then impregnated with fine particulate from the mineral crusts that form where certain seeps dry. Those crusts contain microscopic platelets that align under tension, giving the membrane an uncanny ability: water that strikes it spreads almost soundlessly, then is conducted along pre-scored paths to drooping edges, where it falls in silent strings into channels at their feet.

When the invisible rain comes — the condensation-event that wets every surface — they deploy these membranes like petals closing. They know exactly which droplets to shed, which to let through to the moss packed beneath. A child’s first personal membrane is a rite: sized too large so it can be re-cut as they grow, its edges trimmed with beads of bone that record in tiny notches each year’s coldest cycle.

Colors and adornment: they distrust bright fields of color. Saturation, when allowed, is in minute, non-jangling points: a piece of iron-oxide red smoothed to a luster and sewn flat over the heart; pale disks of polished bone worn at the throat, carved with variegated tiny channels that catch condensation and feed slow drips down onto the sternum on long watches; curls of mica suspended behind the ears, not as earrings but as vanes that register faint flows of air against skin.

Hair: their head hair grows thick but never hangs loose. It is braided tight against the skull, often incorporating filaments of plant fiber that can be unspooled in a pinch for emergency lashings. Patterns of braiding change with age and experience: wide, simple bands for children, intricate interlacings that echo water-channels for adults who have earned the right to reconfigure streams.

### What they eat, and how

They eat the basin itself, but always at its edges: never stripping, always coaxing.

Water-films and cups: the tiny, translucent cup-structures in the moss are more than curiosities. The six-limbed wall-clingers drink from them; the people harvest them. Using flat, thin bone blades, they sever cups with a padding of surrounding moss just before they naturally overflow. These are brought to special ledges where the cups are arranged in shallow depressions of polished stone. Left alone, they slowly distill. What remains, at the bottom, is a clear, viscous concentrate that smells faintly metallic and resinous.

They call it something your language would only approximate as “heartwater.” They use a drop of it to flavor and enrich whatever else they eat. It contains trace minerals, microbial communities, and maybe more subtle things: over generations, bodies raised on heartwater carry its patterns. Elders claim that those who grow without it cannot fully hear the stone.

Films and crusts: the black fans and rust-orange stippling across the rock are farmed cautiously. They never scrape an entire patch. Instead, they etch micro-gutters into the rock with a fine, rounded tool, guiding seepwater to produce tiny, fatter tongues of growth at the margins of each colony. Those tongues are then peeled off, like harvestable rind, leaving the center intact.

These growths are sun-wilted on high racks on days when the sky’s milky haze thins. The resulting sheets are brittle, vitamin-rich, faintly bitter. Ground, they become a paste that thickens broths made from animal bone and the pale grasses. Left as sheets, they line the inside of some containers, acting as both food-storage and slow infusion; they leach micronutrients back into whatever is kept there.

Ribbons of fish: they do not spear or net them in the ordinary way. The fish disperse the basin’s light, and that is more precious than protein. Instead, at certain times of the light-cycle, they partially drain select channels into prepared basins, lowering the water enough that the fish’s bodies brush the undersides of overhanging rock. On those undersides, the people have encouraged specific mats of filter-feeders. The fish rub lightly against these mats, picking up a dusting of microscopic organisms that they later excrete downstream, seeding new growths in pre-placed stone bowls.

The people eat these concentrated scrapings: scooped into hollow bone tubes, mixed with heartwater and the pith of young shrubs, it becomes a dense, savory, finger-thick cylinder. Children, in particular, are fed these cylinders as they learn to walk the wet paths — holding nourishment in one hand, stone in the other.

Disk-creatures in pools: the pale, mirrored disks are almost never eaten. Instead, they serve as partners in ice-work. However, very occasionally, when a disk dies of its own accord and releases from the water’s underside, an elder will retrieve the body with reverent economy. Sliced thin and dried, pieces of the disk become part of special ceremonial meals taken in near-darkness during certain sky-streak events. They say that in eating them, a person briefly remembers water as the disks do: not as flow, but as interface.

Animals: the shimmering-furred mammals that peer from under racks are not simply pest-catchers or companions; they are a primary domestic species. They are allowed to forage for the thumb-sized many-legged creatures along the waterlines, but at a controlled rate. Their numbers are carefully balanced.

Once a season, a few of the oldest, slowest are culled, killed without blunt trauma — always by bloodletting along specific necklines, always on a soft mat, always with a bone blade that has never been used to strike stone. Their meat is eaten fresh, lightly dried, or ground and packed into cleaned length-bones as long-keeping rations for journeys beyond the rim. Their hides become the inner garments. Their long, hollow bones become part of the basin’s listening arrays.

Nothing of them is wasted; nothing of them is hurried.

### Domestic rhythms

Their days are not divided by a visible sun, but by modulations in the basin’s glow, in the breathing of the haze and the deep responses of stone and water. They track time in these phases:

- **Thinbright**: when the overhead haze retreats upward, and light feels as if it is coming from a particular, higher strata in the sky. Shadows become barely perceptible, as tiny shifts in gradation along the rock. This is when they work surfaces: scraping, re-placing stones, opening domes.

- **Thickbright**: when the luminous haze sinks low. Light becomes ambient, translucent, as if you stand inside a glowing lung. This is water-work time: diversions, harvest of films, tending of channels. Wind outside is often more violent in thickbright; they hear it in the antlers and bones.

- **Strand-time**: when vertical streaks of dense light appear and the reflective surfaces gather alien patterns. Work stills. People sit or crouch by watercourses and simply attend. This is the closest thing they have to communal worship, though they would not call it that.

- **Underbright**: the nearest the basin gets to night. The glow does not disappear, but it thickens downward, leaving the air itself slightly dimmer and the water’s surface a more obvious mirror. This is for indoor crafts, story-work, coupling, deep listening beneath furs.

Children are apprenticed not to trades, but to *fluencies*. One child might be given to water, learning all the ways flows can be split and braided without shock. Another to ice, drawing freezing patterns with bone points. Another to bone, learning hollows and lengths that catch particular registers of outside thunder. Another to light, watching strand-time reflections and memorizing their sequences.

There are no clocks. There are, instead, test-stones: shallow bowls carved into bedrock where slow drips of water fall at regular intervals, guided by the consistent seep of a higher vein. The level of water relative to etched rings becomes a calendar. Some elders can glance at a bowl and tell you not only the cycle but the phase of storm-brewing beyond the basin.

### Sound, taboo and language

Silence here is not the absence of noise; it is the insistence that any sound must be *long* — able to trail out into the rock without shock.

They abhor impulses: sharp, sudden spikes of energy. A dropped stone, a shouted word, a palm slapped on water — these are violences. They don’t ban harsh motions because they are aesthetically displeasing; they ban them because they bruise the bowl’s interior structure. They have watched, over generations, how a single concussive noise leaves a scar in the stone, how that scar alters the flow of water, which alters the communities of life, which alters what the bowl can hold.

Their voices: low, close to the chest, never pushed. Their baseline speech stays under the threshold at which the hollow bone arrays begin to hum. They have learned exactly where that threshold lies and nestle just beneath it. Much of their talk is not voiced at all. Consonants blur into each other; vowels are short, flattened. Tone carries more than volume: small inflections speak of doubt, of caution, of approval; longer, almost-sung vowel-glides mark emphasis between concepts.

Hand language: they possess a rich, codified system of gestures. Unlike some sign languages, it is not expansive — no broad sweeps or emphatic points. It is compressed into the space of the forearms, wrists, and fingers. Fingers tapping the inside or outside of the opposite forearm speak of flows, of inward- or outward-going streams. A hand held palm-down over the heart with fingers splayed means “listen to the basin first.” A small, tight clenching of the third and fourth fingers only: “danger of concussion.”

They often speak and sign together: voice carrying broad strokes, hands adding the riverine subtleties. Children learn both as native tongues.

Silence is not reserved for solemnity alone; it is their basic courtesy. Entering a dwelling, a visitor will always pause, touch the nearest stone with three fingertips, and wait one breath, feeling for residual vibration. Only then will they speak.

### Work with stone and water

They are not masons in the sense of imposing shape on rock; they are negotiators between the stone’s existing grain and their own needs.

Selection of stones: no stone is just “picked up.” They read the faint radial scars that betray past concussions, the direction of fluting by long-ago water, the micro-shimmer of mineral inclusions. Stones that have been “hurt” by noise are used where they can do least harm — as ballast, at the bottom of walls, never as contact points that meet moving water. Clean stones, unscarred, become bridging slabs or parts of domes.

Mortising without mortar: when they stack stone plates for domes and walls, their fingers search for three-point contacts. A stone is accepted only when it settles into three stable touchpoints with what lies beneath — never two, which would rock, and never four, which would demand micro-shimming that might shift later when wet. This three-point doctrine is so internalized that even their art and storytelling are built around triplets.

Paths of water: they do not merely divert streams; they train both water and stone through mirrored experimentation. Suppose they desire a gentle S-curve in a channel where the rock is currently too straight and fast. Rather than gouging, they will construct a model S-curve in soft sediment in a different, sacrificial nook of the basin, sending a fraction of the flow through it. Over days or months, the water’s habit of curving there influences the main rock’s micro-erosion through whatever non-local property the place has. The real channel will begin to take on hints of the S without direct violence. Only then do they do minor carving, coaxing already-begun tendencies.

Mirror channels are often built in miniature, at child-hand scale. Apprentices learn by watching how the ghost of one path appears in another.

Ice-work: the ice that crusts some pools from below or along strange vectors is not accidental. They plant shards of certain minerals in the pool beds, seeding crystals. They disturb the water at precise, regular intervals with soft, padded tools, setting up rhythms that ice can grow along. They score incipient ice-skins with patterns learned from the disk-creatures’ undersides.

Later, when slabs are harvested, they are carried not bare-handed, but in frames that touch only pre-specified nodes, to avoid shattering their internal geometry. These slabs are placed within shelters where they both insulate and cast light, bending the omnidirectional glow into shifting, slow-moving caustics on ceilings. Children fall asleep watching those moving patterns, learning to read in them the behavior of the sky-strands.

Listening-stones: the rounded, drilled stones that respond to air-pressure changes are not merely warnings; they are memory. Each shaft is a drawn-out ear tuned to a particular frequency of storm. When outside winds press on the basin lip, air is forced down some shafts, drawn up others; they produce infrasonic murmurs that travel through the rock.

Certain families are responsible for tending these stones: cleaning the shafts with soft brushes made from pale grasses; mapping which shaft hums most in which conditions; telling the stories of past cycles when particular combinations of hums preceded avalanches or rare freezes.

One of the first taboos children learn is never to block a shaft with anything dense — no fingers, no pebbles. The one child you saw tested that taboo and was taught not through fear, but through touch: hands placed on the humming rock, learning viscerally what a sudden note does.

### Relations with other beings

They do not think of themselves as top of a pyramid, but as one more density in the basin’s mesh. Still, they distinguish between partners, dependents, and hazards.

Six-limbed wall-clingers: they call them a word that means something like “ladder-keepers.” They never eat them; they cultivate them. Small, draped mats of hair and moss are hung near favored colonies, catching the minute droppings the clingers produce after feeding on cup-plant exudate. These droppings are a potent fertilizer. Ground and mixed into paste, they are used to “seed” new cup-plants in bare, damp cracks. They also smear a thin layer of this paste inside stone containers where they wish to encourage the growth of specific microbial films.

Sometimes, when a clinger species’ glowing joint-lines change subtly, showing new branching patterns, elders take this as sign of a deeper shift in the basin’s water-chemistry. They will then convene quiet meetings to discuss re-routing certain flows.

Furred mammals: besides their obvious uses, these animals are watchers. Their near-translucent ears register mid- and high-frequency noises that the people’s own ears are poor at hearing — the skitter of an unfamiliar insect, the ticking of stone under new stress, the rare wingbeat of a bird that has strayed over the rim. People read the animals’ posture and ear-angle the way you might read a barometer.

In certain rites, special collars made of plaited reeds and embedded bone-hollows are slipped around the necks of selected animals. These collars hum sympathetically with distant low frequencies, training the animals to seek or avoid particular resonances. They become, in effect, mobile sensors mapped onto living intuition.

Antlered cliff-beasts: these “wind-horns” are not domesticated in the strict sense. No one keeps them in enclosures. The boundary between the basin proper and the upper shelves where they dwell is marked by carved sigils in the rock: do not cross without purpose; do not disturb.

The people have certain long-standing exchanges with them. In harsh cycles, when outside storms grow frequent and deep and the animals’ forage fails, the people will leave carefully arranged piles of dried shrubs and grasses on ledges accessible to antlered feet but not to more clumsy creatures. At the same time, they will weave new arrays of threads and bone rings and hang them between certain antler-holes, temporarily modifying the chords the animals emit when the wind plays them.

Thus, in times of particular danger, the animals’ music becomes an amplified and re-tuned warning system. In turn, when young wind-horns shed or break parts of their antlers, they often do so near the basin lip, where people can retrieve the fragments. These are then carved and added to the listening-arrays lower down. There is a mutual, if never negotiated, arrangement: safety for both through shared resonance.

Ribbons and discs: both are largely left alone, allowed to do the subtle work of light and surface. However, children are sometimes given tasks that involve gently coaxing a ribbon-fish across a certain patch of rock or encouraging disks to congregate under a particular area of water. These exercises train the children’s patience and their sense of indirect influence: to bring about a shape not by forcing it, but by shifting tendencies.

Invisible others: beyond obvious fauna, there are beings so small or so distributed that the people do not define them as animals or plants. They speak instead of “listeners inside stone” and “eaters of brightness.” These are the unnamed microbial or quasi-mystical presences inhabiting films and crusts. The people do not pretend to command them; they negotiate, offering stable flow in exchange for particular kinds of growth, waiting multiple cycles to see if the exchange is accepted.

### Birth, growth, death

Birthing: new life comes quietly. There is no howling. A laboring person is attended by a small circle of kin inside a dome whose stone plates have been opened just enough to let in a controlled, soft light. Water is guided along channels cut long ago for this purpose, trickling near but not through the birthing space, so its murmur can cue breathing. They believe that the sound patterns around a child’s first breaths imprint a “furrow” in the body’s internal listening.

The newborn is bathed not in free-poured water, but in heartwater diluted to a safe degree, dripped along limbs and head, then wiped gently away with living moss. This is the first hospitality they extend on behalf of the basin.

Naming: names are not spoken aloud in their first phase. For a number of cycles, a child is referred to by gestural combinations that describe their earliest particularities: “she-who-arches-her-feet,” “he-who-watches-disks.” Only when the child has participated, even clumsily, in some basin-task — placing a stone, redirecting a drip — is a spoken name given, often incorporating soft consonants reminiscent of flows or the rustle of moss.

Death: they die indoors, when possible. As the end approaches, a person is set not on a bed but on a slightly inclined slab of stone that has been part of the dwelling since its building. Under their head is a cushion of old moss and dead shrubs, chosen for their ability to wick away small flows. Around them, channels are opened to let water course near, not touching.

At the moment of death, there is usually no wail. Instead, kin place their open hands on the stone around the body and breathe out at the same moment, intentionally matching their exhalations. It is believed that this shared, soft pressure-change helps guide whatever subtle part of the person remains toward the basin’s underways, joining the deep architecture of flows and responsiveness.

Bodies are not buried in soil — there is little. Nor are they consigned to open water, which would be too abrupt. Instead, bones are separated gently over time. Flesh is given to specific, prepared channels where microbial partners are thickest, in a kind of cultivated decomposition that feeds particular films. Bones are cleaned and then placed either into the hollow listening-arrays (becoming permanent ears) or stacked in the organ-pipe arrangements along certain cliffs, where they continue to vibrate with outside weather.

Ancestral presence: not imagined as ghosts, but as modifications in how the bowl responds. After the death of a well-loved elder, people will say: “The rock hears this more sharply now,” meaning some pattern of flows, some taboo, some method has been etched more deeply into communal habit.

### Thought and attention

They do not separate “science” from “devotion.” Their understanding of cause and effect is practical and meticulous, but always infused with regard.

They track patterns in everything: sequence of sky-strands, timing of invisible rain, combinations of antler-chords that precede certain forms of ice-growth. They have no writing in the usual sense, but they inscribe records in physical arrays.

Memory-racks: thin, flat bones are hung on racks near certain working sites. Each bone is drilled with small holes along one edge. Through these, strands of plant fiber are threaded, knotted in positions that encode data: “year of three long-thin-strands with low thunder,” “time when high wind came without outside storm.” Children learn to read these knots with their fingertips, eyes closed, building an internal historiography of method and outcome.

Stories: told not in roaring fireside sessions, but in low, slow recitations accompanied by constant, tiny adjustments to water nearby. A storyteller will sit by a channel, shifting pebbles as they speak. Listeners watch the changes in flow and hear the story as much in stone and water as in words. A tale about an ancestor’s mistake will be told while deliberately sending a small flow down a wrong path, then correcting it; a myth about distant, noisy beings beyond the rim might be accompanied by dropping a single grain of gravel from a barely-safe height and watching the ripples.

Explanations: they do not say “why” in quite our way. Their language folds “why” into “how much” and “in what pattern.” They would ask: “In what curve does this occur?” or “How many flows meet before this begins?” The idea of a stand-alone cause is alien; for them everything is interaction.

Belief: they are not animists in a vague way. To them, specific parts of the basin are explicitly alive with specific dispositions.

- Certain rounded stones are “rememberers of pressure.”
- Certain cracks that exhale mist are “mouths that can be provoked” — never stamped on, never plugged.
- Certain patches of rock with complex fluting are “old wounds” that must be smoothed, covered, or simply avoided in daily stepping.

They recognize their own mind-like quality in the basin’s slow thinking: its ability to distribute habit non-locally, its anticipation of repeated flows, its reluctance to accept new paths without long persuasion. They are, in a sense, conservators of an alien nervous system.

### The outside and the below

The world beyond the basin is known, but as peril rather than default.

Journeys beyond the lip are rare, undertaken with heavy preparation. Those who go are chosen for their proven ability to keep quiet even under shock. They wrap their outer membranes more tightly, add extra bone-hollows to their harnesses, and carry long, tapered rods tipped with softly padded heads — feelers to probe snow and ice before committing weight.

Above: they know of other hollows, other basins carved into the mountain range, but these are stories told with caution. Some are said to be “broken bowls,” where sharp noises once cracked the stone’s ability to hold water softly; there, water now drops in sudden cataracts, loud and destructive. Others are imagined as “hard-bright places,” where the sky’s light focuses and burns, and no haze softens it. Whether these are actual locations or cautionary myths is unclear even to them.

Below: the underworld is not a single cavern but a lattice of water and void. They interact with it through drilled stones and misting cracks. They never, as a culture, send anyone directly down. A lone legend tells of one who tried — who descended through a widened crack with ropes and membranes and bone tools — and did not return. In the cycles after, certain listening-stones took on a new, faint hum never heard before; some elders say this is his continuing influence, etched forever into the underways, a caution and a comfort.

When they imagine the afterlife, if you could even call it that, it is as continuity of responsiveness: to die well is to have your presence added cleanly to the basin’s capacity to feel and adjust, not as a jagged scar but as new subtlety.

---

### What they fear, what they hope

Their greatest fear is not storm itself but an event they call, in signs more than words, “the cracking.”

This is the imagined (or remembered?) instance of someone or something producing a sound so sudden, so wide-band, that it shocks the basin out of its resilience. They have tales of old times when outsiders came with hard, ringing tools, or when a great slab of ice from above plunged into the bowl — nobody is certain whether these are mythic or actual. In those tales, water flees its gentle paths, the haze retracts, the light becomes spear-like, and much of the subtle life dies. The cracking looms in their ethics: every child is taught that each avoided sharp impact is a contribution to warding off that grand concussion.

Their hope is quieter. They hope for deepening dialogue with the basin: for more precise understanding of how to teach it new shapes without harm; for increased intricacy of films and growths; for the day when they can read the sky-strands’ patterns not only as omens but as messages they can, in some slow way, answer.

They make small experiments toward this. On certain flat, high shelves of stone, they arrange micro-arrays of water and reflective surfaces, trying to send back modified versions of the sky’s own impossible reflections. They do this with the caution of someone tapping the shoulder of a giant they barely comprehend.

---

### To call them

There is no single name they would give you for themselves. Their self-description is always relational: “we-who-keep-the-basin-unbroken,” “those-who-slow-fall,” “hands-in-water.” If forced into a single term, it might be something like *the Attenders*.

They live in the bright cold not because they are indifferent to it, but because they have learned to interpose layers of understanding between raw elements and their own fragility. They are, in essence, a people who have chosen attentiveness as their primary technology. Everything else — from their three-point stonework to their taboo against sharp sound — follows from that decision.

And so they go about their days in that small, saturated bowl: shifting pebbles by millimeters, re-angling drips, smoothing scars in stone with moss and time; listening for the deep, infrasonic murmurs of far-off storms; reading the annealed shimmer on ice; teaching their children to step always as if the rock beneath is listening — because it is, and because they are, and because in this place, the difference between the two has long since blurred.
