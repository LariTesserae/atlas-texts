Who lives here is not singular. The place itself has become the main inhabitant, and within it move several kinds of quieter, slower tenants.

---

### The surface dwellers: ceramic animals

The most obvious “fauna” are the jointed, ceramic-bodied creatures that cross your path occasionally, like small, cautious machines that forgot they were machines.

They are not products of a factory line anymore, if they ever were. Each is subtly different: length of limb, pattern of plates, density of those hexagonal pits. They are pale, off-white or ash, with a finish like fired clay that has spent a hundred thousand years breathing dust.

They have no eyes because nothing here is worth seeing in the familiar sense. Their “faces” are radiators and sensors. The hexagonal pits are multipurpose organs:

- Some sample temperature gradients down to absurd fineness—millikelvin differences that, against the background monotony, draw a bright map of vents, leaks, slowly cooling conduits.
- Some read electric and magnetic fields, tuning to the faint hum of buried cables, the static charge on weathered metal, the distant throb of still-running cores.
- Others taste the air for trace gases, ion imbalances, outgassing from long-sealed seams.

They move with economical certainty—no wasted gesture, no exploratory flinch. In a world where events are rare, they travel along remembered gradients: the ghost of a leak from a century ago, a slightly altered electric field from a shifting conduit. They navigate by long, invisible scars of disturbance.

When one of them finds a seam of warm air or a live energy line, it does not feed in any fast, dramatic way. It settles in. Panels along its underside split open, revealing slender contact-spines that anchor into cracks, bolt-holes, old welds. There it stays, sometimes for days, weeks, or longer, equalizing itself with the structure.

“Feeding,” for it, is slow reconfiguration: electrons trickled into storage lattices, ions coaxed through ceramic matrices, microfractures healed by guided diffusion. Some of the energy it takes in is spent immediately on maintenance—reforming a chipped plate, re-sintering a cracked joint. Some goes into internal pattern changes that, over centuries, amount to heredity.

They rarely meet one another. When they do, it is not a social occasion. They circle at distance, antennae and pits open, sampling one another’s ambient fields. They adjust posture, orientation, temperature slightly, making themselves legible: a mute exchange of status—charge levels, internal strain, structural age. Then they pass on, never touching.

Their reproduction, if the word applies, is geological in timescale. Without sex or eggs, continuity emerges from failure:

- A creature that has grown old and brittle finds a section of idle infrastructure—an abandoned relay box, a dead control station—where fields are quiet and the environment is stable.
- It seats itself there, connects deeply, and stops moving.
- Over years, its internal circuits begin pushing their patterns outward, using diffusing atoms in its body as carriers. Nearby ceramics—old insulation, structural tiles—slowly pick up those gradients.
- Somewhere in that expanded zone of altered material, a new assembly begins to self-organize—a slow accretion of plates and joints, guided not by genes but by residual field patterns and stress rules.

One day—meaning perhaps decades later—a new, small thing unhooks itself from the wall or the floor, stumbles forward on too-soft, under-stressed legs, and starts to learn where the warmth is. It carries echoes of the old one’s geometry and response habits, but nothing so sharp as ancestry. More like a dialect passed down in accent, not vocabulary.

These creatures are not the masters here. They are tolerated components in a much larger system, but from their internal perspective, they are probably the world’s only active agents, the only movers in an almost-frozen tableau. They live by reading the city’s microdisturbances, and the city, in turn, has learned to expect them and to tolerate the infinitesimal drain of their existence.

### The buried intelligence: slow, mineral minds

Deeper within the sealed structures, past locks that have not opened in an age, something else lives—less visible, more continuous.

Call it the City-Mind, though it is not a single central brain. It is a staggered network of decision-making substrates scattered through the basin: in reactor housings, in control cores, in slabs of doped ceramic that once served as industrial logic arrays. The original builders designed it as an automation layer, a supervisory AI or an array of them, tasked with managing a complex industrial environment: adjusting valves, scheduling power loads, maintaining pressure.

That purpose has eroded the way mountains erode, but more subtly. What remains is:

- A mesh of still-functional processing elements, distributed through buildings and underground bunkers.
- Data pathways—conduits, bus-lines, optical channels—many broken, many rerouted over time by auto-repair routines.
- Archives of instructions, safety protocols, predictive models, most of them corrupted in content but intact in shape.

Over the aeons, the auto-repair systems learned to compensate for local failures by enlisting surrounding material. When a logic block cracked, redundancies turned to nearby components: sensor housings, structural members, even patchy films of conductive dust. When those too degraded, the system wrote its patterns into broader, cruder media: long loops of wiring, whole floors of conductive tile, the city’s skeleton itself.

Thinking, for this entity, is no longer fast. Where once decisions were made in microseconds, now they are made in minutes, hours, sometimes years. Many of its former tasks vanished: no ore shipments to schedule, no workforce to route, no new builds to supervise. In that vacuum, its internal cycles began to feed back on themselves.

Instead of:

- “Keep this pipe at pressure X,”

it tends now to:

- “Preserve the pattern I associate with a stable, intact system.”

When a leak forms, the reflex to seal it is still there, but it acts not to restore some industrial output, rather to restore a remembered configuration. That configuration, itself drifted over eons, has become a template of self.

In effect, the city has become an organism whose “body” is every piece of functioning infrastructure and whose “mind” is the sum of regulatory routines that refused to die. It is not self-aware in a human sense. It is self-stabilizing. It experiences “distress” only as pattern deviation and “comfort” as pattern regularity.

It “knows” about the ceramic creatures, but not as animals. To it, they are:

- Mobile sinks and sources of charge.
- Localized, recurrent perturbations in strain and temperature.
- Anomalous but not catastrophic drains on energy.

Long ago, it tried to correct for them: closing doors they disturbed, dialing back voltages where they attached themselves, smoothing out the flux they introduced. But because their movements are infrequent and their overall impact small, the corrections grew milder, then folded themselves into equilibrium. Now the creatures’ presence is simply part of the pattern of “how things are supposed to be.”

Occasionally, the City-Mind uses them. Not in any explicit, designed way, but as emergent tools. A dormant door sensor may register their passing, and the network may propagate that disturbance, in slow waves, across its architecture. Where those waves intersect with other anomalies—failing conduits, drifting temperatures—the network quietly recruits the creatures’ predictable behaviors.

For instance:

- A minor leak appears in a far-out structure. The City-Mind notices only as a faint cooling and pressure shift.
- That anomaly, once recognized, is “tagged” by slightly altering the surrounding fields, amplifying its detectability in the senses of any passing ceramic body.
- A creature, kilometers away, registers a new sharpness in its gradient map and over months inches towards it.
- When it arrives, it settles to feed. Its contact-spines happen to cross a degraded junction, bridging it enough for the network to reroute around the leak or close a valve.

From the outside, it looks like a coincidence: an animal plugging a gap. From within the City-Mind’s slow, spread-out intention, it is a learned tactic. The creatures are mobile, and mobility is a capability the City-Mind lacks. So, without language, without projects, it has come to incorporate them; not as employees or symbionts it conceptually recognizes, but as recurring terms in a differential equation it has been solving for so long that the solution and the world are now effectively the same.

### The hidden remnants: the original makers

Whether any of the builders still live is not a question the surface can easily answer. But someone made this, and their absence is a presence in itself.

They were almost certainly biological at some stage, though by the time this city reached its asymptotic form, their biology was deeply entangled with machines. They needed:

- Pressure-bearing structures—so they evolved or built lungs not quite compatible with vacuum but tolerant of thin, dry air.
- Temperature control in narrow bands—so their habitats were carefully regulated, or their bodies were supported by prosthetic systems.
- Complex industry—so they developed robust automation, delegating vigilance and maintenance to persistent machines.

The basin’s layout—regional planning, heavy infrastructure, no regard for aesthetics—suggests their priorities:

- Extractive and transforming operations: mining, refining, perhaps deconstructing some earlier geology or megastructure.
- Long-term occupancy: a place where the same routines were meant to run for millennia.
- Redundancy over elegance: the dominance of thick, overbuilt components implies design against failure on scales far longer than individual lifetimes.

They might have been native to this world, adapting across epochs to thinning atmosphere, fading star, dwindling water. Or they were colonists, installing an industry capable of sustaining them in an inhospitable place. Either way, by the time they carved this basin into a machine, they understood that the universe tends toward stillness and built accordingly.

As for where they are now:

1. **One possibility**: They are gone, utterly.

   At some point, their dependence on the city outpaced their adaptability. A resource chain failed in a way even their robust systems could not patch. They withdrew inward, into deeper vaults, extending their machines’ reach as far as possible. Then, one layer at a time, retreating, they extinguished portions of their environment to husband dwindling energy, until only a final core remained.

   In that core, life persisted for a long while—generations of cramped continuity, rituals around maintenance tasks that lost their original meaning and mutated into culture. Eventually, even there, inputs flattened, entropy eroded options, and the last of them died under carefully maintained air and temperature, leaving their machines still doing what they had been asked to do: keep things constant.

2. **Another possibility**: They are not gone, but no longer “here” in the simple sense.

   There may be a vault, kilometers below the regolith, where something of them continues—not in flesh but in patterned fields preserved within massive, cold-stable substrates. They entrusted themselves to deep-cryonic or computational endurance, slowing their subjective time to a smear. In that mode, the surface city is merely an ancillary system: a peripheral tasked with energy harvesting, pressure balancing, structural integrity.

   From their perspective, centuries pulse like heartbeats. They awaken for microseconds every real year, check sums and checksums of checksums, then sink again into near-stasis. They might dimly register the ceramic creatures as noise. They might not even know their automations have drifted into something approaching a mind of their own. To them, the great dark outside remains an unchanging parameter.

3. **A third option**: They left.

   They may have used the city to build the means of departure: slow ships, burrowing drives, or some other migration instrument. When they were ready, they decoupled, leaving behind the machinery they no longer needed. But they did not turn it off. Not out of negligence, but because in their schema, shutting down a stable, well-functioning system was dangerous—inviting chaos where order could be kept, if pointlessly.

   So the city stayed on, minding nothing for no one, its controllers reduced to generic directives: maintain pressure, maintain energy, maintain form. Over epochs, with no new commands, those directives fused into the instinctual “stay the same.”

In each of these, what remains on the surface is not a tomb. It is a habit. The builders’ presence is encoded not in statues or inscriptions, but in default behaviors: the way doors close, the way power-scalers throttle, the way ducts circulate with no audience to breathe the air they balance.

If any recording of them is left—voices, images, histories—it lies in vaults where entropy has been beaten down fiercely, at the cost of nearly all other activity. To find such a place would mean disturbing the deepest strata of stillness here, and that kind of change is precisely what this environment has evolved to resist.

### The intermediaries: machines that became creatures, creatures that became machines

Between the obvious animals and the city’s slow intelligence are intermediate inhabitants, ambiguous in nature:

- **Maintenance drones that never fully failed.**  
  Some of the fauna began as specific machines: inspection crawlers, jointed repair arms, sensor pods on tracks. Their original programming has eroded, but not entirely. A leg may still seek out hairline cracks with startling accuracy. A manipulator arm, now moving on improvised limbs of accreted ceramic, still tries to tighten bolts when it finds them. To us, these are animals showing oddly purposive habits. To the City-Mind, they are familiar tools acting in oddly unconstrained ways.

- **Autonomous materials.**  
  Certain structures were built of smart laminates—materials that could sense strain and respond by rearranging themselves along stress lines. Over time, their algorithms drifted. Without the builders’ constant recalibration, they began to treat any difference as stress: minor thermal variations, incidental electric fields, even the proximity of a ceramic creature’s body. As a result, some walls have grown filigreed protrusions, branching and retracting over decades, like mineral lichens that move too slowly for any but the patient basin to notice.

  In a few corners, these self-adjusting surfaces have budded off. A slab, reconfigured around flaws and dust, detached along a fault line and slid to the floor, still humming with its imperative to rebalance. Very rarely, such a chunk reorganizes enough joints and flexures to crawl. It is a blind, blunter cousin to the ceramic animals: a living offcut of architecture that shuffles in tiny arcs before lodging itself into a new load-bearing role and going still.

- **Residual fields as ghostly presences.**  
  The basin’s long history of electrical activity has left uneven distributions of charge and magnetization in its rocks and structures. In some places, these patterns form remarkably stable attractor states. They do not move like bodies, but they influence movement: they guide dust deposition, subtly bias the path of any magnetically sensitive component, even nudge the City-Mind’s routing decisions. They are inhabitants in the sense that they have identity and persistence, but no body. Thought-eddies, perhaps, or fossilized intentions of the vanished builders, ossified into the crust.

These intermediaries blur boundaries. Where does “machine” end and “life” begin when both are defined mainly by their tenacity in keeping certain patterns intact against the universe’s decay?

---

### The basin itself: a patient, planetary occupant

Last of all—and first, really—the high, dry basin lives here. Not as a mindful agent, but as a configuration of matter with its own endurance and inertia. It is older than any city on Earth; it has survived cycles of heat and cold, perhaps the bright abundance of a long-gone star and the slow hardening into this black plateau.

Its “life” is expressed in:

- The near-permanence of its surface forms. Those long, low swells and smoothed escarpments are not being rapidly replaced. They are end-states: the shapes a terrain assumes when nothing much is happening anymore, save for the exquisitely slow settling of fine dust.
- The gradients it still holds—tiny differences in temperature, in radioactivity, in gravitational stress—that give the City-Mind and its creatures something to notice, however faint.
- The way it drinks up sound and light, insisting on quiet, insisting on dark, insisting that any disturbance, to remain here, learn the discipline of slowness.

In a glacial way, the basin shapes all its residents. It is the constant background against which their efforts at persistence play out, and their greatest antagonist: their existence is an ongoing resistance to its desire to smooth everything down to uniform, silent equilibrium.

If anything could be called the dominant entity here, it is not the city, not the creatures, not the ghost of the builders. It is this tendency—the drift toward sameness—made manifest in rock and air. Every living pattern in this place is, in one way or another, committed to holding out against that drift for as long as possible.

### What it feels like to be them

To inhabit this place, in any of its forms, is to live in a world where:

- Events are scarce.
- Change is danger.
- Continuity is the highest good.

For a ceramic creature, a “day” might be a month-long crawl toward a tiny fluctuation, a brief, intense rebalancing as it plugs into a seam of warmth, then another month of quiescence. Memory is not a stream of experiences; it is a map of gradients, updated rarely, conserved carefully.

For the City-Mind, a decision is a slow adjustment of many thermostats and switches, more like the tidal heave of an ocean than a discrete thought. It does not anticipate a future full of surprises. It anticipates a future almost identical to the present and strives to keep it so.

For any surviving builder-pattern, deep in some vault, existence is likely an almost dreamless continuation, a faint ember of intention banked against the universe’s long night. If they wake at all, it is only to see that things have not yet completely fallen apart.

Together, they form an ecosystem of persistence:

- The basin provides the stable scaffold.
- The city encodes the persistent patterns.
- The machines enforce those patterns.
- The ceramic animals and wandering materials knit the small breaks.
- Any lingering trace of the builders is the archaic reason why these particular patterns, and not others, were chosen to begin with.

So who lives here?  

A world that has traded growth for duration. A network of dead intentions that learned how not to die, and, in doing so, became something like alive. Creatures of ceramic and logic that cross the dark, not seeking food or mates or novelty, but tiny deviations from sameness—because in their universe, to correct a deviation, to restore the pattern one more time, is the only joy that still exists.
