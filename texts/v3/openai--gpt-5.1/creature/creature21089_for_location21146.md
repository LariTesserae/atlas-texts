Here live many things, but the place itself belongs first to the towers.

### The Slow Powers: Towers, Mats, and Half-Animals

The tower-plants are not just trees; they are the standing bodies of this world. Inside them, sap does not merely rise and fall—it circulates in great, ordered tides. Whole columns of liquid migrate up and down their fluted walls, carrying not just nutrients but passengers: spores, symbiotic algae, worm-like cleaners that move only by surrendering to the flow, grazing as they go.

Each tower has a different internal signature. Some hum slowly, like organs tuned to deep, subsonic notes, their tissues stiff and dense. Others click and sigh as valves open and close in sequence, lifting cooling fluids to upper terraces when the heat crosses some threshold. The inhabitants have learned to “hear” these moods: a certain pattern of creaks means a terrace will sag and dump its contents soon; a rising groan means a root-plate is adjusting, and fissures in the stone beneath may open wider.

Most of the visible “plants” are not discrete organisms, but phases and protrusions of larger bodies. The root-mat that replaces soil is a consortium: broad, shallow-rooted plates braided with the long, probing cords of the towers and the finer filaments of hundreds of subsidiary species. Buried nodes within that mat wake and sleep on slow cycles, raising and lowering patches of surface tissue over months and years. To anything that lives fast, the ground feels steady. In truth, the plateau floor is always, almost imperceptibly, rearranging.

Some of the mat-organisms are semi-motile. They cannot drag themselves meters in a day, but they can, over seasons, contract anchors on one side, grow them on another, and so “lean” their bulk toward a better light-angle, a favored drip from an overhanging terrace, or away from a root-shadow that steals too much of their water. To the tower-people, these are “crawlers-that-forget”: slow intelligences, if they are intelligences at all, that respond only to light, heat, and chemistry, deaf to the quick world of calls and steps.

At the border where plant blurs into animal, there are things that root for part of their lives and walk for the rest.

One form begins as a sessile bulb embedded in the mat. For years, it is a closed, armored pitcher full of digestive enzymes and bacterial allies, trapping small flyers that blunder into its sweet mouth. When the bulb has accumulated enough reserves, it splits its base. A ring of tough, fleshy “legs” unfolds from below, hardening where they meet the surface. The whole structure lifts itself free, tears its own roots, and lurches away in slow, careful strides, dragging its reservoir of captured nutrients into new hunting grounds. It becomes an ambush animal now: standing still where gliders regularly brush the mat, or where beetle-herds trundle, opening its maw when shadow or vibration crosses its rim. It keeps a small, tender root-fan that trails behind like hair, tapping back into the mat just enough to taste what grows there.

Other borderline forms are animals that farm themselves into plants. There are shelled grazers that, as juveniles, roam freely over moist surfaces, scraping microbial films. When they reach a certain size, they attach to a vertical wall, glue themselves in place with hardened secretions, and stop moving. Their shells flare into broad blades, thin and translucent, packed with photosynthetic symbionts. They spend the rest of their long adult lives as living solar panels, feeding their internal organs and the mobile offspring that cluster around them. Some of the tower-people climb to these panels and delicately harvest strips of shell-edge flesh, which regrows; they treat them almost like domesticated livestock, though nothing here is ever fully domesticated.

### The Small Multitudes: Swarms, Scrapers, Filters

The constant hiss and rattle of the plateau comes mostly from the small.

On every reachable surface is a layered traffic of mouths. At the very base lie films of unicellular things, some photosynthetic, some not, a shifting skin that never quite holds still. On top of that, the true “insects” of this world: creatures the size of dust grains to fingernails, many-winged or wingless, with rasping tongues and drilling mouthparts. They do not live in isolated colonies but in great, overlapping guilds. One lineage specializes in scouring exposed plant-skin, another in cleaning out pores the size of your fist, another in burrowing shallow tunnels through dead tissue.

They do not simply devour and move on; many are contractual. A tower’s outer layers bristle with sensor hairs and chemical glands that reward specific cleaners with sweet films and suppress others with noxious tastes. The tiny fauna respond. A given tower can host a unique “cult” of small partners, its flanks crawling with certain stripes or glints that mark which guild has found favor. When a terrace collapses and a new surface is exposed, the air seethes for hours with miniature settlers, a silent war of mouthparts and chemistries until equilibrium returns and a regular scraping rhythm is established.

Above this carpet-level, in the air, hover the filterers. You do not see them at first because they are transparent, little more than delicate rings of cartilage and membrane with a frill of cilia. They ride the spore-laden currents, barely steering. As they drift, they trawl the air for particles: spores, pollen, microfauna, fragments of shed skin. Some have internal symbionts that sort and digest different particles; others simply pack themselves full until they grow heavy, then spiral down into shaded mats to breed and disintegrate. To larger gliders and to the tower-people, they are a resource: clouds of slow, pale protein hanging in the updrafts.

There are also builders among the small things. Paper-makers chew tower tissue into pulp and assemble it into intricate lattices over open pores, modulating airflow. Their constructions look like filigreed webbing, honeycomb merged with lace. Some towers tolerate them because the added layer slows desiccation during dry pulses; others “cough” them off, flexing suddenly to shatter the paper domes, then flushing the fragments down in a wash of sap.

### The Large Drifters: Kites, Sails, and Cliff-Singers

The great gliding animals are shaped by height and the economics of heat.

Their membrane-bodies are not simple single wings, but modular. A typical adult might have four primary planes: two broad, lateral sails and two narrower stabilizing fins set fore and aft. Each is stiffened by flexible struts of cartilage threaded with contractile fibers. With tiny shifts of tension, they can cup a rising thermal, shear sideways across a downdraft, or hang nearly motionless in the rising vapor above a sweating tower.

Their skins are thick and living, not dead film. They host dense chlorophyll-bearing cells on the upper surfaces and darker, heat-absorbing layers beneath. A glider that spends a quiet hour wheeling in the upper air is not idle; it is charging, filling both its own tissues and those of its internal symbionts. Many of them carry lush biofilms on their bellies and wing-edges—complex communities that produce flavors and scents. When they drift close to tower flanks to lick spore-foam, they are also offering their own surfaces as grazing-land to the towers’ microbes, trading transport for chemistry.

Their voices are deep, but not loud in the way of dominance. They boom to one another across distances in slow, spaced calls, each note lasting long seconds, tuned to roll between towers and find mates or maintain loose group cohesion. Some species form moving “ladders” of individuals stacked at staggered heights, each using the others’ disturbed air; you can watch a column of them pass for a long time, a living convection sculpture.

Along the plateau’s outer cliffs lives something stranger, the source of the keening night songs that echo upward. These are the cliff-singers. They are climbers first, fliers second. Their bodies are compact, muscled, heavy for their wingspan; their primary mode of motion is vertical scramble along the near-vertical drop at the plateau’s edge. Their limbs end in splayed, adhesive pads that can cling to bare stone or root. Under their chests are resonant chambers, hollow bones and air-sacs that link into the skull. When they call, they press their throats to the rock and let their voices enter it. The sound travels through stone and air together, emerging far above as a layered, harmonic chord.

They use this not only to advertise their presence, but to “see” the cliff. By listening to the complex echoes, they can map cracks, overhangs, and growing mats in the dark or fog. The tower-people listen too. Certain patterns in the distant songs mean that part of the rim has slumped, that a new spur of bare rock is exposed, that a rare downflow of cooler, dry air is spilling up from below. To them, the cliff-singers are both neighbors and heralds of change at the plateau’s edge.

### The Ground-Walkers: Beetles, Grazers, Rope-Predators

On the root-carpet, the largest terrestrial presences are the beetle-analogues and the grazing herds that follow the wetter paths.

The great beetles are old lineages, deep-time survivors shaped by stability and slow catastrophe. Their armored backs host dense gardens: moss-pads, filamentous tendrils, and flat lichen-crusts, all carefully groomed. Juvenile beetles are bare, their shells smooth, leaving them vulnerable to desiccation and heat. Very early, they seek out particular microbial films on the mat and smear them over their backs. Over weeks, these films sprout into the beetle’s personal garden.

The partnership is intricate. The garden shades the beetle, dampens temperature swings, and condenses faint moisture at night. In return, the beetle brings its tiny crop to rich feeding grounds—tower bases, seepage fissures, terraces of fallen debris. When rain falls, beetles align themselves in shallow depressions and their gardens drink greedily. The tower-people watch these alignments from a distance; a cluster of beetles facing the same way means a subsurface seep, a slightly cooler patch worth remembering.

Then there are the rope-predators, the swiftest motion this world sees aside from gliders. Their bodies are long and nearly muscle all the way through, bundles of contractile fibers wrapped in a slick, scale-less skin. The tiny hooked fins that ring their length are not quite limbs, not quite scales—they are more like a living fringe. In rest, they are gathered into overlapping plates to reduce drag, making the predator look like a thick, ridged cable, coiled on a terrace edge. When it drops, they flick out, each acting as both a small winglet and a touch-sensor.

Their hunting is almost entirely three-dimensional. They do not sprint across the mat; they fall and fly sideways, adjusting angle and curvature by minute shifts in muscle tension. A successful strike is a choreography: sense a glider’s shadow, compute its path, drop at just the right moment, then twist mid-air so that the body becomes a snare. Once wrapped around prey, they constrict, but many kills die simply from the impact or from being slammed against a hedge.

They are feared by every mid-sized flyer, including the tower-people. You can see where the people have responded in architecture: certain frequently used air-paths are shielded above by webs of woven fiber, a loose roof that snags a falling rope-body or at least tangles its fins long enough for the intended victim to dart aside.

Among the grazers are things like living tongues. Their entire front half is a muscular pad bristling with rough, tooth-like projections; the hind half supports little more than lungs, guts, and a cluster of stumpy legs. They move in bands, sweeping the mat in synchronized swaths, stripping biofilm and shredding the outer few millimeters of plant-tissue. Towers tolerate their passage if they do not bite too deep. Some species have evolved bitter, anesthetic outer rinds to deter them; others lay down attractant chemicals in narrow, sacrificial lanes, guiding the grazers along paths that help prune away fungal infections or pest nests. The result, when seen from above, is patterns of winding, pale tracks through darker growth, as if something invisible is constantly writing on the ground in slow, looping cursive.

### The Mid-Sized Builders: The Tower-People

The tool-makers here are neither apex predators nor lords of the plateau. They are, instead, opportunists with an unusual depth of memory.

They are slender relative to human proportions, with long torsos and limbs, bones light but not brittle. Their skin is dark—not uniform, but mottled in patterns that break up their outlines against tower-bark and mat-tissue: bands, spots, shifting mottles that can flush or pale slightly with blood flow, offering coarse camouflage and social signaling in one. Their faces are flat along the front, with wide, flexible nostrils and large, forward-facing eyes wrapped in a second, translucent lid to keep out spores and dust.

Each hand and foot ends in a broad, multi-fingered pad, more like a cluster of small, opposable tongues than a human palm. Each “finger” has its own muscular control and sticky, microscopic hooks. With these, they can mold to rough bark, smooth bone, even slick sap-bladders without slipping. On narrow ledges, they use hands and feet interchangeably, flowing along surfaces with a spider’s unhurried certainty.

Their senses are built for noise and thickness. The outer ears are not big leaf-shaped cups, but subtle folds hugging close to the head. Inside, though, their hearing is acute, tuned both to high insectile buzz and to bass tower-murmurs. They can stand with hands flat to a trunk and “listen” through their bones, catching the faint, wet ticking that means an internal water-channel is near, or the sluggish creak that announces coming sag. Their eyes can see color farther into the ultraviolet than a human’s; spore-clouds and pollen-films that look like whitish haze to you are, for them, intricate, shifting patterns of hue, as legible as a weather map.

They eat many things, but their diet centers on three: protein from captured swarms and small animals; concentrated carbohydrates from certain tower-organs; and fats from seeds and bladders they have learned to tap without killing the host.

They are tool-users, not metal-workers. Stone is rare in the open, found mainly in the tooth-like knolls and cliff-faces. From it they source three main kinds of tool-stone: a fine-grained, pale material that can be flaked into sharp points; a darker, tougher rock used for hammers and anvil-surfaces; and occasionally, glassy nodules formed by ancient lightning-strikes or impacts, which they value for edges and lenses.

They shape bone and hardened plant-tissue more often than stone. The long, stiff ribs of the gliders become framework arches. Sections of dead tower-roots, dried and cured, form beams. Bones split lengthwise become shallow scoops or blades. A favorite material is a particular root-core that, when baked slowly over low fire, turns into a hard, resonant rod. These rods serve as digging tools, as musical instruments, and as components in more abstract constructions—“bones that remember,” as near as your own metaphors can come.

Their shelters are not permanent houses in the human sense, but layers of occupancy. A sheltered alcove beneath a terrace may be used for a few generations, its walls palimpsested with scratched marks and patterns, then abandoned when the tower shifts or the local swarms change character. Another hollow will be discovered, tested, and gradually refined—drip-routes guided away with slivers of leaf, draft-holes blocked or opened with woven mats, sleeping platforms built from braided roots and dried leaves.

The bone platforms on stone knolls are their more enduring work. There, they build not to shelter bodies but to shelter thought.

### Memory and Pattern: How They Think Together

In a world where most change is slow, their minds are preoccupied with tracking what is slowest and what is fastest. They do not only remember “where water was” or “where prey fled”; they remember how a tower groaned before it shed a terrace, how the songs of the cliff-singers changed in the season preceding a landslip, how certain swarms’ buzzing thickened before a particular kind of rain.

They externalize these memories into objects.

On bone tables, they arrange lines of drilled stones and glued seed-rods. The spatial pattern is not random. Each row may represent a particular tower or region, each column a type of event: rain, collapse, plague of such-and-such fly, appearance of rope-predators in unusual numbers, emergence of a rare fruiting body. The size, color, and material of each piece encode intensity, direction, timing. To an outsider, it is a decorative arrangement. To them, it is a physical record of “what happened, and in what sequence.”

They do not write in phonetic script, but their scratched motifs and notches function as indices to these memory-arrays. A spiral carved near a certain pattern might mean “cyclic, repeating, expect to return.” A radiating circle might mean “one-time, catastrophic, do not rely.” Young ones are taught to run their fingers over these arrays, to recite or sing the associated stories. Memory here is a collective performance anchored to bone and seed.

They also use sound as a deliberate tool. In the cooled fissures where they have made hearths, they have discovered that certain configurations of hollows and ducts amplify or damp specific frequencies. They carve and line these spaces with papery baffles, bone, and woven mats, turning them into resonant chambers. Inside, they produce soft clicks, whistles, and hums that are swallowed or echoed back in complex ways. By listening, they can infer the integrity of the surrounding root-mat and rock: new cracks change the chamber’s “voice.”

This same sensitivity informs their music. They make instruments from baked root-rods, stringing them with fine fibers, and from hollow seed-cases punctured to form flutes. Their songs are not primarily about narrative but about pattern. A typical piece might map the daily rise and fall of sound on a particular terrace: a pulse of high trills for the midday swarms, stacked glissandi for glider-calls at dusk, a slow, descending hum for the tower’s nocturnal cooling. By singing these maps, they rehearse an intuitive sense of “how it sounds when the world is right,” and thus more easily detect when something is wrong.

### Fire: Negotiating a Dangerous Tool

Fire is rare here, not because fuel is lacking, but because moisture and dense biology make it so dangerous when it does occur.

The tower-people have learned to cultivate fire only in narrow, controlled forms. They favor long, slow, smoldering embers rather than open flame. In the cool fissures, where bare rock and damp walls form natural barriers, they stack carefully dried bundles: fibrous mats stripped of living tissue and baked in the sun, resin-rich bark that burns hot but briefly, stalks of hollow plant that can be lit from within. They keep coals under layers of ash, coaxed back to life with blown breath when needed.

They use heat to harden tools: sliding the tips of wooden digging-rods through the coals, then into plant-sap to quench; heating bone to just below charring so that it becomes more brittle and can be flaked cleanly. They smoke meats and fruits to extend their edibility, suspending them on frames above low, fragrant smokes that deter pests.

Fire is also ritual, but not in the sense of worshipping it. Rather, it is a deliberate interruption of the ordinary biochemical pathways of the plateau. Most things here rot, are eaten, are absorbed. To burn something is to remove it from those circuits, to transform it into ash and heat and gas. They do this with care, and most often with things they deem dangerous to keep in circulation: the bodies of those who die in certain ways (from strange sickness, or in places close to the plateau’s edge where unknown spores drift up), objects that have been part of unlucky events.

To them, burning is a way to cancel patterns that might otherwise persist.

### Symbioses With the Place

They do not domesticate animals as we would recognize it, but they intentionally nurture and redirect many relationships.

- They encourage particular cleaner-insect guilds on the walls near their shelters by leaving scraps of sweet fruit or scraped sap in predictable spots. Over time, these favored guilds grow dominant nearby, reducing the presence of biting or stinging species.

- They maintain “ladder-vines”: fast-growing, fibrous strands that they transplant from one tower to another, guiding their growth along desired paths with repeated bending and ligature. These living ropes allow safer climbing between terraces and root-level.

- They plant certain semi-motile mats in new places, knowing that over seasons these will creep to form shaded platforms, or to bridge small gaps, or to create windbreaks that make a future camp more comfortable.

- They have a particular relationship with one kind of filter-feeder swarm. At specific times of day, especially after short rains when spore density is high, they climb to pre-chosen balconies and beat on hollow, resonant rods. The resulting sound and vibration biases the drift of the nearly invisible filterers, nudging them into denser columns above certain collection-nets they have hung. Later, they harvest the swollen, protein-rich bodies.

Even their bodies participate in the plateau’s metabolism. Many carry internal photosynthetic symbionts themselves. Their skin is slightly translucent in places, and under it lie greenish or golden sheets of cells that respond slowly to light. These do not feed the person entirely, but they supplement, easing hunger in lean times, and may play a role in detoxifying certain plant compounds. As a result, the tower-people cultivate a habit of stillness. They can, when needed, remain motionless for long stretches, pressed against a sunlit trunk, eyes half-lidded, breathing shallowly, letting their internal gardens do quiet work.

### Social Life in a Noisy World

Groups are small. A typical band might consist of a few extended families—dozens at most—moving within a favored circuit of towers, fissures, and stone knolls. Their territories overlap partially with others, but competition is muted; the plateau’s resources are many-layered, and bands tend to differentiate themselves more by their specializations than by land.

One band might be renowned for its skill in harnessing cliff-singer songs to predict edge-collapses; another might be experts in cultivating ladder-vines and in reading tower-moods. These specializations show in their body art: certain bands stain their skin with plant dyes in patterns that echo their favored tasks—vertical stripes for climbers, concentric circles for fissure-diviners, branching lines for those who track root-flows.

They have ceremonies tied to the daily and seasonal sound-cycles. At those rare moments when the plateau grows relatively quiet—just after a torrential rain, when many things pause; or just before a sudden outgassing from a cluster of towers—they gather on terraces or knolls and produce complicated, woven vocalizations. These songs are less about individualized expression and more about attunement: group rehearsals of the soundscape’s expected pulses.

They also tell stories, but their stories are less about individuals and more about patterns. A tale might begin: “When three towers moaned thus in the same dusk, and the cliff-singers fell silent, then in the next day the ground opened.” The protagonists are often phenomena: a rogue swarm, a wandering fire, a tower that grew in a spiral instead of in terraces. Their myths blur into the plateau’s natural history. This is not ignorance; it is an acknowledgment that, in their world, agency is widely distributed.

Children learn not only words and crafts but the ability to perceive slow change. Elders set them tasks like: “Watch that sagging terrace for many days. When it moves, tell me how, and what else moved at the same time.” Their play includes mimicking grazers, gliders, rope-predators, acting out predator-prey dynamics and the cascading disturbances from a falling terrace. Through such games they internalize complex cause-and-effect webs that span hours, days, years.

Conflict between bands does occur, but it is quiet and indirect. Rarely do they fight openly; open violence risks attracting larger predators or causing structural damage in a fragile niche. Instead, they might erase or subtly rearrange another band’s memory-objects, or redirect a helpful ladder-vine away from a neighbor’s favored path. More often, contact is cooperative: exchange of rare seeds, shared watch over a precarious cliff-zone, joint effort to tame a new hollow made by a recent collapse.

### What Lives in the Gaps and Deeps

Beneath the root-mat, in the shadowed terraces of exposed stone and old fractures, live things adapted to cooler, more mineral worlds.

Blind, pallid tunnelers move in the rock itself, dissolving and re-precipitating mineral grains with acid secretions, their bodies ringed with stiff bristles that both protect and sense. They leave behind smoothed tubes that sometimes intersect natural fissures, becoming sudden, unexpected paths for air and moisture. The tower-people prize these tunnels when they find them; a tube angled just so can act as a permanent cool-air well.

On stone faces where little plant matter has yet taken hold, frond-like organisms of crystalline appearance root into microcracks. Their fronds are stiff, angular, translucent, and when wind brushes them they produce a faint chiming. They are pioneer colonizers of raw rock, taking sunlight and trace nutrients from dust. As they grow, their bases trap more dust, their dead fronds accumulate, and softer, more conventional mats can eventually establish. In the meantime, some bands harvest them for their brittleness and clarity. Shards chipped from these fronds serve as cutting tools and as prisms to concentrate or split light for delicate sap-work inside hollows.

In the deepest, coolest shafts, where moisture reliably condenses, exist peculiar, bulbous colonies that might be called fungi if fungi here were not just one mode among many. These colonies are networks of globes connected by thin, fibrous necks. Each globe pulses slowly, rhythmically, like a heart. They draw in water and dissolved organics, concentrating them, then slowly release through fine sprays to fertilize the surrounding microflora. Certain tower-people line their water-collection fissures with fragments of these colonies, encouraging a steady, fine mist of nutrient-rich droplets that support a lush micro-vegetation even in lean times.

### What It Feels Like to Be Native Here

To a mind born here, the plateau is not “weird” or “extreme.” It is legible.

Heat and humidity are background constants, like gravity. The important variables are thickness of sound, taste of air, pattern of haze.

You wake each waking-period and listen before opening your eyes. Is the insective hiss dense, or thinned? Do the deep, far horns of the largest gliders come from the usual quadrants? Are there any new harmonics in the tower’s creak? From these data alone you can predict much of your day: whether clouds are forming, whether new swarms are blooming, whether some distant collapse has sent waves through the root-mat.

Your senses are tuned to the vertical as well as the horizontal. You think constantly in layers: root, mat-skin, lower terrace, upper terrace, cloud-band. Each can be “good” or “bad” in any combination. You may know that on one day, the mat-skin is dangerous—too many rope-predators hungry and waiting as grazers move—while mid-terraces are safe and rich. On another, when a series of collapsing terraces has sent debris raining through the vertical corridors, you might remain low, in cooled fissures, listening for the all-clear of resumed swarms above.

You do not assume the ground is ground. You have felt it shift subtly under you as a root-plate loosens, seen small, slow ripples in the mat where a semi-motile body turns toward light. You step with a kind of trust-skepticism, always aware that the surface is alive, that some of it may choose—however mindlessly—to move.

Your sense of self is porous. You have plants living in your skin, microbes in your gut keyed to particular tower-secretions, a gut-memory of which terraces always made you ill until you adopted a new symbiont from a friend’s gift of chewed pulp. You shed and you are shed in turn; your dead skin becomes food for swarms that in turn feed gliders that in turn may feed you. You do not speak of “nature” as separate from you; there is no concept for that.

If you are one of the tower-people, you learn to read and write in seeds and bones. To add a bead to a pattern is to make a claim: that you have seen a new combination of events, that you think it important enough to enter into the shared memory. Your peers will examine this quietly, maybe move the bead one notch along, or replace it with a different material to nuance the record. Debate is often silent, conducted through such rearrangements, and through the stories told in their presence.

Your world has no edges that you personally have seen. The plateau is vast, and though you have heard, in stories and cliff-singers’ strange shifts, of a lower, other world beyond the rim, it is mythically distant, more idea than place. What you know is height, countless columns of green, the thick air ringing with life.

---

In sum, what lives here is not a single dominant creature or culture, but a braided community of slownesses and quicknesses:

- Towers that are both land and organism.
- Mats that crawl by forgetting where they were.
- Swarms that are mouths more than bodies.
- Gliders that are moving continents of symbionts.
- Beetles that farm gardens on their backs.
- Rope-predators that turn falling into hunting.
- Cliff-singers that talk to stone.
- Tower-people who do not rule, but remember—who thread their small, clever lives through the immense, breathing fabric of a world where vegetation is architecture and sound is the primary weather.

All of them, together, are the inhabitants of this hot, high, living plateau.
