They are latecomers to their own catastrophe.

Whoever lives here now occupies a world they helped make and then failed to complete. They are not ancient in the sense of weathered elders, but ancient in intent: beings who began a large, slow project, misjudged its consequences, and now live inside the aftermath like animals inside the mechanism of a stalled clock.

They are tall in the gravity that once ruled this place. Under water, that translates into long frames and a certain leanness, not because of starvation but because weight no longer supports them. Bones that began thick and load‑bearing now persist as compact pillars inside a more flexible body, sheathed in tendon and slow muscle. The limbs that mattered most in air—legs, the coil of spine, the hinge of shoulder—are all still there, but extended, splayed, adapted to a new medium.

From a distance, they would read first as vertical: pillars of muted color moving through the dusk. Closer, you would see the articulation.

Their torsos are deep rather than broad, ribs arcing wide to house lungs that have become less about short, sharp breaths and more about pressure management and storage. In the time since the flood, they have not fully abandoned air; instead, they have turned their own interiors into mixed environments. Pockets of trapped gas remain inside them, held in reinforced sacs branching off from the old lungs. They surface rarely, if ever, now; their air is recirculated, metabolically refreshed, a dwindling but persistent remnant of the old world they once moved through.

Because of that, their heads are neither fishlike nor wholly terrestrial. The face is short and armored with fine, interlocking plates of cartilage and calcified skin—something between keratin and coral. Where a nose once took in air, now there are narrow, valved slits that open and close as needed, regulating pressure and the faint exchange of gases with the surrounding water. The mouth is low and wide, capable of opening into a broad, almost circular intake or pinching down to a narrow slit to nibble. Teeth exist, but not as a single row of biting blades: instead, they have arranged themselves into mosaics of rough, grinding surfaces, good for rasping plant matter off stone or crushing the supple, mineral‑rich organs of sessile creatures.

Their eyes are an uneasy compromise between a world that once had a sky and the new semi‑darkness. They are relatively large, placed forward but with enough lateral spread to see both ahead and slightly above. The iris is almost indistinguishable from the pupil: a broad, dark disk that dilates until only a narrow ring of reflective tissue surrounds it. They do not rely on sharp detail; faces and tools are recognized more by contour and motion than by texture. Around the eyes, filaments grow—delicate, whisker‑thin cilia sensitive to microcurrents and the faint changes in temperature that mean a vent, a breath, another body.

Along their neck and upper ribs, cut into the sides of the torso, lie slits that were once nothing at all and have become crucial: vascular fans pressed thin and close to the surface. In air, they would have been vulnerabilities; in water, they serve as gills of a sort. They do not fully replace the old respiratory system, but augment it—slow, constant extractors of dissolved gases. Blood moves through them in languid pulses; they have tuned their lives to low metabolic rates, to patience.

Their skin remembers rough weather. Even now, under water, it keeps a certain dryness in appearance, as if it were made to resist something that no longer touches it. It is mottled: base colors of stone‑gray, dull umber, dark olive, overlaid with paler streaks that follow the directions of old muscles and tendons. These colors are not fixed. Fine chromatophores embedded beneath the outer layer can darken or lighten regions slowly over minutes, not seconds. Camouflage here is not about sudden vanishing; it is about becoming less noticeable over the length of a small migration, receding into the prevailing dusk as you move.

The hands are the most deliberate part of them.

Each hand has four long fingers and a shorter, offset thumb‑analog, all jointed not in delicate, many‑phalanx segments, but in three thick, powerful sections. The tips are not claws but hardened pads, capable of fine friction against stone. Between the fingers, folds of thin skin can web out when the hand is splayed, giving extra surface for pushing against water—but when they work stone, the webbing pulls taut and vanishes between the callused joints.

The feet are less differentiated. They end in broad, splayed structures that can flatten to push against rock or curl to grip ledges. When they were air‑dwellers, their gait would have been steady, almost ponderous, adapted to steep slopes and carved steps. Under water, those same limbs sweep in slow arcs, a cross between climbing and swimming.

They do not rush. Their world does not reward it.

Language, for them, was once something that could fling itself across open space: calls over canyons, shouts across terraces. The remains of that habit persist in the shape of their throats and jaws, but water blunts it. What carries better here are lower frequencies, sustained, modulated. So spoken language has flattened into drawn‑out hums, pulses, and clicks, layered with gesture. A single exchange might last minutes, notes rising and falling as their bodies drift relative to one another to catch the best angle for sound. Hands speak too: not in quick flashes, but in measured sequences of shapes and positions that can be interpreted slowly, accurately, through veils of fronds and dimness.

For close interaction, they touch.

They are sensitive along their sides, ribs, and forearms to vibration and minute conductance differences in the water. Laying a palm along another’s arm, or pressing flank to flank, is a way of sharing information as tangible as sound—feeling the pattern of another’s pulse, the way their gill‑slits open and close, the micro‑twitches of muscle. Formal greeting and council both likely involve such slow, deliberate contact in enclosed spaces where disturbances in the water can be controlled.

They remember dryness, but not clearly.

The flood that took their city is older than any living individual but younger than the foundations. Their histories span both regimes. There are stories, told and retold, of walking up steps that are now cloaked in draped vegetation, of seeing a sky where now only weight and dim bands of light hang. That remembered world has, with time, been half‑mythologized. For the youngest, “air” is less an everyday element and more a kind of ancestral strangeness—something harsh and restless their forebears endured and briefly mastered before choosing, or being forced, to live within the mountain’s lungs instead.

Because this place has no true outdoors anymore, they think in terms of layers, enclosures, gradients.

Their city, before it drowned, was already built like a layered organism: terraces nested against cliffs, halls tucked behind halls, paths twisting inward rather than outward. They were never plains‑dwellers; their sense of safety came from solid stone hemming in three sides and a controlled opening on the fourth. After the flood, that inclination serves them. They know how to fortify an interior, to take a corridor and turn it into a throat that can be opened or closed, filtered or clogged.

The sealed halls that now serve as inner sanctums or gardens represent the highest expression of this. Over time, they have learned to weave the aquatic vegetation into semi‑permeable membranes, layering mats of fibrous fronds and resin‑sealed rope to create baffles that modulate flow, temperature, and chemistry. Within such spaces, the water is quiet, the light is slightly clearer, and the life inside is curated. Here, they cultivate:

– Dense mats of fine, feathered plants that strip specific minerals from the water and, in doing so, become nutrient‑rich food.

– Bulbous organisms coaxed into domestic forms—descendants of wild nodules and ventside encrustations—whose pulsing bodies they harvest in segments, each piece regrowing over many months.

– Threads of luminous filament, concentrated to create soft, starry light sufficient for work or ritual.

The beings themselves are omnivores tilted heavily toward what the flooded stone provides. They rasp fronds from rock surfaces, cultivate sediment‑trapping mats into edible layers, and, occasionally, take slow animals: the translucent eels, the smaller turtle‑like forms, the finger‑length fish that graze on microbial films. But each kill is an event, planned around. They trade in patience; most days are spent tending, rearranging, shepherding growth.

They are not numerous.

The flood reduced them, first abruptly, then slowly. Families lineages that once spread along multiple terraces are now narrowed to a few small clusters of kin. Each occupied quarter of the city—each surviving “neighborhood” nested on a ledge or within a canyon slot—holds perhaps a dozen, two dozen individuals. Between these enclaves are long tracts of abandoned stone and opportunistic life, traversed only occasionally by lone swimmers or small, cautious groups.

Children are rare and therefore precious, but also slow.

Gestation for them is long. Underwater, with buoyancy aiding the body, the carrying parent—whose sex is not rigid but tied to phases of life and social role—can move more freely than in air, but the metabolic cost of creating new life in a low‑energy world is substantial. Birth is not explosive. Infants emerge already partially adapted: eyes sensitive to dimness, gill‑fans pulsing weakly, limbs capable of clinging to a parent’s torso or back. For months, perhaps years, the young rarely leave the innermost chambers, their sense of the world assembled from muted colors and the patient touch and murmur of elders.

Play, for them, is a slow experimentation: learning to control buoyancy, to press just hard enough against currents to move without losing the feel of the stone under their palms. They chase the faintly luminous nodules at corridor roofs, trace etched lines along old basins with their fingers, imitate the gestures they see in councils. The concept of “running” is absent; instead, there is “rising too quickly,” “drifting without anchor,” both things they are warned against.

The towers and the carved basins in the city speak to an older mind, not erased.

This was never a purely pragmatic people. Even before the flood, they rearranged stone in ways not strictly required by shelter or storage. The tall, narrow towers that still punctuate some shelves—too thin to occupy—were likely not merely decorative. They marked something: vantage points over gradients, or alignments with the paths of distant lights seen once in a while through canyon slits, or resonators for sound. In air, wind might have moaned through their gaps. Under water, their stacked shapes hum faintly when distant tremors pass through the rock, translating slow seismic changes into local whispers.

The basins, smooth and ringed, are older than the flood and older, perhaps, than the immediate memory of why they were first carved. They sit at junctions of paths, at corners where flows meet. In a dry age, they might have held water poured by hand, tracking the rise and fall over days, measuring rainfall or seasonal springs. Under water, they seem useless, but still they are maintained. Algae is cleared from some; others are deliberately left to silt. To the survivors, they have likely become multi‑layered symbols: of cycles, of loss, of the difference between water chosen and water imposed.

If their ancestors truly began to cut the stone for deeper purposes—channeling heat, coaxing minerals out of the mantle—then these living beings see themselves as heirs to a half‑finished pact with the world. The grooves that run inward to warm, mineral‑rich rock are not just scars of an abandoned industry; they are also arteries in a body that has not finished growing. Some enclaves might still tend them: widening a cut here, clearing a clog of precipitated crystals there, regulating the warmth that seeps out into their gardens.

In terms of thought, they are neither survivors clinging to bare existence nor engineers continuing a singular project. They are something in between: a culture committed to honoring both failure and potential.

They do not pretend the flood was entirely natural. Oral memory preserves too many linked facts: bursts of warmth from the depths, the opening of vents, the steady rise of vapor and then water over generations. Some lineages may carry the guilt of having pushed too hard into the stone, of having hoped to harness heat and mineral winds and instead drawn up something they could not manage. Others frame it differently: the mountain itself deciding to swallow them, drawing its walls closed in a slow inhalation.

Ritual, for them, is about negotiation with slowness.

They move to meet cycles that are now more geological than seasonal. Where once there might have been ceremonies for the first dry thunder of a yearly storm, now they cluster around the opening of a new, warm fissure, or the moment when light from above changes character over a period of many days—perhaps because of surface weather they will never directly feel. They align their gatherings with subtle shifts in background murmur, with long‑interval tremors that ripple through the mountain’s body.

Such events bring them out from their enclosures into what were once public plazas and now are open, almost vulnerable spaces. They circle a basin, hands on the etched rings, feeling for tiny differences in water movement. They cling to the lower faces of a tower and listen, eyes closed, to how it vibrates. Their bodies become instruments for detecting change so slow that no single life could otherwise perceive it.

Their ethics are shaped by the weight at all scales.

Water presses on them constantly, a reminder that every void left unfilled will fill, every gap collapses if not braced. They do not easily tolerate waste—not because of moralistic austerity, but because in their environment, anything loose and unpurposed tends to become a hazard. A rope left hanging free will tangle a limb or smother a vent; a slab left unstable will, someday, fall. So they are meticulous about tying off, sealing, wedging. Objects exist in relations more than in isolation. A tool has less meaning as “a thing to own” than as “a thing that holds this stone in place, that weighs down that mat, that lives in this nook for this task.”

Socially, they lean toward consensus made over great lengths of time. In a world where nothing moves quickly without cost, sudden unilateral decisions are viewed as akin to a rockfall—dramatic, dangerous, unpredictable. Council happens in chambers where currents are minimal, around a central feature that reminds them of continuity: a venerable pillar, an ancient basin, a particularly old frond colony. Voices exchange slow patterns of sound, hands mark agreements on stone with new grooves or added lines around basins. When a choice must be made—whether to abandon a terrace, to re‑route a warm plume, to risk contact with another enclave—it is ratified in marks in multiple places, so that memories of it persist distributed through the city.

They are not alone in mind.

Other thinking beings might exist deeper in the drowned basin below, at pressures these upper dwellers dare not endure, but those would be more conjecture than knowledge. What these plateau inhabitants do know are shadows of others: occasional migrations of slow, massive creatures that pass far beneath, sending faint pressure waves through the rock; drifting clusters of luminous organisms whose patterns seem too regular to be purely animal. They read potential intelligence in these things, but at a remove, the way one might see intention in lightning or tides.

Contact between humanlike enclaves within the same city is not constant either. Distances that once were a half‑day’s walk along a canyon rim are now long, careful traverses through murky corridors filled with veiling vegetation and spaces where depth drops away. They make such journeys in pairs or trios, carrying woven bladders of curated water to trade, coiled ropes of specially treated fiber, carved stones inscribed with long‑remembered patterns. When they meet others, there is a formal exchange of touch, of pulse, of slow sound. Information moves this way: not quickly, but with fidelity. Rumors take a generation to cross the whole drowned region. There is no anonymity.

They do not idolize the before. Nor do they reject it. Their city is both ruin and scripture.

Everywhere they look, intention is fossilized in stone: cut steps no one will walk in air again, carved ceilings no longer needing to keep out rain, unfinished right angles leading into mineral‑weeping grooves. They read these as both evidence and lesson. Some groups interpret the prior ambition—to score the world, to redirect heat and matter—as hubristic, a caution against forcing the mountain beyond its capacity. Others see it as noble but incomplete, arguing that their task now is not withdrawal but more subtle continuation: to learn how to guide change without triggering catastrophic floods.

In practice, this means they still build, but differently.

New structures rarely rise tall. Instead, they thicken existing stone: braces against sagging ceilings, wedged supports under shifted terraces, woven reinforcements along crack lines. When they do raise something freestanding, it is often small and richly textured, like a sculpted cairn that serves as a habitat for specific useful lifeforms—a basket of stone for a particular frond species, positioned just so in a vent’s plume.

They also carve, but not deeply. Surface etchings proliferate where they gather: records of council decisions, stylized maps of current flows and warm spots, family lineages rendered as branching grooves. Over time, these markings accumulate into palimpsests. Silt and algae blur the older layers; new generations inscribe over them. The rock becomes a history book that cannot be read quickly, a mass of lines and circles and notches one must learn to navigate the way one learns the currents.

Above all, they have become, in their way, keepers of a young ecology.

They know the timing of the slow exhale and inhale of the water mass—the almost‑tides that barely stir the long curtains of vegetation. They have mapped, in their embodied memory, where each type of life best thrives: which fronds like the cooler, dimmer reaches; which bulbed organisms fatten fastest near faint warmth; which crustaceans will strip clean a surface without gnawing the underlying plant. Their workday is a long sequence of assertive but gentle corrections: thinning mats here, reinforcing them there, nudging a growing stone‑shelled creature away from a delicate architecture, salvaging a fallen block before it buries a cherished basin.

In this sense, they are gardeners of a scale that would have been impossible in air. They do not dominate the system; the water and stone still set the primary terms. But they have become partners with the slow expansion of life over their drowned city, steering it just enough to keep certain corridors open, certain chambers clear, certain gradients of heat and chemical intact.

If you could stand beside one of them, if “standing” still had meaning, you would recognize nothing as familiar culture in the details—no clothing as you know it (only wraps of woven fiber binding tools to limbs, protecting sensate slits), no fire, no skyward gaze. Yet the pattern would be unmistakable.

They are beings who remember that they once changed their world on purpose, and who now live inside the consequences, continuing to choose.

They live in the weight of the water not as prisoners, but as apprentices to it. Their city, half‑made and half‑undone, is their textbook and their shrine. Their children learn to read the slow drift of sediment as others once read clouds. Their elders touch stone warmed from within and feel, through long habit, for any sign that the mountain is shifting again in ways that matter.

What lives here, in the drowned high places, is a people whose native element has become time itself—thick, heavy, and everywhere, pressing in from all sides, asking them to move with it or be ground gradually into silt.
