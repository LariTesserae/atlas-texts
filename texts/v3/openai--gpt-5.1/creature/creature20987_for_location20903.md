They are a people who decided, at some distant hinge in their history, that growth was more trustworthy than construction, and that slowness was a kind of safety.

They have bodies built for this air. Their skin, whatever hues it comes in, tends to the matte and the porous, tolerant of constant damp. Hair is usually cropped or braided close, not for fashion alone but because loose strands mildew here, and every surface is a potential wick. Their clothes are layered and vented: woven from long bast fibers and fungal threads, textured to dry quickly, cut to expose joints and backs-of-knees to the air. Where they interface with the living infrastructure—climbing collars, bark-ladders, root-bridges—they wear sections of resinized padding grown to fit their limbs like a second bark, more graft than gear.

No one is in a hurry. They move with the unforced balance of those who learned to walk young on a substrate that shifts back underfoot. Steps are instinctively testing: heel or toe pressing gently first, letting the root-mat answer before committing weight. You can tell who is native by how rarely they slip, how naturally they use the slight rebound of the ground to ease their joints.

They do not keep hard edges on themselves. There are no armor plates, no gleaming helmets; where they use metal it is wrapped, banded, buried in plant tissue. Bracelets and belts tend to be living—vines guided around a wrist, a loop of grafted cambium across the shoulder that stiffens just enough when a load is slung from it. Tools are worn in holsters that are part of the wearer: a fleshy pocket in the hip-skin if one has consented to that level of biotech, or more modestly, a belt grown from braided aerial roots that continues seamlessly into the bark of a nearby column when they stand to rest.

They are technicians in the way gardeners are technicians: half intimate knowledge, half superstition. Everyone knows a little about the city’s physiology just as everyone knows a little about their own. Children learn to read the color of sap beading on a wound in a structural column—too pale: nutrient deficit upstream; too opaque: fungal overload. They grow up pressing ears to trunks, not to hear romance in the “heartbeat of the city,” but to distinguish between the slow creak of lignin under strain and the off-tempo stutter of a misaligned internal pump.

Profession, as they divide it, aligns less with industries and more with layers of the organism they inhabit.

Closest to the surface are the Tenders. These are the ones whose hands are always stained green or dark with humus, who carry pruners and splice-knives and coils of mycelial cord. They manage the visible forms: trimming a bridge-vine that has grown too thick and begun to distort a walkway, reinforcing an overburdened pillar with a new ring of guided cambium, persuading a cluster of cistern-leaves to fuse more perfectly along their seams. Many of them speak aloud to the tissue they’re cutting—not because they believe the city listens as a mind, but because tone and breath matter; their calm becomes their blade’s steadiness, and any roughness echoes in their own muscles. Their work is slow, often done kneeling in the damp, and they accept that much of it will not show for seasons.

Below them, in both status and literal position, are the Rootwrights. They disappear into tight, humid spaces where the root-mat thickens, threading themselves through crawl-tunnels that are more rhizome than room. Their work is unseen but felt by every footstep: choosing which ancient metal casings to let the roots swallow and which to quarantine; nudging the drainage lines by gently persuading new root-fibers into a particular grain; monitoring the deep, unseen sumps where water and silt accumulate. They are part plumber, part archaeologist, part gravedigger for obsolete machines. Many carry old scars of anaerobic infections: a line along the calf where something in the dark bit or burned, a patch of skin forever paler where a corrosive fungal bloom once took hold. They view the city as a layered text—every cross-section through an old, compressed conduitscape is a story.

Moving through the cambial middle are the Synthesists, custodians of the graft-ports and the internal nerve systems. They are less concerned with leaves and more with signals. You see them where bark thins around a conduit node, palms pressed flat to the faintly luminous plates that mark fiber-optic clusters buried in xylem. They carry looped diagnostic bands that fit snugly around a branch or a wrist, exchanging data through the same biochemical channels that move nutrient and hormone analogues. Their language includes both technical diagrams and an inherited vocabulary of plant metaphors: “latency” is “wintering”; a patch of noisy bandwidth is “stormed phloem.” They calibrate the timing of the city’s light-cycles, the opening and closing of fluid valves, the slow choreography of mechanical digesters hollowed into knots.

Then there are the Machinists, who occupy the niches where metal and leaf share walls. They work in the humming knots: the generator clusters sunk into root-mat, the fermentation bladders cradling machinery like eggs in a nest, the compact mills inside old tree-burls. They are as familiar with the fatigue patterns of vine-fiber belts as with the hairline cracks in an alloy impeller. Their ingenuity lies in making every repair a collaboration rather than a contest: instead of replacing a corroded bracket with a thicker one, they’ll invite a neighboring root to grow through a perforated sleeve, turning the bracket into a living composite. They speak in units of seasons as easily as in hours; a system that will self-correct in three months with a little guided growth is better, in their view, than one that could be brought to peak efficiency today at the cost of perpetual tension.

Above, in the dimmer, leafier volumes, move the Canopyfolk. They are climbers, rope-walkers, those who maintain the high cisterns and the translucent fermentation bladders that hang like ripening fruit. Their shoulders are roped with muscle, fingers calloused where living vines have repeatedly tightened and slackened in their grip. They tend flowers engineered to secrete specific catalysts, coaxing them to drip at just the right rates; they mend the fibrous skins of vats from the outside, patches of new tissue coaxed to grow thick over old micro-tears. They are the ones who see sky most often, in narrow shards, and many of them carry a quiet, private habit of pausing whenever the canopy thins, just to let unfiltered light touch their faces for a breath.

There are others, of course: the Splicers who develop new strains of structural vine or load-bearing fern; the Historians who catalog what lies entombed in the deep mats and the old rings of bark; the few who leave for seasons to negotiate with the drier world outside for metals, dopants, seeds that the basin cannot produce.

Their society has almost no straight lines, physically or politically. Authority flows much like water here: pooled in low places of competence, filtered through layers of custom, redirected around obstructions rather than blasting through them. There are councils rather than councils-of-state: one concerned with the hydrology of the basin, another with the metabolic budget of the city, a third with external trade. Membership in these groups is less an office than a recognized gradient; someone becomes “of the hydrology” because others consult them, because their advice, tested against seasons, proves sound. When their judgment falters over time, people stop climbing the stairs to their knot-chamber, and slowly their influence recedes, not with exile but with disuse.

Law, such as it is, is mostly about flows and thresholds. You do not plug a leak in your own quarter if it will starve the rootfields two levels down of their ration of seepage; you do not introduce a new growth-line without having first grown it in miniature inside an isolated pod and watching it through three rain cycles. Transgression is most often framed as imbalance: you took more light than the canopy above you could spare; you harvested a supporting beam too young; you rerouted power too suddenly for the living conduits to adapt. Sanction is corrective: forced labor in the root-rooms to learn what your actions cost, hours spent listening to the groan of overburdened trunks so that “load” ceases to be abstract.

They have rituals, but they look unassuming to a stranger. When a new child is born, the Tenders of that locality will choose a thin, fast-growing shoot and guide it into a loop around some mundane piece of infrastructure—a stair rail, a low gallery beam. As the child grows, so does the loop, slowly thickening into a living ring that frames a view they pass by a dozen times a day. It isn’t their “own tree” in any proprietary sense; it is simply a mark that their life, too, is being recorded in tissue. When they come of age, they cut a small window in that ring and resin its edges, making a permanent aperture: a reminder that, unlike wholly plant growth, they have the capacity to look out and choose.

Death is not dramatic here. Bodies are not burned; fire is too disruptive, too greedy for oxygen in a place that treats air as another fluid to be shepherded. Instead, the dead are taken down by the Rootwrights into the deep, quiet cavities near the old sumps. There, particular fungi and detritivore plants have been carefully curated for generations. The corpse is wrapped in fiber and set in a cradle of mycelium, and then the living leave. Over months, bones soften, flesh dissolves; over years, the matter that was a person becomes duller soil, then thicker roots, then the faint contribution to the strength of some overlying column. Names are not inscribed in stone; they are associated with regions: “she’s in the south buttress,” “his weight’s in the old spill-terrace.” Remembering someone is, in part, remembering where you step because of them.

There is very little overt religion, but there is reverence. It is directed not toward the city as a conscious god—it has no voice, no clear will—but toward the long continuity of function. They tell stories in which the basin itself is older than their lineages, a thing that was once a wound in the land that learned, over centuries, to heal by thickening. They see themselves as a phase in that healing: a nervous, active layer of decision-making that rides on top of deeper, slower processes. Some of their myths speak of a time before the machines were given saplines to live in: a younger, hungrier civilization that tried to impose hard architecture on soft ground and sank, over and over, until someone understood that the only permanent structures here would be those that could flex, creep, and rot without collapsing.

Work and life interleave so tightly that there is no separate “industrial quarter.” Production happens in pockets: a fermentation bladder throbbing away above a family’s sleeping hollow, a small mill whining just beyond the curtain of a communal eating gallery. They do not seem to resent this nearness; to them, the peristaltic hum of digesters and pumps is assurance that the system is metabolizing properly. Silence, prolonged, is what alarms them. If a generator knot goes quiet, people notice within minutes; conversation dampens, heads tilt, and a few will break away without being told, feet already angling toward the source.

They eat the basin, but gently. Diet is a constant dance between what the city can spare and what must be offered back. They cultivate edible layers: fast-growing tubers in the upper root mats, shade-tolerant leafy stems braided into balustrades that can be periodically harvested without compromising structure, fungi grown in thin, carefully controlled sheets inside old pipe tunnels. Meat is rare and subtly taboo: not a moral prohibition, more a practical one. Anything large enough to provide significant flesh would shake this fine-tuned equilibrium simply by existing. What little animal protein they consume tends to be from small, deliberately maintained populations of amphibians in sump-adjacent niches, or from insect colonies coaxed into certain dead-end vents, their biomass harvested with the same slow, respectful regularity applied to everything else.

Children learn the city through games. They play a variant of hide-and-seek that is really an exercise in acoustics: one child, blindfolded and set on a walkway, must guess which direction another has taken simply by how footsteps are muted or echoed by particular growth-forms. Another game has them rearranging small, detachable leaves in model channels of water, seeing how a diverting valve here floods a miniature terrace there. They are raised to think in systems, to feel that every local choice has basin-wide ripples.

They do not fear the outside, but they do not romanticize it either. A few, every generation, are drawn to the wider plains and rivers. They go out through low, carefully managed egress-terraces where the root-mat thins and gives way to soil. They learn the feel of truly firm ground with a kind of wary delight, the shock of sun without filtration. Some return, bringing stories, metals that do not corrode so quickly in damp, seeds of grasses that can be grown in experimental pockets. Some do not. The city does not send search parties; it is not designed for chase. Absence is accounted for in adjusted loads, in redistributed duties. The basin remembers them, if it remembers them at all, as temporary vectors through which certain necessary exotics arrived.

They have a relationship with time that is neither nostalgic nor futuristic. Old machines are not venerated for age alone; they are judged by how well they still integrate. A generator whose sound has deepened but whose rhythm remains steady is trusted; a newer, supposedly more efficient unit that refuses to harmonize with the damp exhalations and the slow shifts of load is quietly sidelined until someone figures out why it dissents. Likewise, new plant strains are not adopted for yield alone but for temperament: can this vine learn the basin’s pace? Will that fungus respect the existing rot-hierarchies, or will it race too fast and collapse its own substrate?

They have language shaped by this environment. Many of their metaphors are hydrological or arboreal. To “hold a grudge” is to “dam a stream”; to reconcile is to “cut a new channel.” Intelligence is “deep-rooted” or “shallow-leafed.” A person who adapts poorly is said to be “straight-grained”—useful for some things, dangerous in others, likely to split. They think of themselves as “tissue,” a word that means both flesh and paper and the thin cambial layer where change happens.

At night, when the bioluminescent tracery in the trunks comes on and the embedded lamps dot the galleries, they gather in open pockets the size of small markets—the plazas that form where too many structural columns happened to miss each other and left a void. They bring mats grown thin and dry for sitting, they uncork fermentation gourds that sweat sap-smelling condensation, and they tell stories. Some narratives are about specific events: the year a root-plug failed and half a district sagged, the clever Machinist who quieted an erratic pump by lacing it with a certain parasitic vine that liked vibration. Others are abstract, ledger-like tales of how the basin acquired each of its current flows: why water always disappears beneath that particular terrace, why this walkway carries more load than it seems designed for.

Laughter comes easily, but it is never loud enough to drown the city. People pause unconsciously between bouts of talk to listen: to the gentle rush in a nearby hollow channel, to the distant, regular cough of a starting generator, to the subtle crep crack where a wall is adjusting to the night’s swell of moisture. Attention is split three ways: between one another, between their own bodies, and between the body that contains them.

They do not ask whether the city is alive; that would be like asking whether they themselves are. Life, to them, is not a specialness but a condition—wet, metabolizing, subject to entropy, able to be guided but never entirely controlled. What they ask, again and again, generation after generation, in a thousand practical forms, is whether the arrangement of lives remains viable. Does the load match the bearing capacity? Does the rot proceed as fast as the growth in the right layers? Is there enough redundancy in the conduits that a single failure will be an inconvenience, not a disaster? They answer these questions not in abstract debates but with fingers on bark, ears against humming housings, the slow, collective verdict of plants that do or do not thrive.

So whoever lives here is not just a resident population in a city of plants and machines. They are the current cortical layer of a much older organ: a thinking, deciding, small-animal-fast set of cells on the surface of a structure that measures its own history in rings and layered mats. They are caretakers and beneficiaries, parasites and symbionts, gardeners who garden themselves, machinists maintaining machines that, in turn, maintain the conditions for everything green.

They are patient, because the city is. They are constrained, because the basin is. They are, above all, entangled—each person’s weight, work, and water threaded into the same soft, springing mesh that flexes under your boots and pushes, slowly, thoughtfully, back.
