Those who live here are people of water and stone, but they were not the first.

The first makers are absent—gone long enough that even their memory has decayed into myth and misinterpretation. Nothing alive here looks like them. The surviving inhabitants are an aftergrowth, a second ecology grafted onto a machine.

They are small, by surface standards. Most adults stand no higher than your chest, dense-bodied and low to the ground. That compactness is not merely stature but strategy: weight kept close to the stone, center of gravity tuned for slickness. Their legs are bowed and heavily muscled, with tendons like wet cords, built to grip narrow ledges and brace against sudden rushes of water. The soles of their feet are broad pads broken by fine ridges—living tread that can flex to find purchase on algae-slick tile.

Their skin is a turning point between mammal and amphibian. It is thick over joints and spine, but thin and almost translucent in webs between fingers and toes, at the throat, at the inner arm. In those soft places, a tracery of veins lies close to the surface. They live half in water, half in air; their bodies have learned to drink both. There is hair, but it grows sparsely and in selected zones: a dark pelt along the back for heat and protection, wiry tufts at elbows and hips to trap moisture and slow its loss, a rough mane at the neck that can be braided and bound.

Facewise, they have taken on the architecture’s palette: pallid at first glance, grayed and freckled by mineral stain. Their eyes are big for their skulls, with pupils that slit to narrow beads under direct flame but spread wide and soft in the ambient glow of phosphorescent films. The sclera is not white; it is smoked glass, tinted with fine floating flecks of pigmentation. Looking into them is like peering through water that remembers dust. Within that dark, their irises are a faint, pearly grey or bluish-green, chosen less for beauty than for sensitivity. A low lid—something between nictitating membrane and thickened inner eyelid—can slide over the eye surface, smoothing out glare or sealing against a sudden splash.

Their noses are small ridges more than protruding structures, just enough cartilage sheltering a set of fine, wet nostrils. But their sense of smell is not delicate; the olfactory area within is deep and convoluted. To them, every drip-line, every basin, each runnel of runoff has a recognizable signature: the iron note from a hairline crack that taps a rust-veined seam; the fungal edge of a chamber that has gone too long unscoured; the faint animal sharpness of a predator that passed hours ago.

They are not graceful in the way a dancer is; they are graceful in the way a mechanic’s hands are—efficient, practiced, making no wasted movement. On wet ledges, they move with a grounded, three-point economy: one hand or foot always anchored. They know exactly how much force a rawhide-wrapped pole will bear, how far a slick stone will let them lean. Much of their language about character is couched in metaphors of footing and balance. An impulsive child is “one who runs across smooth stone”; a reliable elder is “a hook in the wall.”

Their mouths are wide, with lips that crack if allowed to dry. They do not, if they can help it, allow that. Between jaw and ear, a thin fold of skin—more mobile than the rest of the face—can tighten shut, shielding the eardrum against pressure changes or opening to drink in echo. Their necks are corded, containing both robust muscles and additional vascular pathways; blood here can be rerouted to retain heat in calves that spend hours in chest-high water, or to cool a head bent over a fierce lamp-flame.

They are not beautiful in any classic surface sense, but viewed within their context, their build is precise, almost elegant: everything that could be polished away by conditions has been.

They call themselves, in their own saturated clicks and throaty vowels, something that translates poorly. The closest gloss might be “Those Who Keep It Running” or “The Tenders.” Their identity is not bound to a tribe or lineage so much as to a function relative to the structure, which they regard as both ancestor and environment. They speak of “the Great Channel” and “the Gathering Skin” the way a surface culture might speak of “forest” or “mountain”—as both location and being, both thing and process.

Their society is small in population but dense in relation. A hamlet’s worth of people spread through a cat’s-cradle of chambers: a few hundred individuals, perhaps, divided into kin-clusters that share labor, scaffolding, breeding pools, and storage caches. They track belonging not by surname but by the particular section of the labyrinth whose pulses and groans you know best. To be of the Southern Runnel is to have grown up to the rhythm of its surge; to be of the Breathing Pillars is to know, by fingertip and bone, the subsonic thrum of air shuttling through those hollow shafts.

They do not call areas “north” or “south” except when teaching outsiders, because such abstractions are less useful than “up-gradient” and “down-gradient,” “toward the old overflow,” “outflow-side of the long cistern.” Direction, to them, is always relational to water movement, to where sound thickens or thins. They can navigate with their eyes closed by humidity alone.

Though the structure feels like a single organism, their social organization is a patchwork. Each local cluster centers around two of their own: one whose role is to listen to the stone, and one who tends to living things.

The listeners are the Echo-Keepers. They are not a separate bloodline, but a profession into which certain temperaments drift. From youth, an Echo-Keeper’s days are spent in the corridors where whispers turn to bells or vanish entirely. They learn to catalogue the building’s acoustic moods the way others learn to count: this long, descending groan means a support has taken on new load; this thin, high hiss is a crack venting air; this absence of usual drip-sound in a far gallery means the main surge has shifted upstream. In a culture with little in the way of abstract mathematics, the Echo-Keepers are their physicists: empirical, pattern-oriented, suspicious of coincidence.

Their tools are simple—hollow bones, stretched skin drums, weighted cords dropped down shafts—but their craft is complex. They time the return of a clap’s echo by heartbeats; they compare the quality of yesterday’s resonance to today’s, holding months of sonic history in memory. When they etch the flow-maps on bone panels, it is not paint they lay down but remembered sound. A good Echo-Keeper can stand in a chamber, produce three calibrated clicks, and then tell you, eyes closed, what lies in each branching corridor within shouting distance: a flooded sump, a breeding pool, a too-quiet cavity where stone may be separating from its mortar.

The other central profession belongs to the Wet-Stewards, those who manage water and life together. They oversee gardens cramped into troughs, breeding tanks slick with eggs, symbiotic membranes that must be cut back or fed at precise times lest they choke their own channels. Where an Echo-Keeper reads the structure’s stone voice, a Wet-Steward reads its skin—its slimes, its growths, its small, translucent citizens. They know which dripping arch biofilm can be pressed for pungent antiseptic, from which weeping seam the lichen-crusts powdered for glow-paint produce the longest-lasting light. They also know which pulsing tissues to avoid until they fruit, and precisely which pods can be harvested without triggering a runaway bloom.

Most individuals rotate through both roles in some measure—this is too small a society for strict caste systems—but at any given time, there are always a few whose opinion on stone or slime is given particular weight. Around and beneath these specialties spread more general crafts: hide-workers who keep the rawhide bindings perpetually damp then tight, bone-etchers whose panels carry the only written records, young climbers who run errands along the highest, thinnest ledges where elders dare not venture.

They live in tucked-away pockets of relative safety: side alcoves just high enough above the surge line but close enough to a reliable leak for constant humidity. There, they’ve wedged in wood and formed clay to make shelves, platforms, sleeping ledges. A household might seem sparse to you: a handful of oil bowls, a few carefully carved tools, coils of rope painstakingly braided, bundles of dried tubers and strips of lichen-tanned hide. Possession is limited by what can be secured against sudden water, what can be carried quickly along a narrow bridge when a collapse shifts your world.

Their young are raised not in cradles but in shallow pans of warm, carefully changed water, lined with smooth stones and woven reed. An infant here is never dry. Hands pass them from pan to pan, breast to water, water to breast, until they toughen. Toddlers learn to stand first in water just above the knee, where a fall is slowed, and only then on bare stone. They are taught from birth the rhythm of the surges: the counting song that marks the time between roars, the gesture that indicates “climb now” without words. When the building groans, even a child knows to hold its breath, to listen for a telltale rattle of loose stone.

Their domesticated animals are not docile herbivores; nothing here is. They maintain pens of half-tamed amphibians—smaller cousins of the pale arm-length beasts—that supply meat and gelatinous eggs. These creatures will happily eat their handlers if allowed, so they are handled with long bone hooks and heavy nets. Lank, long-limbed ledge-dwellers are not theirs; those belong to the structure’s own adapted fauna and hover in uneasy semidomestication near the boundaries of light. But there are smaller cohabitants: wetted rodents bred for fur and for their ability to sniff out bad air pockets, tiny crustaceans coaxed to live in jars and troughs as living filters, winged things lured into predictable roosts with scraps so that their guano can feed starved soil.

They themselves are omnivorous without sentimentality. Meat from whatever is caught, algae scraped from chutes, bulbs dug from their thin beds, membranes “milked” for nutrient-rich ooze. The rank sweetness of perpetual near-rot does not revolt them; to their palate, a note of putrescence is almost comforting, a sign that things are breaking down properly and will feed them again in other forms.

Their art is subtle, hard to spot at first in this layered dim. It’s in the way they knot rope—a distinct pattern indicating which surge-cycle this line was last re-tensioned. It’s in bone panels etched not with pictures but with abstracted patterns of ripples and drip-fall, each swirl a stylized memory of water evolution in a particular chamber. It’s in their lullabies, which mimic the soundscapes of safe rooms, teaching children by song what “right” echoes and drips should sound like, so that any deviation will scream in their nerves.

But they do make pictures, on occasion. In certain rarely dry crevices high above the usual splash zone, where mineral crusts are stable and the smoke-soot doesn’t quite reach, you can find scratched figures. They are not of themselves, or not often. They tend to depict the structure: vertical stacks of bowls, ladders between them, sinuous lines of channels. In some, small simplified humanoid forms cluster around a massive, smooth-limbed figure that could be one of their myths’ First Builders—or simply the idea of something larger and more knowing than they. In others, the tall shapes are ghostly, half-sketched, as if the artist had only faint hearsay to go on.

Those myths diverge by basin. One clan’s story says the world was bone-dry once, and the First Builders brought their own water in machines that passed it around in circles, teaching stone to drink and exhale. Another insists the world was all flood and roar, and the First Builders cut this structure out of that drowning mass, a gill in the flesh of some endless ocean. Both agree that when the First Builders left—or were taken—the remaining ones had to learn to keep it going by hand.

Whatever their origin, they have spent so long in the machine that they now think in its terms. Their calendar is not lunar or solar; it is surge-based. They count years in “long saturations,” intervals marked by a slight uptick in the baseline moisture or a subtle change in mineral banding on the walls. Their notion of “day” and “night” is faint; there is always some light, always some dark. Instead, they talk about “lull-watches” (when the surges are distant and muted) and “loud-watches” (when water rushes through the ducts in earnest). Work suited to sharp hearing—stone-listening, echo-mapping—is done in lulls. Work that can ride the roar—moving heavy objects on floating logs, dredging channels—is done in the louds.

Their relationship with the structure borders on devotion but never quite resolves into organized religion. The Breathing Pillars, for instance, are seen as both tool and spirit: devices whose workings no one understands, yet whose behavior is predictable enough to rely on. People go there to press their wet palms against their vibrating stone and leave small offerings—curved shells, etched bones, strange pale roots. They are not praying in the way you might; they are feeding an ecosystem they do not control but have co-evolved with. “If we keep its mouths clean,” an elder might say, patting a drainage hole after clearing it of muck, “it will keep breathing for us.”

Death, as you glimpsed at the grid of square basins, is accepted as one more shift in flow. Bodies are placed in that room in a period timed so that their breakdown coincides with a lull; the family comes and goes, chanting soft call-and-response that echoes gently in the square-pool geometry. They do not mark graves. Memory attaches instead to functions: “Grandfather, who found the hairline leak before the wall slumped,” becomes, in time, simply “the one who listened well at the fifth curve.” Individuality dissolves slowly into a legacy of attunement, of some small improvement in how the community reads and maintains its world.

They are not unaware of hunger, fear, or violence. Disputes arise over access to a particularly rich biofilm sheet, over who failed to maintain a channel so that crops were flooded, over whose negligence allowed a predatory amphibian to eat a child. When it comes, violence is quick and close—no room for distance weapons in cramped chambers. Heavy stones, sharpened bone shards, the sudden shove off a slick edge. Yet there is also a strong counterpressure: each death is an irreplaceable packet of experience in an environment that doesn’t forget easily but does not forgive carelessness. Elders—those whose bodies have begun to fail under the damp and the load of repetitive climbing—are kept near, consulted constantly, their knowledge siphoned as carefully as any water.

They practice a kind of cautious expansion and retraction. When a collapse opens a new cavity, a scouting team—always including at least one Echo-Keeper—goes first, sounding walls, testing air for dangerous composition by how flames burn, how their own chests feel. If the chamber is stable and moist enough, they will begin the slow process of taming it: scraping away dangerous growths, guiding leaks into usable channels, laying down the first sacrificial planks and rawhide lashings that will take the brunt of future erosion. In this way, their inhabited world slowly follows the building’s own decay, riding it like a wavefront. Old rooms, too thin or dry or treacherous now, are gradually abandoned, their lamps taken, their tether-points harvested, their panels of etched bone migrated to safer, wetter walls where the next generation can still read them.

Above them, in the higher vaults where only winged creatures and the boldest young climb, there are rumors of a different class of living thing. Once in a while, someone returns with a story of touching cold, dry stone so old it feels like bone, or of glimpsing a seam where faint, sharp-smelling air passes in and out, unlike the heavy breath of their usual space. These tales are told and retold, not as promises of escape but as warnings: dryness is death, a fire that burns skin to crackling. They imagine any “outside” not as freedom but as a brittle desert where water is stolen from bodies in moments.

Down below, beyond the lowest cisterns they dare to maintain, they imagine the opposite: a depth of water without shape, where weightless things drift and no stone holds. Some say creatures from there occasionally rise along the shafts, pale and soft, leaving behind strange residues in the catch-basins. When the water shifts in manners no Echo-Keeper can predict, when a surge arrives too soon or too late, some whisper that the deep below has tugged at their engine, reminding them that they float atop something larger and less knowable.

Between these imagined infinities of desiccation above and drowning below, they make their patient lives in the damp middle, tightening lashings, tuning channels, farming the odd gleam of light-catching mineral and the slow drip of sustenance.

Everything here is secondhand: stone someone else stacked, channels someone else conceived, pillars that breathe of their own design. But in slowly adapting themselves to those parameters, these people have become originals. This place is no longer simply an old machine; it is a shared body, with stone as skeleton, water as blood, biofilm as skin—and these small, quiet, constantly moving inhabitants as its attending cells, never fully in control, never fully separate, always listening for the next groan, the next rush, the next subtle change that will tell them how to live another cycle in the cool, echoing dark.
