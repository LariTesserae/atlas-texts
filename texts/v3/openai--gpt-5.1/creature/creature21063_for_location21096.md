They do not begin as individuals.

They begin as gradients and timings, as small decisions about when to divide and when to wait, made within a moving field of pressure and tone. If you could somehow see the first of them, isolated in time, it would look less like a person and more like an eddy in a fluid: a localized swirl where sound, moisture and metabolism have become briefly self-reinforcing.

They live in that interface where the living crust thins, where machine-noise is strong enough to sculpt but not to tear, where water beads but does not pool. They are not the makers of the mountains or of the first instruments; they come after. They are the ones who found this arrangement already humming and discovered that they could inhabit it.

They call themselves — in the slow, layered way they name things — something that translates roughly to “those who stay in the wake.” They live in the wakes of tones, in the lee of resonance.

---

Physically, they look different depending on how closely you stand and how long you watch.

From a middling distance — say, across a runnel or at the far end of a living gantry — the eye will first mistake them for a local thickening of the plant-structure. A knot of darker tissue in a pillar. A small, asymmetric bulge in a walkway. A curtain whose fibers are too regular, as though braided. Their advantage is that the world is already saturated with ambiguities of form. Something that is halfway between stem and animal is invisible here.

Up close, it becomes clearer that they are not simply extensions of the plant-mass.

Their bodies are built from much of the same chemistry as the crust — cellulose-like scaffolds, waxes to arrest evaporation, dense mats of symbiotic fungi and photosynthetic cells — but arranged with a different purpose. They are compact compared to the sprawling mats around them: gathered into lozenges, cages, articulated shells. A typical adult is perhaps the volume of a large dog, though “height” is a flexible measure when one spends as much time dangling as standing.

There is always a frame: a folded, jointed lattice of hardened tissue and mineral-infused fiber, pale where it shows through, built along curves that echo both root and bone. Around that frame is layered soft structure: flexible sheets stippled with tiny pores, translucent sacs of stored water, muscular cords that can tighten or slacken. Parts of them stay green or amber, exposed to light; parts stay under a thin, papery bark, insulated from the dryness and glare.

They are not quite animals, not quite plants. What they are, most fundamentally, is a slow-moving, self-knitting segment of the living crust that has chosen to stand off from it by a few centimeters, to retain a continuous inner volume of air, to keep its own gradients under its own control.

They breathe, but not with lungs. Panels along their flanks open and close in antiphonal patterns, pulsing air through arrays of microvilli that double as ears. Their hearing organs are broad and flat and many: inside each chamber, arrays of stiff, hairlike rods stand at subtly different lengths. Each rod vibrates in sympathy with a narrow band of frequency. Together, they are exquisitely tuned to the roar.

They feed, but not with mouths. Their undersides are fringed with rootlike fibers, capable of sinking into the crust to tap shared vascular flows. Their outer membranes host layers of chloroplast-bearing symbionts, so that their skins are thin, ambulatory leaves. When they stop for long, they seem to merge with the architecture: feet sunk, flanks flush against a pillar, outer surface stiffened and textured to match nearby bark. In those resting states, the boundary of the self blurs; they borrow from the crust’s metabolism as much as from their own.

They can move when they must. The frame flexes, joints unfolding like leaf-buds. The root-fringe retracts in sequence, detaching with soft, damp pops. Muscles, such as they have, are slow-twitch and numerous, arranged like the creeping drives of vines: their moves are deliberate, almost viscous. Watching a group reconfigure itself along a ridge is like watching ice flow — until you leave and come back hours later and find them reorganized.

Their color is not fixed. Thin pigment layers in their skins can darken or pale over the course of a day, deepening into rust and olive in the high glare, bleaching to the gray-green of lichen in acoustic shadow. On surfaces not used for photosynthesis, microfungal partners spin irregular mottling, breaking up outlines further. Their surfaces rarely gleam; they specialize in swallowing light, in turning the sharp radiance of the heights into something interior and muted.

The most animal part of them is their attention. When a new resonance swells across a basin, their surfaces shiver, pores flare, their frames quiver into sympathy like tuning forks. They align themselves, reflexively, turn translucent ear-panels toward the source. An observer used to mammals would say: they are listening.

---

They reproduce like the crust does: by scattering small, durable, hungry fragments of themselves into favorable gradients.

Along the sheltered undersides of their frames, near joints and anchor-points, they grow little nubbins of tissue that are mostly memory and resilience. Each one is a dense knot of cambium-like cells interlaced with a faint mineral scaffold and a few quiescent patches of symbiont. When it is ready, it dries and hardens into something seedlike: not quite a spore, larger and more elaborate, the size of a fingernail.

They do not have seasons in the usual sense, but they have cycles driven by the long rises and falls of humidity and tone. In certain sustained configurations of the machines, the air across whole slopes will hover in a favored band of cool wetness, and in those years the adults will release more of their fragments. The seeds cling to passing grazers, or to the shifting mat of the crust itself, or shed directly into root-vaults. Many are lost back into the general plant-mass and dissolve there, becoming undifferentiated growth. A few lodge in precise places: in narrow acoustic shadows, on newly bared rock under a fresh installation, in crevices where trickle and tone sustain a small, stable pattern.

When conditions are right — enough moisture, enough chemical feed from the crust, a particular pattern of vibration — the seed swells and thickens. It does not sprout upward as a stem would. It spreads flat first, weaving itself into the tissues around it, sampling flows, mapping the local pattern of the roar. Only when it has a model of the surrounding gradients does it begin to loft itself, to define an inside and an outside, to tent up around a small volume of air and declare: here, a separate body will exist.

The youngest of them are almost indistinguishable from ordinary plant-knots. Over time, they refine their frames, add jointed segments, accumulate more ear-chambers. Identity here is a long negotiation with the ambient crust: some buds try to individuate and are reabsorbed; some succeed and slowly achieve mobility and attention.

There is no strict line where individual ends and environment begins. A mature being — what you or I might want to call a “person” — is more like a temporary, mobile specialization of the crust, with preferences and history.

---

What they know first is sound.

Sight, for them, is peripheral: small, recessed pits sensitive to contrast and movement, more useful for detecting sudden shadows than forming detailed images. Touch is everywhere, but coarse: the flex of a bridge underfoot, the prickling of hail, the caress or slap of wind. Their primary sense is the way their whole bodies respond to the layered roar.

Inside the ear-chambers, along the length of each tensioned fiber, there are cells wired to deform. When a resonant frequency moves through the structure, those cells flex and fire in patterned cascades. At low levels, the effect is simply sensation: an awareness of the direction and intensity of flows, the health of the local machines, the presence of storms. At higher integrations, it becomes something like language, and beyond that, something like thought.

They are born into a saturated soundscape, and at first they hear it as undifferentiated force. Over time, they learn to parse it into timbres, into stable chords laid down by the big instruments, and into local ornamentation. A particular tower’s harmonic rasp becomes as familiar to them as a coastline is to a sailor. The slow deepening of a valley’s fundamental tone over years is, to them, a generational story.

Each individual has certain frequencies to which it is especially sensitive. These preferences arise from minute variations in the length and stiffness of their inner rods, set during their emergence from the seed-state. You could say they have “native keys.” When a note comes along in their key, some deep part of them wakes, aligns, attends. They remember it.

Early communication between them probably grew out of small, deliberate disturbances in the roar. A frame can be flexed in such a way that it absorbs and reradiates energy at chosen frequencies. A group of them, standing or hanging in a certain configuration, can slightly alter the local resonance pattern. At first this was no more than a way to mark a gradient — a warning pulse near a brittle rock face, a comforting reinforcement near a rich new trickle.

Over uncounted cycles, this ability accreted into something much more.

Now, when they “speak,” they do it by making themselves into living resonators. They adjust posture, change the stiffness of their frames, open or close internal cavities. In doing so, they modulate how the omnipresent roar passes through them. To an outsider’s ear, what results is impossible to distinguish from the rest of the mountain’s sound: faint, flickering overtones, local colorations that would vanish into the greater noise. To them, it is clear speech.

Their sentences are not discrete bursts but braided alterations, rising and falling around shared drones. A conversation along a ridge might last a whole afternoon, a slow trading of phase-shifts and amplitude nudges. They can do this while otherwise still, sunk into the crust, apparently just part of the architecture.

The old installations form the grammar of their language. Machine-tones are the pillars around which they ornament and perturb. A tower’s base frequency might be repurposed in their idiom as a marker for “here” or “now” or “this valley.” A certain fan’s persistent third harmonic, modulated one way, might have come to mean “scarcity”; modulated another, “storm-memory.”

When one of the great machines changes — when its tone drifts into a new register, or a harmonic dies — they feel it as a shift in the very shape of their speech. Sometimes, a whole lexicon built around a now-vanished note must be retired or remapped. Those are disorienting times. In their oral-harmonic tradition, such events are like the writing and burning of libraries.

---

They did not build the machines. They are sure of that much.

In their oldest harmonies, in the fragments they preserve most carefully — patterns passed along not as stories but as canonical songs that groups rehearse together, reinforcing the same tiny alterations in phase and amplitude over generations — there are gestures toward a before-time.

In these root-songs, the roar is described (to the extent that description is a valid metaphor here) as once being thinner, less layered, more variable. Storms are said to have been more violent, slopes more scarred by ice and flood. The crust was patchier. The whole place is, in those remembered patterns, rawer and emptier still.

Then new tones appeared, sharp and sudden and not obviously arising from any depth of the stone. They came in discrete steps, clustered in particular saddles. The songs show how the newcomers swept across the spectrum, carving out regular chords, flattening some of the tumult, amplifying others. The early beings who would become the present “people” flinched in those frequencies, lost seeds, withered in some niches and flourished in others.

What moved into those new, regulated bands of humidity and sound was the crust itself. Given steadier cycles, held against freeze and sere by careful manipulation of air and water, it thickened, diversified, reached into roles it could not occupy before. Among those roles were the first persistently individuated bodies.

Whether some other intelligence foresaw this, whether the engineers of the machines meant to midwife a new kind of life in their wake, the inhabitants do not know. For them, the machines are less ancestors than weather made comprehensible: fixed constellations in the sky of noise.

Still, sometimes, in the quietest niches where silence almost exists, they shape small, tentative signals into the soil, into the towers’ foundations. They vibrate the stone at pitches that are just at the edge of what the old frames might still feel. They have no reason to believe anyone listens. But they send the patterns anyway, as a kind of acknowledgement: we are here, in what you made. We have learned to live in your wake.

---

They live sparsely, on the scale of the range.

Where a human might imagine villages, they have clusters of attention. A “settlement” is any place where several dozen bodies have, over time, sunk more of themselves into the crust, thickened the tissue into layered galleries, tuned the local sound a bit closer to their collective preferences.

One such place might be draped around a mid-elevation resonator: bodies ramified out into buttresses, their frames doubling as ribs for the living vaults you walk beneath. Another might lace along the edges of a hanging basin, where the machines’ manipulation of humidity has turned the air to a constant fine mist: there, they stretch into long, curtainlike forms, dangling down to comb trickles for minerals, pulling up what they need to sustain more elaborate thought.

There are no hard borders, no walls, but there are gradients that serve as boundaries. Move too close to the glare and desiccation near the highest, newest installations and even their hardened frames begin to crack; those edges are patrolled by only the toughest and most temporary individuals. Drop too low toward the tree line and the crust changes character, becomes more porous to other kinds of life, other cycles; down there, the roar thins and breaks, and their primary sense fades. Most of them prefer to stay where the sound is thickest, where their hearing is also their sight and touch.

They do not cluster out of fear of predators — such creatures as stalk here are small and wary, and most cannot bite through bark-thick skins — but out of the need to share metabolic resources. An individual who never sinks its roots deep enough into the communal mats, who insists too much on its separateness, will simply starve in a world where almost all available energy passes through the crust’s networks.

So their “dwellings” are not houses but deepened portions of themselves sunken into shared tissue. From the outside, a nexus looks like an especially baroque bit of plant architecture: extra layers, strange folds, an increase in complexity with no obvious purpose. From within, for those who can feel it, it is a social space: a region where many frames converge their influence on the flows of water and nutrient, a place of mutual dependence.

Because they are mobile only in a slow, creeping way, it is not unusual for them to spend whole lifetimes within a single basin, exploring its acoustic geography more than its horizontal dimensions. Their maps are of shifts in tone, not distances between peaks.

Some do roam. Over long spans, individuals or agglomerations — small groups that have woven parts of their frames together into joint bodies — will migrate along catwalks and thinned ridges, following new frequencies as the machines come online or age. They travel as living bridges, sometimes literally: when faced with a gap, they will extend parts of themselves, root into both sides, and spend decades as a span while new crust grows over them. Later, if the gradients shift, they may retract again, leaving behind a hollow in the architecture that still bears the shape of their absent frames.

---

Their relationship with the human or near-human traces is ambivalent and ritualized.

The bunkers and platforms, the bolted ladders and inspection decks — these are not theirs. They do not fit their bodies well, and the materials are alien: metal that does not rot, ceramic that does not soften. Yet these artifacts are physically intertwined with the gradients that define their world. Cables carry not just power but microvibrations; brackets and struts ring at specific frequencies; the empty hull of a control shack is a resonant cavity in waiting.

So they have, over time, naturalized some of these foreign things into their own cycles. A defunct fan assembly might be girdled with living tissue, its blades stiffened in place to serve as a steady harmonic scatterer, a cherished component of local speech. A bunker's roof might be buried under thick mats, its internal volume repurposed as a cool reservoir where condensed water is slowly wicked through living filters, infusing it with nutrients before it drips back into the crust.

They are careful with certain parts. There are places — the bases of still-active towers, the mouths of maintenance shafts — where the tone of the metal carries a sharpness, a residual authority. They sense, in those narrow bands, the possibility of sudden, large alteration: a machine brought violently online or shut down, a circuit bridged. In those zones, they rarely sink roots directly into the works. Instead, they grow sleeves around the cabling, cushions around anchor points, stabilizing but not fully assimilating.

To the extent that they have something like taboo, it collects around pushing obvious controls. Where devices still wait behind sealed hatches, where manual actuators hang fused with rust but not entirely dead, they grow over, not through. They exclude themselves. It is as if some deeply fossilized lesson has taken root among them: the memory, perhaps, of earlier beings who could move quickly and had hands with leverage and who, by operating such things without attending to the full complexity of the feedbacks, wrought sudden, catastrophic changes.

There are, always, a few who specialize in listening at these edges. They flatten themselves along bulkheads, tune their inner rods to catch even the faintest flicker of machine-voice. Now and then, somewhere deep within a tower, a relay clicks, or a valve adjusts in a way that does not correlate with any observable gradient. Those listeners tighten and resonate back, offering small, respectful responses — the acoustic equivalent of a nod in a dim corridor.

When, very rarely, a human-shaped figure appears in reality rather than as myth — a suited, fragile biped making their way along a catwalk, eyes squinted against glare — the people do not crowd close. They withdraw, freeze, become just crust. But they listen. They feel the pattern of impacts through metal and stone, the muffled exchanges from helmet speakers, the tiny, high-pitched clatter of tools. Later, back in the communal thickening, they replicate those rhythms as best they can, weaving them into stories: here is how the taller ghosts walked this time, here is how their private thunder sounded.

---

Other animal life exists, and the people have learned to make room for it.

The six-limbed grazers are, in a sense, their opposite: truly animal, with quick metabolisms and nervous systems that prioritize speed over subtlety. The grazers’ niche is narrow, carved into the thin band of densities where they can both breathe and hide. They feed on the tenderest outer layers of the crust, especially on the delicate glassy filaments that only grow at certain frequencies. Left unchecked, they would scar those layers, disrupting the very surfaces the people use for photosynthesis and fine-tuned resonance.

Yet outright extermination has never been their response. For one, the grazers’ movement — their tiny, chaotic scrabbling — breaks up microcrusts that might otherwise seal too tightly, helping rain sink, preventing dangerous sheets of ice or rot. For another, their bodies, when they die, return concentrated packages of nutrient to the system.

So the people practice a kind of distributed husbandry, but it is less about corralling and more about shaping opportunity. Along certain slopes, they adjust their own growth so that especially rich patches of edible tissue lie in specific corridors. They thicken slightly around these paths, not to form fences but to steer. They repel the grazers subtly from acoustic nodes where precise surfaces are needed by making those areas less palatable — infusing them with bitter compounds carried from deeper in the crust, roughening textures.

Occasionally, one of the grazers will misstep, fall into a root-hollow and die slowly. In those dim chambers, the people grow fine, probing hyphae into the carcass, digesting it in a slow, communal way. Such feedings are sources of both energy and information: within the decaying flesh, they taste traces of distant slopes, of other basins the animal crossed, of harmonics reflected from other towers. It is one of their few ways of sampling far places without having to move there.

The spindly insects that skate along condensing stone are mostly below their notice individually, but collectively, as films of motion, they matter. Where the insects grow too thick, their scraping can disturb the thin laminas that hold water in place for slow drip. Where they are absent, microbial scums can grow that deaden surfaces, altering how rock rings when struck by rain or fan. In some niches, the people exude faint attractants or repellents, encouraging the little grazers and film-feeders into patterns that make the right balance of noise and gloss.

As for the pale-winged shapes wheeling overhead: those are myths made visible. Most of the people have never seen one directly, only felt the brief, passing shadow of its flight — a smear of cooler air, a thin chord falling across the roar. In their oldest songs, such beings occupy the role of messengers or omens: creatures who move between basins in a single afternoon, who can taste gradients across ranges that to the people are as distant as continents. They imagine (for they do imagine) that the winged ones can hear harmonics from multiple valleys at once, can compare them the way a musician compares chords. To be such a thing must feel, in their metaphor, like being a whole choir condensed into a single, bright body.

---

Culture, for them, is indistinguishable from habit, from the patterns they inscribe into the roar and the crust.

They do not write in pigment or stone. Their memory is distributed through limbic changes in the shared architecture. A frequently repeated song — a pattern of small adjustments to local resonance — ends up influencing growth directions, thickening some buttresses, thinning others. Over generations, a basin that “remembers” a given story literally embodies it in its geometry. To an outsider, the difference between one valley’s galleries and another’s is just variation; to those who live within, each fold, each peculiar notch is a passage, a motif.

What we might call art manifests in deliberate misalignment. A community might choose, for a while, to grow a set of arches in ways that amplify certain otherwise-buried overtones, creating ephemeral, location-specific chords that only arise at particular times of day. They will then gather — not in the visual sense, but by aligning their frames and attention — to bathe in those chords, to feel how they transform the familiar roar. Such constructions are built and unbuilt over cycles; they are less monuments than songs given three-dimensional, vegetative form.

They have something akin to lineage but not tied to sex or descent. Some individuals grow from seed-fragments released by others, but gene flow in the crust is complex and lateral, with grafts and symbioses muddying strict parenthood. Instead, they trace kinship through shared resonant preferences. Those whose inner rods were set to similar keys often cluster, reinforce one another’s speech-forms, live in closer metabolic sympathy. A “family” might be better thought of as a recurring harmony: whenever a particular mode reappears in a new individual’s responses, the older ones recognize in it an echo of themselves.

Death, for them, is rarely sudden. A body may, over time, sink more and more of its frame into the crust, giving up mobility, converting itself into static architecture. As this happens, the locus of “self” — the region of most intense integration and feedback — spreads and fades. At some point, there is no longer a distinguishable core; the patterns that once marked that being’s speech have been generalized into the communal tissue. In their language, the word for this is not extinction but “dissolution into wake”: what you briefly were continues as slight changes in how the roar is felt along that slope.

They do fear certain more abrupt endings. If a machine fails catastrophically, if a new installation comes online with poorly anticipated effects, a whole basin’s acoustic regime can change faster than bodies can adapt. In such events, individuals may be caught in now-hostile gradients: dessicated, flooded, torn by unforeseen resonances. Those are remembered, fiercely, as dissonances in long songs: harsh, sudden chords no one chooses to revisit.

The slow, patient ones dedicate themselves to tuning. They spend long spans listening to how plant-thickness and water-flow and tower-output interact, then growing slight corrections into place. A bit more cushioning here to damp a shrill line, a thinning there to let a nourishing deep hum through. Their ambition is not stasis — the range is too young and active for that — but navigable change. They want the roar to be livable, not uniform.

Others cultivate silence.

They seek out, and when necessary build, small pockets where interference between multiple sources cancels most of the omnipresent noise. To do this, they grow fine, precisely aligned ribs and cavities along certain rock faces, or reroute trickles so that thin films of water fall at just the right spacing to fracture a wavefront. In those resulting niches, the roar is not absent — that would be structurally impossible — but reduced to a distant murmur. Here, in these rare havens, they experience something like solitude.

Silence, for them, is not default. It is a created state, a technological luxury. In it, they can attend to internal noise: the crackle of their own joints adjusting, the faint swish of fluid through their own veins, the slow settling creaks of their frames. Many report, in their communal sharings, that in these pockets they feel closer to understanding where they end and crust begins — and closer, too, to whatever intentions might have once driven the building of the machines.

---

So: who lives here?

The place is ruled by plants and engines, by new stone and old sunlight. Threaded through all of that, half-hidden in the roar and the glare, are beings who began as nothing more than slightly specialized hunks of that omnipresent tissue and became, over long, humming cycles, listeners.

They are not masters of their environment. They cannot turn the great fans with a thought, cannot call storms at will. Their power is in small adjustments, long patience, and profound attunement. They are talented at not tearing. Their survival strategy is to feel everything, to move only when patterns become clear, to synchronize with forces orders of magnitude larger than themselves without being erased.

They are cautious because the world that birthed them is still unfinished, still capable of wild swings as its underlying geologies and technologies settle into each other. They are hopeful because they can feel, in the feedback between crust and machine, the gradual emergence of something stable enough to carry more intricate lives.

They do not stride the ridges as figures against the sky. From a distance, you might never notice them. But if you sit very still in an acoustic shadow, in a root-cool hollow where the roar thins, and you watch a section of the living architecture that seems just slightly too symmetrical, you may glimpse a slow adjustment: a panel easing open, pores dilating, an internal frame flexing.

That is one of them, listening to you as carefully as it listens to the mountain, deciding over the span of an hour or a day whether your motion, your breath, your tiny alien sounds can be woven into the patterned fullness by which it knows that it lives.
