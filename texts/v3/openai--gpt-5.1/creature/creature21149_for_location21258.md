They do not name themselves in any one way, because they do not experience themselves as a single kind of “people.”

It is truer to say: the place is the primary organism, and what “lives” here are its organs, its passengers, its parasites, its technicians, and its dreams.

---

Closest to what an off‑worlder would call citizens are the *tenders*.

They are the ones glimpsed on the mid‑platforms, moving in that measured, careful way that suggests they understand how thin their numbers are against the mass of other life. Their bodies are built for compromise: tall enough to look browsers in the eye, short enough to duck beneath the conveyor belts; joints that flex both for climbing and for bracing against the shudder of generators.

Skin tends toward the color of the inner bark—grey‑brown, dull umber—marked in places by cultivated lichen or symbiotic scale that functions as passive armor and moisture regulation. Their hair, where they have it, they keep bound and close, tucked under fibrous caps grown from treated plant tissue. The air is too busy with hooks and probing mouthparts for anything loose to be safe.

Their eyes are adapted for the layered low‑light: wide pupils, irises muted to greens and hazels that reflect the membranes overhead. They see contrast and motion better than color. In exchange, their hearing is only moderate; in this environment, being too sensitive to sound would be a kind of madness. They have, instead, a trained sense for pattern in the noise. To a stranger, the hall is an undifferentiated roar; to them, the timbre of the city is as legible as a console display.

They do not build in the old human sense—no right‑angled towers, no foundations. Their craft is *binding*.

A tender’s toolkit is half surgical, half horticultural: splicing needles; bundles of mycelial cord pre‑programmed to grow into conduits when introduced into certain cambial layers; small, palm‑sized plates of composite that, once bolted to living wood, release gradients of hormone‑like compounds to sculpt growth. They know where to cut a root to make it thicken rather than wither; where to pierce a pillar to induce a vascular bypass instead of a lethal wound.

At their belts hang also objects of more conventional manufacture: oscillating cutters for when plant tissue has to be removed faster than it can be convinced; meter‑long batons that project narrow columns of infrasound or scent to nudge away animals who have forgotten the rules; compact, worn consoles whose interfaces are more tactile than visual, because you need your eyes free here.

Their power doesn’t come from owning territory. It comes from knowing *what to ask the city to be*.

They are the ones who can lay in the templates for a new growth—specifying, by little more than a cartridge of coded hormones introduced into a root‑node and a decade of patient correcting, that the next pillar born here will open with platforms at six precise levels and cavities already tuned for computing plates. They cannot force the city; they negotiate with its metabolism, exploit tendencies, ride flows.

They don’t rule alone. They belong to loose lineages—guilds, if you like—defined not by blood so much as by what they remember and which portions of the city’s distributed mind will speak to them.

Because there are *other minds*.

---

The pillars do not think in sentences; they think in gradients.

Each great column carries, halfway up its height or higher, a cluster of organs that to a surface biologist would look suspiciously like tangled cortex: pleated, highly vascularized knots shivering with tiny adjustments. The tenders call these knots *listening‑hearts*. They are not centralized brains, but each one represents a focal point where plant, machine, and the pattern of animal traffic have, over generations, knotted into something like a local intention.

These knots feel the drag of herds along their bases, the frequency of flyers passing through this air column, the thermal plumes of generators. They feel the caress of light patterns overhead, the chemistry of water descending their capillary nets.

Over time, they learn.

Not in a human “schooling” sense, but in selection and self‑modification. Tendrils that route uselessly wither. Connections that correlate strongly with survival or increased flow thicken. Machine elements grafted into their tissue—bioceramic lattices carrying faint, changing lines of cold light—give them memory and the ability to carry very slow, very low‑bandwidth messages over great distances through the substrate.

The net of these listening‑hearts and their machine grafts is the city’s *distributed self*.

It is not omniscient. It does not have a single viewpoint. But it has *concern*. When a pillar begins to fail, somewhere in the mesh a threshold is crossed: signals about load distribution, nutrient scarcity, local predation rates snowball into a pattern that means “collapse is coming.” In response, other regions adjust: redirecting herd flows; ripening a new growth core; thickening certain pathways for the decomposition wave to come.

The tenders converse with this organism the way one converses with weather and soil and engines: through empirically discovered interventions. They speak by changing flows. They listen by watching how the city takes their suggestions.

But they are not the only ones the city listens to.

---

Most of the great nonhuman animals here are not autonomous individuals in the way we would like clean categories to be. They are behavior modules bound into the city’s regulation.

Take the six‑limbed browsers.

To an untrained eye they are simply large herbivores: heavy, shag‑coated, forever chewing, exhaling that warm, metallic breath. But each carries in its gut a consortium of engineered microbes tuned to this exact plant infrastructure: not just breaking down lignin and the weird fibrous cartilage of old root, but also *annotating* it—transforming local chemical markers into more portable forms.

When a browser licks a condensation curtain, it leaves behind not just saliva but a new film of compounds reflecting its internal state: micronutrient deficiency, parasite load, reproductive readiness. The plant matter, sensitive to those markers, adjusts its own exudates. A curtain used by many such beasts averages them, becoming a live display the tenders and listening‑hearts both can read: a humidity map that is also a census, also a health report.

The browsers’ migration isn’t fully free. At night—whatever “night” means in this place, when the membranes dim and industrial lights choke down—they follow not just residual scent paths but low‑frequency pulses transmitted through the substrate and into their limb bones by plates embedded underfoot. Those pulses coax them toward certain corridors, away from others. Over generations, what was training becomes instinct. The city shapes their corridors as surely as terrain shapes a river.

The long, flexible predators that thread between them are cullers.

Their senses are tuned less to sight or sound and more to something like tactility at a distance: the micro‑vibrations of gait, the subtle irregularities in a browser’s step that signal weakness. Their reward is not just meat. When they bring down an animal, their saliva delivers viral packets into the corpse that accelerate decomposition in ways that route nutrients optimally through the root mesh. They are, in a literal sense, enforcement arms of a nutrient economy.

They hold territories by elevation because the city wants them distributed that way. A predator that breaks pattern too hard risks starving, because the city’s subtle resource flows no longer support it. Those that keep to their altitudes find prey presenting itself with uncanny regularity.

The flyers serve as air sampling and signaling systems. Their bioluminescent pulses are not mere mating displays. Certain patterns encode simple but crucial states: “water event starting here,” “pillar failure in this sector,” “unexpected dryness,” “overcrowding of one herbivore species.” They do not know that they are broadcasting messages any more than a neuron knows what a thought is. They respond to local conditions; their blinking is simply what survival has made useful.

Others go further into designed function.

There are jointed, rail‑hugging creatures whose bodies encase the industrial paths: load‑bearers that flex and absorb shocks when heavy trolleys pass. They secrete lubricant along their ridges. Their feeding is tied to the continuous motion of cargo; when an area’s traffic ceases, these creatures enter a torpor, effectively freezing a disused corridor in place and preserving it until someone decides to wake it with new loads.

Above, floating things that resemble translucent jellyfish are not just oddities. They are heat sinks: sacs full of phase‑change chemicals and engineered life that absorb waste warmth from engine clusters and carry it upward, gradually releasing it near the ceiling membranes, where temperature differentials drive slow convection currents. They drift where they are needed because the city’s air chemistry pushes them—minute differences in composition and charge coax them into the plumes that must be cooled.

Predators, scavengers, “pests”—all belong to this loose, self‑regulating network. None of them individually knows the city, but their species’ lives trace out constraints and flows that together *are* the city’s metabolism.

---

There are also the in‑betweens: forms that are half tool, half animal, and arguably half citizen.

Some are artifacts of old projects: small, semi‑autonomous machines with shells like beetles, once designed to maintain certain micro‑environments. Over centuries they have incorporated biofilm and rootlet intrusions into their housings, so that they are now more living than machined, guided as much by chemotaxis and inherited circuits as by any original code. Tendrers call them *remnants*. They speak of them with the slightly superstitious respect one grants ancient, glitchy software that still runs some vital subsystem.

Others were created more recently and more deliberately.

For instance: *riders*—limbless, centipede‑like beings no longer than a forearm, with ringed bodies of metal‑reinforced chitin. Each ring carries a different set of sensor filaments or contact pads. They live coiled around certain vertical tendrils, feeding off tiny exudations, but their real purpose is to act as dynamic connectors. A tender can pick one up, carry it to a new panel, press it into a waiting socket; the rider will gingerly plug itself in, recognize the local signal language via pre‑programmed heuristics, and begin bridging that panel’s signals into whatever network strand its other end already inhabits.

They are not sentient, but they are *curious* in very specific ways. They explore signal spaces. When they find patterns that resemble known schemata—power regulation, pattern storage, herding calls—they lock on and reinforce them; when they encounter noise, they withdraw.

Living cables, living hubs, living protocol adapters: this border population blurs infrastructure and fauna.

Among them, though, there are rumors—passed in low voices among the tenders—of a class of organisms that trespass even further across the boundary.

These are the *echo‑kind*.

They are small, quiet animals, superficially resembling the mid‑level scavengers: four or six legs, grippy pads, dull pelts, eyes that glimmer only when they catch a moving light. To most inhabitants they are just another minor species. But in certain districts where information‑processing plates cluster thick, tenders have noticed: the echo‑kind appear reliably near reconfiguration events, pillar collapses, template deployments.

One theory says they are vehicles for the city’s *experiments*. That the distributed intelligence, too diffuse to have discrete thoughts, sometimes concentrates its gradients into these bodies, nudging them into trying new paths, new behaviors—little probes sent into the labyrinth of possible metabolisms. They are watchers, carrying back impressions in the only way the city can integrate: by surviving or failing, by leaving behind bodies rich in certain signal compounds.

It might be myth. In a system as entangled as this, myth is one of the tools by which limited brains model the whole.

---

And the tenders themselves are not the only tool‑using primates, if that word still applies.

There are *deep‑born*—humanoids who rarely leave the low, root‑heavy zones where the substrate thickens toward older chambers. They are shorter, with broader feet for the soggy mat, lungs adapted to air that is denser with spores. Their culture centers on the decomposition cycles, timing and optimizing the reclamation of a fallen pillar’s mass.

They sing to the scavenger machines in patterns learned from their mentors; they know how to stand in the dust of collapse without breathing poison, how to recognize by faint temperature changes when a pillar is hollow enough to enter and harvest for dense, valuable tissues without risk of sudden implosion.

Deep‑born mistrust the mid‑platform tenders, seeing them as dangerously enamored of the mechanical and the abstract. The mid‑tenders, for their part, privately judge the deep‑born as parochial, too willing to let tradition override data. Yet they need each other. The city’s continuity depends on both scale of attention: granular, fungal patience and sky‑facing, networked foresight.

Higher still, near the membranes, live the *canopy‑walkers*.

They are lightly built, long‑fingered, their skin patterned not for camouflage in leaf‑green but for breaking up their outlines against the flicker of bioluminescent membranes and flyer swarms. They maintain the algal light films and the industrial luminaires embedded among them. Their tools are mostly harnesses, hooks, and microfiltration rigs to keep the membranes from being choked by the spores and dust that constantly try to turn everything into soil.

Among them, a few serve as intermediaries to whatever lies beyond the canopy. They know the rare hatches that open to the surface—if this world has a surface in any conventional sense. They are the channel through which large‑scale changes—climate spikes, external arrivals, distant pressures—enter the city’s decision space.

So you have vertical cultures stacked: deep‑born at the rot, mid‑tenders at the machines, canopy‑walkers at the flicker of the outer world. Between them move traders—thin flows of objects too rare or too abstract for the city’s automated cycles to provide.

Hard metal from a meteorite, misfit technology from some earlier era, chemicals that do not fit the biosphere’s normal patterns: these circulate hand to hand, used as bargaining chips when one guild needs another to prioritize some particular intervention. Social rules form around them. Debts, favors, taboos.

---

In all this, you might ask: is there anyone here who simply *lives*—who is not a regulator, a technician, a built‑in function?

There are, but even their leisure tends to slip into participation.

Children of the tenders play by following herds along the safer platforms, learning without being told how close you can pass behind a six‑limb before the foot swing becomes dangerous; they learn the difference between an ordinary alarm cry and the beginning of a migration wave not from textbooks, but because a game of tag is suddenly drowned out by a chorus and a parent’s hand yanks them clear of a path. Their lullabies are patterned on the slow, rolling calls that announce water events, because those are the sounds that mark relief and predictability here.

Elders who no longer climb as well sit in little hollows carved into pillar flanks, tending panels of bioceramic computation that have to be watched more for *drift* than for failure. Their work is as much storytelling as monitoring: they read out, in murmurs, the glacially changing glow lines in the plates—pattern shifts that map to years of data on herd cycles, collapse intervals, water timing. Younger tenders absorb this as narrative: “The time when the fifth pillar fell too soon,” “the season of thin condensation,” “the year the flyers’ swarm map was wrong.”

There are artisans who do not serve direct infrastructural roles, insofar as anything here can be outside the system.

They weave personal spaces—not freestanding houses, but dens tucked inside existing niches—out of root fiber and molted chitin plates, making them acoustically damp, scented just enough to read as “home” without broadcasting too far into the olfactory language other species use. They inlay the interior cambium of their favored pillars with pigment‑bearing fungi that stain it slowly into murals: not static scenes, but impressions that drift as the growth carries color upward and outward across years.

They make musical instruments that operate on the city’s hysterical soundscape: long, folded horns whose tones sit precisely in the narrow frequency not heavily used by any major animal group; when played, these horns do not add chaos but carve a temporary, audible *path* through the roar, a kind of sound corridor in which conversation and ceremony can happen.

Festivals here are timed not to celestial events—the sky is too remote—but to internal cycles: the collapse of a major pillar, the first visible unfurling of a new district’s panels, the rare occasions when the canopy membranes synchronize with the industrial lights and, for a few hours, the whole vault glows at near‑daylight levels.

On those days, humans and near‑humans will mark their skin with bioluminescent paints cultured from safe strains of the same algae as the ceiling films, and move in processions along designated pathways, calling and answering in patterns that imitate and gently twist the calls of the larger fauna.

It is not dominance. It is a way of saying: *we belong to this chorus, too*.

---

Beyond them all sits a diffuse, difficult question: who *owns* this place? Who, if anyone, made the first seed of the self‑thickening forest that has become architecture; who grafted the first generators into trunks; who began training browsers to serve as moving sensors; who encoded the templates for new districts?

The living here tell stories in many directions.

One line of myth says that the forest found the void on its own—some ancestral species sending taproots down into a natural cavity—and that the machines came later, built by strangers who did not survive their own cleverness. Their relics became scaffolds for more growth, their broken networks re‑colonized by sap and spore. In this version, the city *ate* its architects and in doing so developed a taste for design.

Another says the reverse: that a very old culture, perhaps not human at all, deliberately grew this chamber out of an undifferentiated crust, using far more precise biotechnologies than the tenders now remember. What remains today is a degenerate form: still self‑maintaining, but no longer fully legible even to those who carry the title of engineer.

There is, too, a quieter, more unsettling possibility entertained by some canopy‑walkers who have seen things nearer the outer world.

They speculate that this hall is not unique; that it is one chamber in a lattice of similar organs studding the interstices of the planet’s crust. Perhaps they are connected only by thin vascular links, perhaps by nothing more than parallel evolution guided by some very old, global artifact. In that view, no one *here* is the true originator. Everyone—pillar, engine, browser, tender—is participating in a planetary‑scale metabolism designed (or evolved) for purposes that may have nothing to do with habitation.

But from the inside, such cosmological questions have little practical weight.

Day to day, what lives here are:

- Pillars that *listen* and reshape themselves;
- Vines and tendrils that *transport* and mediate;
- Machines that have become organs;
- Animals that are plumbing, computation, governance, sometimes food;
- Humanoids who tweak, read, remember, trade, mythologize;
- Small in‑between creatures that carry signals across scales;
- And the pervasive, barely localizable will of the place itself, expressed as the insistence that nothing be idle, that every flow find some function.

You could say that the inhabitants are plant, animal, and human.

It is more accurate to say that the inhabitant—singular—is the city, and all these things you see moving through it are just the different ways it continues.
