They are not citizens here; they are *phrases* that the place keeps repeating.

Even to call them “they” is uneasy, because the longer a lineage abides in the basin, the less it makes sense to separate it from the city-plant, and the less its boundaries stay put. Still, there are bodies with habits and histories, and they recur enough that you can speak of kinds.

## The ones who ride the roar

The basin is built around sound, and so its principal inhabitants are those that have made sound—not just hearing it, but inhabiting it—their medium.

### 1. The Resonants (or “city’s children”)

They are the most entangled with the plant-mind, and in some sense the basin invented them for itself.

From a distance, a Resonant looks like a pale, upright coil of cartilage and skin, as if a spine had learned to walk without ever bothering to add a true head. Up close, the illusion of simplicity breaks.

- Their bodies are composed of stacked, flexible rings, each ring studded with mobile ossicles and diaphragms: living loudspeakers and microphones.
- Where another creature might have a skull, they have instead a shallow, bowl-like widening, rimmed with frills and vibrating curtains of flesh. This “crown” can open into petals or close tight, tuning them to different bands of frequency.
- They carry almost no pigment; their tissues are translucent, faintly lit from within by the glow of metabolizing cartilage. Watching one in dim light is like watching a column of breathing frost-glass.

They stand in the open plazas where the roar is thickest, swaying minutely, their rings expanding and contracting. They are not passive. Every tiny change in posture modulates the way pressure waves flow around and *through* them, and they amplify certain bands while damping others, sculpting the basin’s acoustics in real time.

They do not speak in any human sense. Their “language” is the act of becoming a temporary acoustic organ for the city:

- By bowing, they deepen certain pillars’ resonance.
- By arching, they send narrow beams of specific frequency racing along vine-bridges, popping ground-sphincters open in a cascade.
- By clustering in the right patterns, they can briefly synchronize multiple architectural organs, triggering the basin’s internal “weather events.”

In return, the plant-city speaks *through* them. Nodes of tissue at the bases of their rings knit directly into shallow roots below. When they root themselves (press their lower coils into special, softened patches of ground), you can follow visible pulses of sap-like fluid entering their frames. Their rings stiffen; their frills flare; and for minutes or hours they are as much extrusion as animal.

Do they have individual minds? They have preferences: some return reliably to the same standing points; some form familiar groups; some react with distinct agitation when key columns slump or die back. But their inner life appears braided into the larger awareness of the basin. When a Resonant dies—its rings collapsing like a concertina left in the rain—the city absorbs its tissues with unusual care, leaving behind pale, calcified crescents that fuse into nearby trunks. Those crescents become new acoustic nodes. It is as if no thought is ever truly lost, only fossilized into structure.

They are, in many ways, the “priesthood” of this place, except that the god and the clergy are the same organism, flexed into two different modes.

## The great traversers

The basin has scale, and so some of its inhabitants are correspondingly vast. They live not *in* the architecture but *between* its layers, stitching vertical and horizontal ranges together.

### 2. Vault-Whales

In the broadest chambers, where the ceiling arches thirty meters above the floor and the pillars thin into occasional ribs, there move the Vault-Whales.

They do not truly fly. They are buoyed instead by the basin’s own pressure-field and warm, moist drafts:

- Their bodies are long and deep, lozenge-shaped, sheathed in leathery skin the color of old leaves.
- Along their flanks run bands of hollow, gas-filled chambers that compress and expand with rhythmic creaks, allowing fine control of buoyancy.
- Their “heads” are wide, blunt domes fringed with curtain-like filters of stiffened cilia, always quivering.

Vault-Whales feed primarily on the endless dust of life that drifts here: microscopic spores, planktonic seeds, flake-shed tissue, airborne bacteria that thrive in the moist air. They swim slowly through the basin, their ciliary curtains combing the medium. Everything caught is shunted along mucus-slick grooves toward inner digesting folds.

They are not merely passive filters, though. Along their bellies, arrays of muscular plates can slap and shudder in complex patterns, sending out deep, infrasonic pulses that only the plant’s tissues and a few specialized animals can detect. These pulses:

- Map the resonant architecture for the Whale, giving it a three-dimensional portrait of pillars, cavities, and currents.
- Nudge the city’s hydraulics; repeated passage of a Vault-Whale along a route slowly “engraves” preferred channels, encouraging the growth of low-resistance paths of air and vapor.
- Serve as slow, stately communications among the Whales themselves, whose meetings are rare but palpable: a sudden, unified deepening of the background tremor, an undercurrent of sonorous conversation that can last for hours.

On their backs, parasitic gardens grow: mats of epiphytic plants and colonial invertebrates, anchored in shallow pits in the skin. When a Whale brushes close to the canopy membranes, these hitchhikers trade spores and larvae with the higher flora and fauna. In this way, the Vault-Whales are the basin’s long-range dispersal engines, keeping far-flung niches genetically coherent.

They breed infrequently, and always in the oldest, most central hollows, where the architecture is thick with memory and the pressure-field is most stable. There, amid a thunder softened into immense, slow pulses, they spiral around each other, bodies singing like struck drums, until a new, small Whale peels away from one flank, already buoyant, already humming to the city.

---

### 3. Striders of the Rim

Where the architectural pillars grow denser toward the basin’s wall, there move the Striders: towering, many-kneed beasts built for the tangle and the edge.

They are not as tall as the Vault-Whales are long, but they *feel* taller because they anchor to both ground and canopy:

- Each Strider has six to ten legs, depending on age, each leg ending in a split pad that can grip both slick leaf-membrane and roughened trunk.
- Their torsos are narrow but elongated, a ganglion of tendons and muscle sheathed in flexible bark-like skin.
- Above, instead of a single head, three or four necks branch upward, each bearing a different sensory crown: one a spray of whisker-like acoustic antennae, one a flat, plate-lined “ear” that can clamp onto pillars, one a bouquet of simple eyes crowded around a moist patch of olfactory tissue.

Striders move with unhurried certainty, threading between pillars, stepping onto and off suspended platforms, sometimes climbing nearly to the canopy roof before descending again. Their route choices are uncannily efficient: they seem to know which structures are load-bearing, which are in the process of being sacrificed and digested, which have become resonant nodes not to be disturbed.

Their primary diet is structural fatigue. They seek out tissues that the plant-city has marked as expendable—brightened chemically, stiffened just beyond useful elasticity. Using rasping plates in their lower jaws and grinding stones in their gullets, they break these old supports down into smaller chunks that can more easily be metabolized by the city’s saprovores and root-mouths.

In return, the plant-city gives them something like guidance. When a Strider lifts its plate-ear and clamps it onto a pillar, pulses of vasicular sound flow directly into its bone. It stands still for long minutes, listening. When it moves again, it often heads straight for an incipient weakness—a sagging span, a column that has started to crack—arriving just as a nearby Resonant begins to shift its own stance to stabilize the overall load. The Strider will strip and eat the failing tissue, taking weight off the system. Its timing is too consistent to be chance.

Their social lives are quiet but intricate. Multiple Striders will sometimes converge on a single failing area, working in eerie synchrony, as if guided by a common blueprint. Young ones trail older, learning which auditory patterns correspond to which structural cues. In old age, a Strider will deliberately seek a certain kind of small plaza and then simply stop, its legs locking, its body hardening as the plant-city’s tissues grow into and around it. It becomes a pillar. New platforms sprout from its flanks. Its sensory crowns atrophy last, twitching faintly as they are gradually converted from nerves into conductive fiber.

The boundary between animal and architecture, for them, is literally where they come to rest.

## The more numerous, more mutable lives

Below the vast movers and the half-plant clergy, a multitude of smaller forms thickens the basin’s web. They are each less central, but they are so adaptive that in aggregate they define the place as much as the grander residents.

### 4. Threadlings

The Threadlings are almost ubiquitous and almost invisible.

They are filaments, no thicker than a human hair when quiescent, coiled like ferns along the undersides of leaves, around the mouths of ground-sphincters, inside the ribbing of pillars. Only when the right sound passes do they betray themselves:

- A wave of mid-frequency vibration strikes a canopy membrane; suddenly hundreds of fine threads uncurl, stiffen, and stand out perpendicular to the surface, turning it from a smooth sheet into a bristled harp.
- A low thrum passes through a root; deep in a crack, bundles of Threadlings loosen, forming a lacework that makes the local tissue more porous.

They feed on minute flows: chemical gradients in the sap, stray ions carried by ever-trickling water, the mechanical energy of passing pressure waves which they convert, through piezoelectric-like structures, into metabolically useful forms.

Their real importance lies in their effect on the basin’s *acoustic geometry*. By stiffening or relaxing in response to specific frequencies, they dynamically alter the reflective and absorptive properties of surfaces. In human terms: they are the movable baffles and curtains of the endless concert hall.

- Predators cultivate pads of Threadlings along their ambush routes, “teaching” them with repeated calls to create quiet pockets where prey’s wave-signatures will be muffled.
- Certain pollinators carry Threadling spores on their bellies; when they visit a new membrane or trunk, they plant a familiar acoustic signature there, extending a patch of known sonic behavior.
- The plant-city itself leverages them to fine-tune its global resonance, seeding them heavily where it wants flexibility and thinning them out where it desires fixed, predictable echoes.

Over generations, some Threadling lines have been partially internalized by larger animals: a Strider’s plate-ear is lined with them; the ribs of Rib-gliders carry specialized clumps. In those bodies, the Threadlings’ responses have become more stereotyped, less ecumenical. They have become *organs* instead of wild devices.

If thought here is distributed, Threadlings are among its tiniest, most numerous synapses.

---

### 5. Echo-Fish

The few visible pools and narrow streams are noisy in their own right, not merely with splash and trickle but with a constant, almost metallic tinkling. That is the work of the Echo-Fish.

They are not fish in any strict sense—they breathe the saturated air and only dip into the water intermittently—but they are shaped by water:

- Bodies like folded ribbons, iridescent, their surfaces ciliated and studded with flexing plates.
- Along each side: a series of small, stiff fins that can snap open and shut, producing spikes of sound in tight, controlled bursts.
- A head reduced to a mere wedge with a cluster of pressure-pits on either side.

Echo-Fish harvest the echo-field of the basin. They have evolved to exploit a particular quirk of this world: the way sound can “shortcut” through matched resonances.

By emitting specific click-patterns into a pool or stream, an Echo-Fish can make its call *appear* in a distant cavity whose acoustic profile matches the water-body’s current shape and volume. Natural selection has turned this into alarm, communication, and feeding tool:

- A school of Echo-Fish hears the distant thrash of a predator in some other, sonically adjacent pocket. They respond with a burst of clicks that, transported through the basin’s odd geometry, erupt somewhere else entirely as a pulse that opens a sphincter, diverting a nutrient-rich seep toward their own pool.
- A lone Fish, sensing low nutrient content, will “ring” its water until it finds a resonance match with a more productive pocket; the plant-city, sensitive to these patterns, may gradually adjust flows so that the poor pool fattens and the rich one thins, smoothing extremes.

They themselves are prey to many things—Pool-Skimmers, quick vine-jawed ambushers, flexible-tongued Resonants seeking protein—but they are prolific, and their eggs can survive desiccation. When a pool dries, its mud may retain dormant Echo-Fish embryos for months, even years, until a rare, rich rain reconstitutes their world and they hatch in a synchrony of tiny metallic pings.

Echo-Fish are among the first outsiders adopt when they enter and remain in the basin long enough. Some small intruding line—say, a lizard-like creature wandering in from the plain—will learn, over generations, to produce its own sharp snaps with its jaws or tail, to piggyback on the Fish’s resonates. Thus, new threads of access to the city’s remote senses condense.

---

### 6. Archivores

Tucked into old, ossified corners—where pillars have long since hardened into mineral-loaded stumps and the city has layered new growth over old—live the Archivores.

They are slow, almost plantlike animals:

- Flat, disk-shaped bodies anchor flush against ancient surfaces, radiating soft, fleshy spokes that burrow shallowly into the substrate.
- At their centers, raised mounds of denser tissue cup translucent domes housing dense clusters of nerve.
- Their upper surfaces are furred with tiny, glassy hairs that vibrate in sympathy with the stone-like ribs beneath.

What they feed on is *pattern*.

The plant-city’s architecture is not merely structural; it is a long-term record of its own states. Repeated stress, recurring storms, centuries of particular resonant modes all leave microfractures, layered elastic deformations, and mineral deposits within old tissue. To an Archivore’s probing hairs and internal ossicles, these microscopic arrangements become tactile scores.

By flexing, pressing, and subtly deforming the substrate with their radial spokes, Archivores can “play back” old resonance fields in miniature, causing the ground under them to emit faint ghost-sounds: echoes of historical storms, the faded signatures of long-extinct fauna, the remembered stress of ancient collapses.

They digest the resulting flux of mechanical and electromagnetic noise the way others digest proteins and sugars, their central domes flickering with microseizures of activity as each replayed pattern is analyzed and metabolized.

Their presence is tolerated and even nurtured. The city’s active growth occasionally arcs around them instead of smothering them, leaving pockets of accessible “archive” intact. Resonants, especially older ones, sometimes visit Archivores, coiling their lower rings around their disks and entering into mutual seizure: the animal flooded with historical noise from below, the plant’s higher mind tasting its own long-ago states through the intermediary nervous system.

No single Archivore contains the whole history of the basin, only a localized excerpt. But taken as a population, distributed in quiet alcoves throughout, they are the basin’s only hint of autobiography—a way for the organism to feed on, and perhaps be shaped by, what it once was.

## The boundary folk

At the basin’s apertures, where the muffled roar leaks out into the wider, quieter world, dwell creatures whose entire being is bent toward translation.

### 7. Gate-Sieves

They appear, at first, to be just part of the plant—thick, curtain-like growths spanning the arched mouths that connect inside and outside. But they are something in between:

- Each Gate-Sieve is a broad sheet of semi-autonomous tissue, studded with clusters of sensory nodules and muscular valves.
- On the basin side, they present a porous, fibrous face, riddled with tiny openings that dilate and constrict in patterns.
- On the outside, they are smoother, duller, with only a few larger vents opening onto the plain.

Embedded within their substance are partner animals: small, blind, wormlike entities that live their entire lives threaded through Sieve-labyrinths. They act as messengers and regulators:

- When an external creature—say, a plain-dweller looking for moisture—approaches a gate, the Gate-Sieve tastes its odor, its heat, the subtle vibrations of its footfalls. The worms relay this information deep into the gate-body by changing their own writhing patterns, which the plant-tissue reads as a code.
- If the approach is judged compatible—neither predator of crucial basin species nor carrier of destructive fungus—the Sieve relaxes, its pores dilate, and a smear of warm, humid air and soft sound leaks outward, beckoning. The visitor feels a subtle pull, like the draft from a warm room into a cold corridor.
- If the approach is wrong, the Sieve stiffens, pores cinch, and for a time the gate becomes nearly impermeable; even the subsonic throb of the basin dulls.

The worms’ reward is a diet of rich sap and the detritus caught in the Sieve. Over generations, lineages of them have specialized: some respond chiefly to chemical cues from the outside, others to deep acoustic states inside, making the gate not a simple sensor but a small, integrated decision-making organ.

Gate-Sieves are how the city modulates its contact with the outer world: sometimes permissive, seeding seedlings and spores outward through relaxed valves; sometimes hermetic, battened down against droughts, fires, or waves of migratory grazers.

A few outside species have learned to “knock” correctly—specific percussive patterns on the outer Sieve-face that make the worms writhe in the way the city interprets as “ally,” granting them passage in both directions. Those are among the few true commuters between regimes.

### 8. The Returned

Occasionally, some line that originated elsewhere in the world enters the basin, survives, and adapts so thoroughly that it becomes one of the basin’s native notes. A mirror process, rarer but present, occurs in reverse: individuals born inside leave and establish small, persistent colonies outside.

They are collectively the Returned.

No single morphology unites them, but there are common changes they share, marks of the basin they carry outward:

- Modified hearing. Structures once used for ordinary sound—ears, lateral lines, tympana—are overbuilt, their ossicles fused into complex, nested arrangements. Outside the basin’s omnipresent roar, these organs border on overkill; the Returned experience the quiet plain as if moving through thin, brittle glass, every faint rustle a shock.
- Ghost-thread organs. Many carry internal or external remnants of Threadling cultures that once colonized them. These manifest as patches of skin or mucous membrane that bristle or soften in response to infrasonic or electromagnetic shifts, giving them a sixth or seventh sense for weather, distant footfalls, or tectonic murmurs.
- Altered neurology. Their brains (where such exist) tend to show a repeating, branching arrangement of nuclei, echoing the plant-city’s vascular logic. Some Returned exhibit mild “entanglement episodes,” where they react a heartbeat *before* a local stimulus—as if, even outside, some channels to the basin’s odd causality remain faintly open.

Most Returned form small communities near the basin rims or in other depressions with high humidity and warmth. They build nothing in the conventional sense; instead, they encourage local vegetation into shapes that recall the basin’s architecture: low arches of interwoven branches, thickets tuned by selective pruning to resonate when the wind blows just so.

They do not reproduce the city; the full organismic complex does not establish easily outside its geological and climatic niche. But they seed *tendencies*: stands of plants that hum more than is natural, patches of ground where rainwater behaves in oddly disciplined ways, pockets where animals congregate in patterns that make more sense if you’ve seen the basin’s inner order.

From the perspective of the basin, the Returned are like scout-signals sent into a broader medium—weak, lossy, but persistent.

## The intelligences

You can keep listing species, each a tuning fork for some corner of the soundfield, but eventually the question presses: where, here, is mind?

Some of it is clearly smeared across the plant-city itself, as earlier implied. Some of it is gathered in the nervous tissues of animals. But there is also a third locus: *temporary minds* that only exist when many bodies and tissues line up just so.

### 9. Choir-Events

At unpredictable intervals, a particular combination of circumstances converges:

- A pattern of internal weather: certain canopy segments tighten while others slacken, creating standing waves in the air.
- Striders cluster along a stress line, their plate-ears all clamped in parallel on a series of pillars.
- Resonants in several plazas adopt similar postures, their ringed bodies tuned to overlapping bands.
- Threadlings across a broad swath of surfaces stiffen in a coordinated phase.

When enough of these nodes fall into a compatible arrangement, the basin experiences a Choir-Event.

For a span ranging from a few seconds to several minutes, resonance locks:

- Vibrations that would normally disperse instead recirculate in closed loops, intensifying.
- Small time-distortions that are usually local and faint synchronize, producing an extended region in which cause-and-effect relationships momentarily narrow, become less noisy, more predictable.
- Animals whose nervous systems are plugged into the architecture—Resonants deeply rooted, Striders pressing their plate-ears into pillars, even Vault-Whales close enough to walls—undergo shared episodes of *coherent alteration*: they all experience the same processed stream of environmental data.

It is as if, for that span, the basin ceases to be a collection of many semi-coupled perceivers and becomes one larger, sharply-focused observer.

What is “thought” for this composite entity cannot be directly known from outside, but its *effects* can be mapped:

- After a Choir-Event, growth patterns alter subtly but consistently across wide regions, as if an overarching decision had been made about where to thicken, where to let collapse.
- Predation and foraging behaviors shift in tandem among multiple species, as if a new “rule of the game” had been promulgated: these pools, not those, are now safe; hunting here will be wasted effort, try there instead.
- Gate-Sieves adjust their standards, for a time admitting or barring classes of outsider that were previously treated differently.

The basin is always awake, always self-regulating. But in Choir-Events, it is briefly *self-aware* in a more acute sense, its distributed subminds interlocked tightly enough to generate high-level, global adaptations.

You could live your whole life here and be nothing more than a minor eddy in its ongoing thinking. Or, if your lineage becomes sufficiently integrated, there may be moments in which your body and brain help carry a fraction of a thought larger than anything you alone could contain.

## What it is like to be one of them

To inhabit this world as one of its creatures is to have your individuality threaded into rhythms larger and older than you.

- As a young Resonant, you learn not from elders in any didactic sense, but by letting your coils relax into root-sockets and feeling how posture choices bring pleasure or discomfort as the plant-mind’s load redistributes through you. “Right” behavior is whatever eases certain long, slow aches in the architecture.
- As a Vault-Whale calf, held buoyant in your parent’s lee, you are bathed from birth in the infrasonic gossip of elders and the endless low murmur of sap. The basin’s shape imprints itself into your bones as surely as tides shape coastal rock.
- As a Strider, each decision of where to step is partially yours and partially imposed, an impulse traveling up your leg from some distant stress crack. You are rewarded with surges of rich sap when you relieve the right supports, with gnawing emptiness when you stray.

None of these beings would conceive of themselves as separate “from nature” or “from city.” There is only inside and more inside, gradients of coupling.

Even among the less integrated—the Pool-Skimmers, the Echo-Fish, the climbing predators that snap from overhead ribs—there is little sense of a stable background world upon which life happens. The ground flexes, the ceilings breathe, the rules of distance shift with each day’s acoustic weather. Reliability comes only from the overall persistence of the roar and the warmth, and even those have moods.

Fear exists; pain exists; predation and death are not rare. But they are held within an unbroken continuity of signal. To die here is to have your last sensations hammered into the surrounding tissue by the same sound that carried all your earlier experiences. Your body is metabolized; your nervous patterns, to the degree they have ever left imprints in Threadlings, Archivores, and plant-conductive networks, become part of the basin’s enduring, if diffuse, memory.

---

## In the end

Who lives here?

- Vast, whale-like filterers of pressure and spore, traversing the open volumes between living walls.
- Many-legged Striders that eat old architecture and die into new columns.
- Pale Resonants, half-animal, half-organ, whose posture is the city’s voice shaped back onto itself.
- Filamentous Threadlings, reweaving the acoustic fabric with each twitch.
- Echo-Fish and Pool-Skimmers and column-borers and rib-gliders, each tuned to a different stratum of vibration and flow.
- Archivores reading the fossilized echoes of ancient roars.
- Gate-Sieves and their worms, acting as sentient valves between this saturated world and the thinner quiet outside.
- Returned creatures on the rim, carrying fragments of this logic into a larger, sparser landscape.
- And, overarching them all, a plant-city whose sense of self is smeared through sap, sound, and coordinated flesh, occasionally sharpening into the brief, bright lens of a Choir-Event.

They live not *on* a place but *as* its ongoing attempt to make sense of itself: bodies as hypotheses, niches as phrases, the entire basin one long, low, unending note, elaborated into countless harmonics that happen, for a while, to be alive.
