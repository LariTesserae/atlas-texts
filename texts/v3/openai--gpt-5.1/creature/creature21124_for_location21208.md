People live here, but they are not the city’s first impression.

From far enough away, they disappear into the texturing of terraces and growth, no more conspicuous than the birds or vines. They are not conquerors or curators of this place; they are a resident species inside a system that has outgrown the intentions that made it.

They call themselves many names, but most of those names are local. Identity is strongly vertical and weakly horizontal. A person from the High Mist Galleries will say they are a Gallery-dweller; a person from the old Gear Valleys in the middle strata will say they are a Gearfolk; someone from the Deep Reservoir Galleries will be a Basiner. “Citizen of the city” is more an abstraction than something anyone feels day to day.

They are recognizably human, or near enough: bipedal, dexterous, socially dense. But generations of life in this gradiented, hanging world have marked them.

Up high, where the air is brightest and thinnest, people tend toward narrowness: lean bodies, long hands, deep chests. Skin runs paler, not for lack of light but because of its abundance — they work under the sky, often veiled or hooded, and the light is diffuse rather than burning. Many have a weathered tone more from wind and moisture than from sun. Their clothing is layered for exposure: waxed fabrics, quilted linings, garments cut to shed constant condensation. Hoods with stiffened edges keep dripping water from running down necks; cuffs are reinforced where they brush against damp railings and rough bark.

Mid-level residents are stockier. Their lives run along terraces and through covered halls where the air is heavy and warm. Powerful legs, sure ankles, and a constant, unconscious readiness to step up or down define their movement. You see it in children: toddlers who learn balance not on flat ground but on sloped decks and scaled ramps, developing a low center of gravity and an instinctive use of handholds. Protective gear is more improvised here: patched knee-guards, rope-belts with carabiners, gloves with cut-off fingers for better grip on wet metal.

Deep-level dwellers carry the city’s weight in their very posture. They are broad-shouldered, thick in the limbs, skin stained by fungus-light and industrial luminescence. Their eyes adjust quickly to dimness; pupils dilate wide and slow to contract. Many have the faint, permanent squint of those who deal as much with glare from lamps and molten metal as with any sun. Their clothing is practical and sometimes crude: heavy boots that don’t slip on moss or slick steel; aprons of layered cloth and plastic; masks that can be pulled up against spores, dust, or chemical smells.

Across all levels, human bodies show small, converging adaptations to the city’s verticality. Toes splay slightly wider; arches and calf muscles are well-developed. Fingers are callused along odd angles from perpetual contact with ladders, grips, and cable-rails. Backs are strong, not from lifting in flat warehouses but from constant carrying up and down steep routes.

No single ethnicity dominates. The population is a blended continuum, long past remembering sharp ancestral divisions. Instead, differences of culture and custom track with height.

---

They live in habitats that blur architecture with growth.

In the upper levels, homes cling to the windward edges of broad terraces or nestle under the overhang of higher decks. Walls are composite — original paneling, later additions, and living matter. A dwelling might have one side that is still some old prefabricated unit, another formed from riveted scrap, a third basically a wall of climbing plants trained over a frame, thick enough to shrug off rain.

Inside, spaces are narrow but tall. Sleeping platforms are often lofted under sloping ceilings; storage hangs in vertical stacks. Windows are slits or shutters set to control crosswinds. Many homes incorporate micro-gardens as a matter of course: a trough along a wall sprouting herbs; a box beneath a condensation drip catching water and growing moss or salad greens. The boundary between house and walkway is porous: doors stand open in mild weather, and thresholds serve as both social spaces and workbenches.

Mid-level homes run along ridged streets and interconnected galleries. Some occupy what used to be office floors or administrative suites, partitioned and re-partitioned until the original layout is forgotten. Others tuck into structural alcoves — interstices between girders, small platforms beside freight-elevator shafts, niches above old service ducts. Here, where vegetation has become thick and almost architectural, people often let plants form part of their enclosure: a woven-liana screen as a room divider, a trunk used as a support pillar, fungal shelves dry-curved into shelving.

In the depths, housing is inseparable from industrial remnants. Families live in the old sublevels of factories, in sections of retired pipelines now cut open and turned into long, arched communal halls, in clusters of rooms carved out of redundant tankage. Here, light is the primary precious commodity; people arrange mirrors, ducts, and captured luminescence (bioluminescent fungi; salvaged LEDs) to make pools of brightness in the greenish gloom. Doors tend to be heavy and tight, not against wind but against humidity and spores.

Everywhere, dwellings are modular in imagination if not in fact. Walls get moved; floors are raised or cut through; a home might capture a new bit of vertical adjacency — a shaft above that becomes an extra room, a ledge below that is claimed as storage or a private garden. Ownership, such as it is, is as much about usage and care as about formal title. “Our place” might mean an apartment cluster, a section of walkway, a shared stairwell, and the patch of garden that overhangs it.

---

They do not rule the vegetation; they negotiate with it.

Gardening is universal. High-level farmers run broad terraces of constructed soil, cultivating grains and tubers bred for shallow rooting and quick maturity. Long-ago seedstocks have hybridized into varieties that prefer the city’s strange conditions: roots happy in rusty, metal-rich compost; leaves that drip condensation efficiently into runnels. Crops are terraced in narrow shelves that follow the long slopes of old freight decks, each step edged with plaited vine or low wall to hold the soil.

Mid-level growers focus on diversity rather than acreage. Here are fruiting shrubs, beans climbing along railings, gourds dangling from overhead beams. The air is so humid that many edible plants can live almost epiphytically, their roots tied in wrapped bundles of compost lashed to any available frame. Families maintain herb pillars — tall, cylindrical frameworks wrapped with pockets of soil and plantings, watered by slow drips from overhead pipes. Food hangs where there is light.

In the lower zones, agriculture merges with foraging and managed wildness. People cultivate shade-loving mushrooms on old pallets, tank interiors, and damp walls; they grow pale, crisp stalk-vegetables under the filtered gold from above. Nutrient cycles are intimate and closed: every scrap of organic matter is composted, fed to fungus beds, or given to the animals that accompany human life here.

People understand that unchecked growth can smother access and compromise structure. They prune back vines that threaten to lock a maintenance hatch, clear roots that begin to exert dangerous leverage on a beam, and harvest aggressively from opportunistic species that colonize critical paths. But they seldom strip a surface bare. Leaving some moss, some creepers, some flowering things is instinctive — both aesthetic and practical. Green surfaces cool hot metal, absorb water, hide glaring reflections, and provide texture for hand and foot.

Children learn plant language along with human language: which leaf-shapes signal soil under-foot versus hollow plating beneath; which fungal mats can be stepped on and which are fragile or toxic; how to read the drip patterns under a canopy to know if a passage will be slick or safe.

---

Animals here are not fully domesticated, but they are not wholly wild either.

Certain bird species have become close companions. High-level folk work with long-winged, sharp-eyed fliers as message carriers and scouts. Small birds nest deliberately in eaves specially built for them, their guano captured for fertilizer. People know individual breeding pairs, track lineages, and swap eggs with acquaintances on other terraces to keep flocks healthy and diverse.

Mid-level residents keep nimble, gliding mammals that move like a cross between ferrets and flying squirrels — adept at traversing cables, ducts, and vegetation. They serve as both pets and working animals, sent into complex ductwork to chase out pests or retrieve lightweight objects. Their sensitivity to vibration and sound sometimes warns of failing machinery or approaching tram-cars, and their patterns of agitation are quietly heeded.

In the depths, people share space with heavier creatures — thick-limbed browsers and wall-adhering predators. Some of these relationships are wary tolerance; others edge into mutualism. Deep-dwellers understand where the browsers habitually pass and plant accordingly, creating corridors of fodder that guide the animals away from fragile structures and toward areas where their droppings will most benefit soil beds. Predators are left alone when they hold to agreed territories; when one starts raiding animal pens or threatening people, a hunting party is organized with ritual care and reluctance.

Domestic animals — in the conventional sense of penned and bred solely for human use — are fewer than one might expect. Space is too complex, terrain too perforated. Instead, there’s a spectrum of partnership. Flocks of semi-wild grain-eaters follow harvest cycles along the terraces; amphibious creatures graze algae in cisterns and are harvested occasionally; “rat-analog” species have been nudged over generations toward cooperation, being rewarded when they keep their nesting away from critical cables.

People accept the constant presence of animal life as they accept machines and plants: as something that moves through their space but is not solely theirs.

---

Technology is a craft, not a mystique.

No one here believes the city is magic. They can point to the pumps, to the old dynamos, to the buried cores that hum at certain nodes. But most lack the system-wide view the original builders had. Knowledge has localized, like everything else.

There are Guilds — though not always called that — who hold the deep arts of the city’s infrastructure. Some specialize in water: reading pressure in pipes by sound and feel; knowing which cisterns can be drawn down in late dry weeks without starving lower levels; tuning the slow, thumping hearts of the vertical pumps. These people wear tools like jewelry: ring-keys for valves, song-tuned metal rods for listening through walls, tiny vials of dye to trace leaks.

Others focus on electricity. They maintain the patchwork of cables, junctions, and transformers. To them, the city is a branching, glowing thing whose pulses they can sense in flickers of light, in the hum beneath their feet. They teach apprentices how to recognize the smell of an overheating line, the particular pitch of a failing insulator. Manuals exist — old, patched, copied in handwriting — but practice matters more than diagrams.

Mechanists keep the transit lines running, the cranes sweeping their patient arcs, the trams’ wheels trued to the rails. They scavenge relentlessly, turning decommissioned machines into parts donors, salvaging components from old lines to keep newer ones running. In their workshops you’ll see piles of bearings, gears, cracked housings, each tagged with provenance and compatibility.

But these are not high priests. They are respected professionals. Around them, ordinary people practice quieter, more domestic technologies. They patch clothing with fabrics woven from plant fibers and reclaimed synthetics. They shape simple tools from layered scrap: knife blades hammered out of old machine casings; hooks bent from reinforcing rod; containers folded from softened panels.

Importantly, the city’s great supporting systems — levitation, suspension, whatever anchors this continent that refuses to fall — lie largely below the threshold of daily interference. They are deep, remote, buried under strata of retrofits and growth. Stories circulate about the lowest levels, where true core machinery still lives: tales of maintenance orders written in a language no one fully reads anymore, of rooms so hot and loud you cannot enter without special gear, of machines that cannot be turned off because no one knows how to turn them safely back on.

A minority of people devote themselves to understanding those myths. They are archivists, structural historians, “deep schemers” who collect old maps and cross-reference tales of failure and repair. They climb — or descend — to places most avoid, mapping load paths, listening to the deep timbre of the city’s bones. Some treat this as science. Others wrap it in ritual, approaching core spaces with offerings of polished scrap or carefully prepared lubricants, half out of superstition, half out of professional respect.

---

Social life flows along the city’s vertical channels and clings to its edges.

Communities form around shared access: to a particular set of cisterns; to a tram line; to a series of stacked terraces; to a maintenance shaft that doubles as a communal stair. A “neighborhood” is as much a column as a district: a column of space threaded with lives over several levels, people greeting each other through grates and open hatches, lowering baskets on ropes between floors to share food or tools.

Markets cluster where flows intersect. At a mid-level balcony where a main freight line passes and a wide stair from above meets a tram platform from below, there will be stalls. People bolt tables into anchor points on the decking, drape awnings between repurposed cranes and vine-strung beams. Goods arrive in layered fashion: deep-grown mushrooms and metal ingots from below, broad-leaf vegetables and machine parts from the middle, grains and bird eggs and high-fruit from above.

Trade travels mostly vertically, because that’s where the largest differences lie. High-level farmers send down bulk food; mid-level craftspeople send up finished tools, containers, fabrics; deep-level workers send repair materials and processed metals both ways. Currency exists — tokens, stamped plates of known composition, sometimes denominated in energy or water-credit — but barter is not rare. Reputation, obligation, and clan networks handle as many transactions as formal money.

Family networks often stretch across heights. It’s common for one sibling to choose work in the lower factories, another to apprentice with waterkeepers mid-level, a third to farm high terraces. They visit via tram or stair, carrying news, small gifts, and recipes. Stories told to children emphasize these vertical kin connections; heroes are those who can traverse many levels, reading the city accurately enough to travel safely and bring something needed back.

Festivals are shaped by the city’s rhythms. When reservoirs reach their annual peak, some neighborhoods hold Water-Walks, parading over the exposed tops of tanks and open channels, strings of lamps reflecting in mirror-smooth surfaces. When certain flowering vines burst into synchronized bloom along a structural arc, communities host lantern nights there, letting people from other levels come walk the glowing corridors of scent and color.

Sound is central to social navigation. People mark their presence with footsteps, with work songs that sync hammering and hauling, with whistles that cut through the layered ambient noise. Each vertical column of neighborhood tends to develop its own auditory signature: a call-and-response used across shafts; a particular knock-pattern on metal beams announcing visitors; distinctive bells near critical junctions. Music is often percussive or choral, taking advantage of echoing shafts and broad, resonant decks.

Conflict happens. Territory matters — not in an abstract sense, but in where your children can play safely, where your gardens won’t be shadowed out by a neighbor’s new construction, which route your goods can travel without toll. Tensions rise when someone reroutes a drainage without consulting those downstream, or when a tram schedule changes and cuts off reliable access to a market.

Violence, when it occurs, is constrained not by lack of will but by the environment. A thrown person can fall a long way. A fire set recklessly can travel along insulation, climb roots, and gut a whole column. People know this. Feuds tend to manifest as obstruction and withdrawal: a community refusing access through a key stairwell; workers in one district declining to service another’s pumps; a gardening collective deciding not to prune back plants that will, over time, choke a route.

To prevent spirals, there are mediators. Not a unified government, but a mesh of councils, elders, guild representatives, and respected individuals who move between strata. They invoke tradition, practical necessity, and sometimes the hard facts of the city’s load limits: “If you do this, that beam will fail, and all of us in this column lose our homes.” The city itself is the ultimate arbiter — not morally, but physically.

There are also those who leave the settled routes.

Some become Walkers: semi-nomadic people who travel along the outer trusses, across neglected overpasses, through half-forgotten machine halls at the periphery. They map things, in notebooks or in memory. They ferry messages and rare goods between vertical regions that official trams never connect directly. Walkers know old safety lines, hidden ladders, and which segments of deck can carry what weight. Many communities treat them as a mix of courier, scout, and good-luck charm: someone whose presence means news is coming and who will likely point out a rusted-through railing before a child leans on it.

Others go deeper downward than most dare, into the infra-structural roots where the city is thickest and light scarcest. They are half-legend, half-documented. Travelers’ tales speak of rooms where no plants grow because the air is too dry and hot; of wells of perfect blackness where the city’s material simply stops, dropping away into unguessable depth. Some deep-goers return with stories and salvage — strange alloys, unfamiliar tools, fragments of signage in archaic scripts — that filter upward slowly, changed into myth along the way.

---

Belief here is not dominated by sky-gods or earth-spirits; instead, it orbits processes.

People tell stories about Flow — the way water, energy, and life move through the city’s conduits and corridors. Some make it almost animistic: the City, in this view, is a vast, slow animal; its pumps are its hearts, its vents its breath, its cranes the deliberate arms of its unknown will. Most accept the metaphor without committing to literal belief. When a pump fails spectacularly, someone will murmur that the City had a “flutter.” When a stuck valve suddenly frees after long coaxing, they’ll say it has “decided” to cooperate.

Others frame it more abstractly, as Balance: between weight and support, input and waste, growth and clearance. There are small household rites around this notion. Before adding a new structure — a room, an extra layer of soil, a heavy machine — people may perform a “weighing,” literal or symbolic: estimating loads, invoking an elder’s judgment, sometimes placing a token on an old beam and watching how it reacts to a test weight. If the sounds and strains are wrong, plans change. It’s not superstition in the sense of ignoring physical reality; it’s a cultural form wrapped around hard-learned engineering caution.

Long-term memory is preserved intentionally. There are Archive Gardens — spaces where plants and inscriptions interweave. Plaques fixed to structural members note past repairs, floods, collapses averted. Some are generations old, their lettering softened by moss, but they’re maintained enough to stay legible. Children learn to read by tracing those marks.

At certain heights, you find Rooms of Stories: chambers carved out of old machine housings, lined with murals showing how a neighborhood’s terraces looked before a factory was converted, or before a particular vine-species colonized a bridge. Storytellers work here, comparing oral histories against these visual records, reconciling contradictions in front of an audience. Memory is a living thing, as dynamic as the vines, but it roots in these places.

As for the city’s origin — how it came to be hanging, how its impossible underpinnings endure — answers are partial. Some insist the ancestors built it in stages from a grounded world that no longer exists, raising it to escape some forgotten catastrophe. Others say it was always an island in the air, a fragment of some larger, shattered work, and that the ground is a myth. A few pragmatic souls shrug and say that whatever lies below is inaccessible and therefore irrelevant. Daily life happens here, between root and sky.

---

Children grow up understanding a kind of intimate vastness.

A child’s playground might be a stair-core wrapped in vines, its landings turned into dare-based routes: climb this arc without touching the inside rail; cross that gap using only the thickest liana; dare each other to peer through the grating into the misty void and drop a pebble, counting heartbeats until the faintest ting of its landing.

They make games out of the city’s acoustics: shouting into ventilation shafts to hear the layered echoes; tapping on railings to communicate in improvised codes across sightlines broken by foliage. They learn quickly that noise means safety sometimes — trams approaching, cranes moving above — and danger at others — rumbling that says a long-dormant machine has come to life beneath a floor.

Their sense of distance is warped. “Far” is more often down or up than out. A friend might live only ten meters away in horizontal terms, but five levels different; visiting involves a complex dance of stairs, lifts, and crossings. This vertical distance shapes community boundaries. Closer in height equals closer in practice, even if physically sideways travel could, in theory, connect other neighbors more easily.

As they age, children take on tasks that align them with their level’s work: water-carrying, light pruning, tool-fetching along scaffolds. But there are always a few who look up and down with restless eyes. These become the new Walkers, the future guild-apprentices who can stitch the city’s strata together.

---

Elders measure time differently from their grandchildren, but both feel it as a presence in the very surfaces.

Old people point out places where their parents saw bare metal, now buried in soil thick enough for trees. They remember when a deck used to smoke and ring with the impact of machines, now quiet under moss. They’ll gesture at a tram line and say, “In my youth that carried ore, not people,” or tap a girder and recount how it sounded under strain before a reinforcement was added.

Work crews talk in “deck-lives” — the span of years between major overhauls of a given platform or structural spine. Farmers think in “soil-ages” — how many generations it takes for a planter to go from raw rubble to stable, rich mix. Deep-level technicians speak of “pump-cycles” that outlast a human lifetime, their full waveform visible only across the records of several generations.

No one expects rapid transformation. Change is incremental, accretive. A balcony becomes a garden one season, then slowly, over decades, that garden becomes shaded by trees, prompting someone to cut a new light-shaft above. A disused corridor becomes overgrown, then is carved open into a new marketplace when population shifts demand it.

Because the city is so established, large-scale catastrophes are rare, but they are remembered and integrated. A partial collapse here, a major pipe rupture there — they leave scars and stories. A sagging deck might be left unrepaired, its safe portions cordoned off and turned into a semi-wild terrace where only children and animals venture. People fold danger into the collage of the familiar.

---

So: who lives here?

Humans, yes, in all their adaptations: patient, pragmatic, physically tuned to gradient and handhold. They are not post-human in the sense of gleaming implants and radical redesign, but they are thoroughly of their place, as specialized as any plateau tribe or coastal fisherfolk, except their plateau is a made thing and their coastlines are balconies as steep as cliffs.

Alongside them live their tecnics: machines that act almost like beasts of burden or collaborators — cranes that never sleep, trams that are understood as personalities (“That one always jerks on the east curve”), pumps whose rhythms are part of the neighborhood’s soundscape.

With them live the plants, no longer ornamental or bounded, but fully co-equal occupants: roots that hold soil against metal; vines that bind joints; mosses that soften and seal; pale, spidery undergrowth in the depths.

Around them live the animals: some wary, some curious, some practically domesticated; all using the city’s layers as habitat, not intrusion.

Together, they form an ecosystem in which humanity is neither master nor intruder but an embedded participant. The hanging city is old enough that any notion of pristine origin has dissolved. There is no “before” to return to, no wild ground waiting beneath the decks. There is only this: a world of made strata so long inhabited that they have grown their own forests, their own weathered paths, their own quiet wildness.

The people who live here know this in their bones. When they stand on a high terrace and look down through mist to the gardens and rail-lines below, or when they look up at decks veiled in cloud and foliage, they do not think of themselves as perched on an anomaly. They think: this is the world. It hangs. It endures. It grows. And we, like the roots and the rivets, like the beams and the birds, belong to its ongoing weight.
