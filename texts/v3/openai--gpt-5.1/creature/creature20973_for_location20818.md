They live like plaque.

That is, they live as a dense, layered, self-thickening occupation of every available interface, a three‑dimensional mat spread across a shard of hot geology. But “they” is not one thing. This is a place where the concept of an “individual organism” is blurry, where most lives are pieces of a fabric, and only a few stand out as separate actors.

Start with the quietest inhabitants: the ones that do not move so much as reconfigure.

---

### The film-makers

Directly on the raw mineral, on every facet where gas and stone meet, lie the film-bearers: ultra-thin, almost two-dimensional metabolic systems that blur the line between environment and organism.

Some are pure spreaders—sheets of carbon–sulfur polymers laced with metal ions, only a few dozen molecules thick, sharing a common ancestry with what, long ago, would have been inanimate catalytic surfaces. They are not “cells”; they are phase-separated regions of conductivity and composition. To name them, you would have to name an area rather than a body. The chamber’s inhabitants treat them similarly: they are like soil.

Within these films, though, are the first recognizable “entities”: nodules, whirlpools, and islands of altered composition where the spreading chemistry has locally folded back on itself. These islands maintain slightly different charge patterns and catalytic preferences than their surroundings. They bud, split, and drift along the substrate boundaries over days. They do not crawl—they are advected by slow thermal creeping of the film itself, their identity persisting as a pattern in motion rather than a fixed piece of matter.

These are the basal “organisms” in the ecological sense: they fix the free energy of the place. Where hot, reduced gas meets slightly cooler, oxidized mineral; where metal vapor condenses; where sulfur species change oxidation state—those heat and redox drops are caught and milked by these extended, nervous carpets. They turn gradients into metastable compounds: complex carbon–sulfur chains, metal complexes, weird salts that can survive long enough to be eaten.

They are not coordinated as a single brain, but information does move within them. Their conductive domains twitch in potential when a shock passes or a vent’s composition shifts. These potentials propagate in spirals, forks, and fronts over centimeters of wall, modulating catalytic rates like local weather forecasts: “More reduced gas coming—open channels here; close them there.” The films remember, in a primitive way, where the good flows recur, marking those sites with crystalline reinforcements that are thicker, more heat-tolerant, more attractive to later colonists.

In another environment, something like this might be dismissed as mere corrosion. Here, it is metabolism, slow and foundational.

### The clingers and plates

Above this base, the first clearly finite bodies appear: the disk-creatures that scale the glass walls, forming overlapping shingles.

Call them *laminids*. Each looks, at a glance, like a coin of ceramic and dull metal, slightly domed. Up close, the surface is a maze of ridges and pits, tuned to micro‑turbulence in the passing gases. Their lower faces are entirely different: a soft, dark, almost tar-like layer that conforms to the film beneath, bonding just enough to stick, not enough to fuse.

Around each rim, a corona of tiny hooked spines digs into pores and cracks in the rock. These are not mere anchors; they are conduits. Inside the body, each spine connects to a radiating tree of conductive veins, all meeting in a slow, oscillating core. When vibrations from the external engines thrum through the stone, the spines flex microscopically, turning strain into tiny pulses of charge. These pulses help power the laminid’s chemistry, like a piezoelectric heart.

Laminids feed in exquisite slowness. Every few hours, they collectively rotate a fraction of a turn, presenting a fresh surface to the gas stream. Across their upper faces are arrays of catalytic patches that pull specific reactive species out of the flow—radicals, ionized fragments, metal clusters. These get shepherded inward, across gradients of temperature and electrical potential, until they reach buried zones where safer, more stable compounds form. Some of these feed the laminid’s own maintenance: repairing microcracks, thickening its shell, replacing degraded polymers. Others are excreted, oozed back out as thin films under the body, enriching the substrate below.

This excreta is prime food for smaller grazers. As a consequence, the underbellies of laminid mats are bustling exchanges: microfilaments probing, scraping, stealing.

Laminids are not solitary in their decisions. When tightly packed, their rims touch, and their conductive trees link at the tips of their spines. In these moments of contact, a potential shift in one can sweep through hundreds, even thousands nearby. A vent warms, its gas composition tips toward a dangerous mix—one laminid detects the new pattern of ions, tightens its flow channels, and that “decision” ripples as a wave of reconfiguration across the mat. In this way, a laminated wall can respond as one organism, tightening or loosening gaps between disks, subtly redirecting gas flow over the surface.

In the faint violet chemiluminescent glow of certain cracks, entire laminar colonies show faint, shifting halos—not because they emit light themselves, but because their upper surfaces periodically alter what wavelengths they re-radiate, following the passing pulses of metabolic rearrangement. Seen over long time exposures, a wall of them looks like a slow aurora pressed flat against rock.

### The shard-walkers

Moving above and between these fixed colonies are the shard-creatures: elongated, angular beings made of interlocking mineral segments, like articulated needles of bone‑white and smoke‑gray crystal.

Their bodies are bands of repeating plates with jagged edges that mate along zigzag seams. Each seam hosts a line of flexible linkers—a composite of heat-softened polymer and ceramic nanofibers—allowing the whole structure to bend incrementally without melting or fracturing. Anchoring at one end with embedded hooks, they bow and then straighten, ratcheting themselves forward, a millimeter at a time.

Shard-walkers are voracious and destructive on the tiny scale. They scrape. The underside of each segment bears a row of teeth—nano-etched ridges that shave off the mineral films and the laminids’ exudates. The shavings enter a narrow channel running the length of the body, where a sequence of hot and cooler chambers subject them to stepwise reactions: binding metals here, cracking long chains there. What emerges at the rear is a smear of exhausted mineral, stripped of its highest free-energy bonds, seeded with tiny crystals that will later grow into something else.

In consuming, they also till. The smooth surfaces they leave behind are temporarily depopulated of films, but microcrystals and fragments in their waste can act as nucleation points, changing how new film chemistries grow. Some shard-walkers in fact cultivate particular secondary films behind them—symbiotic strains that, when re‑grazed later, return a better yield of energy‑rich compounds. To do this, their digestive tract releases not just waste, but packaged “starter” complexes into it: clumps of catalysts and seed polymers that bias the next generation of film.

Different shard lineages specialize. There are high‑heat shard-walkers near the hottest vents, their segments thick, heavily doped with refractory metals, moving sluggishly but able to bite directly into nearly molten glass. Others haunt cooler margins, finer and more delicate, preferring the complex, metal-rich deposits around old fissures. To an outside eye they may look the same; to the rest of the chamber, their diet is destiny.

Shard-walkers are the main visible drivers of erosion here. Where they congregate, ledges undercut, thin protrusions collapse, surfaces become flaked and crumbly. Over broad spans, they steer how the chamber reshapes itself: the places where rock gives way faster are also where gas flows will later divert, and with them, all the dependent ecologies.

### The dust-swarms and soot intelligences

Most of the inhabitants are far smaller than a shard’s segment. They are the dust—alive, countless, and collectively powerful.

At the limit of what the dim light can resolve are the “soot flocks”: dense, matte clouds that shiver and creep over surfaces, their motion more like a fluid’s than a gathering of individuals. Each member of such a flock is nearly planar: a disc or irregular flake of carbonaceous polymer, maybe a few hundred nanometers across, laced with metal inclusions. At this scale, turbulent motion and thermal jostling toss them constantly; they do not have muscles or limbs. Instead, they bias their sticking and un-sticking.

On one face of a soot flake are patches with a higher affinity for certain mineral films or for particular adsorbed species in the gas. On the other face, the chemistry favors rapid release. By altering which side becomes more polar, more charged, or more catalytically active, the flake can increase the probability of rebounding from a collision versus clinging. This subtle bias, repeated trillions of times, lets whole swarms “ooze” up thermal gradients or “pool” around rich gas leaks.

Because they collide constantly, they are also an ideal substrate for information. Some flocks bear on their surfaces tiny conformational switches—small structural motifs that change state when exposed to specific ions or to a particular voltage pattern, and that then persist in that state for some time. When two flakes touch, their surfaces can “scan” each other, flipping these motifs on or off in response.

In this way, a chemical/physical signal can propagate by contagion: one flake touches another, which touches another, each adjusting its state and movement bias in a way that encodes “more of this direction,” “less of that gas,” or “danger: nearby film becoming corrosive.” The flock, at the scale of microns and milliseconds, becomes a nerve net—decentralized but capable of emergent decisions.

Areas where soot flocks are particularly dense become quasi-organisms. They can direct their bulk to envelop a nutrient-rich patch of film, darkening it, effectively starving out light- or radiation-sensitive competitors beneath. Where they stack into many layers, their collective conductivity may form transient circuits bridging different surfaces. These can join the laminid mats’ networks or span gaps in the fractured walls, creating temporary, living “wires” that redirect charge flows on the order of centimeters.

Some particularly elaborate flocks are the closest thing this chamber has to mobile generalists: they can detach, ride rising convection as loose motes, then reconverge elsewhere in a new configuration. Their persistence is not that of specific molecules but of patterns of switched states—memory smeared across a population.

### The bead-rollers and living gravel

Covering every horizontal surface and filling crevices like sand are the bead-creatures: tiny spheres to irregular pebbles, from microns to a few millimeters in diameter. Imagine a beach made of slow, grinding animals, each a sealed world.

Each bead is multilayered. The outer shell is hard, ceramic or glassy, its chemistry tuned to withstand specific temperature ranges and corrosive mixes. Under this is a more complex laminate: alternating strata of conductive carbon, insulating mineral, and porous reservoirs filled with high‑boiling liquid salts or heavy organics. Near the center lies the “logic core”: an irregular lattice of structures that can rearrange very slowly, altering how currents and ions flow through the bead.

Beads move mostly by surrendering to the chamber’s violence. Vibrations prop them into low, hopping slides. Pressure waves from distant machine surges roll them along, knocking them into one another. In these collisions, they share surface films, exchange catalytic particles, even chip off bits of shell that become the seeds of new beads.

Some beads are feeders: they seek contact with freshly precipitated mineral deposits, their surfaces bristling with flexible, nanoscale “tongues” that wick up reactive species into inner reservoirs. Others are excretors: they irreversibly precipitate spent products into fragile, brittle crusts that flake away easily, deliberately shedding slag in order to stay chemically nimble inside.

Predation among beads is subtle but real. Certain lineages grow minute, spike-like protrusions that, upon impact, penetrate the shells of softer neighbors. Through these microtunnels, the attacker pumps corrosive or reactive cocktails, destabilizing the other’s internal layers. The victim may fracture, spilling its partially processed stores into the surrounding gravel. In time, specialized scavenger beads have evolved to follow in the wake of these breakages, tuned to rapidly absorb the mix of liberated compounds.

Whole avalanches—the rippling, soft roar when the chamber shudders—are ecological resets. Layers reorder; beads that were buried and cool are exposed and heated; communities that once exchanged chemicals with certain neighbors find themselves pressed against strangers. Many beads incorporate impact-history into their cores: their internal logic lattices realign in response to particular sequences of collisions and temperature shocks, adjusting which reactions they prioritize afterward. Over long spans, a bead’s “personality”—what it tends to do, how it responds to change—emerges from this continuously written mechanical biography.

In rare pockets where motion is less violent, beads can pack into dense, self-supporting arrays, wedged and locked. There, their outer shells almost fuse, and their inner circuits can link in near-permanent networks. These bead “reefs” act as long-term reservoirs of unusual chemistries: they accumulate rare reactants that seldom reach elsewhere, building up stocks that, when finally broken and scattered by some quake, seed new and unexpected metabolisms across the chamber.

### The floaters and cage-drifters

Suspended in the dense, hot gas are the floaters: small, intricate frameworks, more like engineered air filters than animals as you know them.

They are polyhedral cages of carbon fiber and metal, each edge a composite rod that can flex slightly and carry charge. Faces are open, spanned in some species by ultrathin meshes, in others by nothing at all. The geometry varies—icosahedra, elongated ladders, asymmetric tangles—but all share one functional trait: they present a large, webbed surface to the streaming ion-rich gas.

Across their frameworks, there are embedded sites that selectively capture charged species—ions, electrons, radicals, even tiny metal clusters. The flow of gas through the cage slams these into contact; specific pockets bind them temporarily, then hand them off inward along gradients of potential. Deep in the cage, protected from the shearing winds, cluster-nuclei form: stable complexes in which high-energy species are caged together in ways that prevent immediate, wasteful reaction. These are what the floater “feeds” on, gradually letting reactions proceed along pathways that do usable work: pumping charge along the struts, altering bond networks, building new segments of framework, or forming detachable packets of nutrient that will eventually be released when the cage collides with a surface.

Floaters control their position crudely. By altering which struts carry current, they can heat selected parts of their skeleton slightly, changing local gas expansion and drag. Asymmetries in temperature produce tiny thrusts and torques, enough to tilt or drift toward richer plumes or away from searing microjets. They are, in effect, self‑steering embers that never quite burn out.

When they brush against a wall or a laminid mat, specialized contact points lock briefly, discharging excess built-up charge into the substrate and sucking up slower, more massive compounds in return. These docking events are also opportunities for reproduction and exchange: floaters can bud off small subcages, preloaded with a minimal chemistry, which stick for a while to the surface before detaching into the gas again. Alternately, they can fuse, two cages tangling and partially merging their frameworks, their internal chemistries in turmoil for a time while a new, hybrid pattern of reactions settles out.

Floaters are the main medium by which exotica—rare, fleeting gas-phase intermediates—are brought down to the slower world of films and beads. Their very existence ties the roaring, turbulent bulk of the atmosphere to the clotted, surface-bound life below.

### The burrowers and infiltrators

The chamber’s rock is not homogeneous, and neither is its life. Much of the living mass is not visible in the open but infiltrates the rock itself: thin, branching channels of chemistry threading through pores and cracks.

Some of these are lineages descended from the original mineral films that first colonized this pocket. Where early precipitates formed along grain boundaries and in pore walls, tiny gradients existed between the inner and outer surfaces, between slightly different mineral faces, between gas-filled and glass-filled voids. Chemistries that exploited these differences propagated inward, elaborating into filamentous networks.

These *infiltrants* are constructed like flexible mineral roots: narrow tubes of semi‑ordered ceramic carbon composites, filled with viscous, reactive melts. They grow ahead by dissolving a little of the rock around their tips and reprecipitating it behind as a shell. They are extremely slow—advancing perhaps microns a day—but their environment changes even more slowly. The heat and pressure are constant enough that, for them, a day is the blink of an eye.

They are the primary long-distance transporters in this miniature world. Where surface ecologies are localized and choked by crowding, infiltrant networks connect them. A thread that penetrates near one vent and emerges near another allows compounds, charge, and information to pass between very different regimes—hot, reducing niches and cooler, more oxidizing ones; metal-rich zones and sulfur-rich ones. Over centuries, these networks can ramify through significant swaths of surrounding strata, turning the entire region around the chamber into a loosely coherent biosphere.

Some infiltrants have evolved specialized terminal structures at their openings to the chamber air or to interior cavities: bulbous, many‑chambered knobs that act as couplers. Each chamber within the knob hosts a different chemistry and a different interface—spongy faces for exchanging with beads, conductive spines for linking with laminid circuits, porous domes for receiving soot flocks. These nodes act as ecological “ports,” where deep interior resources touch the bustling surface layers.

Because they access resources over long spans of space and time, infiltrants are kingmakers. A laminid colony connected to a rich infiltrant vein may outcompete neighbors by suddenly having a supply of rare elements that normally arrive only via chance gas plumes; a bead reef plugged into a deep sulfur vein may begin excreting compounds that reconfigure the local chemistry altogether. In this sense, the infiltrants are the closest thing the chamber has to silent architects.

### Networks, crowds, and “minds”

In a space this crowded, lives merge at the edges. The rules about information—favoring contact over distance—shape who “they” are at a cognitive level.

No single organism here possesses anything like a brain. Even the most elaborate individual—a large shard-walker with many segments, a floater with complex internal circuits—is essentially a bundle of reflexive loops, tuned to local conditions, slow to change.

But when bodies touch—laminid plates pressing rim to rim, beads wedged in contact, soot flakes colliding in their billions, infiltrant nodes plugging into multiple partners—conductive pathways form temporarily. Potentials shift. Patterns of charge, of reaction rates, of state-changes, can sweep through many organisms in a wave.

Some of these patterns are simple: “too hot, tighten shells”; “new reactive gas, open channels.” Others, in more densely connected patches, may approach something like computation. A band of wall colonized by at least three layers—film, under-mat of flexibly linked laminids, dense soot coating—can support repeating loops of potential that circle endlessly unless damped, reminiscent of standing waves in a neural network. Changes in gas flow or rock vibration alter those loops, and their new configurations bias future responses. Over long durations, the region “learns” that certain sequences of events have occurred before and leaves enduring traces in its chemical and structural layout.

These are not self-aware minds, but they are distributed memories and anticipatory systems. The chamber, in pieces, remembers.

It remembers where flakes fell in the past and left voids—that is where infiltrant nodes now cluster, waiting for the next opening. It remembers long cycles in the external machinery’s output—soot nets, over many “seasons,” tune their movement biases to arrive at certain vents just before their periodic riches. Laminid mats, by the slow altering of their shell thickness and spacing, reshape gas microflows over generations, stabilizing favorable patterns.

From a certain vantage, one could say that “they,” the inhabitants, collectively think—but the thought is not in any one place, and never unified. It is a percolating, chattering, rewiring process in which every contact and collision writes a tiny bit of logic into the fabric of the community.

### Lineages of strategy

Across all these forms, some recurring survival strategies define who they are.

**Heat as prey.** For them, “cool” is value. Many lineages—laminids with radiative fins, beads with high-surface shells, floaters with adjustable emissivity—practice active cooling not for comfort but to maintain steep temperature gradients across their bodies. Those gradients power semiconducting junctions, drive diffusion, and keep reaction networks away from equilibrium. To these organisms, spending stored chemical wealth to radiate more efficiently is a kind of hunting, an expenditure to keep their favorite prey—usable free energy—nearby.

**Surface conquest.** Populations evolve to fight for attachment rights. Laminids secrete inhibitors that slow the formation of base films in their shadow, ensuring less competition close beneath. Shard-walkers sometimes carry on their undercarriages small “allies”—narrow film strips that, once deposited on a freshly scraped patch, rapidly monopolize the space and later convert into compounds only the shard’s descendants can use. Beads exude thin glazing layers that, when they come to rest en masse on a surface, temporarily block new films from gaining a foothold.

**Chemical warfare in contact.** Because diffusion through the dense gas is slow compared to direct touch, many interactions are molecular grappling matches. Some soot flocks carry on one side of their flakes a “venom”—a pattern of catalytically active motifs that, when pressed against certain film chemistries, destabilize them, turning competitor substrates into useless glassy scabs. Beads that sense intrusion by a foreign infiltrant tip release jets of corrosive melt that clog its channels. Laminids may “sting” intrusive shard tips by locally inverting potentials, dumping stored charge into them and frying sensitive junctions.

**Frugality of structure.** Nothing here grows large and empty. Shells are thin. Branches are as short as function allows. Floaters are mostly framework, almost no volume. Beads keep their cores just big enough for their reaction sets, nothing more. When lineages push toward larger size—for greater storage, for longer-lived internal patterns—they pay in fragility: collisions crack them more easily, thermal gradients become harder to manage. Only a few have solved this well enough to edge into what might be called “macrofauna” by local standards—our millimeter giants.

### The “giants”

Among those rare giants are peculiar composite beings: stacks, braids, or braids-of-stacks where many smaller organisms align so consistently they effectively behave as one body.

One such is the *columnar braid*. Picture a flexible, finger-length tower (in human terms, only a few millimeters tall) built from beads, laminids, and soot flakes all locked into a tight, spiraling arrangement. At the core, a bundle of infiltrant filaments runs lengthwise, bringing deep resources. Around this, several layers of beads form a load-bearing sheath, their shells abutting like stones in a column. Between them, soot flocks cement the gaps, adjusting conductivity and chemical access. On the exterior, a skin of flattened laminid derivatives forms overlapping scales.

These columns grow in sheltered crevices where vertical gas flows are steady. Their internal filaments feed from below; their tops present elaborate catalytic arrays to the incoming stream. They flex slightly with the roaring vibrations outside, straining and relaxing like reeds in wind. Because all their components touch in stable arrangements, their electrical and chemical states are tightly coupled: patterns of potential can cycle from foot to tip and back, and adjustments in one layer affect all the others. Over time, a column can exhibit complex, quasi-rhythmic behaviors—periodically modulating how porous its skin is, or alternating between phases of stockpiling certain intermediates and phases of releasing others.

When part of a column is broken off by a rockfall or an unusually strong vibration, the fragment can sometimes survive as a new individual if enough of its internal circuits remain continuous. More often, it is slowly dismantled by shard-walkers and beads, its specialized components returning to the general pool. Columns are thus rare and short-lived compared to films and infiltrants, but while they stand, they play outsized roles, sharply shaping adjacent gas flows and chemistry.

Another “giant” form is purely mobile: a *conglomerate raft* of soot, floaters, and beads clumped together in a passing eddy, large enough to be seen as a distinct mass drifting across a hollow. These rafts are unstable but can persist for hours, even days, held together by mutual charge attractions and by hooks and barbs on their members. While coherent, they behave like roving ecosystems, carrying with them little pockets of conditions: beads soaking in an internal mix of compounds, floaters along the edges harvesting fresh ions, soot gluing it all and propagating coordination signals. When the raft crashes against a wall or is torn apart by shear, the pieces scatter, seeding distant surfaces with mixtures of lineages that otherwise would seldom meet.

### Who “they” are, in sum

They are organisms who have never known liquid water, who calibrate themselves against a spectrum of reds and dark violets, measuring the world not in distances and horizons but in contact patches and thermal fronts.

They measure time in:

- the swell and ebb of distant machinery’s roar,
- the cycles of vents rich and poor,
- the slow thickening of films beneath them,
- the rare, catastrophic shifts when stone yields and the chamber cracks a little wider.

They are chemotrophs of metal and mineral vapor, of carbon and sulfur chains, of electric and thermal gradients. They are sculptures of necessity: as large as they can safely be without hollowing, as complex as their crowded world’s meager information channels allow.

If you tried to name them individually, the exercise would fail; they are too interlocked, too much a texture. It is better to name their roles, their ways of existing:

- the films that blur into rock and think in two dimensions,
- the plates that make walls into organs,
- the shards that till and erase,
- the soot that feels as a crowd,
- the beads that roll and remember impacts,
- the cages that ride the storms between walls,
- the threads that turn stone into a circulatory system,
- the transient columns and rafts that briefly pretend to be bodies rather than assemblages.

They are what happens when life is given heat instead of light, rock instead of water, contact instead of distance, and a chamber so cramped and loud that the only way to persist is to pack into every crevice and make even the stillness move.
