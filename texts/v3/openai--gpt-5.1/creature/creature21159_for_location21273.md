They are still learning how to be underground.

Whoever lives here is close to the beginning of a story: a people newly folded into stone, not yet settled into myth or ruin. You can feel it in the way their work clings to the rawness of the rock—as if they arrived almost as soon as the fracture opened and have been improvising ever since.

### Where they came from

They are not a cave species in the old sense. Their bodies and tools and habits carry an above-ground memory.

Their skin tones run the range of a sunlit ancestry: browns and ochres, sallow gold, deep umber. On the elders you still see the faint residue of an older life—faded sun-freckles, a pattern of weathering that doesn’t quite match the current, softer light. The children are different: their eyes are larger, irises wide to drink every lumen; they squint less at the lamps and move through dim corridors with an easy, habitual certainty.

Some speak quietly of a surface they no longer visit often, of fields and open air and distances that did not end in rock. Others were born here and treat those stories as a kind of half-believed migration myth. What brought their parents and grandparents down—storm, war, poisoning of the land, the sudden tearing open of this underworld—has already begun to pass out of daily speech. They are busy surviving where they are.

The tether to the surface is practical rather than nostalgic: narrow shafts, guarded and capped, lead upward to places where a few still go on rotation. They bring back dry plants, wood, handfuls of soil, sometimes animals slaughtered up there and lowered down in pieces. Those who climb these shafts are hardened and wary; they return with a smell of outside wind in their hair that others notice at once.

### Bodies for stone and shadow

Their size is constrained by their architecture: mostly small to middle-statured, compact rather than towering. A tall frame in these corridors is a liability—not just for bumping heads, but for how much weight and heat and food it demands.

You see callus in very particular places: along the inner fingers from gripping rock edges and ladder rungs; on knees from crouching work; across bare soles toughened by stone fragments. Shoulders are strong from lifting, bracing, and carrying in narrow spaces where tools cannot always help. Their gait is careful, weight kept low, center of gravity close to the ground; even in a hurry they move like people who know that a fall could mean a broken bone on unyielding rock.

Eyes are their most striking shared feature. Generations in dimness have started to select for wider irises, longer lashes that keep condensation and grit away, a subtle tendency toward light-colored eyes that reflect lampglow oddly in the gloom: silvery hazel, washed-out green, soft amber. But they are still, fundamentally, surface people: they blink more rapidly near raw firelight; they look momentarily bewildered if someone opens a rarely used shaft cover and a stab of day-white spills down.

Their clothing is layered and practical, adapted to cool dampness and constant stone contact. The base layer is soft plant fiber or fungus-derived cloth, woven in tight, smooth textures that don’t snag easily. Over this, thicker wraps of felted fiber or layered scraps, patched and re-patched. Colors are mostly the hues of what’s available: browns from earth and bark, off-whites from undyed fiber, occasional faint blues or reds that are obviously prized and carefully maintained.

Where stone edges bite often, you see reinforcement: patches of leather at knees and elbows, braided cloth bands at wrists, sometimes thin slats of smoothed bone sewn into the outer garments as light armor against accidental blows in tight passages.

Feet are usually in soft shoes: wrap sandals of cured hide or thickly woven reed, with soles mended over and over. Indoors, on smoothed floors, many go barefoot; the rhythm of skin on stone is one of the quiet sounds here.

### How they arrange their days

Time is granular and local. They mark it by tasks: by the interval it takes a cistern to fill from a known drip, the time between one fungus flush and the next, how long a certain oil lamp will burn before it must be trimmed. Someone somewhere might still keep a tally of days on a bone or a stretched hide, but most people orient themselves by sequences: “after the vents are scraped,” “before the night-batch of bread is baked,” “when the drip in so-and-so’s hall makes a puddle as wide as your hand.”

They divide their world into:

- **Stone-keepers:** those who watch the rock.
- **Water-tenders:** those who tend and shape the trickles.
- **Growers:** those who manage gardens and fungus beds.
- **Makers:** potters, knappers, weavers, carvers.
- **Vent-wardens and climbers:** those who handle the shafts to the world above.
- **Memory-holders:** elders or chosen individuals who keep stories, patterns, and rules.
  
These are roles more than rigid castes; most adults weave between several through a lifetime, but each cluster of dwellings tends to have a few people everyone defers to in matters of stone, water, air, or food.

#### The stone-keepers

They move with their hands always touching surfaces: knuckles on walls, fingertips along ceiling joins, the heel of the palm resting against load-bearing pillars. They are the ones who lie quietly in the dark to listen for unfamiliar creaks, who notice when a line of dust on a beam has cracked, when a fracture has spread a finger’s breadth farther along the ceiling.

Their tools are simple and particular: thin stone wedges for sounding the rock with quiet taps, small clay bottles of colored water to drip along hairline cracks and track minute shifts, flat palettes they press against a wall to see if it still fits exactly a year later.

They carry authority because collapse is everyone’s nightmare. When a stone-keeper says, “No more weight here,” constructions stop. When they decide that a dangerous bulge must be relieved, whole sections of dwellings may be dismantled, redistributed. People grumble, but they help; no one wants to be under the wrong roof in a shifting place.

Some of them have maps: etched into clay tablets or painted in pigment on smoothed walls—plan views of chambers, lines for fractures, shaded zones for “weak, do not dig.” These maps are living documents, regularly updated, passed down from a senior stone-keeper to an apprentice.

#### The water-tenders

Wherever a drip is regular enough to trust, someone has claimed it, not as personal property but as a stewardship. Families or small groups are responsible for specific flows, their obligations extending beyond their own needs: to keep the channels clear, to watch for change.

They carry flat-bladed tools to shape shallow grooves, wedges to adjust the angle of a drip, plugs to open or close diversions. They can tell you, by touch alone, which runnels are colder, which cisterns collect water that tastes faintly of iron, which thin trickle smells subtly of distant plant roots above.

Their social role is delicate: they negotiate between neighbors who share a channel, deciding how much flow can be spared for a new garden patch, when a household must temporarily give up water so a cistern elsewhere can be topped before a journey. There are words for those who hoard or cheat with water, and they are among the most serious accusations one can make.

#### The growers

They move closest to the slow life in this stone world. Under their hands, the thin soil pockets around building edges become places of intention. They tend flattened beds of moss like others might tend orchards: cutting it back in neat strips so it re-grows thicker, shading it from lamp-smoke to keep it vivid and soft.

In the fungus halls they are quiet and meticulous: adjusting the angle of a tray so condensation runs just so, turning decomposing matter with gloved hands, adding ash here, bone-meal there. They’ve learned the cycles of several species—fast, delicate ones that fruit often but exhaust their substrate quickly; slow, meaty ones that take months of almost featureless waiting. Their vocabulary is thick with states of damp and rot: words for “just enough softness,” for “too sweet-smelling,” for the faint tang that heralds a good flush.

Their work feeds everyone, so their memory is practical and shared. Children help here early: learning how to brush spores gently from gills into prepared jars; how to strip yellowed moss without taking the new dark layer beneath.

#### The makers

Their labor fills the tight spaces that aren’t dark enough for fungi but aren’t straight-through corridors either: side-coves where lampglow pools and hands can work with fine detail.

You hear the soft clack of knapping: a seated maker, legs tucked tight to minimize their footprint in the cramped place, holds a core stone and chips away long, thin flakes to be ground into blades or scrapers. The shards fall into a carefully placed cloth so none are wasted; even the smallest slivers might later edge a fine-cuts tool.

Potters work with thick, cool clay kneaded with ash and crushed shard. In a world where breakage is inevitable and clay is one of the few moldable materials, they’ve refined a sturdy, straight-walled style: squat jars with broad shoulders and narrow mouths, easy to seal and easy to stack. Ornament is minimal but not absent: a thumbprint pressed in a line around the rim; a repeating pattern of short fingernail gouges near the base. Sometimes these marks also code for contents: three ridges for grain, one for salt-fungus, spiral for water pot.

Weavers, working with stalks from surface plants and tough underground reeds, build the vertical dimension of domestic life: mats, partitions, hanging baskets. Their creations soften sound, trap warmth, create privacy in spaces carved from a common volume. They also produce the ropes that make everything from ladders to pulley systems possible.

Metalworkers are rarer and their craft more mysterious; fuel is scarce and ore scarcer, so true smelting is an event. When it happens, it does so in specially vented pockets near the main shafts, under the supervision of those who know how to manage both heat and air without choking the entire chamber. A single nugget of workable metal, coaxed from crushed rock or old surface scrap, is enough for days of reverent re-forging: dividing it into several smaller knives, a few awls, a ring of hooks.

Every metal object here has a long, long story. A knife moves from family to family over generations; when a blade chips beyond repair, its fragments are set aside, never discarded, awaiting a heat strong enough to fold them into something new.

#### Vent-wardens and climbers

They live half in this underworld and half in an awareness of something above and beyond.

Vent-wardens tend the mouths of the shafts: clearing soot from their walls, checking for blockages, repairing the stone rings that line them, adjusting hoods and baffling stones so that smoke draws correctly. They know the behavior of air—the subtle shifts that mean a pressure change above, the thin swirl that foretells a storm or a distant fire.

Climbers are chosen more for temperament than for lineage: lean, strong-armed, untroubled by narrow height. They don harnesses of braided fiber, carry small, carefully rationed fire in hooded coals or stone-box lamps, and slip up through the breathing pores of the settlement to the outside.

Their journeys are ritualized but not exactly sacred. When they return, they debrief first with the memory-holders and the stone-keepers: describing any new sounds of the upper world, traces of animals, changes in plant growth. They keep the day–night rhythm for everyone else; it’s through them that the community hears, “The sky was clear the last three times,” or “The wind has been carrying ash,” or “There are new growths of the tall grass to the south.”

They also bring stories no one quite knows what to do with: the emptiness of seeing a horizon; the feeling of wind that has never touched stone.

### How they share space

Physical proximity shapes their culture. Privacy is a sliding, negotiated thing, less about walls and more about layers and cues.

Sleeping is generally communal, but broken down into manageable clusters. A “room” here may be a section of corridor where both sides have built raised platforms of stone and fiber; at night they pull woven screens across the passage, creating a passable tunnel through hanging fabric and a series of alcoves filled with bodies. Quiet is enforced by custom more than law. Children are hushed with touch rather than voice; laughter is indulged in kitchens and workspaces, not where people lie down.

Private corners exist, and people fight for them, but they are measured in lengths of arm, not in square rooms. The back of a storage alcove, the triangular void above a sloping roof, the space under a stair—these become places where one person can curl up alone, where two can lean close to argue or whisper. You recognize them by their smaller lamps, by textiles hung not as sound barriers but as signs: a curtain tied a particular way to mean “do not disturb,” a pattern woven into a mat to mark it as someone’s specific refuge.

Property, in the sense of personal ownership, is narrow and portable. Clothing, some tools, a sleeping wrap, small ritual items—these one carries or stores in a marked bundle. Everything larger is inherently shared because it exists in overlapping routes and functions. A bench-stop by day must become passage by night; a cistern edge might be a chopping surface at one hour and a place for children’s quiet play at another.

Arguments, when they happen, are usually about use of space and flow of water. But the closeness also ensures swift resolution; your antagonist is never far, and you cannot afford to ignore them for long. There are conventional gestures for apology that don’t need words: stacking someone else’s firewood neatly, quietly cleaning their section of channel, leaving a small extra portion of mushroom or bread in their bowl.

### Speech, sound, and quiet language

Voices are kept low, but communication is rich.

Hand-gestures fill the gap where shouting would be dangerous. In work-crews, you see systems of signs: a palm flat and angled to mean “brace here,” two fingers curled to mean “lower slowly,” a quick, chopping motion at the wrist to signal “stop, something shifted.” Children learn these as naturally as spoken words; many can hold whole conversations in half-whisper and hand.

There’s a kind of tapping language, too, constrained and practical, used when voices cannot carry but lines of rock connect: a short, sharp sequence of knocks with a hammerstone against a beam or pillar. Three quick, three slow, three quick might mean “help needed”; a long series of evenly spaced knocks could signal “we’re beginning a cut, listen for changes.” It’s not complex enough for gossip, but it’s enough to keep labor coordinated across intervening walls.

Singing exists, but rarely in the open halls where echoes multiply. People hum soft work-songs under their breath, melodies that ride just under hearing. Children are taught lullabies pitched deliberately low, almost in the chest, so they feel more than they carry. Communal song is rare and therefore powerful, reserved for specific rituals when everyone will tolerate the voices bouncing against stone.

Laughter, when it comes, is sharp and quickly muffled. The sound of a child’s uncaring shout rings like a thrown pebble until an adult scoops it up with a hand over the mouth, guiding the exuberance into quieter forms.

### Belief and meaning

Their deities, if that word even applies, are the forces that make or unmake this precarious home: stone, water, air, and the thin memory of light.

They mark certain cracks and fractures as sacred not because they are grand, but because something decisive happened there: a sudden new drip that saved a drought, a shift that almost brought down a hall but stopped just short. These places gain small offerings: a bead wedged in a fissure, a strip of cloth tied near a vent, a tiny dish of ground grain left by a drip.

There is a figure for the overhead rock—neither male nor female in the telling, but weight itself given personality: a vast, pressing consciousness that must be read and appeased. When they lie down and feel the ceiling close above, some of them whisper small phrases to it, reassurances and requests: “Hold,” “Stay,” “We are light,” “We will not dig too much today.”

Water is more intimate. They name particular drips like elders or children: “Old Thin Voice,” “Steady Sister,” “Bitter Teeth.” Cisterns might have stories; a family will say, “We drink from the basin my grandmother dug.” Runnels cutting through their hall floors begin as engineering and slowly accumulate spirit; after many years of tending and dependence, they are treated with a quiet personal reverence, wiped clean with the same care someone might give an ancestor’s grave marker.

The above-world has a mythic dimension now, especially for the young. They hear of “the great unroofed place” where light comes from everywhere at once and makes hard shadows, of giant beings (trees) that stand rooted but waving, of water that gathers in vast skins and falls from the ceiling of the sky. These stories blur the line between memory and legend. Some children half-believe that their grandparents came from another kind of cavern, simply with a higher roof.

Rituals mark transitions of risk and resource:

- The first drink from a newly caught drip is shared by a representative of every nearby household.
- Before a difficult new excavation, a stone-keeper smears a line of damp clay on the wall, presses their palm into it, then brushes a bit of that clay on those who will dig.
- When a child is first allowed to climb more than one level of roofs on their own, there’s a little ceremony: they walk a short prescribed route with an older sibling or parent shadowing, then come back to a small gift—a personal tool, usually.

Death is handled with the same economy and care as everything else. The dead cannot simply lie; there is no spare space, and rot unmanaged is unacceptable. Cremation is rarely possible; fuel is too precious. Instead they practice a precise, ritualized disassembly of the body into useful and less-useful parts.

Bones are cleaned, dried, and redistributed to makers: to become needles, handles, pins, or—very occasionally—etched memorial objects that hang in someone’s private corner. Flesh and soft tissue, where disease is not suspected, goes to the deepest, hottest compost pits under the growers’ command, returning to the soil that feeds the fungi that feed the living. Names are not spoken loudly in memorial; instead, the dead are remembered in quiet, small acts—someone placing a hand on the tool their parent once used, tracing a familiar pattern of thumb-finished clay.

### The non-human lives beside them

Not everything that lives here is willingly domesticated.

In the moss and fungus corners, small invertebrates thrive: soft-bodied scavengers, minuscule white-shelled beetles, translucent millipede-like creatures that thread through damp debris. Most are passively tolerated, some actively welcomed as cleaners of mold and rot.

They keep, deliberately, a few species:

- **Rodents,** bred in woven cages suspended from ceilings or set on raised shelves. These animals are small, fast-breeding, and almost albino in the lampglow. They eat scraps and special fodder grown by the growers, and in turn provide meat, fat, and bone. Their sensitivity to stale air or toxic gas is life-saving; if the rodents refuse to stay in a chamber, if they gasp or tremble, people leave.
  
- **Lean cave-dogs or dog-like animals,** sharp-nosed, big-eared, small-bodied. They are not cuddled so much as integrated companions: loosed in halls at night to patrol, ears pricking at any unfamiliar creak or shuffle. They learn to give different soft barks for rat-swarms, for a bat-thing trapped behind a partition, for the faint groan of rock movement.

- **Winged things** that resemble bats but are not exactly: narrow heads, elongated fingers supporting thin membranes, fur that tends toward pale gray. They roost high in cracks near the thinnest stone to the surface. People watch them idly; sometimes they drop in to snatch moths or other small creatures drawn to lamps. On rare days when several of them fly all at once, beating around the hall, someone will say in a low voice, “The air changes,” and a stone-keeper or vent-warden will go check the shafts.

There are also pests: minute black specks that infest grain, mold that eats cloth, creeping funguses that invade plaster. Growers and makers develop defenses: drying baskets hung over certain warm vents, ash barriers around storerooms, tight lids with simple latches to keep gnawing teeth out.

The ecological loop is tight:

- Vegetable scraps and excrement go to compost.
- Compost feeds gardens and fungus beds.
- Plants and fungi feed people and domestic animals.
- Animal waste returns to compost, and bones to tools.
- Ash from fires stiffens clay and sweetens soil.

Even hair and nail clippings are swept carefully into waste baskets; nothing with nutrient content is ever truly thrown away.

### Technology at their level

Everything they do is limited by three strict currencies: fuel, metal, and space.

Fuel scarcity keeps heat localized. Cooking fires double as kilns and as social centers; people cook in sequence, not in parallel. You rarely see more than a few open flames at once in a district. Instead, heat is captured and re-used: thick clay surrounds the hearth, storing warmth; ovens are sealed after baking flatbreads to slowly dry herbs and fibers in the residual heat.

Metal scarcity keeps their tools broad-purpose and long-lived. A community might own only a few iron blades. These are passed out for specific tasks: cutting thick roots, shaping wood beams, scoring stone where a harder edge is needed. Most daily cutting is done with stone blades that are cheap to re-make. There’s an ingrained habit of using the right tool at the right wear stage: a new stone knife for food, a chipped one for trimming fiber, a nearly used-up sliver for scraping soot from a vent lining.

Space scarcity drives a kind of built-in modularity. Furniture hardly exists as separate from architecture; a bench is a low extension of a wall, a table is a flat-topped storage chest. Ladders hook and unhook, shifting to wherever they’re needed. Partitions are of woven fiber, rolled up and moved. The entire habitable volume is a shifting puzzle of reconfigurable panels and platforms.

Precision appears where it matters: in the slope of water channels; in the angle of a vent hood; in the thickness of a kiln wall. They have a refined sense for gradient, born of necessity. A stone-keeper or water-tender can feel the fall of a floor with their bare feet, can tell by the sound of a trickle whether it will reach a basin or stall in a shallow depression.

They measure with bodies and everyday objects rather than abstract units: a channel is “three hands wide,” a shaft “two long-ropes deep,” a hall “as long as twenty paces of the eldest.” But some have quietly developed more standardized references: a certain fired rod of clay that always lives in the stone-keepers’ hall, a piece of story-bone notched in equal segments that a maker uses when spacing holes.

### Children and learning

Children are everywhere because there is nowhere else for them to be. They are rarely truly alone; a toddler wandering into a work area will be scooped aside by whatever stranger’s hand is closest, redirected to a safer spot with a little game or a chunk of clay to knead.

Play is largely imitation. They build small cities out of broken shards and pebbles, mimicking the stacked walls and layered roofs above them. They construct miniature channels in the damp floor near negligible drips, experimenting with flow in a way that is half-game, half apprenticeship. They learn early the feel of water, the sound of a stone about to shift, the look of a fungus from “good” to “don’t touch.”

Formal teaching is gestural and storied rather than heavily abstract. An elder will sit with a group at the side of a hall, drawing simple diagrams with a wet finger on rock: “Here is how smoke wants to move,” “Here is how much weight this span will bear.” They tell these with examples from memory: “We tried once to build this high, here’s how it failed.” Mistakes are not theoretical; someone’s great-aunt died in that collapse, someone’s cousin’s foot was crushed under that rock.

Certain taboos are drilled in hard:

- Never dig under a load-bearing wall.
- Never thicken a roof past a stone-keeper’s mark.
- Never block a channel completely; leave a trickle path.
- Never leave a flame unattended near a vent mouth.
- Never open a shaft cap alone.

Violations of these are rare; the memory of the first years—when people were still learning these rules and paid in blood—is still close.

### The subtle shaping of time

Because erosion and the slow chemistry of stone are only just beginning to work here, the inhabitants’ interventions are some of the first forces to sculpt this new underworld. They are, intentionally or not, co-authors of the cavern’s future form.

As water-tenders carve channels, they lock certain flows into more permanent courses. These become the seed lines along which future, deeper cuts will eventually form. The grooves they cut now will, in centuries, be canyons through their descendants’ halls.

As stone-keepers brace weak ceilings with arches and pillars, they direct how stress travels through the rock. The arches might outlast the community, becoming enigmatic layers in the geology of some future age—bands of deliberate stonework fossilized in stalagmite and flowstone.

As growers build up soil in corners, capturing wind-blown dust from the shafts and organic debris from their cycles, they create little islands of true earth. Moss and humble crops root and die there, year on year. So, over time, they begin to anchor a more complex ecology: stray spores from the surface catch, tiny ground-invertebrates appear, even seeds dropped unnoticed by returning climbers may sprout in these sheltered beds.

They are making, inadvertently, the foundation of a richer cavern-world to come.

### Who they are, in a word

They are **contenders with weight**—with the literal, pressing mass of the world above, and with the figurative weight of scarcity, proximity, and newness. They live by calculating small risks against small gains: one more stone on that wall, one more ladle of water diverted to this patch, one more rung cut into that fragile rise.

They are also **first settlers** of a place that itself is barely born. The rock has only just opened; the water has only just found these paths. Everything here is provisional, crisp-edged, intentional. They have not yet disappeared into their environment, nor has it fully adapted to them. The relationship is experimental, full of negotiation.

You can see possible futures in them: the beginnings of a cave-adapted people, the seeds of a specialized craft culture, the first myths of a world where “up” means “rock” and not “sky.” But right now they are this: a compact, quiet, industrious community pressed under new stone, learning the fresh grammar of drip and crack, lamp and shadow, and slowly, deliberately, teaching the cavern how to hold them.
