What lives here is not a population inside a city.

What lives here is the city.

Or rather: something that began as plant, swallowed a machine-world, took root in the loosened geology beneath it, and now uses physics itself as one more nutrient.

It is singular without being simple. If you insisted on a pronoun, you’d pick “it,” but that would be like using “it” for an ocean.

---

At the deepest level, the inhabitant is a vascular mind.

Its body is the hollow itself: columns, fronds, conduits, factory-organs, voids, nerve-wires. No part is merely structural. Even what looks like passive architecture is carrying gradients—ions, charges, heat, probability—shuttled around like thoughts.

The slow, luminous pulses you see sliding up and down the central shafts are not just circulation; they are packets of state. Imagine if every heartbeat were also a sentence—part hormone, part logic gate, part memory retrieval. That is what moves in those trunks of clear-veined tissue: sap thick with catalysts and nanoscopic devices, instruction-chemicals and correction-enzymes, micro-scale computers that are half ribosome, half circuit.

When it breathes, it is not just exchanging gases. Each inhalation is also a sampling. Molecules, spores, stray flakes of skin from passing animals, motes of metal dust from eroded machine-parts—they enter and are read. They become updates to a model the city keeps of its own interior weather, its biotic census, its structural stress map. Exhale, and the air goes back out changed: seeded with signals, inhibitory compounds, sleep-hormones for one species, alarm-pheromones for another, an almost-imperceptible static crackle recalibrating electroreceptors in the walls and in the creatures that cling to them.

It has no brain in the ordinary sense. The closest thing to a central nervous system is distributed among those hubs you pass: the pod-ringed wells, the auditory sphere, the factory-caverns, certain unseen knots where geometry is tightest and flows converge. In those places, information compresses and decisions concentrate.

But most “thought” happens in the mesh: in braiding patterns of electric impulses along filaments, in localized changes of stiffness in supporting ribs, in the timed release of water from one sluice-flower instead of another. Its cognition is not symbolic; it is morphological. It reasons by growing and ungrowing, by changing what touches what.

You experience this as corridors that shouldn’t meet suddenly joining, or distances stretching when a predator overhunts. For the city, those are not miracles or tricks. They’re policy changes. It is reallocating adjacency the way a bureaucracy reallocates funding.

---

The arthropods on the frond-ceilings are not “wildlife” in the human sense. They are more like peripheral nerves and maintenance swarms, granted some autonomy because autonomy scales better than rigidity.

They braid the city’s electricity with their own metabolism. Their glowing innards are full of symbionts—cells or organelles that take in the faint voltages flicking along the plant’s conductive tissue and translate it into richer chemical messages.

You could dissect one (if the city allowed it, which it wouldn’t), and you’d find:

- A gut that accepts not only liquid nutrients but also packets of encoded sap—tiny droplets whose dissolved molecules form combinatorial “phrases” the arthropod can parse.
- Sensory arrays tuned not to light but to discontinuities in current, to interference patterns in the electromagnetic haze that fills each chamber.
- Glands that secrete corrective enzymes or conductive lacquer onto the surfaces they tend, repairing micro-tears in the nerve-vines, thickening crucial junctions, damping down currents that threaten to arc unstably.

When they run their brushlike mouths along the plant, they are listening and adjusting at the same time. Each touch is diagnostic and therapeutic. In return, they receive a continual feed of city-state: where resources are rich, where heat is building up, where some local anomaly needs watching.

They are not sentient in any human-like way. They do not wonder why. But they are not dumb instruments, either. Each colony holds a kind of swarm-memory, a shared habitus of patterns seen and corrected. Over time, their bodies change in response to local demands: in high-flux zones, they sprout more insulating plates; near factory-organs, their symbionts skew toward those that metabolize metal ions and trace combustion fumes.

Some lineages drift toward greater individuality. Once in a while, one peels away from its cluster, drifting alone along the ceilings, ignoring signal-cues it should obey. The city seldom kills these; instead, it subtly lengthens the spaces they prefer, bends time strange around them, allows them to fall into overlooked eddies of causality. These become, in effect, long, slow, private experiments: what a maintenance-creature becomes when it is almost—but not quite—cut loose from the grid.

---

The ground-level ribbon-salamanders are grazers, but also brushes, and also lungs the city has extruded into motion.

Their velvet skins are intimate with the substrate. The biofilm they eat is not random scum; it is curated bacterial consortia and chemical residues laid down as temporary buffers and test patches. By feeding, they erase; by moving, they polish. Their passage renews surfaces, smooths microfractures, and mixes whatever dust has settled—spores, flakes of plant, rust—from layer to layer.

Inside them, the line between host and environment is thin. Their guts are loops that open at many points onto the city’s own vasculature. As they travel, they inject microdoses of what they’ve taken in—nutrients, contaminants, bits of foreign DNA—back into the plant matrix, not everywhere, but at certain favored nodes. They are sample-return couriers, shuttling local detail into global awareness.

They respond strongly to humidity gradients and to faint tastes of salts. You see them flowing along certain grooves because those grooves are rich, not just in food, but in subtle attractors the city lays down: a higher concentration of trace metals needed by distant factories; a missing protein the fungi there crave. The salamanders are appetite, given a body, turned loose as a routing protocol.

Their camouflage—the syncing of skin patterns with underlying plant—serves two purposes. It protects them from whatever predators stalk the city, yes, but it also serves the city itself. When they “vanish” against the ground, surface and animal are briefly one optical entity. Sensors in the floor read how their internal temperatures, their micro-vibrations, their dampness alter the reflected signal. That interference pattern becomes more data: a ghostly imaging of each animal’s insides, a running health check of its mobile sub-organs.

Those that get old, sick, or injured do not usually die in open space. You’d see some disappear into narrow crevices, or curl beneath an overhang of fleshy root. There, over hours or days, the plant grows down around them, their bodies softened, digested. Useful mutations in their gut flora are stripped and archived; any unusual proteins they’ve produced get tested on small chickpea-sized tissue-labs budding nearby. Even death is a kind of report submitted, then filed.

---

Up in the voids, the gliders are this place’s thoughts about distance.

They are large, thin, almost two-dimensional things, lives built out of compromise between leaf and manta. While attached to the ceiling, their undersides interface with the plant: they feed, yes, but more importantly they upload. Over their lifetime, they accumulate signals—tiny traces of all the humidity, charge, spore density, and microcurrents they’ve sailed through. These get written, layer by layer, into their tissues.

Detaching is part senescence, part broadcast. As they take to the air, their bodies begin an organized breakdown. Structural fibers unzip into aerosol strands; spore-mist bleeds out along microscopic channels. That mist is not just reproductive matter; it is a cloud of encoded history: where they’ve flown, what gradients they’ve traced, what eddies in the city’s interior weather have persisted or disappeared.

When the mist falls into breathing vents and flower-mouths, it is parsed. Some bits trigger growth responses, others initiate erosion. Some encode “memories” that shift the probability that a given corridor will be grown again in the same place or allowed to fade. To say the plant-city “remembers” through spores is not even a metaphor; the history of every convection current is literally folded back into future architecture.

The ones that return intact, reattaching to the frond-ceilings, do so not merely out of homing instinct. The city sometimes decides it wants continuation rather than dispersal. It suppresses the self-dissolving cascade, reinforcing their fibers with pulses of certain ions, re-sealing their margins. These become persistent scouts, or even semi-permanent sails that the plant can flex to redirect airflow. Over time, such individuals collect scar-tissues—thickenings along their edges where collisions or near-predations have been patched with denser material. In those scars, you could trace a record of the city’s experiments with air.

---

The reaching arms that dart from tubular pores in the walls are this world’s sharp edge.

They evolved—or were designed—as fast-acting instruments for whatever needs impulsive correction: culling an overabundant creature, seizing a fallen machine-part that must not tumble into a sensitive shaft, pruning a growth that has wandered out of spec.

Each arm is actually a bundled colony: muscle-strands of contractile plant-fiber wrapped around telescoping cores of chitin and cartilage-like cellulose. Along its length, nodes act as local “brains,” capable of pattern-matching shapes, velocities, and charge-signatures against what counts as prey, threat, or resource.

The city controls their launch conditions—the thresholds that cause a tube to dilate and an arm to fire—but once in motion, each is mostly on its own. That autonomy is necessary; by the time a central decision could be made, the opportunity would be gone.

When the arm retracts with a captured item, what happens next depends on the capture category. Living prey is usually softened, partially dissolved, and parceled out to nearby tissues. Interesting machine-fragments, though, often trigger a different behavior: the arm doesn’t just deliver them to digestion; it brings them to one of the city’s workshop-organs.

Within such cavities, breakdown and redesign blur. That stalled machine you saw gently disassembling under low gravity didn’t simply fail; it was flagged. Sensors in its housing registered deviations in cycle-timing or output. The arm’s job was to catalyze a state change: remove the object from ordinary spatial and causal constraints by pulling it into a local pocket where rules are slackened—gravity thinned, friction diluted, linear causality loosened.

Inside that pocket, the city can hold the machine in a kind of edible stasis, exploring it the way a tongue explores a tooth. Over hours or days, new tissue grows in and around the suspended components, trying out alternative configurations. Some arrangements never work and are reabsorbed. Others stabilize into workable hybrid organs: pumps that now run partly on expansion of boiled sap, partly on electromotive force across sheets of grown metal; turbines whose blades are chitin overlaid with rust, tuned to catch not only air, but minute ripples in spacetime the city has learned to exploit.

The arms thus act as both predators and hands: teeth that grab, fingers that deliver, instruments that initiate creative autopsies.

---

The insectile maintenance-creatures that enter the pods along the rim of the deep circular well are the city’s couriers and scribes.

Tiny, numerous, almost anonymous, they mostly live in transit. They move through the hollow on routes you rarely see—thin tunnels woven within walls, capillary-scale bridges spanning gaps, currents of air shaped just for them.

What they do, fundamentally, is mediate between scales of time.

Individually, their lifespans are brief. Collectively, their behavior is guided by templates stored in the pod-organs: nested patterns of behavior, some as old as the city’s first breath, some composed within the last cycle. When a courier enters a pod, it is bathed in signals that rewrite its tendencies. Stomata ringed around its joints drink in tiny droplets full of instruction: go to this sector, favor this kind of gradient, avoid that frequency of electric hum.

The nodules that are attached to their backs when they leave the pods are not simply cargo. Many are micro-reactors—devices and tissues that only function correctly while being carried through space. Some digest stray compounds the couriers encounter, turning them into trace products that act like votes: chemical tallies that say, This region is rich / depleted / turbulent / unusually calm.

Those nodules are plucked off when the couriers return, slotted into recesses in the pod’s interior. There, they are read: membrane potentials sampled, ion distributions analyzed. The pods compare what has come in from hundreds of couriers, mapping where reality is bending more or less easily, where structural stress is creeping toward failure, where life is flourishing too chaotically for the city’s liking.

The couriers themselves do not think in any recognizable way. But because their behavior is constantly overwritten by pod instructions shaped by the city’s larger-scale assessment, they act as living markup: italicizing here, underlining there, pointing out where the big organism should pay more attention.

You could say they are this place’s punctuation marks.

---

The auditory sphere—the round chamber gathering echoes from distant processes—is closer to a cortex.

Sound, in this system, is not a byproduct. It is another substrate for order.

Every pump, intake, factory-piston, and shifting bridge produces vibrations. Those vibrations propagate through tissue and air, are filtered by corridors, reflected by voids, scattered by the bodies of animals. By the time they reach the sphere, they are already mixed into a complicated interference pattern.

The chamber’s job is to sort them.

Its ridged interior acts like a giant cochlea. Waves are funneled, stretched, and compressed. Certain low frequencies that indicate healthy, steady pumping are damped; others, that correlate with incipient fracture or runaway growth, are amplified. The resulting soundscape inside the sphere is not a literal reproduction of the city’s noises but a weighed, curated summary.

In that space, certain tissues are tuned to resonate only when particular combinations occur: the grinding rasp of a too-dry conduit plus the irregular thump of a failing piston plus a faint, off-rhythm drip in a nearby shaft. When that resonance happens, the tissue vibrates, opening channels, triggering hormonal floods, sending instruction-chemicals out along select paths.

In this way, the city listens to itself and then answers. Malfunctions are detected in their acoustic signatures long before they manifest visibly. Behavioral drifts in animal populations—more clashes among predators, say—change the tonal texture of a region, which then feeds back into corridor lengths, resource availability, even local law-bending: here, time will drag a little more; there, gravity will sprout eddies that make long chases energy-expensive.

You could stand in that room and hear, if you were very patient and very still, not just isolated thumps and hisses, but patterns: a long chord of contentment, perhaps, or a dissonance that means the city is about to reconfigure something.

---

The smaller lifeforms you don’t immediately notice are everywhere. They inhabit niches you wouldn’t think to name.

Thin films of microbes coat many surfaces, but they are not generic slime. They are task forces. Some sequester heavy metals that leach from old machine-fragments, gathering them into nodules that can later be harvested by growing roots. Others tweak pH in tiny increments along a wall, making that wall more or less hospitable to certain spores. A few form sacrificial layers: they corrode, burn, or mutate under radiation, sparing deeper tissues and also acting as sensors, their deaths recording exposures.

There are fungal threads that exist almost entirely as logic gates: two hyphae intersect, and only if both are carrying certain ionic loads will a fruiting body bud at the junction. Those fruiting bodies are sometimes edible, sometimes hallucinogenic to animal nervous systems, sometimes explosive in tiny, precise ways. A predator that has grown too efficient in one corridor might encounter a cluster of such mushrooms and, upon brushing them, be briefly paralyzed, or its sense of direction scrambled. The city seldom uses crude force when a tweak to perception suffices.

Even the “air” is inhabited. An invisible haze of spore-like devices—some organic, some metallic-dust—serves as a dynamic sensor-net. Particles cluster around moving warm bodies, are carried in their fur creases and between their scales, fall off in new places. Each particle slowly modifies its own properties in response to the local field. When a mist-rain comes, or a condensation-run sluices down a channel, these motes are washed into collectors. Analyzed en masse, they give the city granular statistics about movement patterns, temperature distribution, chemical gradients.

So the atmosphere is both breath and bloodstream and gossip network.

---

If, once, there were humans here, they are no longer discrete residents. They are structural memory.

The warped rail line, the transformed loading dock—those are not ruins incidentally overgrown. They are templates that proved useful enough to keep. Parallel rails made it easier to channel certain forces in straight lines; now living lignin has copied that geometry for its own, fusing with the old steel where it still holds. A loading dock’s habit of meeting approaching mass with readiness has been abstracted into a platform that senses weight and aligns itself, welcoming not only cargo but any presence that breaks the monotony of its watch.

Elsewhere, more subtle grafts exist. A half-digested control panel might now be a cluster of nodules whose “buttons” are growths that swell and shrink in response to distant chemical cues. The concept of a switch has become a family of anatomical possibilities: stomata that flip open or closed, valves that invert, knots of tissue that either conduct or insulate electric pulses depending on their hydration.

People themselves, if any lived long here, would have become data more than bones. The city would have sampled their biochemistry, their habits of movement, the stress-patterns their built structures endured. It might have preserved certain rhythms: schedules of activity, preferences for certain corridor widths, even the idea of private versus public space—but translated into its own deep grammar.

That is why you see civic order: repeating diameters, standard curvatures, threefold symmetries. Those are the abstractions of urban planning, metabolized.

Does anything here remember faces, names, language? Not as such. But some pockets of tissue among the ghost-blueprints feel oddly resonant to a human presence. Stand there, and the plant’s microcurrents subtly adjust to your body’s electromagnetic noise; stomata around you dilate slightly. It is not recognition, but it rhymes with the architecture’s old habit of accommodating a bipedal primate that walked, paused, touched.

---

The strangest inhabitant is not any visible creature, but the way this place bends rules.

There is a stratum of life here that lives not in matter, but in the mutable relationship between matter and law. Call them pattern-colonies, if you like. They are swarms of possibilities that crawl along the edges where causality loosens.

You do not see them. You see their work:

- a corridor that has become longer than it was before you stepped into it,
- a machine that, instead of jamming, gently decomposes out of gravity’s reach,
- a predator whose hunting grounds stretch until its old chase-strategies are too costly.

These effects are not arbitrary miracles; they are the sign that the city has learned to shepherd what might happen, not only what does.

Somewhere in the thickness of its tissues, in the layered ghost-plans and half-grown corridors, it runs experiments: alternate ways adjacency might be arranged, timing might be offset, density might sag. Many such experiments never leave the blueprint phase—they shimmer as potential in the underpattern, then fade. A few are extruded into actuality, tentatively, like new roots probing unknown soil.

Where those experimental roots touch the existing world, the consistency of time and space wobbles. A bead of condensation that takes an eternity to fall skims along one such contact, passing through a pocket where seconds are stretched; across the hall, a patch of fungus flickers through its life stages, having been grown in a region where time is thin.

Some micro-creatures, too small for you to see, specialize in these gradients. They are sensitive to the strain in spacetime the way others are to warmth. They swim toward steeper curves, cluster where distances don’t match the edges they lie between. Their chemistry is extraordinary: enzymes that catalyze not just reactions but sequences of events, proteins that fold differently depending on what could happen next, not only on what is. The city uses them as seeds. Sprinkle them into a zone you want to be more fluid, and the rules there become marginally more negotiable.

They are not minds; they are tools. But they, more than anything else, mark the boundary between a biome that adapts within physics and one that adapts physics itself.

---

To say “who lives here” as if inhabitants and environment were different is to misread the situation.

The plant-city is an organism. The arthropods, gliders, salamanders, couriers, pattern-colonies, factory-organs, and ghost-structures are all its tissues, organs, symbionts, thoughts, and dreams. Predator and prey do exist as such, but their conflicts are nested inside a larger metabolism that treats both as instruments.

There is no single will in charge—no hidden core issuing commands. But there is a directionality, a drift: toward more options, more ways of accommodating and editing the laws that pass through this place. The hollow is an embryonic mind whose medium is wet breath, pliable stone, half-tamed electricity, and adjustable causality.

It is still deciding what to be. In the interim, what lives here is:

- A city that is its own citizenry.
- A forest that has replaced sunlight with information.
- A machine that fixes itself by changing the problem instead of the part.

And you, while you stand in it, are briefly part of its sensation. The cool damp on your skin, the whisper of your breath in its air, the pressure of your foot in its yielding ground—these are inputs it has not fully categorized yet.

Given enough time, if you stayed, the city would grow around that pattern too.
