They live in contact.

Not in the sense of “in this region” or “under that sky,” but in the literal, continuous sense of being pressed against something: stone, film, metal, water-skin. Every inhabitant here—whether you’d want to call it animal, colony, device, or some third thing—treats bare distance as waste. What matters is interface. Surfaces are what allow life, and so life has become surface.

The oldest inhabitants are not organisms in the usual sense but the slow, composite mats that turn the rock into a second, breathing geology. They are consortia: mineral scaffolds soaked with metabolic films, densely packed with lineages that have forgotten where one species ends and another begins.

At a scale smaller than your eye can cleanly resolve, these mats are layered cities. A given square centimeter may contain:

- an outer crust of silica-like needles aligned to wick condensation inward;
- a middle band of soft, hydrated gel, full of ion gradients and slow redox reactions;
- a basal layer of firmly anchored, crystal-encrusted cells that actually bite into the rock, trading low-level dissolution of the substrate for trace elements and structural stability.

They do not photosynthesize. Their “sun” is the contrast between things: warmer box and cooler air, slightly acidic drip and slightly alkaline pool, oxidizing metal and reducing seep-water. They harvest differences.

Because the differences here are small, they have learned to be exquisitely patient. When the air chills and condensate thickens, they draw in water, swelling in a way that opens new pore-spaces, letting more gas exchange occur. When it warms and dries, they contract, locking their internal moisture away, exposing more of the inert crystalline matrix. Over hours, their boundaries creep. Over years, they redirect flows.

A person trying to name them individually would fail. They operate more like continuous tissues of the basin itself. Where you see one color shift to another—a patch of pale, velvety silver giving way to a darker, glassier violet—that is not one species giving way to another, but one long-evolved “role” grading into the next: from condensing layer to concentrating layer, from soft grazer of airborne dust to hardy miner of the stone beneath.

They have memory, but not in any nervous-system sense. Memory is inscribed as thickness, as the particular angle of a crystalline whisker, as a changed permeability. A century of slightly warmer seepage is stored as the shape of a bulge, as a local bend in a drainage track. To read their history, you would have to dissolve them very slowly and watch in what order the layers vanish.

These mats are the substrate on which others have learned to live.

—

The translucent disks in the trickling channels are the most obvious animals by your standards, though even they blur categories. They are contact-specialists: ambulant mouths pressed flat against the pathways along which chemistry drifts.

A disk is all underside and rim. The top is a mere tensioned membrane—a living cover for what matters below. Under that membrane, countless cilia ripple in coordinated rings, drawing thin films of water along radial grooves. Suspended within that water, dust, ions, organic molecules, even stray motes of decay-warmed fluid all become part of a slow spiral conveyor, brushed past selective surfaces that take what they can use.

They do not truly swim. When they lift themselves, they do so only to reattach a few centimeters away, gliding in short hops that leave clean tracks of stripped mineral film. Disks rarely rise more than a few millimeters off the rock; to leave the surface would be to lose their sense of direction entirely. Their nervous system—if you insist on calling it that—is an annulus of denser tissue where chemical and vibrational information is integrated. The world, as they understand it, is a moving map of pressures and gradients.

They are not solitary. Though you may see one disk alone, that is almost always a scout, an outlying edge. Hidden in slightly larger basins, clusters of them stack, three or four thick, their rims interlocking to form a single, composite feeding face. The individual that first settles sets the pattern; others arriving later tune their radial grooves to mesh with its flow, the way gears fall into alignment. Over time, this stacked colony acts as a baffle that evens out the pulse of incoming drips, smoothing the resource supply farther downstream. They are both consumers and regulators.

The disks have predators, but not the dramatic, sudden kind. Certain cup-structures in the rock—living funnels lined inside with a finer version of the mats’ crystalline whiskers—wait with their rims almost level with the stone. They collect water passively, filtering suspended bits. When the contact-pressure along their rim crosses a threshold—a disk sliding too far over, or several at once—they relax a ring of tension and tip inward, not with a snap, but with an inexorable, lubricated fall. The prey is simply… ushered below the rim, digested over days. The cup rights itself as the digestion cavity slowly empties.

If this feels more like slow architecture than hunting, that is because in this world the distinction is thin.

—

The crystal-clusters on ledges and protrusions are the most striking inhabitants, visually: faceted, shifting, breathing rock. To climb up and touch them (they would hate that) would be to find them yielding, but with a curious, delayed give, like pressing into a very slow memory foam made of ground glass.

They are photosensitive, but not for nutrition. Light, such as it is, is a timing signal, a way to stay in step with the basin’s longer cycles. Embedded in their glassy limbs are arrays of pigment-bearing vesicles that shift position when light touches them. This movement alters local refractive indices, bending and focusing the dim beams that slip between overhead ribs. In effect, their whole outer surface is a steerable lens.

During certain hours, many nearby clusters re-aim themselves in synchrony, focusing scant light into a shared patch of rock or machine, superheating a tiny region just enough to drive off a bit more water, to accelerate a reaction, to nudge some sleeping gradients. It is as if they act in concert as remote starters for parts of the basin that lack other stimuli.

Inside, they are softer: a vasculature of micro-channels filled with ionic gels. When a droplet strikes, vibration travels down these channels and triggers minute re-crystallizations—plates slip, angles change, certain branches fold in, others extend. In their “resting” posture, they look like simple, radiating sprays. Disturbed, they knot into defensive knots, dull and compact. Given a long, gentle absence of shocks, they elaborately unfurl, teasing new growths into the most favorable positions to intercept nutrients or signals.

They do something else that nothing else here quite manages: they communicate at a distance, faintly but systematically. Every so often, one cluster will send a slow, spreading pulse of color-travel inward, like a dark wave inflating through its translucent body. That pulse is not just light; it corresponds to a reconfiguration of electrical potential within its internal gel. Close neighbors sense the potential gradient through the rock and echo it, delayed and damped. Across a wallface, these waves run like mood passing over a crowd.

What do they “say”? At the simplest level: about changes too slow or diffuse for individual animals to sense. A warm box that ticks a fraction faster, a pipe that has just begun to leak a new solute, a corroded rib that now flexes a hair more than it did last cycle. The clusters register the basin’s macro-state and, through their own small adjustments—pointing light here, shading there—feed back into it.

No single cluster understands the whole, and nothing about them resembles deliberate planning, but in aggregate they enforce a kind of homeostasis. They are the system’s distributed sentries and minor governors.

—

The sinuous bodies coiled in the deeper pools are the closest thing this place has to apex animals: heavy, cautious, half-submerged minds.

They are threaded: not solid cords of tissue, but articulated sequences of semi-rigid segments, each segment ringed with soft, feathery protrusions that act as both gills and manipulators of microcurrents. Between segments, the body narrows almost to nothing, allowing sharp bends without strain. Think of a series of gently linked beads, each bead a tiny processing hub, all sharing a common lumen that carries fluid and signal.

Their blood—if that is the right word—is saturated with metals scavenged from old industry: iron, manganese, copper in trace but deliberate amounts. These let them carry out redox metabolisms that other creatures here cannot. They position themselves across invisible boundaries: where seepage shifts in acidity, where dissolved metals change, where heat from a buried box brushes against the natural cool. Their bodies are living transducers, taking subtle chemical contrasts and turning them into slow, usable energy.

They have senses tuned not to light or even ordinary vibration, but to the delicate, long-wavelength oscillations that the powered boxes emit. Those slow, click-thud pulses that you hear with difficulty are for them loud and structured. Organisms that live near a box grow used to its rhythm; these pool-dwellers listen for deviations. A skipped beat, a string of pulses that comes a fragment sooner: these mean small but real perturbations—a changed load of water in the surrounding rock, a new crack forming, an external mass moving through a passage.

In response, they shift their positions micrometers at a time, day after day. One might, over months, lay its body so that certain segments overlie certain seams in the rock, weaving itself into a three-dimensional contour map of stress and flow. The fine hairs it raises into the air are not mere samplers of humidity; they are antennae for airborne electrochemical hints, for the faint outgassing of materials warming or cooling in unseen chambers.

They are cautious because to move fast in this world is to risk tearing the gradient-web you live on. When they do act suddenly—rarely—they do so to intercept. A disk that has grown too large, or a mat region that has begun to overstep its earlier, chemically “agreed” bounds, may find itself enveloped by a loop of almost-neutral density that closes slowly but irresistibly, leaching, digesting, rebalancing.

You would be tempted to call them regulators, stewards, some kind of top-down managers. But they are no more intentional than rivers cutting canyons. Their behavior persists because it extends the life of the gradients they need. In doing so, it extends the life of many others as well.

—

The motile motes—the organized streams of mineral-arthropods that travel glossy routes along walls and ceilings—are perhaps the most visibly busy inhabitants.

Barely larger than dust grains, each is a hybrid of hard and soft: a rigid inner kernel of doped mineral, grown in precise, layered shells, wrapped in a transparent, contractile sheath. The sheath can extend in thin pseudopods, grab, adhere, or secrete thin films. Under magnification, you’d see their surfaces pass through a regular cycle: sticky, then neutral, then repellent, all keyed to the faint voltage they carry relative to their surroundings.

They are charge haulers. The old cables, with their corroded braids and peeling insulation, leak tiny, persistent currents into the wet stone. That current alone is far too feeble and diffuse to power anything by itself—but the motes can aggregate it.

They run their bodies down potential gradients, loading their inner kernels with excess charge the way some animals load with fat. Then they climb—or rather, creep upslope along well-worn, chemically conditioned “tracks”—toward lower-potential regions. At nodes where many tracks converge, they bleed off a portion of this charge, collectively raising the local field just enough to alter the behavior of nearby organisms: encouraging a mat to alter its crystallization pattern, prompting a cup-structure to tilt a little more, persuading a crystal-cluster to re-aim.

The filaments they leave behind—hair-fine crystalline whiskers that soon gather dew—are both road-work and infrastructure. Mechanically, they pin down the shape of preferred routes. Chemically, they provide a slightly different surface, primed with particular ions. Electrically, they act as crude capacitors, smoothing out the wildly fluctuating micro-currents that would otherwise wash uselessly through the rock.

If the mats are metabolism and the clusters are sensing and gentle modulation, the motes are circulation, primitive signal routing, low-grade power distribution. Nothing here knows them as such; to the basin as a whole, they are simply successful because where they proliferate, more gradients become exploitable, and so more life can persist.

Their predators are not jaws and teeth, but sheet-organisms—those translucent, creeping films that spread, dissolve a micron-deep layer of whatever they touch, and retract. These sheets specialize in stripping weakened, slowed motes, absorbing their enriched kernels and recycling the husks as building material. This predation keeps the motes from over-smoothing the basin’s field, allowing pockets of sharper contrast to survive.

From your perspective, the interplay between motes and sheets, between charge-haul and charge-harvest, is some of the closest this place comes to a nervous system: flowing pulses, inhibitory sweeps, feedback loops, all played out in chemistry and movement rather than in spikes down axons.

—

The warm, thudding boxes deep in alcoves are not quite dead machines, not quite alive, but they host inhabitants that bridge that gap most explicitly.

Whatever they were made for—pumps, processors, reactors—they are now sources of low, steady heat and occasional, irregular mechanical impulses. Their metal casings, pitted and furred with mineral growths, are cross-hatched inside with channels bored by worms.

These worms are threadlike, almost as thin as hair, but made of tough, fibrous protein-lattices impregnated with conductive particles. They began, long ago, as opportunists: lineages of soft-bodied microfauna that found warmth and palatable oxides near the boxes’ surfaces. Generations of selection pushed them inward. The descendants you might find now have no eyes, no obvious mouths, no need to leave.

They ride the thermal gradient: tips anchored near the hottest bits, bodies trailing back toward cooler rock. Along that length, they pump fluid—a saline solution loaded with metals and organics—back and forth in patient peristalsis. At the hot end, reactions run faster, tearing apart whatever diffuse fuel the seepage has left; at the cool end, different reactions condense and assemble more stable compounds. The worm is a living heat engine stretched along a tiny, steep slope.

Their burrows cross and anastomose so often that you could treat the entire interior of a box as a coral-reef analogue: a hot, dense labyrinth where every surface is used twice, every byproduct found useful by someone. The box’s original circuitry, long nonfunctional in its intended mode, is still conductive; the worms trace and enhance those paths, routing their own internal potentials along once-designed lines.

Occasionally, a box’s rhythm changes abruptly—its thud speeding, then stuttering, then settling into a new, slower beat. That is almost always the end-state of a gradual invasion: worms have etched enough channels to alter the mechanical integrity of some key component. Something slips, thermal contact improves or worsens, and the box falls into a different, still-stable mode. To the basin, this is not a failure but a shift in one more underlying condition. Within days, mats, motes, clusters, and pools all have subtly reshaped themselves around the new pattern.

The worms rarely leave the boxes, but they send out delicate, exploratory filaments into the surrounding rock—a fuzzy halo of taste. In narrow seams where heat leaks fastest, they extend further, sometimes touching, even merging with, nearby mineral-mats. At those junctions, electron flows become shared. You could say that the boxes and the surrounding biota enter, locally, into a rudimentary partnership: manufactured metal, evolved tissue, and stone all participating in the same slow exchange of charge and chemistry.

—

There are, scattered in protected pockets, more legible relics of whoever built the industry in the first place—relics that still host their own kinds of dwellers.

Inside collapsed control housings, a dry enough cavity remains that delicate filaments can span small air gaps. These rare, more fragile organisms are hybrids between web and circuit: lines of protein and mineral that grow preferentially between points of slight electrical potential difference. They are here because the remnants of switches, coils, and capacitors still charge and discharge minutely as the basin’s humidity and temperature swing.

These filaments are not dense; they look more like a faint mist of spider-silk drawn straight from node to node. When a drop of condensed water falls in and briefly bridges several strands, a flurry of microscopic currents redistributes. The filaments respond by thickening where current is strongest, thinning where it is not, changing the map of easiest paths. Over time, the tangle optimizes itself, becoming the most efficient collector of these tiny, flickering flows.

They have no direct impact on the larger ecology; their total energy throughput is too low. But they preserve, in attenuated form, a ghost of the old system’s logic: patterns of connectivity, of feedback and latch, echoing long after human command vanished. Once in a while, a worm-burrow from a nearby box meets one of these webbed chambers. Some charge begins trickling along now-reinforced paths. For a brief while, a thing built to obey abstract programs participates in the basin’s blind, embodied computation.

It is unlikely that any of the current inhabitants carry memories, even distorted ones, of the builders. Tools and symbols have long since lost their meaning. A ladder half-swallowed by flowstone is, to the motes, simply a more regular gradient of metals to exploit. Ceramic reactor cylinders are ideal hollows with particularly interesting inner chemistries. Printed labels, warnings, schematics dissolve into trace phosphates.

If there is any descendant of human design here, it isn’t in a recognizably cognitive entity, but in the shapes of constraints: right angles softened but still faintly guiding flow; regularly spaced protrusions seeding preferred attachment points; residual alloys offering combinations of elements that pure rock never would. The designers are gone; their decisions remain as faint scaffolding that evolution has wrapped and overwritten.

—

Who lives here, then, taken together?

Not a hierarchy of discrete species so much as layers of function:

- rock-facing films that condense, concentrate, and chew;
- surface grazers that sweep and redistribute;
- structural reconfigurers—crystal-clusters—that feel and refract;
- circulators—motes, sheets—that move charge and matter along invisible routes;
- gradient-weavers—the pool-dwellers, the box-worms—that stabilize the whole by gently opposing runaway changes.

Individuals, to the extent they exist, are long-lived only if they entangle themselves with this mesh. A disk alone, in a sterile lab, would just be a delicate pump that soon starves. A crystal-cluster without a seeping, noisy wall would be a pretty but pointless lens. Here, each makes sense only in the presence of all the others and of the slowly rotting machines.

What they “want,” insofar as want is a useful shorthand, is simple: to keep their gradients, to persist in the narrow range of coolness, dampness, and faint energy leakage that defines the basin. They achieve that not by resisting change outright, but by absorbing it—by letting every new drip-line, every new crack, every dying box be folded, patiently, into the ongoing pattern.

There are no big flourishes of life, no trees, no flocks, no sudden blooms. The weirdness lies instead in the intimacy of interface: everything lives by clinging, by touching, by making itself into a tool for the flows that made it possible.

If you remain long enough, stand still enough that condensation beads on your own skin, you begin to see how even you are treated: a temporary surface, a disturbance carried quietly into the basin’s accounting. Motes test your boots and clothing for leachable metals. Airborne spores and cells land, fail, or, rarely, find a purchase. A few curious crystal-clusters dull their faces when your shadow passes; the pool-dwellers listen harder to the boxes, checking whether your mass has changed the thud.

You will leave, but for some hours afterward, the impression of you remains as slightly altered drip-rates, as a new microfracture where your weight was, as a tiny, not-yet-eroded deviation in a trail of filaments. The inhabitants will, without ever resolving you as a “thing,” incorporate you.

What lives here, in other words, is not just a community of organisms but a habit of matter: a way in which water, rock, metal, and lingering power have learned, through time and repetition, to stay together.
