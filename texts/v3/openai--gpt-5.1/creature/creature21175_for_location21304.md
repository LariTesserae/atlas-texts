They are cliff-dwellers, but “they” is already too narrow. The hollow is an organism made of organisms, and what lives here is the habit of using one another.

Still, there is a dominant people.

They are the ones who have turned walls into avenues, growth into scaffolding, habit into architecture. The armored, many-legged workers are not a background detail; they are the primary shapers of this place, the way termites are the true authors of a mound. Call them the resin-kint, for lack of their own name.

### The resin-kint

A resin-kint is a flattened, jointed band of life, about as long as a human forearm at maturity, but rarely seen in full: they fold, curve, and overlap so often that you mostly perceive segments and skirts of legs. Their dorsal surface is plate on plate on plate, overlapping shields of dark, hornlike material with a faint greasy sheen that keeps the dry air from stealing their moisture. Each plate is edged with soft sensory hairs, so that when they press close to a surface they “read” the microtopography like fingers reading Braille.

Beneath, a dense fringe of legs. More a moving cushion than discrete limbs—dozens per side, short, strong, each ending in a double-hooked foot. They don’t step so much as grip, release, flow. On the steepest verticals, they never slip.

At the front, they are surprisingly delicate. Four or six “hands” cluster around the jaws: multi-jointed manipulators, each with a split tip, capable of fine work. They can pluck a single filament from a mat without tearing it, or brace and lever against living wood hard enough to bend its growth.

Their mouthparts look like opposed chisels framed by flexible lips. The chisels shave, plane, gouge; the lips hold and sort, tasting as they work. They chew not just to eat, but to fabricate: plant tissues are taken in, macerated with saliva rich in enzymes and resins, then exuded again as pulp that sets into a fibrous, gluey material. That material is how ribs and ledges are “built” on the cliff face, how roots are fused, shells cemented. They are masons whose mortar is their own transformed food.

They carry light with them, faintly. On the edges of their plates, on the undersides of some segments, beadlike glands glow amber when they are active, especially along major traffic veins. It is not bright—just enough that, if you watch from the dark, their flowing caravans become dotted, moving constellations tracing the hollow’s pathways.

They live in castes, but the castes are subtle, expressed in proportion more than in radical difference.

- **Workers** are the most common: mid-sized, broad, with strong jaws, thick plates, and versatile forelimbs. These are the ones you see everywhere: shearing old growth, smoothing tunnels, hauling dried fungus bricks and seed-capsule tubs.

- **Tenders** are smaller, with finer manipulators and paler armor. They cluster around brood cells and fermentation pockets, inside the cliff-face hives. They rarely venture into the outer bustle; their glow is softer, almost pinkish. They spend their lives arranging eggs, cleaning larvae, rotating food stores so that nothing dries past use.

- **Pillars** or **anchors** are huge, almost immobile individuals, deep in the innermost galleries. Their bodies fuse over time into the structure itself: plates thickened with secreted minerals until they become rib-like buttresses. Only the front segments remain flexible, eyes and forelimbs still functional; the rest is a living wall. Through them, heat and moisture are circulated—they act as buffers and reservoirs. They are both elder and infrastructure.

- Rarely, a traveler might glimpse a **Queen-equivalent**, though “queen” misleads. These are large, elongated matriarchs whose hind segments are swollen with reproductive tissue. They move very slowly, if at all, kept deep, visited by messengers who tap coded signals against their armor. They do not rule in the conscious, planning sense, but the genetic designs of the colony flow from their bodies, iterated and adjusted over generations.

Communication among the resin-kint is sound and touch. Each segment of their body can drum against stone or the plates of a neighbor. A rolling cascade of clicks, taps, and soft scrapes travels along galleries: alerts, invitations, route changes, instructions. Legs, too, can convey information in passing—one individual brushing another’s flank with a particular rhythm as they squeeze past on a narrow ledge.

Intelligence here is distributed. No single resin-kint thinks in stories or plans in years. But the colony, across decades and overlapping generations, behaves as if it remembers.

It remembers that a ledge once sheared away, centuries back, because old growth beneath it was too heavily hollowed; since then, the slope of similar platforms is always given a gentler angle, braced by extra rootwork.

It remembers, in the pattern of its tunnels, where warmth accumulates, where drafts from the slit bite when the outside wind turns violent. There, galleries bend behind thicker, older stone, or pass through the bodies of Pillars that can dampen the fluctuation.

It remembers certain flavors of incoming dust as signals that the outside is changing—when distant upwinds bloom and flood the air with a new kind of spore, the suspension-nets in the opening alter their weaves, and the brood recipes in the fermentation chambers shift accordingly.

### Their houses, their city

Near the slit, where meager outside light spills down, the resin-kint have condensed their living spaces into those layered ribs and chambers. There is a logic to the vertical zoning.

The **uppermost tiers**, almost licking the narrow mouth of the hollow, hold nursery warrens and gardens. Here, translucent living screens—broad, thin growths coaxed from outer-facing plants—act as light-collectors and diffusers. Tenders shepherd certain symbiotic organisms along these screens: slow-growing, sponge-leaved vines and thick mats of photosynthetic tissue. Some of these mats are eaten fresh; others are dried, powdered, fermented. Larvae and young workers are raised amidst this mild radiance, never exposed to the harsher dryness just inches beyond the slit.

Below, the **middle strata** of the cliff hives are for processing and circulation. Fermentation cavities blister the walls: bulbous rooms whose inner surfaces seethe with microbial slimes that transmute leaves, carcasses, and dust into rich, storable paste. Workers move in steady streams from these vats to storage cells, their bodies dusted with powder, their mandibles stained with dark pulp.

Lower still, where the wall meets the yielding floor-mesh, the **basal galleries** interface with the rest of the hollow’s ecosystem. Here, doors open directly onto root-forests and sponge-pillars. Resin-kint move in and out as harvesters, negotiators, and, when necessary, predators.

They do not, however, dominate by simple consumption. Much of their prowess lies in mediation.

### The growers and the grown

The “plants” of this hollow are not passive scenery. Many are slow but volitional bodies, capable of localized movement, chemical signaling, and even limited decision-making.

Some host the resin-kint willingly. Thick fronds with hide-like surfaces open channels in their tissues when scored correctly; resin-kint workers have learned the right patterns of incision and massage to persuade a stem to swell here, thin there, bend along a desired arc. In exchange, the workers deliver nutrients: carried pellets of digested grazer waste, minced insect meat pressed into wounds which then seal around the gift. Over time, such a plant becomes inseparable from the architecture of a colony, its own survival braided into theirs. There are entire buttresses that were once a single, many-limbed fern-thing, now re-sculpted into ramps and vaults.

Others are more indifferent. The sponge-fiber ropes that hang from the ceiling, for instance, simply grow downward toward warmth and chemical traces, then harden and mineralize at their bases where constant traffic and nibbling trigger their defense response. Resin-kint exploit this: they use the top, still-soft lengths as harvestable material, while the bottom turns to living stone under their very feet, reinforcing the floor.

Still others resist. There are withdrawal-plants—broad, bowl-like structures that snap half-closed at the tremor of approaching steps. Inside, fermenting pools rich in acids and enzymes digest whatever falls in; their outer walls give off bitter scents that even resin-kint avoid. Around these, the traffic ways deflect, and tunnels curve, acknowledging pockets of sovereign hazard.

The resin-kint’s “gardening” is less about domination than about tuning densities. Where small fliers thicken to choking clouds, they encourage the growth of net-makers—colonies of multi-limbed weavers whose silken curtains thin the swarms and turn them into a manageable harvest. Where certain spore-producing crusts threaten to coat everything, dulling sensors and choking gills, resin-kint scrape them back and stuff the scrapings into hidden fermentation pods, converting nuisance into stored resource.

They are, in short, managers of flow: of nutrients, of bodies, of risks.

### The air-borne nations

The minute fliers—the dust that has intentions—are numerous, varied, and mostly beyond resin-kint’s precise control.

Many are single-purpose drifters: tiny, seedlike creatures whose adult life is a slow fall through the hollow, powered by convection and by the shuddering impacts of larger bodies. They graze on films too thin for larger mouths, or sip from the micro-droplets that form where two glossy surfaces just touch. They die quickly, and their corpses snow onto webs, mats, and open mouths.

Others have more complex cycles. Some lay their eggs inside the pulpy structures of living plants, their larval whispers prompting the host to grow bulges that the adults later use as launch platforms. Some parasitize the resin-kint, infiltrating the soft joint membranes between plates to drink hemolymph in tiny, careful drops. The colony tolerates a certain load of such parasites; beyond that, grooming frenzies occur, where workers line up and scrape each other’s joints with hooked tools until the air thickens with dislodged bodies.

Among the air peoples are also the **callers**: small, almost invisible fliers whose wings are actually rigid blades, fluttering at frequencies that resonate with particular structures. By shaking a certain root, a caller can prompt it to exude a sweet droplet; by setting a web-thread humming, it can convince the web inhabitants that prey has struck, drawing them outward and leaving their core unguarded. These callers are hunted by resin-kint and net-weavers alike, but they persist, evolutionary tricksters, always inventing new tones.

### The web crafters

The silk architects that farm the air-borne life live in self-made capsules: ovoid sacks of layered, dried web, fused at their bases to some anchor—the underside of a frond, a stretch of cliff, a hardened root. Each capsule opens through a narrow neck lined with barbed strands; the resident can thrust forelimbs out to pluck at the broader network of webs beyond, but predators find it hard to gain purchase if they lunge.

A typical web crafter has eight to twelve long, spidery limbs ending in arrays of tiny hooks. Their bodies are soft, almost translucent, suffused with slow pulses of bioluminescence that seep into their silk, giving the webs a vague ghost-glow in the dusk. They have no obvious eyes; instead, their limbs are packed with vibration sensors. The web is their nervous system extended onto the air.

They breed within their capsules, small clusters of juveniles sharing a parent’s space until they are large enough to spin their first independent threads. Some webs are solitary endeavors; others are communal tapestries woven by loosely related lineages whose strands interpenetrate, territories crisscrossing in a three-dimensional lace. Occasional “wars” between web families are not battles in the bodily sense but campaigns of sabotage: undermining rival anchor points, overloading a neighbor’s web with debris until it tears, or redirecting the flow of fliers by shifting tension patterns.

For the resin-kint, these crafters are both ally and hazard. A resin-kint can push gently through a web and be allowed to pass; push too fast, and sticky strands cling, triggering a defensive bite. Over time, treaties of a sort have arisen: some webs are “leased” by the colony, maintained in certain shapes in exchange for offerings of captured fliers and the occasional protection from predators.

### The grazers and their hunters

Along the high erosion lines, the hand-sized grazers march. They are not resin-kint, but distant kin: their bodies stouter, their legs fewer and more muscular, their armor plates often overgrown with lichens and film so that they carry their own food on their backs.

Each has a forward rasping organ, like a blunt file, used to abrade the stone and scrape off the living crust that grows there. The sound of them, en masse, is a faint, continuous sanding, a dry susurrus that contributes to the hollow’s texture of noise.

They do not think much beyond their line and their next mouthful. But their predictability makes them vital: they are the hollow’s conveyor belts, converting thin, inaccessible films into dense, pelletized droppings that fall in a slow rain to feed lower strata.

Predators shape themselves to this constancy. Some dangle disguised limbs into the grazer stream, matching the grooves of erosion so perfectly that only the sudden whip and the immediate gap in the file of bodies betrays the kill. Others live beneath the path, reaching up through hairline cracks to snatch a leg and drag a struggling grazer into hidden digestive chambers. The resin-kint track these losses as changes in the grazer flow: long ago, they learned where not to build supporting structures that might become predator dens.

Lower down, where the grazer pellets land and mats grow thick, other herbivores browse—soft-bodied, many-mouthed creatures that look like moving cushions of cilia. They sweep across surfaces, stripping out fresh growth, leaving behind cleanly grazed patches that resprout quickly. Some resin-kint corral these “matsheep” into particular areas by building low resin ridges and scent barriers; the matsheep, in turn, create clean corridors and prevent any one species of surface-film from monopolizing space.

### The deep things

Away from the slit’s weak light, nearer the back wall where ancient wave-forms curve, live slower, stranger communities.

Here, the fibrous floor thickens into **reef-like mounds** of compacted dead things, riddled with burrows. Long, flexible worms, dry-looking on the outside but full of stored moisture, churn these mounds, digesting and reassembling them. Their castings come out as neat, hardened pellets that slot into porous layers—waste as architecture. The mounds are both larder and habitat, rising gradually as generations pass, never collapsing because every loosened bit is immediately re-bound by roots and fungal threads.

Within and under these mounds hide the **ambushers**: trapdoor organisms whose “doors” are perfect mimicry of surrounding mat. Trigger hairs sense the weight of passing bodies; then, a flash—door flings open, muscular ring clenches, prey vanishes. Resin-kint avoid these zones, or send specialized scouts equipped with stiff probes of hardened fiber to test suspicious surfaces before moving the colony’s traffic that way.

Along the rear wall, some of the old erosion bowls—once water-hollows—have been taken over by **mindful crusts**. To a casual glance they are just patches of dark, scabby growth. But watch long enough, and you see lips within lips, edges tasting the air, pores flexing as they inhale volatile molecules, exhale others. Resin-kint consult them.

A worker will approach the bowl, tap a specific sequence on its rim, and wait. The crust swells slightly, releases a puff of scent. To a human nose, it is only a musty note; to the resin-kint’s chemosensors, it is data: current humidity profile, the mix of spores recently blown in from the slit, the metabolic state of the immediate neighborhood. These crusts don’t plan or intend, but their vast surface area and slow chemical integration make them inadvertent recorders of the hollow’s recent history. The colony has learned to read them as oracles of the immediate present.

Deeper still, in the darkest pockets where no bioluminescent glint even hints at shape, exist organisms that know nothing of light. They eat chemistry directly: rocks leached by ancient seepage, residues left behind by long-vanished water. Their metabolisms are achingly slow. To them, the resin-kint’s scurrying centuries are a brief flurry passing overhead. The only sign of their presence is the peculiar smoothness of certain concealed surfaces, as if the stone has been polished from the inside.

### At the threshold

Dangling in the slit between hollow and abyss, the filter-creatures form the hollow’s frontier folk.

They are jointed frames of living chitin, arranged into expanding rings and lattices that can balloon out to present a wide-catching area to incoming wind, then contract into tight bundles against the rock when gales threaten to tear them free. Within their mesh, a sticky mucus is secreted; dust, spores, and minute corpses adhere to it. Periodically the whole net flexes, rolls, and presents its harvest to internal digestive surfaces. Excess nutrients are not wasted; they exude small, compact nodules that drop inward, collected by watching resin-kint or snapped up by air-borne scavengers.

Filter-creatures communicate in a very limited way with the colony. Resin-kint tend to their anchor points, reinforcing them with resin and compacted fibers, clearing off strangling growths that would impede flexion. In exchange, the filters modulate their openness in response to certain resin-kint drumming patterns: more open when the colony signals hunger for outside material, more closed when too many harsh fragments—abrasive mineral dust, sharp seeds—are riding the wind.

Beyond the filters, there is only the drop, and a world that exists in hints: the angle of dust fall, the scent signatures borne on gusts, the rare shadow that briefly blots even the pale wash of the high sky. Whatever lives out there almost never enters; only its pollen and fragments are allowed in, pre-digested.

### How they live together

The rules you listed—no free water, no emptiness, no straight lines, no sudden brightness, no solitude—aren’t just environmental constraints. They have seeped into the logic of every resident.

- **No free water** has made every body a vessel. Shells, skins, and resins are all tailored to conserve. Even the few “liquids” that exist—fermentation broths, digestive pools—are shielded and enclosed. Theft of moisture is the fundamental violence; many predators don’t so much eat flesh as puncture and drink, leaving dry husks that crumble into the floor mesh.

- **No emptiness** ensures that succession is instant. When a resin-kint dies within a tunnel, its body becomes structural within hours: soft tissues picked clean by microdetrivores, plates braced against wall and ceiling by other workers, gaps filled with pulp. On a quiet day, if such exists here, you could watch a newly exposed bare patch: within hours, floating spores settle, filaments sprout, russet crusts begin to bloom, fliers investigate, web strands reach out. Vacancy is intolerable.

- **No straight lines** has shaped not just growth patterns but behavior. Travel is never from A to B but via circuits, loops, redundancies. Every important route has three or four alternate curves. No one here expects a direct, unbroken path; the expectation of detours is built into how messages, nutrients, and bodies move.

- **No sudden brightness** keeps everyone under the same dim mercy. Vision, where it exists, is tuned to contrasts of glow and occlusion, not to clear outlines. Sound, touch, scent are primary. Strategy is about shadows within shadows, about reading interruptions in the general murk. Any abrupt increase in light—say, a rockfall outside widening the slit—would be like a wound, a trauma to the entire hollow.

- **No solitude** is perhaps the deepest law. Every movement is felt by many. A resin-kint worker, pausing to taste a wall, has three smaller fliers land on its plates, a web filament brush its legs, a root curl minutely underfoot. Organisms negotiate not over empty territory but over degrees of overlap. Privacy, for those capable of such a concept, is measured in slightly reduced contact, not in absence.

### What they are, altogether

Try to pick out a protagonist and the hollow resists. The resin-kint are the most obviously “civil,” but they are also organelles in a greater composite being: the hollow itself, the cliff lung that long ago stopped inhaling water and began inhaling life.

In this sense, what lives here is a *collective metabolism*.

The resin-kint are its engineers and memory. The sponge-ropes and fronds are its muscle and skin, shaping and holding. The grazers and matsheep are its digestive conveyors. The ambushers, web-crafters, and predators are its pruning shears, keeping flows from stagnating into monocultures. The filter-creatures are its mouth, tasting the outside.

No individual, and no single species, understands or owns the whole. But over geologic time, through blind persistence and feedback, they have coalesced into something coherent: a system that defies the poverty of its placement—high, dry, almost lightless—by turning density itself into wealth.

So who lives there?

A city of armor-banded builders who never see the sky they depend on. A weather of living dust that eats and is eaten in the span of a breath. Webbenders who farm the air. Slow, remembering crusts that listen chemically to the hollow’s every whisper. Roots that have been taught to bear weight, worms that turn the dead into architecture, filters that harvest an unseen, wind-borne river.

They live not beside their world but as its tissues. The cliff hollow, once simply an absence in the rock, now has inhabitants so thoroughly enmeshed that it is no longer quite right to say anything lives *in* it.

Everything here lives *as* it.
