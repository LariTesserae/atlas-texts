They are small against their world, and they know it.

Bodies first, because the place has had its say on their shape.

They are not tall. The tallest of them, standing straight on a dry ridge, might brush two meters, but most run shorter, compact. Height is a liability in low canopies and cramped passages; bulk wastes warmth in air that leeches heat into stone and water. Instead they are built to thread: narrow-shouldered, long-torsoed, with limbs that look a little too long until you see them in motion, folding and unfolding through ladders, ledges, and root-windows with fluid precision.

Their skin runs along the color of their habitat: wet stone, leaf-shadow, the pale underside of fern fronds. On the youngest it is a matte, milky grey-brown that darkens with age into something like damp bark. Not all of them share the same hue; some families carry a faint greenish cast, as if the algae that coats the rocks has found a softer host. Others show undertones of violet-black in the thin skin at the throat and inner elbows, deepening when they are cold or frightened.

Hair is always black or very, very dark—brown like soaked peat—and never hangs long and free. Long hair wicks chill into the scalp, catches on branches, sponges up the constant drip. So they keep it disciplined. Children wear it close-cropped or shorn. Adults bind it in tight braids hugged to the skull, or twist it in short cords that lie flat, pinned with slips of bone or tooth. Only deep inside, in the warmest chambers, do you see hair loosed fully, and then only among kin.

Their faces are simple, open planes adapted to a world of close focus. Eyes are large but not bulbous, set under rounded brow-bones with soft folds at the corners from squinting into the dim. Irises tend to the pale side—grey, amber, washed hazel—and their pupils widen fast and stay open in the half-light, so that from a little distance the eyes look almost black. The whites show more when they laugh or startle; surprise here is a bright arc of sclera between wet shadow and stone.

Their noses are narrow and long, with fine bridges that can fit easily between reed-masks and hood-wrappings. The nostrils flare wider than you might expect, not for scent alone but to feel the air, to judge temperature and moisture in the faintest shifts. Lips are full, mostly from the constant damp; they crack if they dry, which is rare and unpleasant. Ears are rounded, neither large nor small, but very expressive: they track sound unconsciously, canting toward a drip that’s changed rhythm, a footstep two levels down that doesn’t quite belong.

Their hands and feet bear the clearest imprint of this place.

Fingers are long, with broad, spatulate tips and skin that wrinkles quickly when wet but never seems to soften into uselessness. The pads are thickened, not calloused in the hard, brittle way of desert walkers, but rubbery, gripping stone even when slick with algae. Nails grow strong and flat and are kept short, squared off with stone graters so they do not snag in woven walkways or tear on wet bark.

Their feet are more like careful tools than blunt supports. The arch is low, spreading weight over more surface. Toes splay flexibly, especially the first and second, which can clamp with surprising strength for climbing up a wet trunk or holding to a ledge while both hands are engaged. They wear foot-wraps rather than shoes: long strips of treated fiber wound intricately around sole and instep, knotted in patterns that allow the skin to breathe and water to drain while still protecting against the sharp newness of the rock.

Their posture is slightly forward, ready to catch, to reach, to step sideways rather than stride straight. In stillness they often stand with knees bent and one hand resting lightly—always touching something that is not themselves. It is not shyness; it is habit: an unthinking confirmation that stone and root are where they should be.

Their lungs are deep. In this heavy air, breath must be steady rather than forceful. They do not pant when they climb. They draw in slowly, filling the base of the ribs first, then the chest, and then they let it go in a long thin stream. In childhood they learn to match their breathing to the omnipresent drip: three heartbeats in, three out, four in, four out, until each has found the count that brings them calm. In the quietest hours you can walk the high paths and hear it: the long converge-and-part of many people breathing at once, framed by water.

Their senses are tuned in ways the open-sky folk might misread.

They see well in dimness, but not extremes of shadow and glare; a sudden beam of direct light can blind them for long seconds. They stand in those shafts with eyes half-lidded, letting the bright stab at them slowly until their pupils remember how to shrink. In their own world’s usual light, they can pick out every nuance of wet green and dull grey, read the health of a moss-bed at ten paces, see the slight thickening in a root where a tool is hidden.

They hear less in the sense of distance and more in pattern. Echoes confuse direction, but they are experts at cadence. A three-beat series of drops followed by a pause belongs to that particular overhang; if it changes, something has clogged or fallen. The rhythmic slap of bare feet on wood reveals the age and mood of the walker as clearly as an open face. They do not bother with names for many individual sounds. They think of them as lines in a woven cloth: you read whole swaths at once or you are lost.

Their sense of smell is constrained by the city’s damp neutrality; rot and blossom and metal are all softened. But within that narrow band they are acute. They can tell the difference between the clean mushroomy scent of a healthy beam and the sour sweetness that means hidden decay. They can track the particular tang of a kin-group’s hearth, the resin and herb mix each uses for fuel and fumigation.

They taste mineral differences in the water with practiced tongues. Children are taught to distinguish the slight bitterness of high-iron drips from the thin, sharp taste of a new fissure that might carry unseen silt or toxins. Festivals sometimes include little contests: blindfolded youths given bowls from different channels, asked to place each to its source by flavor alone.

They are not hairless. A fine, sparse down covers their forearms, thighs, lower back, a layer of constant roughened warmth that breaks the chill the way moss baffles a rock. In colder seasons, or during long wet-flows when the whole under-valley cools, some sprout thicker patches along spine and chest, an echo of their climbing mammal neighbors. They do not think of it as fur. It is simply “thickening.” Older people wear it proudly.

Their clothing is less decoration than negotiation with the place.

Next to the skin they favor woven fibers from a sedge-like plant whose strands remain supple even when permanently damp. These under-garments fit close: long tunics and leg-wraps, sleeves that can be unrolled to the wrist or pushed back to mid-forearm. Over that come looser layers: jackets with many small folds and overlaps that trap warmth without hoarding water, knee-length over-skirts or kilted wraps knotted high when they wade. All of it in colors stolen directly from their world: clay-browns, moss-greens, lichen-greys, the dull blue of deep shadow.

Decoration is subtle and almost entirely textural. A line of stitches in brighter fiber along the cuff; a patch where the weave thickens in a particular knot-language describing clan and role. Some wear small stone or shell beads threaded at the ends of braids, but even these are smooth and quiet. Nothing jingles. Nothing clacks. They have no use for ornaments that announce themselves to the air.

Hoods and caps are common: soft, thick coifs that can be pulled down when the drip grows too insistent, or when one must climb under a particularly active curtain of water. Reed-mesh visors, treated with oil from some glossy-rooted plant, help divert the constant spray from eyes during long work in exposed channels.

Over all these layers, when they go into the wilder reaches or out along the long watercourse, they don cloaks that are both garment and tool: woven mats of tough root-fiber, lined on one side with densely packed moss. Worn moss-out, they are camouflage and comfort; turned inward, they make an impromptu bed-roll, a damper against cold rock.

They move with extraordinary quiet. Not an enforced stealth, simply a cultural default: feet placed carefully, weight rolled from outer edge to inner, knees never locked. Children are corrected gently but constantly when they thump or splash. The goal is not no-sound, but good-sound: noise that blends with water and leaf, that does not send panic shivering across the membranes of tens of neighbors at once.

They live in clusters that mirror the city’s architecture: nested, overlapping, interdependent.

A “house” is seldom a single structure. It is a vertical stack and horizontal sprawl of chambers grown and built over generations: a ground-level work space with sloped stone floor draining seamlessly into a narrow channel; above it a sleeping loft of springy root-lattice; above that a storage nook pressed against a still-living trunk; off to one side a small, enclosed warm-room centered on a slow hearth. Add to this the semi-open platforms under eaves, the tiny niche where someone has woven a private meditation mat between two load-bearing roots, the netted balcony where cured fish hang in the dim.

A kin-unit—what they call a “tangle”—might occupy half a dozen such interlocked pods, connected by internal ladders and crawls invisible from outside. A tangle is not nuclear; it is fluid. It often spans three or four generations and includes loosely adopted outsiders, people taken in after a fall, after the loss of their own tangle in a collapse or flood, or simply in youth when they abandoned a distant basin to follow a lover’s path.

No one here lives entirely alone. There are those who prefer solitude, who keep to high niches or little houses pressed into the city’s shadowed edges, but even they are woven into maintenance schedules, water-right agreements, and ritual circuits. A person who tries to stand apart soon learns that nothing in this under-valley stands long without someone else’s hand at its seams.

They arrange themselves socially along lines of water and light.

Each long ridge and its flanking channels form a “reach.” A reach has its tangles, its shared workyards, its attached gardens, its customary sleeping quiet-hours and drum codes. The people of a reach tend to share a particular style of stonework, a preferred plant for roof-crops, a specific recipe for the fermented tuber-cakes that travel well on long walks. They say things like “That is high-Reach mosswork” or “She has the walk of South-Flow,” mapping identity onto slope and current.

Overlaid on this is another structure: the “thread.” A thread is not about physical closeness but about function. There is a Thread of Channels: the people responsible for maintaining, cleaning, and gently re-routing the complex watercourses that make the city possible. There is a Thread of Roofs, who watch the load-bearing limbs and live foundations, who climb routinely to the highest accessible points and consult quietly at dawn about hairline cracks and subtle sags. There are Threads of Food, of Fire, of Stone, of Story.

Thread-members cut a small mark into the left sleeve of their outer garment—a particular knot, a pattern of reinforcement in the cloth. It is easy not to see it until you need it; then your eye cannot miss it. In a sudden surge of water, when a cascade clogs and threatens to pour into a living quarter, people shout a single word, the name of the relevant Thread, and arms bearing that mark rise, hands already reaching for the tools they carry almost always.

No Thread is purely hereditary, though children often follow their elders into work that is literally under their hands. Apprenticeship begins young, about the time a child can stand comfortably in cold knee-deep water or climb twice their height without an adult’s constant presence. Instruction is mostly by mimicry and correction rather than formal teaching: a master of stonework stopping a younger person’s arm mid-swing to adjust the angle of a wedge, guiding their hand once and expecting them to read the feel thereafter.

Decision-making ripples outwards rather than falling from above.

Every reach has a circle—a literal ring of smoothed stones in some sheltered, slightly higher place, often under one of the brighter shafts of light. When something needs to be discussed beyond the scale of a single tangle or Thread—a proposed diversion of a channel, say, or an offer of alliance from an upstream basin—those who care to gather there. There is no single presiding chief. Elders sit, younger folk stand or crouch behind them, and anyone can speak, provided they rise and lay one hand on the central stone while they do so.

This touching is more than symbolic. Their ethic is that one does not say what one is not willing to have borne—by stone, by others. It slows speech. People think before they offer words that will be remembered by all in the circle and by the stone itself.

Among them circulate certain people whose role is both humble and vital: the “binders.” A binder does not command. They listen, walk between reaches, attend to frictions and mismatches. When a quarrel over garden water threatens to unweave years of quiet co-work between two tangles, the reach’s Threads may call a binder. They arrive, carrying no tools but a staff and a soft-wrapped drum. They stay in the contested space, sleep in an unused loft or under someone’s eaves, and talk, slowly, with everyone involved, seeking not compromise in the abstract but a pattern that will not pull other things undone.

Their justice is largely preventative. True punishments are rare and community-shaped: someone who repeatedly neglects maintenance, endangering others, may find themselves forbidden from certain works, made to take on less structurally critical tasks until they can be trusted again. Exile is a word that tastes of panic; they will do almost anything to avoid it, because outside the woven safety of communal vigilance, the under-valley can kill with a single unnoticed crack.

Their spiritual life is not a separate realm above these practicalities; it seeps through them the way moisture seeps through stone.

They do not, as a rule, worship distant beings. There are no high gods of sky or fire. Those exist elsewhere, perhaps, but not in the low world that breathes around them. Their reverence attends instead to processes: drip, flow, settling, binding, release.

They name the perpetual fall of water “The Saying.” Children are told that the world is always speaking itself into being here, one drop at a time. When someone comes to a difficult choice, an elder might send them to sit alone under a particular steady trickle and “listen to what is already being said.” The lesson is less about mystic revelation and more about patience, about aligning oneself to the slow, persistent pace at which the world changes in this place.

Ceilings are another object of uneasy devotion. They call the overhead stone “the Held.” It is both guardian and threat. The oldest stories, told in the deepest reaches on nights when everyone’s bones ache from some far-off tremor, speak of the time before the Held formed this basin—of a roaring river in open dark, of a whole landscape that fell in a day. Whether this is history or myth matters less than how it teaches them: that what seems overwhelmingly solid can move suddenly, that the stability they live within is conditional and alive.

They mark that fact regularly in ritual.

Once each season, at the faint turning of the light when the shafts take on a slightly different angle, every reach holds a “Listening of the Held.” Work slows for half a day. Drums are not struck. Fires are banked lower than usual. People gather in various silent spots—under their own roofs, in shared halls, on high walkways—and for a set count of breaths they do nothing but attend to the stone above.

Any new sound—a faint creak, a trickle where none dripped before, a distant grind—is remembered, described later in the circle, weighed against all the other reaches’ reports. The Roof-Thread, who know the ceilings best, use this communal intake to decide where to focus their climbing in the coming days. But underneath that practical layer is another current: the feeling that in taking that time to listen, they are acknowledging the Held as something with which they are in relationship, not just a brute fact.

Death is both intimate and diffused. Bodies do not burn; fire for such work would be extravagant, and smoke, dangerous. Nor do they bury deep in soggy ground that turns fluid in floods. Instead, they have found another path: they allow the dead to join the structure.

In the dimmer skirts of each reach there are “joining walls”: low, thick retaining terraces made of interlocked stone and interwoven root with many narrow, carefully faced niches. When someone dies, wrapped in the cloth that touched their life most often, their body is laid in one of these openings. The mouth is sealed with an intricate lattice of living switch-roots trained in advance; over seasons those roots thicken, merge, take in what lies behind.

It is not a place of horror for them, but of quiet pride. Children run their hands along those walls learning the lineage of support—“Here, that is where your mother’s mother rests; she holds this corner of the garden still.” People speak of those walls as if they were elder kin: “We will not move this terrace; it is thick with our own.”

Stories travel along such bindings.

The storytellers—those of the Thread of Story—do not stand apart, cloaked and mysterious. Most are also gardeners or stone-workers or water-tenders. What sets them apart is memory and craft. They can chant the long genealogies, tracing not just people but structures: “In the Snake-Reach, under the third fall, when the upper shelf was bare and new, they set the first cobble,” and so on, folding events and physical changes into one continuous narrative.

They prefer to tell in half-light, in damp halls where sound wraps tight around everyone present. Their best tales are less about great heroes than about the city itself: the time when an entire roof-tree split silently over months, giving no sign until a child, sitting up late, heard a new tiny creak and called the Roof-Thread in time to save three tangles; the old flood when a Thread of Channels, faced with an impossible surge, chose to sacrifice one garden to redirect water away from ten.

They do not romanticize self-sacrifice, but they do honor clear-seeing, the ability to understand how one’s choice will travel through the mesh.

They have no writing as we would mark it, no ink on leaf or chisel in stone. The stone is too valuable as structure, the leaves decay. Instead, they write in knots and braids. Along certain paths, especially high and low ones where few casual feet pass, hang cords of plant fiber strung between roots or through carved eyelets. On these cords are tied specific sequences of knots: some as simple as a child’s game, others bewilderingly intricate.

Members of the Thread of Message tend these lines. They maintain the code and can “read” a cord with fingers in the dark, each knot a syllable of concept rather than sound. Slow news—of a new marriage between reaches, of a dangerous shift in the mid-roof, of a strange animal seen in a usually quiet basin—travels via these braids. Urgent messages still go by runner or by drum, because even here some news should be carried by breath.

Magical thinking exists, but it is anchored in material understanding. They have charms not to ward off imaginary demons of flame and void, but to remind, to focus. A Channel-worker might hang a small polished stone over a tricky sluice, not because they think the stone will *do* anything, but because its swinging will draw the eye each time they pass, prompting constant small checks.

Their children learn by falling, but never far. Early games are played on the safest platforms, just a drop of a body-length or two into waiting moss. They learn how much noise a misstep makes, how many eyes snap toward them when a board thumps. There are games of stillness too: who can move from one side of a chamber to the other making only sounds that exist already—breath, drip, leaf-brush.

They are not without joy. It runs in narrow channels, but it runs strong.

Laughter here is soft but intense. People hunch, shoulders shaking, faces hidden in hands or against a friend’s shoulder, teeth flashing briefly in the dim. To shout with mirth would feel as wrong as shouting with rage in a sleeping-hall. Instead they release pleasure in bright, staccato chuckles, in the quick touch of a palm against a wall as if to share the shock with the Held.

They have festivals calibrated to their light.

When, once or twice a cycle, a particular shaft channels unmuted sun down into a small courtyard or patch of wet bare stone, that time is marked and anticipated. For days beforehand, people from that reach adjust their routines so that work can pause during the beam. Others from adjacent basins may hike in, working their way over rickety gangways and slick paths in the hours before.

In the moments of full light, they are almost reverent. They set out pale things: sickly saplings, convalescent elders, baskets of seeds and eggs. Children are nudged forward, squinting, to put their hands directly in the beam and feel bone and tendon under the sudden clarity. Some folk sing then—not loudly, but with more sustained tone than usual, wordless melodies that seem to marvel at the way sun carves depth out of everything it touches.

No one looks up the shaft for long. The mind balks, perhaps, at the thought of that distance, of a world beyond their Held that they have never seen. Instead they watch how the light pools and shifts across surfaces they understand.

They do have notions of an “above,” but it is myth-wrapped, half-terrifying. Children trade stories of creatures who live in a place so dry that skin splits and lips bleed, where water falls from *the top* and is not caught, where stone becomes powder and blows across the ground. The brave among them say, in quiet corners, that one day they will climb a shaft as far as any person has ever gone and look out. Their elders do not forbid this talk, but they do not encourage it either. They know the stories of those who tried, and who fell, or who returned with their lungs scorched by thin, alien air.

Love, for them, is as braided as their streets.

Pair-bonding exists, but exclusivity in all things is a poor fit for lives so interlaced. People take lovers within and across tangles; sometimes those loves endure, sometimes they drift like channels, joining and parting again. Children belong first to the tangle, then to their birth-parents. No one lacks adults who will carry them, correct them, teach them the drip-counts and particular patience of the under-valley.

Jealousy is not unknown, but it is often directed less at shared affection and more at the allocation of careful attention. To be neglected—by a partner, by a Thread, by a reach’s decision-makers—is a dangerous thing in a world where a missed inspection can bring a ceiling down. Their quarrels over intimacy often use the language of maintenance: “You let that path rot,” someone might say, “and when it fell you acted surprised.”

Outsiders exist, but they come rarely and are taken in selectively.

Far along the main watercourse, in basins where the Held dips lower and the world hums louder with stone-voice, are other clusters of life. Some are kin to this city, branches of the same long-ago collapse. Others might be drastically different: fungus-tenders who live in near-dark, people who have coaxed true fire into tamed coils despite the damp, hunters who follow the migrating herds of half-tame climbing mammals.

Trade is thin and slow. A stranger arriving along a channel will be met first at the water’s edge, not with weapons drawn but with gaze and questions. Who are your reaches? What do you drink? What cracks have you crossed? People of the Thread of Story will want their tales; people of the Thread of Stone will want their knowledge of how ceilings sound elsewhere.

If they are judged not-dangerous, they will be invited in, lodged perhaps in a high loft near a knot-cord line so others can watch comings and goings. They may stay, if they wish, and if a tangle chooses to take them. Over time, their customs get smoothed at the edges, braided into the existing code: a new recipe, a new way of knotting, a new rhythm added to the complex hush.

They have enemies, but few of them are creatures you can stab.

Sudden rockfall is an enemy. Creep—the slow shift of a whole wall or pillar over years—is an enemy. Flood surges when a far-off chasm finally clogs and lets go: enemy. Rot that travels invisibly inside a key beam is an enemy. The under-valley is full of beings that bite and pierce and poison, but these they respect and work around. The great threats are those that ignore intention, that answer only to mass and gravity and water’s pressure.

There have been wars. Not often, and never wars of conquest; what use is ruling a reach whose ceilings you don’t understand? But drought above can dry the drips and send competing basins vying for control of strong, reliable channels. Misunderstood signals can lead to sabotage—someone widening a crack upstream not realizing it will erode a distant foundation. Old songs remember the time two reaches fought literally over a thin trickle in the rock, each trying to claim more by diverting it slightly. In the end, the pressure behind the diverted flow built, and the rock split altogether, sending the entire drip down some unseen fault and leaving both basins parched.

That story is told to any hotheaded youth who proposes settling disputes by force. “You may win the trickle,” the elders say, “and together lose the stone.”

In conflict, they favor strangling over bludgeoning: not in the sense of hands on throats, but in the tactical sense of cutting flows, blocking paths, disabling ladders. Direct violence is so risky in these tight quarters; a wild swing can crack a support, a stray push can send three bystanders into a cold channel. So those who truly wish harm learn to work quietly on the infrastructure itself, to shift the world beneath their enemies’ feet.

Such saboteurs are feared more than any direct warrior. When discovered, they are treated with a severity that startles an outsider. Depriving a person of threads and tangles, marking them publicly as one whose touch cannot be trusted, is for them worse than any bodily pain.

They do not have gods, but they have taboos.

To spit into a channel is disgusting, not because saliva is vile but because the channel belongs to all. To waste heat—burning a fuel hot enough to send a thick plume of smoke when a low ember would do—is seen as childish and threatening. To carve deeply into an unexamined wall without calling a member of the Thread of Stone is not just foolish but arrogant.

The strongest taboo, perhaps, concerns loud singular sounds. An involuntary scream when someone falls through a rotten board is forgiven. But to shout for effect, to slap beams together for drama, to make sound “for no reason” in a way that jars hearts and shivers roofs—that earns cold looks and swift correction. There are quiet places reserved for necessary loudness: certain deep sumps where the rock is thick and water-sound dense, where a person can go to wail out grief, to howl a song, to beat a drum with all their strength. Elsewhere, the world is kept in the register where words can travel without panic.

Children are taught all of this not as doctrine but as navigation: maps of where their bodies end and the living city begins.

Who lives here? People who have let this place write itself into their bones.

They are not saints. They gossip in low voices over shared drying racks, sometimes cruelly. They hoard little comforts: the warmest corner of a loft, a patch of ceiling directly under a beloved trickle. They envy those tangles who have settled near rare brighter shafts. They tell petty lies, hide minor damage in hopes someone else will fix it, take more fish than is strictly fair from a common trap when no one seems to be watching.

But even their selfishness is hemmed by awareness of consequence. A child who lies about having checked a channel and is later found out when a gap collapses will carry that shame like a chill for years. Not because they are punished harshly, but because everyone understands so exactly how close the world came to tearing.

They move through their days with that dual knowledge: that they are tiny in a vast, slow-moving system, and that their tiniest acts—tightening a knot, re-setting a stone, quieting an impulse to slam a door—ripple through that system in ways they cannot entirely see.

They live in the wet breath of the earth and call it home. They do not yearn, in any collective way, to leave it for the dry hypothetical above. Their myths of “sky” are less aspiration than cautionary tales about unbound wind and unfiltered fire. For them, safety and meaning are found in contact: hand to root, eye to slow water, shoulder to shoulder in cramped halls as the Held overhead exhales and the Saying continues, drop by drop.

In the end, who lives here is not just a people but a pattern: humans thinned and thickened by stone, by moisture, by proximity, folded into an underworld so completely that to pry them free would be to tear the place itself.
