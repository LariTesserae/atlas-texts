Who lives here:

Not one kind of inhabitant, but several, layered like the sediments under the streets. They don’t arrive all at once; they precipitate out of the same conditions that made the city. Some are recognizably human, some only marginally so, some not at all. All of them are wet-adapted, patient, slightly provisional.

---

### The people who claim it as a city

The dominant residents look human at a glance, but closely they are products of this basin’s damp adolescence.

They are short on ornament and long on insulation. Clothing here is less about style and more about maintaining a workable microclimate against that cold, insinuating moisture: layered, sealed fabrics, composite fibers that bead water off on the outside and wick condensation away from the skin within. Coats with internal piping for warm air from building vents. Gloves that keep the fingers sensitive enough to manipulate fine machinery slick with condensation. Boots with soles that can flex across slowly slumping ground and find traction on algae-slick steel.

Their bodies carry the mark of this life. Many have a mild, chronic flush to the cheeks and nose from years of damp chill. Hair tends to be kept short, braided, or shrouded; frizz and mildew are recurring enemies. There’s often a faint sheen on exposed skin: not sweat exactly, but the result of long-term adaptation to humidity—sebaceous glands adjusted, pores slightly altered. In older residents, small patches of skin may show a subtle texturing, as if the cells have learned from the city’s bio-reactive panels, becoming better at shedding water, better at holding warmth.

Respiratory systems are quietly modified. Some alterations are deliberate—genetic tweaks or chronic microtherapies to deal with spores, fine silica, and metallic aerosols. Lungs are efficient; mucous membranes trap and neutralize the basin’s dust before it can scar tissue. Influenza here looks strange under a microscope: common viruses wearing new coats made of local particulates, blunted by medical routines as unremarkable as washing one’s hands.

These people are not flamboyant talkers. Speech stays low, close, out of habit and acoustics; in a city that hums in low frequencies, high-volume conversation feels intrusive. They lean in against the wind and the hiss of drizzle. To watch them move is to see a choreography of small, efficient decisions: how to walk in a half-flooded alley without splashing cold water up inside one’s coat; how to slip between a condensing pipe and a moss-thick wall without brushing either; how to time a dash under the exposed edge of a glasshouse roof before a fresh curtain of runoff cascades down.

Their language—whatever name you’d give it—carries a lot of moisture and sediment in its metaphors. Deadlines “slump” rather than slip. A bad plan is “built on loose fill.” Someone unreliable is “like an unsecured bank—fine until the rain really starts.” They talk about the city’s infrastructure the way farmers talk about weather: not abstractly, but with the intimacy of daily dependence. Everyone knows what a pump failure sounds like in the bones. Everyone recognizes the difference between a harmless subsidence and the kind of deep, hollow thud that empties a building in under a minute.

Children grow up with a very different sense of “ground” than in older cities. Toys include mud-stable building sets: modular grids you can insert into saturated soil to keep a fort from slowly listing into a puddle. They learn early not to trust any single map; in school exercises, a teacher might send them out with yesterday’s route plan, then ask them, upon return, to mark where reality has shifted—this lane closed, that walkway gone, a new scaffold connecting two roofs that weren’t neighbors last week. Cognitive flexibility is more drilled than rote memorization.

Professionally, they sort into roles that mirror the city’s half-formedness:

- **Substrate engineers** who read the slow war between land and construction. They walk with sensors and long, needle-like probes, listening to the deep, damp strata. Many of them develop a gut sense—almost superstitious—of when a wall is tired, when a slope is just one more rain away from slipping. Their offices are cluttered with core samples, layers of wet silt in labeled cylinders that look like arrested time.

- **Bio-interface technicians** who tend the live surfaces: façades of algae, lichenized composites, and moss-embedded insulation. They talk about their buildings like gardeners talk about beds: “Block 7’s gone too lean; the panels are fading; we’ll need to adjust the trace minerals in its feedwater.” Their tools are half pruning shears, half calibration wand.

- **Water path mappers** whose entire profession is to understand where, precisely, all this moisture goes. They walk the city with thermal imagers and chemical tracers, tracking leaks, stray flows, ghost currents in the undercity. They maintain the dynamic models that predict which alley will flash-flood during a sudden squall, which foundation will see slow saturation in three months’ time if something upstream isn’t adjusted now.

- **Rail fielders**, less engineers than urban nomads, who ride the short, crablike trains out into the fog at the basin’s edges. They are the ones who see the city’s feelers reaching toward unfinished land and decide—day by day, load by load—whether a line can carry one more container or whether it must be pulled back and the ground allowed to rest.

This population’s defining trait is an almost relaxed acceptance of provisionality. They do not expect final forms, permanent solutions. Every fix is a bridge between now and a later, slightly different now. They inhabit iterative time.

### The ones who talk to roots and rust

Alongside the more conventional trades, there are residents whose work runs right at the boundary between the city’s technology and its accelerated ecology.

Call them **interface cultivators**, though locally they get more colloquial names: rustwrights, panel-herders, fungus-smiths.

They are the ones who understand the strange filaments running between paving slabs, the mineral-vegetal blades that flex like carved soapstone. They’ve spent a career watching which compositions of concrete sprout glass nodules in river contact and which stay inert; which alloys invite the circuitous, lichen-like rust that seems almost to carry current; which polymers, when exposed to the basin’s dust and moisture, grow those fine hairs that wick and glow.

Their labs don’t look like clean rooms; they look like terrariums crossed with machine shops. Benches are crowded with cut sections of pipe clothed in experimental mosses, pans of sediment seeded with proto-root tissues, lengths of beam half-submerged in controlled flows of river water infused with tailored spores. What they’re doing, at base, is breeding: not plants, not machines, but those ambiguous hybrids that behave like both.

Some interface cultivators specialize in living infrastructure: **rooted supports** that can gradually thicken and self-buttress, **bio-conduits** that move water or heat or ions the way vascular systems do. Others work on **instrumented organisms**—plants and lichens whose chemical outputs can be read as data, turning an entire façade into a distributed sensor array.

They are not mystics, but outsiders sometimes read them that way. They talk about “listening” to a panel by watching how condensation forms across its surface, about a wall “liking” a particular nutrient mix because its microalgae shift from dull green to iridescent. They keep precise logs—growth curves, response spectra, failure modes—but they also have instincts honed through repeated experiments with matter that wants, in its own way, to behave.

There are also **degraders**, a necessary counterpoint: people tasked with persuading hybrid materials to break down when the city outgrows them. Conventional demolition here is risky—the wrong shock can wake sleeping strains of experimental biology, release self-propagating composites into the water grid. So degraders work more like undertakers and compost masters, orchestrating controlled decomposition. A disused skybridge might be seeded along its seams with a custom lichen that eats the bonding agents first, letting the span slump safely onto prepared catch-frames. A retired panel-skin might be coaxed to delaminate in layers, each directed into a different recycling stream.

Their knowledge of the city’s half-forgotten experiments is deep and quietly crucial. They know which early bio-reactive bricks will sprout dangerously if left damp in basements, which batches of composite cladding tend to start sweating odd fluids after fifteen years, which generation of root-channelled supports accidentally developed the capacity to reach toward groundwater far beyond their design envelope.

### The ones who live closer to the heat

Beneath and between the factory floors, in crawlspaces laced with ducts and condensate lines, there is another human (or human-descended) presence: **thermal dwellers**.

To the surface workers, they’re maintenance crews and pipe-chasers, technicians who prefer the enclosed, steaming corridors to the open, mist-laden streets. But over decades of selective choice and minor biotechnological tweaks, they’ve become a slightly different population band.

Their workspaces are hot and dripping. Waste heat that leaves vents as ghostly steam aboveground is still dense and thick down here. These people tend to be slighter, sweat-slicked, with skin that tolerates rapid shifts between damp chill and near-sauna. Some have visible modifications: micro-ports along the ribs where cooling garments can plug into the duct network; embedded sensors in ear cartilage that ping softly when a pipe’s temperature or vibration goes out of bounds; corneal overlays tuned to infrared so they can see leaks as halos in the dark.

Informally, they form tight kin-nets, because you don’t crawl into a flexing, humid maze of pipes without people you trust to haul you out if a newly shifting stratum kinks a conduit or opens a sudden drop. They have maps of the undercity no planner fully comprehends: hand-sketched, annotated with nicknames for junctions and chambers where condensation falls in certain rhythms, where a particular kind of moss grows on the galvanizing, where a slow, steady drip has hollowed a little shrine in the concrete.

Over time, some of them shift semi-permanently into this layer, sleeping in dry pods tucked into service caverns, working on cycles that pay more attention to pump rhythms and load curves than to official day and night. They are the ones who hear, in the low city hum, the difference between normal deep pumps and something else—a new vibration, a layering frequency that suggests something, somewhere in the infrastructure, has begun to act on its own.

### Those who tend the water-veins

Along the river and in the terraces of engineered wetlands there live the **riverward people**—not a separate ethnicity so much as a culture shaped by adjacency to moving water and its proliferating, half-known ecologies.

They work with the furred amphibians, the metallic-flecked hunters; they monitor the translucent many-legged invertebrates that skate the film of the water. Some breed them intentionally. The cube-curling invertebrates, for instance, exude trace chemicals when they ball up under stress; these can be harvested for use as coagulants in filtration or as signaling molecules in interface biotech.

Riverward dwellings are clustered along raised boardwalks and anchored pontoons, because everyone knows the banks are ideas, not facts. Their homes smell of silt and boiled algae, of drying reeds and fermented plant gels. In the evenings, when the mist clings low, you can see them moving with lanterns that throw flat, white light along the water’s skin, checking the color and thickness of the surface biofilm, tapping gently at the half-recognized species that have arrived in the night.

They maintain the algae nets that tremble as if under their own internal wind, checking for rips, tuning tension. Some nets are for biomass harvest; others are there because no one yet knows what they’re for, only that when they’re absent certain downstream processes glitch—filters foul more quickly, gels from the chemical works destabilize, nearby wetland plants develop a pallor.

Riverward people are practiced negotiators between agencies: municipal water, industrial intake, bio-research labs, citizen groups worried about their children falling into tanks of unknown reagents. They know where it’s safe to let kids wade and where the invisible gradients of pH or dissolved metals would burn bare skin.

They also whisper about things seen in flood season, when storms push uncharacteristically hard against the basin and the river swells beyond its lazy meander: shapes under the water that are too regular, too symmetrical for mere wood and debris; pulses of light in the depths that are not reflections of anything above.

### Those who have never seen a dry sky

Children here inherit not just the damp but the sense that nothing is finished. They grow in **schools that shift**.

Literally: some classrooms are modules that can be relocated as foundations slump or new industrial zones come online. A child might attend school in three different neighborhoods over a single year, riding trams through steaming yards and past bioreactor façades that glow emerald one week, dull gray the next.

They learn to read not just letters and numbers but gradients: of moisture on a stair rail (slick means watch your step), of moss thickness on a bridge (thick and velvety means this structure has been undisturbed a long time, maybe too long), of condensation patterns on a window (regular beads suggest good insulation; strange fractals might hint at a fault in the wall’s living skin).

They also learn, more formally than their parents, the city’s bio-technical grammar. Early lessons involve cooking experiments that double as training in safe handling of the city’s ambient dust and spores: certain powders that, when mixed with water from different drains, bloom colors that reveal microbial or particulate profiles. They watch with fascinated horror as some mixtures start to grow fine, glassy threads if left in the damp for a day, while others simply dry to harmless crusts.

Some children show an affinity for particular aspects of this world—certain are **pattern kids**, who intuitively grasp the flows in the undercity, predicting where a leak elsewhere will cause a puddle here. Others are **growth kids**, who can coax a test panel’s algae film into a desired hue faster than peers by tiny adjustments in light and nutrient. The city notices; scholarships and apprenticeships appear, quietly linking these children into the appropriate workstreams.

Play, too, reflects the city’s nature. Their games involve building structures on trays of saturated soil and then subjecting them to “rain” from perforated buckets to see what holds. They cultivate pet mosses and small, fast-growing plant clumps in jars, racing to shape their growth around miniature scaffolds. The bravest sneak to the plastic barriers around the crystalline outcrop that pierces the tram platform, daring each other to reach through and touch the forbidden surface. Most stop short; the scorch marks from an old cutting attempt are persuasive. But there are always, in every cohort, one or two who look at that vertical pool of frozen glass the way others look at the open sky.

### Quiet orders and provisional governance

No one truly rules a place this unfinished; they manage it in overlapping jurisdictions.

There is a **Civic Stabilization Office**, staffed by practical geomorphologists and structural risk analysts, whose remit is to ensure that the rate at which the city leans, slumps, and adjusts stays within livable bounds. They issue “quiet closures” more often than grand edicts: a walkway taped off overnight after sensors show a subtle acceleration in its support’s creep, a rail spur quietly de-energized while crews reinforce an embankment.

Alongside it operates the **Environmental Integration Directorate**, which cares less about human safety in the short term and more about the long-term viability of the city’s entanglement with its ecology. They license new bio-reactive materials, approve or deny experimental conduits that propose to tie more of the city’s physiology into the land’s. Their meetings are dense with jargon: terms from synthetic biology, soil science, urban planning, systems theory.

Threaded through these are **informal councils**—associations of interface cultivators, riverward representatives, thermal dwellers, rail fielders—who maintain influence via expertise and mutual dependency. The basin’s experimental fringes, with their condensation forests and vapor towers, are often their doing. A new plot might start with someone’s research grant and end with a cluster of households moving in to tend and live among prototype trees that condense drinkable water from mist.

Overseeing, or at least connecting, these bodies is a distributed mesh of **sensing and forecasting systems**. The city’s machines are residents, too, in a sense: analytic engines that ingest flows of data from pressure sensors in pipes, tilt meters in foundations, biological feedback from wall lichens, electrical harmonics from rust patterns. They output recommendations, warnings, pattern maps. Some of them sit in obvious control rooms, interfaces glowing under human operators’ hands. Others are embedded more subtly, in train controllers that calculate not just schedules but stress patterns in the soft soil; in building skins that dim certain operations when their metabolic indicators suggest overload.

Do the people speak of these systems as citizens? Officially, they are tools. Unofficially, the way residents talk about “what the forecasts are saying today,” or “how the panel-net reacted to last night’s storm,” carries a tone one uses for colleagues. The city’s logic is not top-down; it is negotiated among all these layers.

### The companions that machine and moss provide

Non-human animals that thrive here aren’t just opportunists; many have co-evolved—or been nudged—alongside the city’s functions.

The **rat-like creatures with translucent skin patches** are more than vermin. Their symbiosis with industrial contaminants has made them useful. Their glowing organs and oddly reflective blood serve as moving detectors of certain chemical leaks. In some sectors, they are subtly domesticated: feeders placed near critical transformers encourage colonies to settle where their behavior can be monitored. A sudden exodus of these translucent rats from under a substation’s housing is as much an alarm as a gauge spike.

Likewise the **furred amphibians** thickening along drainage channels. Their metallic flecks are not mere ornament; they incorporate trace materials found in runoff from particular industries. By sampling their skin and eggs, labs can reconstruct a time-lapse of contamination and filtration performance. Some riverward families raise them intentionally in controlled pools, selecting for traits that make them better biomarkers—or, in a few cases, better food.

The **birds** gripping warm vent stacks are scavengers of heat and wire, but they’re also agents in the city’s material flows. Their fondness for metallic nesting materials creates small, mobile caches of conductive waste. A few interface cultivators study them, intrigued by the possibility of training these birds, via shaped perches and subtle electric cues, to help redistribute lightweight scrap to collection points.

Insects here are numerous and specialized. The **gnats tethered to exhaust channels**, the **translucent poly-legged water-skaters**, the **cube-curling glass beads**—they form invisible but essential parts of the city’s housekeeping. Some digest particular biofilms on pipes, preventing dangerous build-up. Others crop newer, experimental mosses before they can choke vents. A failure in these micro-ecologies can result in very human problems: an exhaust pipe fouls, pressure rises, a seam blows, scalding an alley below.

The people have, for the most part, learned to respect these small workers. They tolerate drifts of gnats in certain corridors, knowing their presence means a vent is running within acceptable thermal parameters. They erect modest shelters over favored perches of wire-hoarding birds, both out of practical interest and a kind of quiet fondness.

### The half-seen things in the cracks

Beyond recognizable fauna, the basin harbors residents that don’t fit standard categories.

Some live in the **rust-filigree itself**. Under microscopes, the rust that grows in intricate circuits on certain alloys reveals a structure closer to nervous tissue than random corrosion—branching patterns with consistent ratios, feedback loops that respond to electrical fields. There are technicians who suspect that, across thousands of beams and brackets, a slow, metallic meta-organism is cohering, its timescale so glacial that human observation barely catches the edges.

Then there are the **filaments in the pavement cracks**—those semi-opaque plant-mineral threads that drink runoff. They are not individual plants, strictly speaking, but parts of a city-wide network that appears to be learning the city’s rhythms. When certain districts go through dry spells, the filament density in their cracks thickens in anticipation before the dryness even peaks; when a new industrial process starts leaching a novel compound into gutter flows, filaments downstream alter their internal geometry, as if incorporating that compound.

Whether this network is sentient is a matter of late-shift argument in the undercity bars. What is clear is that it reacts, predicts, adapts, and in doing so has become a silent, low-lying inhabitant in its own right. There are whispers that some interface cultivators have begun to send deliberate signals through it—pulse-patterned irrigations, trace chemical markers—to see if it can be used as a communication medium, a kind of vegetative telegraph.

The **crystalline outcrop** that pierces the tram platform is another node of strangeness. To geologists, it looks like an intrusion of something that isn’t quite mineral in the usual sense—its lattice shows organic irregularities, like a bone that grew under fluctuating conditions. It’s inert to cutting, surprisingly responsive to certain electromagnetic frequencies, and, by all measurements, slowly, very slowly, lengthening. No one lives in it in the conventional fashion, but something lives through it: energy distributes in its depths with patterns that don’t map cleanly to thermal conduction. Some machines in its immediate field behave oddly; condensation on its surface forms and evaporates in cycles not entirely explained by ambient conditions.

Additionally, in the older quarter where the **building wall sweats viscous liquid**, there are microbial consortia that do not match any catalogued biology. They process the wall’s exudate into stratified films, like translucent, flexible nacre. Labs harvest these films for high-performance membranes and optical materials. But in the gaps between sheets, the microbes appear to arrange themselves into circuits, sending biochemical signals back toward the wall. Whatever early experiment produced that structure may have created not just a new material, but a localized intelligence bound to the intersection of masonry and secretion.

These entities have no clear political presence, yet their activities steer the city. A sudden shift in the glassy filaments’ growth can destabilize pavements; a change in wall-seep chemistry can sabotage a production line that relies on it. In subtle ways, the city’s planners are already bending their designs to accommodate, or appease, these non-human residents.

### The ones at the very edges

Out at the basin’s fraying boundaries, where ceramic towers breathe vapor and condensation forests test speculative climates, live the **edgeworkers**.

They are experimenters, yes, but also squatters, pioneers, test subjects. They move into half-formed projects and make them homes before the results are fully known. A tower designed to tune neighborhood humidity might, in its first years, create pockets of peculiar fog that strip heat too fast from skin; edgeworkers learn which nooks to avoid on certain days, mapping the tower’s moods in meatspace while sensors take their own, colder notes.

They build houses that are part lab, part refuge: walls made of test-run composites that may sprout hair-like moisture wicks in winter; roofs of prototype paneling that cycle through color phases connected to internal bioreactors. They accept the risk that a promising façade might fail and begin to crumble into nutrient-rich sludge, forcing relocation or radical repair.

Rail spurs that disappear into fog run past their plots. Edgeworkers ride small, hand-driven carts out along these uncertain lines, scouting where terrain and track have shifted. Sometimes they return with samples—new sediments, unfamiliar sprouts, metallic nodules from a freshly eroded bank. Sometimes they do not return at all, and a future survey will list that spur as abandoned, its last half-kilometer surrendered to a recent slump or a spreading patch of self-propagating composite.

They are not outcasts; they are a necessary vanguard. Urban planners and integration directors depend on their reports, their casualties, their successes. It is in these frayed districts that the city tests whether a new bio-industrial skin can hold up against real fog, whether a condensation forest truly produces enough runoff to feed an added neighborhood, whether a novel substrate-grid actually keeps buildings from slowly migrating downhill.

Children raised in these edge zones grow up with a different sense of “city.” For them, the boundary between built and unbuilt is a daily encounter, not a notion on a map. They watch gullies appear where last week they ran, see power poles end in empty air, hear the deep, soft cough of a distant collapse at night, and know that in the morning someone will arrive with culverts and gabions and geometry to knit the land back into the network.

Some of these children will later move inward, bringing with them a comfort with instability that unsettles more central residents. Others will stay, inheriting terraces of trial evergreens and racks of retired prototypes, living in a museum of what the city tried to be and what it is still becoming.

### The city itself, as a kind of resident

Most of all, what lives here is the city as an organism—made of all these peoples and creatures and materials and flows, but also of patterns that persist even as individual components swap out.

Its “metabolism” runs through components no single inhabitant fully sees: the synchronized cycling of deep pumps and shallow drains; the feedback loops between wall-grown algae and power demand; the slow, responsive creep of supports in soft ground, redirecting stress the way bones remodel under use.

It has what might be called moods. On some days, the mist hangs lower, the pumps cycle slower, condensate drips fall in a more languid rhythm; traffic moves softer, voices carry a softer timbre. On others, after a chain of squalls, there’s a brittle, nervous tension: more sudden clangs from the rail yards, more sharp hisses from pressure releases, more people watching rooflines and embankments with a practiced eye.

The city remembers itself materially. A building demolished in one decade returns as foundation aggregate in another, carrying microscopic legacies of past experiments. A retired rail line becomes embedded in a new stabilization grid, its metal now a substrate for rust-filigree circuits that once powered something else. People walk daily over the literal compressed history of old prototypes and partial successes.

The rules here are, as you noted, provisional contracts between matter and intention. Those contracts are negotiated continuously by everyone and everything that lives in the basin: by the planners drafting contingency maps, by the children re-routing their walk to school around a freshly slumped alley, by the rats abandoning a transformer with tainted coolant, by the filaments in the pavement reorienting themselves toward a new source of condensed water.

So: who lives here?

- Humans who have learned to think in gradients and probabilities rather than in fixed lines.
- Technicians who speak fluently in the dialect where biology and engineering meet.
- Children who trust the mist more than they trust any printed map.
- Edgeworkers who ride rails into fog, accepting that the ground itself is only a suggestion.
- Microfauna and mesofauna adapted to heat leaks, chemical drifts, and the rhythm of pumps.
- Fungal and vegetative networks that thread the city’s cracks, slowly integrating its flows into something like a nervous system.
- Crystalline and rusted structures that may be more than inert surfaces, accumulating pattern and response by degrees.
- A city-mind, not conscious in any single place, but distributed: in panel skins, in root-channels, in data meshes, in the habits of those who move quietly through its damp, unfinished light.

They all live together in this cold, patient basin, in a state that is not yet finished and never intends to be.
