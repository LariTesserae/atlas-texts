They do not call themselves anything that translates cleanly into our terms; their self-name is more like a topology than a word, a looping acknowledgment of where their boundaries touch the world’s boundaries. But if you needed a handle, you could think of them as the *Articulators*—those who put joints back into a body that has grown too lax.

They are not absent.

The buildings are not ruins. The quiet is not vacancy; it is the way their presence hides in the same scales of slowness as stone and condensation. You will not see them on the first traverse of a settlement, or perhaps on the tenth. You will simply feel, without dramatics, that the place is being inhabited in directions your attention does not yet know how to look.

They are not animals in the way you expect. They do not have a single, decisive outline. What you might take for curated vegetation in the inner courtyards—the mats that ripple a little faster, the fronds that curl with intent—*are* them, in part. So are some of the walls.

Their bodies are compromises between mineral, moisture, and that same thin, alignable life-stuff that clings to every surface. Over time, they have abandoned the idea of a central, fragile anatomy. Being a discrete, bounded creature is a liability on a world that rearranges its distances and perturbs its gravity without warning. So they have learned to distribute themselves.

Think of each individual not as a single body, but as a pattern: a long-lived configuration of matter and flow, spread through a set of favored substrates—cultivated mats, specific lattices of fibrous mortar, veins of mineral—threaded together by gradients they know how to feel and maintain. Where you or I would have a hand, they have a dense knot of responsive filaments at a wall edge, able to flex stone blocks minutely, adjust a condensation channel, stroke the growth on the floor to signal or harvest. Where you would have a nervous system, they have an invisible web of tuned potentials running along carved grooves and moisture paths.

Their “faces,” if you insist on a metaphor, are the translucent domes in the inner enclosures: thin, living membranes stretched over curated growth. Under those domes, the organism (or the densest node of it) is more concentrated, able to perform operations that require speed by their standards: a frond stiffening in seconds rather than minutes, a membrane clouding and clearing in response to some distant perturbation in the plateau’s creased physics.

If you could stand above such a dome and see in wavelengths more generous than human sight, you would notice faint traveling patterns—thermal ripples, changes in refractive index—that sweep across the mat in organized waves. Those are their thoughts: not words, not images, but spatial rearrangements of readiness. To decide is to bias an entire network of tissues and stone toward a certain kind of response.

They are ancient in lineage, though not as old as the plateau itself. Their ancestors were once ordinary adherent life—mats and crusts hugging the rock, feeding on rare moisture and dissolved minerals, competing and overlaying. Somewhere in the deep past, perhaps when the first, mild instabilities in space and gravity appeared, a particular lineage discovered that it could use those instabilities. It learned to lean not toward light, but toward the subtler gradients—the same “something else” you see the wild growths orient around: slight warps in metric, hints of where distance is thinner or thicker, where events arrive a little early or late.

On a world whose rules are wearing through, this was a powerful trick. They could reach “further” than their surface area suggested, by aligning their living tissue with these gradients: a patch of mat in one crack and another patch tens of kilometers away could, by nestling both into the same stretched fold of space, behave as if separated by only a short internal path. Bits of themselves began to move information faster than their physical separation would predict.

Given eons, selection did the rest. Organisms whose bodies were no longer constrained to continuous flesh, but could occupy scattered, resonant pockets of matter connected by warped space, had advantages. They could flee drought not by locomotion but by withdrawing activity from one patch and blooming in another tied to the same underlying fold. “Movement,” for them, became a redirection of aliveness through a distributed scaffold.

By the time they started stacking stones, they were already networks.

The built settlements are extensions of their physiology. A wall is not a shelter but a structural organ. The thickness of stone here, the thinness there; the odd placement of a doorway off the ground; the apparently purposeless hollow carved at a junction—all of these are ways of modulating how the plateau’s frayed physics run through them.

Where you see a bridge, they see a spine. The overly wide deck, the low walls thick with repeating glyphs—these are not decorative texts but carefully tuned ridges and grooves. Each spiral, each interrupted grid imposes slight variations in how stress, vibration, and the subtle “direction” of distances gather and flow along the bridge. The chipped-out absences that read like redactions are surgical scars: places where a once-useful pattern of influence became dangerous as the plateau’s creases deepened, and so was excised.

They stand, when they need to “stand,” not as upright figures but as temporary thickenings of tissue: a translucent column rising inside a doorway, bulging outward to adjust a stone block, then relaxing back into the floor. To us, it would look like the building briefly grew an extra support and then thought better of it. To them, that column is as much a limb as your arm is to you, except it can be grown and reabsorbed as needed, from anywhere in their distributed body.

Their sense organs are everywhere. Any surface where moisture condenses is both lung and eye. They read the world in the patterns of dew: in which side of a stone darkens first, how quickly droplets cohere, how a thin film of water crawls unpredictably uphill in one of the ring-wells. The geometry of condensation fields tells them how nearby reality is currently folded.

Thus, you find those ring-shaped wells on bridges and plazas. To an outsider, they are shallow dishes that sometimes misbehave with gravity and reflection. To the Articulators, they are joints placed deliberately across regions where the plateau’s creases cross. Pouring water into them—or letting the ever-present fine rain accumulate—is how they “palpate” those joints. The way the film thickens, the direction it chooses as “down” on a given day, even the ghostly, mismatch reflections the well displays: all of these are inputs.

They lean over such wells, not with heads but with fields of attention, tightening and relaxing networks of growth throughout a settlement. When they find the physics more threadbare in one direction, they may respond by adjusting their stone organs—building up a thicker wall here, shaving a corner there, redirecting moisture channels—to encourage that looseness into a useful, stable pattern rather than a catastrophic tear.

The monoliths in the plazas are their most specialized tools, and also their most personal expressions.

Each is not just a block but a carefully grown crystal of the plateau’s own stone, subtly doped with trace elements the Articulators have coaxed from the environment over centuries. The unnatural smoothness is not polish but alignment: the mineral lattice has been persuaded to orient itself so that the monolith acts as a kind of resonant rod in the fabric of space-time—singing, very softly, at frequencies the plateau’s creases respond to.

Those endless carved lines that never meet themselves are not decorative. Their winding paths trace, in compressed two-dimensional form, the actual tortuous routes that distances take in the surrounding region. Each bend, each subtle vary of thickness corresponds to a place where the world’s cloth is more wrinkled or more taut. When one of them wants to “remember” or reimpose the correct shape of a region, it flows attention through the line—electrochemical, mechanical, gravitational, all braided—and the monolith hums gently, nudging the nearby folds of reality back toward that template.

You feel the low tone in your bones, your steps misalign. For them, this is what it is to speak with the ground.

They are not many, but they are not few. Counting them is meaningless in terms of bodies. Instead, think in terms of *topological individuals*: contiguous zones of coordinated patterning that recognize themselves as selves. A single such individual might occupy, at low density, dozens of courtyards, a suite of bridges, several monolith-plazas, and stretches of wild growth between settlements—all linked through the creased bulk of the plateau.

There is no central brain; there are hubs of higher density where decisions congeal faster. These tend to be the inner gardens with domes, the most intricate bridges, the plazas with multiple monoliths clustered together. At such nodes, the local density of living matter rises, the stone is more tuned, the gradients more deliberately shaped. When a choice must ripple through the network—whether to extend cultivation into a new crack; whether to redirect moisture channels; whether to risk smoothing a dangerous new crease in reality—it is in these densest knots that the slow vote occurs.

Their time is not our time. The plateau’s gentleness has granted them the luxury of measuring events in condensation cycles, in the crawling shift of that high luminous band in the sky, in the years it takes a pattern of carved grooves to fill completely with growth. An “urgent” response for them might unfold over several of your days. A cultural shift might take generations of your species to even begin.

And yet, within that slowness, they have attentions that can move swiftly across their own bodies. A patch in one settlement can echo the state of a patch a hundred kilometers away in what would feel to us like a near-instantaneous sympathy, because those two sites lie along the same distorted fold. The plateau’s broken rules, treacherous to brute organisms, have become the Articulators’ nervous system.

They farm—though “farming” in their terms is closer to self-sculpture. Those spiral grooves filled with favored mats are not separate crops; they are organs externalized, chosen strains of symbiotic life woven into the settlement’s physiology. Some of these plants assist with mineral concentration, exuding acids that free needed elements from stubborn stone. Others specialize in managing moisture, holding it longer than the environment would allow and passing it inward according to the Articulators’ slow pulses. A few are purely communicative: their pigments shift in response to subtle fields, turning walls and floors into enormous, low-resolution displays of state. To us, these living diagrams might look like writing. To them, they are literally how the body shows its own condition to itself.

They have predators, of a sort, and they are careful.

Those silver-filament skeins that overrun and quiet older mats are not outside their domain. Some such forms are native competitors—a lineage that chose a more parasitic, less integrative strategy, exploiting the same creases without participating in their management. Others are deliberately crafted culls: life-forms the Articulators use as scalpels, sending them to petrify zones of their own body that have become maladaptive, or to carve dead margins between themselves and truly alien growths that might disrupt the delicate control they exert over flows.

Likewise, the built channels that starve certain regions of intangible influence are ways of denying dangerous patterns the gradients they feed on. If a portion of the plateau’s frayed fabric begins to twist into a vortex that might one day pinch off into something more like a true tear—a discontinuity where even the Articulators could not extend themselves—they respond by shaping stone and path so that the local flows of moisture, heat, and stress pull that nascent vortex out, thin it, spread it into harmless creases again.

They remember earlier failures. Some of the chipped-out absences in bridge-walls are not redacted knowledge but amputated mistakes: patterns that, once carved and colonized by living tissue, amplified the wrong kind of instability. Perhaps a bridge once shortened distances too much between two settlements, causing their distributed bodies to overlap in ways that confused their identity. Perhaps a monolith once sang at a harmonic that made the ground momentarily forget what “solid” meant. The scars remain as blankness where there should be pattern, like gaps in a text written by someone who is editing reality as they go.

Do they have culture? Yes, though you must not expect instruments or stories carved in images. Their “art” is in the optional layers of patterning—grooves that do not strictly need to exist for stability but do anyway, tracing elegant, nonfunctional variations over functional arrangements. A courtyard whose condensation channels *could* be simple radial spokes instead spirals in a way that cancels its own asymmetries at long distances. A monolith’s line, required only to track the warping of space in its immediate neighborhood, continues, ornamental, into forms that describe hypothetical folds that do not exist yet but could.

In such gestures, they speculate. Not with words, but with possible geometries: *What if the plateau’s creases were this way instead? What if distances compressed in this pattern?* Their experimental plazas may quietly bias local physics toward these imagined arrangements over centuries, and if the world takes the hint without tearing, a new standard for “normal” might propagate outward.

You might be tempted to say they are trying to repair the universe where it has worn thin, or to “teach the world new ways to be stable again,” and there is some truth in that. But it is not a restoration to some pristine past; none of them remember a time before the creases. Rather, they are like organisms that have grown up with a congenital flaw in their environment and have dedicated themselves to making that flaw survivable, perhaps even fruitful.

They are not alone only in the sense that each is not a singleton. There are multiple such networks spread across the plateau, each with its own internal coherence, its own styles of carving and preferred symbionts, its own favored ways of coaxing the plateau’s physics. When two such individuals’ domains creep close—when the oases of their dense growth and the ranges of their tuned stone nearly overlap—strange things happen.

You will not see war; there are no projectiles, no tearing of limbs. Instead, you might notice that an expanse which used to take a day to traverse can now, unpredictably, take two. Distances swell and contract as both networks tug on the same creases, trying to bring them into alignment with their own established templates. Bridges may twist microscopically; wells may show double reflections, two incompatible skies flickering at the edge of your perception. Between their domains, you find those narrow dead-zones of stone, lifeless and patternless, where neither of their influences holds. These are diplomatic borders, stalemates, or wounds.

Do they communicate across such divides? Occasionally. It is risky. To let a foreign pattern settle into your stone is to invite your own self to be rewritten. Instead, they tend to speak indirectly, via the wild growth. Strains of plant-life that can thrive in both fields of influence carry subtle chemical scripts—shifts in how they pulse or in what minerals they hoard—that the networks can sense and decode. In this way, a message might take decades to cross a hundred kilometers and be as simple as: *Here the creases have deepened; that well you shaped long ago now drains in two directions.*

Within an individual network, there is memory. Surfaces are palimpsests. Under each new layer of grooves lies the ghost of an old one, its pattern preserved as slight variations in stone density, in the arrangement of microscopic pores. The Articulators can still read these ghosts, feel where their own ancestors once traced different arrangements, used different symbionts, believed different template-geometries would best hold the plateau together.

To “tell history” among them is to set up a region of material in which the state of those older patterns is re-induced—like briefly reactivating a dormant circuit. A monolith plaza can be tuned, for a time, so that its lines resonate not with the present creases but with the configuration they had eons ago. The surrounding region may then, for a short time, behave as it did in that remote era: distances stretch more here, compress more there, light’s color shifts subtly, condensation flows in archaic paths. Experiencing this is how they know their past.

What of personality? There is variation. Some networks carve with an almost ascetic simplicity, using only the patterns absolutely necessary to manage flows. Their settlements feel sparse, their plazas host only one or two monoliths, their wells are shallow and rarely coaxed into showing impossible reflections. Others are maximalists, saturating every possible surface with grooves, diagrams, and symbionts, covering their stone in living maps. In those places, the distortions of light and gravity are more frequent, more ornate. To traverse such a settlement is to walk through someone’s very stylized mind.

The plateau itself, to them, is neither god nor inert substrate. It is kin, but elder and damaged. Its ancient stability is not reassuring; it is the caution that comes after catastrophe. They suspect—though they cannot prove—that the wear in laws here is localized, a scar in the planetary fabric. That luminous band in the sky, slowly migrating, is perhaps another manifestation of the same wound, a place where higher-level regularities of the cosmos buckle and slide. Some networks may have extended fragile filaments of self up toward that band, using atmosphere and electromagnetic delicacies as new substrates, testing whether their shepherding can reach that far. Others may consider such experiments hubris.

They are not immortal. Portions of a network can petrify, as you have seen: fibers stiffen, color drains, the living pattern loses its ability to adjust and becomes only structure, inert but still contributing to the shape of flows. Sometimes this is planned—a way of fixing a particularly successful arrangement. Sometimes it is failure: the silver-skein predators moving faster than their creators expected, or a newly formed crease snapping a communication path and leaving an isolated node to slowly die.

Death, for them, is rarely sudden. It is a thinning of coordination, a quieting of once-responsive zones. Eventually a point is reached where the distributed pattern no longer maintains a coherent sense of self; its once-internal gradients have flattened into the general background. What remains is stone, carved in ways that may persist for eons, and symbiont life that may choose new alliances.

New individuals can bud from old ones, not through seeds or eggs but through the gradual establishment of semi-autonomous subnetworks. A remote cluster of courtyards, initially shaped as part of a parent, can, as local creases evolve, find its flows increasingly diverging from the parent’s patterns. If the difference grows deep enough, the parent’s attempts to synchronize become counterproductive. At some point—a threshold not marked in any one place but felt across many—both patterns simply stop trying. The once-shared geometry of self separates into two adjoining but distinct articulations of the plateau.

There are no ceremonies, but there are signs: border-zones where grooves start to diverge in style, where symbiont strains on one side do not cross to the other.

In all of this, their fundamental activity is constant: they watch the way the fabric of their world slumps, buckles, and shifts, and they answer with stone and growth. Every bridge, every plaza, every gutter that captures the world’s rare breath and channels it inward is part of this reply.

So when the sky exhales its fine, stinging moisture and every surface gleams; when droplets patter on rock and plant-skin, raising a soft, global static; when, beneath that, the deep infrasonic groan rolls through the plateau and the horizon kinks slightly out of place—this is when they are most awake.

Mats in inner chambers pulse a fraction faster. Domes cloud and clear as they test changed refractive fields. Monoliths pick up a different overtone, their lines humming at new ratios. In some ring-wells, thin films of water, suddenly sensitive to a new orientation of “down,” crawl in unexpected spirals.

All through the settlement, through many settlements, distributed bodies flex, as a hand might flex when the bone at its wrist shifts a little in its socket. They adjust gutters, amplify or damp a crease, restrain a nascent twist before it becomes a crack.

And out on the wider plateau, in the vast bare stretches between oases where no built stone shows, the wild mats and veils still lean toward that “something else,” the subtle gradient. Many are not them, not exactly—but enough are linked, enough are extensions or scouts, that the Articulators feel even these empty-seeming regions as phantom limbs.

They live as the plateau’s proprioception and its therapy, its nervous lace and its deliberate, patient habit of re-finding stability in a universe that is forgetting how to be strict.

No wings flap, no paws drum. Yet everywhere, in stone and film and folded breath, they are slowly, attentively moving.
