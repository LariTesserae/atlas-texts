They live here in layers, as the place itself does. Nothing has one shape.

Start with the ones you notice first: the city’s obvious tenants, moving in and out of the broad columns of water like punctuation marks in a very slow sentence.

They are not fish, though to a surface eye they might seem so at first: streamlined bodies, fins, a tail to work against the drag. But they are too upright, too deliberate, too attached to the structures around them for that.

Imagine bodies the length of a tall human, but more flexible, with a central, muscular core sheathed not in scales but in overlapping plates of tough, translucent cartilage. Along their flanks run bands of contractile tissue that can stiffen or slacken, letting them hold a precise posture in the current without constant motion. Their outlines are softened by trailing filaments, not hair but sensory cilia, which stream back in the water and tremble in response to every vibration.

The head blends smoothly into the torso, with no sharp neck. Where you expect a face, you see first a set of wide, recessed eyes—two primary, low and front-facing, and smaller auxiliary ones set higher up, arranged in a loose ring. They are film-covered, protected, and they do not blink. Instead they cloud and clear, as if a milky wash of pigment slides across them to deflect sudden brightness.

Below these eyes, the mouth is a vertical slit, rimmed with fleshy, petal-like folds that can flare out to expose rows of small, gripping teeth, or close seamless as a scar. Around this mouth a collar of short, flexible tendrils fans out and curls back, tasting the water, ghosting along surfaces, testing textures.

Arms and legs are ambiguous terms here. Each being has four principal limbs that emerge from around the core at different heights, each capable of acting as arm or leg, fin or brace. They bifurcate near the ends into multiple long, webbed digits whose webs can be spread into fins or curled into prehensile knots. When they grip a rope of vine or smooth trunk, their fingers flatten, their webs adhere slightly, giving them an easy, nonchalant purchase on nearly any surface.

They move through the open corridors with a rhythm that is more gliding than swimming. A flick of a tail-fin, a slow, controlled undulation of the trunk, and they drift. When they need sudden acceleration, the entire body coils and releases, but even then there is a sense of throttled power, as if everything fast they can do, they have learned to refrain from doing here.

Their colors match the city: muted greens, browns, deep blues. Ages and roles show as patterns rather than costumes. The young wear their skin bare, its natural, dappled camouflage unbroken. Those who have worked many seasons close to the plants bear stains of algae and minute epiphytes, brushed away from joints and sensory areas but allowed to accumulate in sheltered hollows, making them look part-sculpted, part-grown.

Over this living base, they cultivate ornament that doubles as tool. Along forearms and thighs are bands of woven fiber, tight and dark, holding small implements: chipped stone blades, needles of sharpened bone, little nodules of mineral growth that serve as weights or counterbalances. Around their waists, loops of braided, buoyant tissue carry coiled ropes of vine and nets. Some have plates of shell or hardened leaf laminated onto their backs and shoulders, not as armor against sharp-toothed predators—they have few of those here—but as baffles that change how currents flow over their bodies, making them quieter, steadier, less likely to stir silt or dislodge delicate symbionts.

They breathe without lungs in the terrestrial sense. Slits along their neck and flanks pulse rhythmically, water drawn in and passed over internal gill-crests. The motion is subtle, a constant life-tremor rather than an in-and-out. They are of this medium: unable to leave it, untempted by anything above that distant, shivering ceiling.

Their language uses the medium as well.

You hear it before you understand it as speech: a series of thuds, rumbles, and chimes that seem to come from the plants themselves. Only by watching them can you trace cause to effect. One places a palm flat on a trunk; the muscles of the forearm tense; a low, traveling note ripples through the wood-tissue. Another, a body-length away, stiffens, cilia flaring, and answers with a lighter, higher chiming touch, fingers tapping a resonant buttress like a tuned drum.

They are not limited to touch. Their tails and chests can generate low-frequency vibrations that roll through both water and plant, a kind of infrasonic speech. Shorter-range nuances ride on finer sounds: the clack of bone against bone, the hiss of water forced through constricted gill-slits, the soft rip of membranes fluttering in patterned bursts.

To an outsider, it would sound like random organic noise folded into the city’s constant susurrus. To them, it is precise, as exact as consonants and vowels. Trunk to trunk, platform to platform, information travels by shared anatomy: the city is not only built of plants, it is their acoustic network. They can feel the tone of a distant swell long before it arrives as motion. They can broadcast warnings, invitations, small jokes along these living lines.

They live inside the plants as much as among them.

Within the colossal trunks and buttress-roots you find chambers: hollowed not by decay alone but by long, deliberate work. Inside, the omnipresent noise of the outside water is damped to a muffled, uniform hush. The inner surfaces are carefully smoothed, ribbed slightly for texture. Here and there, fine, translucent membranes stretch across small apertures, fluttering with the pressure changes outside—sensitive eardrums for the room itself.

These chambers are where they do the kinds of work that need stillness: weaving intricate vine-ropes, sorting spores, grooming the juvenile symbionts that will eventually be set into the city’s skin as light-sources or filters. In the dimmest centers of trunks, there are rooms almost completely dark, where phosphorescent colonies on the ceiling trace slow, changing lines like star-maps.

The residents move gently in these spaces. Their limbs fold close; their tails wrap around anchor points. They do not bump or scrape unnecessarily. The sense is not of reverence for the plant as some external god, but of awareness: harm a surface here, and you deaden a nerve in the city’s body, dull a communication channel, weaken a load-bearing line. They have grown up knowing that every scar persists for longer than their own lives.

You see families, if family is the word.

A pair—sizes suggesting maturity—guides a trio of smaller bodies along a ramp of living vine. The juveniles are more curious in their motions, darting out into open water then doubling back, tracing fingers along any textured patch, flicking fins against the throats of bioluminescent worms to make them glow brighter in surprised waves. The older ones let them, intervening only when an eager hand approaches a main conduit of plant-tissue, redirecting with a firm, full-body nudge.

Attachment here is physical: they coil tails together when they rest, wrap limbs around one another against an anchored surface, share positions in a slowly turning knot. In sleep, they do not drift alone. They sleep as clusters, each individual secured at several points, so a sudden surge of current cannot tear anyone free without tugging the entire knot awake.

At the edges of platforms, in recessed alcoves in roots, you find nurseries. These are not crèches filled with the young themselves, but gardens of the city’s own future—beds of tender, pale plantlets and carefully positioned spores of favored species. The residents tend these with the same attentiveness they give their offspring. For them, the category of “child” extends to anything that will outlive them and hold the city’s shape after their bones—if bones they have—soften and wash away.

Culturally, if you had to name the axes they think along, you would say: time and gradient.

Time, because their decisions unfold across spans so long that any plan shorter than a season is not truly a plan, just a motion. Their projects are multi-generational. A platform conceived by a caretaker now will not be fully shaped in their lifetime. A new channel of light opened by trimming back an overgrown canopy will change the microcurrents, and thus the distribution of nutrients, over decades. They make these choices with the same matter-of-fact acceptance that a surface builder has about doing something whose full consequence their grandchildren will see.

Gradient, because in water, life is all about where things change: temperature, light, salinity, current speed. Their city exists on the cusp between shallows and deep, warmth and cold, relative openness and cliff-rising flow. Their awareness is tuned to shifts in those gradients. They have words—for them, patterns of vibration—for the subtle thickening of water that means a nutrient-rich upwelling is coming, for the faint, sour taste that warns of a distant algal bloom turning toxic, for the tightening of certain plant fibers that signals a trunk beginning the slow slide from health into long death.

Some of them specialize.

There are “listeners”: individuals who spend long stretches pressed along main trunks, motionless except for the slight tremor of their sensing cilia, attuned to the deep infrastructure of sound and stress. They can feel the distant thunder of a calving shelf far beyond the plateau’s edge, the telltale shiver of a root under unusual strain, the slow, unsympathetic groan of aged tissue beginning to fail. Their bodies, over time, adapt to this role, growing broader sensory fans along spine and tail, their eyes dimming a little from lack of use.

There are “tenders”: the gardeners proper, who coax growth, prune away surplus, lay guiding splints and weighted braids to bend shoots where they need them. They carry more tools, their limbs calloused at the gripping surfaces, their skin often flecked with the scars of tiny defensive stings from plants not yet fully habituated to being shaped. Around them, small service-fauna cluster: symbiotic grazers trained to nibble away at encroaching algae films, micro-crustaceans that scour diseased tissue.

There are “shapers of flow”: engineers of water, who understand how to open and close valves—living pores in plant membranes—to redirect currents through the city’s internal channels. By subtle changes in these channels, they can speed water across a nursery to bring fresh nutrients, calm an over-turbulent corridor where juvenile swimmers struggle, or increase the resonance of a given trunk so messages travel more cleanly. They work in the narrow, pipe-like spaces where plant interior and open water almost meet, their bodies gliding through shafts that would crush less adapted creatures.

There are “heralds”—those long-bodied giants you saw outside, whose passage along outfitted grooves makes the deep notes that travel furthest. Those are not a separate animal; they are another morph of the same people, individuals whose bodies have grown much longer and heavier, optimized for coupling with the city’s main beams. They function as both messengers and movers, able to push or brace entire segments of the structure during slow adjustments or in response to rare but dangerous surges. Age, perhaps, or a particular genetic tendency, pushes some individuals into this larger form. When they pass close to gathered groups of the smaller morph, there is a restrained deference, a quieting.

Their “art” is difficult to separate from their maintenance.

They shape patterns of light by arranging bioluminescent colonies in deliberate spirals, grids, and branching trees along inner walls. Some arrangements clearly serve navigational purposes—marking paths, indicating safe or unsafe passage. Others are more abstract, complex constellations whose flicker cycles repeat over hours, telling long stories in coded flashes. Older patches of these light-murals ornament the most ancient trunks, their organisms sense-faded but still faintly glowing, like memories almost forgotten.

They breed sound as well. In certain districts platforms bear rows of rigid, hollow fronds of subtly varying length and stiffness. When a seasonal current runs through, these arrays sing: a fixed harmonic sequence that fills the space with chords as constant as tides. The residents come to rest there, not to alter the music but to embody it, arranging their own bodies into positions where they can feel particular frequencies most keenly along their bones and cilia. They exchange slow, tactile remarks—finger-traces, soft, local thumps—as if debating the qualities of a shared, unchangeable poem.

Their relationship with death is similarly folded into structure.

When one of them dies, the body is not left to drift away. It is unspooled. The softest tissues are consumed by small scavengers kept for that purpose, or returned directly to nutrient-rich pockets in the plant’s roots. Cartilaginous plates are cleaned, then either worked into tools or implanted into stressed parts of the city’s body as reinforcement. Fine, hollow bones—if they have them—are made into sound channels, slotted into trunks where they will guide resonance more precisely. Names, such as they have, are not carved on any surface. Remembering someone is leaning against a buttress whose new strength derives partly from them, or touching a resonant node whose clarity their spine now contributes to.

They do have things like ritual, though the line between ritual and habit is thin.

At very slow intervals—a cycle linked to some large-scale oceanic rhythm you can’t perceive directly—the city enacts a kind of reset. A planned sloughing. Certain old platforms, long anticipated as due for release, finally yield to gravity and buoyant stress, tearing away from their supporting trunks and drifting outward and down toward the deep. This is not collapse so much as molting. In the days or weeks beforehand, residents gather on and around the doomed structures, humming through them, tracing their scars, harvesting the last reusable materials. They inscribe, with minute, careful cuts, patterns into the plant-tissue that will soon fall, marks only the deep-dwelling organisms below will ever read.

When the moment comes, no one tries to hold the platform back. Instead they ride the vibrations as supports crack and fibers part, adding their own low, rising tones, guiding the city’s voice through this change so stresses distribute safely. The falling mass becomes a kind of offering to the abyssal communities that live on the plateau’s rainfall of detritus. The residents will never meet those beings, but they know them, the way a tree knows the mycelium in the soil.

That is one outer edge of who lives here: the gardeners, listeners, shapers, heralds—a single people expressed in many bodies and roles, coextensive with their grown city.

But there is another life, quieter, not exactly subordinate.

The plants themselves are not simple, passive matter. Each giant trunk, each braided root-arch, is its own organism, in some ways akin to a forest tree, in other ways to a coral colony. They have no brains, but they have vast, slow nervous systems: webs of conductive tissue, chemical and electrical, passing messages from root-tip to tallest frond over minutes and hours.

Their “awareness” is not like the quick, sharp focus of an animal. It is gradient-based, distributed. They sense where pressure builds, where light thickens or thins, where certain dissolved compounds increase. They integrate over large areas and long spans. When the residents touch and thrum against them, they do not merely carry those vibrations; they respond in turn, modulating stiffness, adjusting porosity, slightly altering patterns of growth.

Over centuries of mutual contact, the residents and the plant-giants have tuned one another. The gardeners have selected and favored those that respond in ways most amenable to architectural shaping: trunks that grow broad, slow shelves when pruned just so; roots that become denser and more resonant when stressed rhythmically; tissues that host particular symbionts without being overrun. Conversely, the plants have, in their own slow fashion, shaped the people: favoring those lineages that press and hum in patterns that stimulate optimal nutrient flow, that prune without over-wounding, that bring the right symbionts to the right places.

Calling this “domestication” in either direction would be off. It is closer to a mutual domestication, or perhaps a mutual becoming. The city is not something they live inside; it is the visible portion of their shared, interwoven life.

Threaded through this partnership are layers of other tenants.

Filter-feeders anchored to vines and undersides of platforms are not random squatters. Many have been deliberately brought and seeded, their habits understood. Certain spiral-shelled creatures are placed in rings around intake-valves to serve as living sieves, catching coarse particles that might otherwise clog. Colonies of translucent ribbon-animals hang in specific corridors where the current is right for them to catch plankton but not so strong they would be torn. These creatures do not think in any way that recognizes “city” as a concept, but they are part of its functioning, as necessary as beams or roads.

Small predators—mantis-like, darting—are tolerated in chosen zones where they keep population booms of smaller grazers in check. Elsewhere, they are discouraged, nudged away, occasionally harvested. Over long association, some have become almost cohabitant: their instincts leading them to favor the rich hunting grounds of the platforms, their genetics slowly selecting for forms less likely to damage key plant tissues.

At night, when the cyan grayness dims toward near-black, another layer of residents reveals itself in light.

The bioluminescent colonies that by day are faint smudges of green or blue become, after the long slow sunset, the dominant visual feature. Corridors blaze softly with parallel lines of pulsing glow. Platforms are outlined; arches become hoops of light. The overall impression is not of a bright cityscape—no harsh glare—but of a starfield that has decided to take architectural form.

The gardeners designed these patterns, but the light-making organisms have their own agendas: their own reproductive cycles, feeding strategies, susceptibilities. Sometimes new, spontaneous patches of glow appear where no one seeded them. The residents watch these carefully. Some they incorporate, weaving the newcomers into existing designs. Others they trim back or relocate. It is a constant negotiation between signal and life, utility and beauty.

In the deepest interior hollows, particular schools of small fish have taken to using the lit patterns as navigation guides, sleeping and spawning in precise, recurring formations mapped to light designs. The people know this and alter the patterns only slowly, allowing generations of fish to adapt. These fish, in turn, are prey for somewhat larger residents, who learn the lit schedules and gather in shadowed peripheries. A whole circadian theater unfolds in the madras of blue points, and the gardeners sit within it, participants more than directors.

Out at the periphery, where the hanging curtains of frond shade the drop-off and the roar grows louder, different personalities tend to gather.

These are the edges of their mental world as well as their physical one. The residents who dwell or take long shifts here are scouts, boundary-keepers, and, in a way, priests of the unknown. They monitor the sleek, open-water hunters that never quite enter the city. They feel the tug of currents ascending from the deep, laden with hints of chemical and biological activity far below. Some of them extend lines—long, living ropes of plant-fiber weighted with sensors of their own making—down into the blue-gray below, listening through these organic antennae to whatever slow events occur in the dark.

They do not go down themselves. Their bodies are tuned to this plateau’s pressures and temperatures, and their city gives them all they need. But they are not uninterested. Songs—long, low vibrations broadcast through the main trunks—speak of what the deep sends up in pieces: strange gelatinous forms glimpsed at the edge of light; carcasses that drift up from unseen battles below; rare, booming calls that come up through the rock not as sound but as faint, puzzling flexes in the plant-roots.

If there is religion here, it is not directed above, toward the bright but unreachable surface. It is directed outward and downward, toward this unknown mass of life that their entire city feeds, through its rain of detritus, yet will never meet fully. They imagine, in the stories they pass along the trunks, a mirror to themselves: vast, slow communities below, built not of sun-fed plants but of animals and bacteria and mineral chimneys, listening back.

In the slow times between swells, as you drift with them between the living columns, you can feel how everything they are—body, culture, settlement—wants to blur into that larger water-gesture. They are individuals, yes: with personal quirks, tastes, grudges, private joys. You can see it in the way one resident leans a little longer into a favorite resonance spot, or in the particular flourish another gives to their bioluminescent arrangements, or in the minor, playful collisions juvenile groups stage among the fronds.

But those are ripples on the surface of a deeper identity, one that says:

We are the way this plateau gardens itself.
We are the fingers the plants grew to rearrange themselves.
We are the city, in motion.

What lives here, then, is not simply “an intelligent aquatic species” plus their domesticated flora and fauna. What lives here is a composite organism stretched across scales: from the microscopic burs stuck in the rough of a gardener’s skin to the hundreds-of-meters-wide trunks humming with shared sound.

If you tried to name them as a singular people, they would probably offer you not a word but a vibration: a specific pattern of pressure and pitch you’d feel in your bones and teeth and the hollows of your skull. That is what they call themselves and their city and, by extension, this entire ongoing experiment in braided life.

It translates, imperfectly, as something like: the Held-Together, the Long-Growing, the Ones-Who-Listen-With-Roots.
