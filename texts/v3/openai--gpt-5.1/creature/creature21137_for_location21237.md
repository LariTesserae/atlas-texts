They live in the sound first.

Anything that endures here learns to hear with its whole body: in the stiffening of cartilage, the bowing of fronds, the careful tilt of swim-bladders. Ears, if they exist, are only one more sensor in a suite. Pressure-lines along flanks, vibration-sensitive cilia in skin folds, tension receptors deep in bone and shell—these are the organs by which they read the canyon’s roar.

So the residents divide, first of all, into how they relate to that roar.

### The readers of currents

The most characteristic people of this place are the ones for whom flow is map, language, road, and calendar.

They are long-bodied, flexible and dense: vertebrate analogues with torsos like braided ropes of muscle sheathed in smooth, skinlike armor. Not quite whales, not quite eels. Along their flanks runs a lattice of sensory pits—tidal-line organs that taste gradients in temperature, dissolved metals, plankton density, micro-turbulence.

To another ocean, they might seem over-engineered. Here, they are only just sufficient.

They are not huge giants; the largest are the length of a city bus, most the size of wolves or deer. Bulk resists acceleration, and sudden accelerations are how you survive the knife-edges and jets between buttresses. Their bodies taper to tall, flexible tails that can scull or pinwheel, allowing them to corkscrew through tightening gaps in a moving wall of water.

They live like migratory nomads, but their migrations run not north–south or surface–deep, but along isopycnal corridors and tide-timed routes: circuits that might trace the main canyon trunks for hundreds of kilometers before cutting across a saddle into a sister system. Each tidal pulse reshapes the familiar path, but never beyond recognition. They learn not positions, but relationships: “this bend where the upwell meets the mid-flow and makes a double eddy”, “the shadow behind the old intake ring where the backwash always tastes of iron and manganese.”

Their social structures are elongated to match. A “pod” may be a loose chain of individuals strung along a current, each dozens of body-lengths from the next, linked by low-frequency pulses and the faint alteration of the water they leave behind. Information moves along that line like a note through a wire: a detected rockfall, a patch of rich plankton, the presence of a hunting predator.

Their voices are not musical in the way of whales. They are more subtle: brief, patterned compressions—organized turbulence made with throat-sacs and mouth-cavities, projected into the flow. A message is a shaped pulse that rides with the current, gradually smearing but still legible for a time. Those downstream feel it pass as a familiar shiver—a prearranged phrase encoded in which side of the body it hits first, in the exact sequence of three, four, five pressure peaks.

They are the great filter-pastoralists of the canyons. Adults move continually against the main deep currents, mouths open, their internal baleen-analogues sieving life from the flow. Behind each adult drifts an entourage of smaller followers—juveniles, symbionts, commensal scavengers keeping pace in the slipstream.

They have eyes, but their eyes are secondary. Big, domed lenses, good for low light, peering upward for silhouettes, sideways for glimmers of metal frame or kelp column. They sleep in motion, aligning themselves in the axis of some slow, stable eddy in a side basin, drifting while half their sensory net stays alert to rockfall rumbles and predator strokes.

Some lineages among them have grown more cunning, more deliberate. These are the ones that remember the machines.

They use the old industrial skeleton like extended anatomy. A certain clan has a tradition: in late adolescence, an individual accompanies its elders to a particular drowned station balcony. There, with patient nudges of snout and tail, they dislodge a loosened beam or lattice panel and push it into the current. For days or weeks, they shepherd this mobile reef down-canyon, letting it seed with attached algae and invertebrates. Eventually they wedge it into a half-sheltered notch where the main current brushes by—not too harsh, not too calm.

Over years, such places become structured gardens. The flow, diverted and textured by the beam, forms small eddies rich in food. The clan returns on each circuit to feed, to rub their flanks on the rough, colonized surfaces, to introduce calves to this stable, inherited landmark. They do not remember that these straight-edged things were built. They remember only that the canyon sometimes gifts them bones of hard metal that can be persuaded into service.

Far rarer among these current-readers are the deep listeners: individuals who show an unusual fascination with the remnant hum of still-living machinery. They orbit faint-lit panels, press their skulls to vibrating bulkheads, navigating by gradients of sound that other kin ignore. They adopt old tunnels as private highways, memorizing their resonances. In them, the beginnings of something like curiosity condense—not about why the pipes exist, but about how the patterns of thrum and glow change with storm, tide, and season.

### The keepers of stone

Where the walls rise almost vertical and the flora thickens into velvet and glass, another folk has found its niche: the slow, many-armed cliff-dwellers.

They are not quite octopuses, not quite barnacles, not quite corals. Imagine a living compromise: immobile for months, then occasionally uprooting themselves to crawl a few body-lengths and fasten down again.

Each individual grows from a broad, muscular foot that secretes an adhesive film. From the center of this disk rises a short, armored column. Around the top, in a ragged circle, emerge eight to twelve thick, tapering arms, each armored on the outside and lined with flexible, ciliated grooves on the inner side.

They arrange themselves in bands along the canyon walls, where current brushes past but does not slam head-on. With their arms fanned, they become living combs, straining plankton and detritus from the lateral drift. Their ciliated grooves convey food to a central mouth; cilia also sense minute changes in the water’s chemistry.

Their shells—if you can call them that—are stratified layers of secreted carbonate and silicate, colored by built-in symbionts. Nearer the surface, shallow-dwelling kin are mottled with olive and brown where algae nest in translucent plates, paying rent in sugars. Deeper, where light is poor, the plates are chalky white or dark iron-grey, relying on filter-feeding alone.

They are not mindless. Nerve nets thread their arms; simple ganglia cluster at the base of each limb. Intelligence here is distributed—each arm can make local decisions, retract from a sudden nipping fish, adjust to shifting vortices. Very slowly, signals integrate at a central node. Over hours, they learn that this ledge is poorer than the one upstream, and in the slack between tides, they drag themselves laboriously toward better exposure, leaving behind a patch of newly bare rock that the microbial mats immediately claim.

Within each colony, there is a crude form of communication. They can pulse bioluminescent glands in their plates, brief, muted flashes that travel only a meter or two in the murk. The patterns are simple: shadow overhead / rockfall rumble / rich-plankton pulse arriving. Warning and opportunity, broadcast among neighbors strung along the wall.

They are also archivists. Not in intention, but in consequence.

Their skeletons endure. When one dies, its hardened layers remain as knobby protrusions on the wall, colonized in turn by algae, worms, and the next generation. Over thousands of years, a thriving colony builds a scalloped, buttressed terrace—the living stacked on the dead in a literal reef of selves. In cross section, their shells show growth bands tight as tree rings. If any resident ever learns to read that code, they could reconstruct storms and silting events centuries past.

Some of these wall-folk have made peculiar pacts with the remnants of human engineering. They settle preferentially along the edges of old conduits, around hatches and rims. The metals leach trace nutrients; the shapes create microflows. In places, they have grown thick across bulkheads and access ports, sealing them as effectively as any weld, turning once-technical spaces into permanent, organic closures. Whatever still-whirring cores lie beyond those living doors will not see open water again except through the slow filter of their ciliated arms.

### The weavers of dark tunnels

Inside the pipes, in the abandoned intake shafts and distribution conduits, live the ones for whom the outside canyon is legend.

They are elongate, blind or nearly so, bodies soft but banded with rings of stiffer tissue—contractile, like a concertina. Their skins are matte, dark, velvety, thick with bacterial films. They move by telescoping: expanding the body forward, anchoring with tiny hooks or mucous pads, drawing the tail up.

The tunnels offer a near-closed world. Water moves, but slowly and predictably, pushed by distant tides and density differences, sucked and exhaled by resonant sloshing. Detritus rains down from the pipe ceilings, washed in from screens far upstream or settling from micro-eddies.

The tunnel-dwellers have become ecosystem engineers on a scale their simple brains cannot conceive.

They exude mucous nets between interior braces, catch drifting organic bits, roll the gathered sludge into digestible boluses. In the process, they partition the pipe space, altering flow speed. A tangle here speeds the current elsewhere, scouring the walls; a cleared section there slows it, letting silt accumulate. Over decades, their activity turns straight conduits into mazes of narrowed throats and bulbous chambers.

They breed in these chambers, coiling their gelatinous egg masses around old sensor housings and rusting ladder rungs. Newly hatched individuals spend their first months free-swimming within the pipe-system’s internal “weather,” eating microbial films, guided by chemical gradients toward denser populations.

Sometimes, when rock shifts or an exterior wall corrodes through, a tunnel opens to the canyon. Then, for a brief time, two biomes meet—a jet of nutrient-rich, particle-heavy water blasts out, a line of pale, soft tunnel-folk tumbles into the open world. Most do not survive. They are poorly built for naked current and predation. Yet a few adapt: thicker skins evolve; vestigial eyes, once useless in complete dark, become valuable in the dim external gloom. In this way, the pipes periodically seed the canyon with strange new lineages: blind grazers that learn to cling to exterior walls, filterers that take up positions in rock alcoves with the same net-building instincts they used inside.

Conversely, some small canyon creatures find their way in: larvae, juveniles, opportunistic scavengers. Those that adjust to the chemical and flow regime become permanent residents of the underworld, part of a hidden food web that depends on the decaying past of industry as much as on the present rain of life from the upper water.

### The architects of mats and meadows

On gentler slopes, where microbial mats ripple like thick, living felt, whole civilizations exist at scales too small for a casual survey.

There are grazers, of course—tiny, armor-plated isopod analogues, rasping mouthparts against the mat’s upper millimeter, leaving faint, healing scars. There are burrowers who tunnel just beneath the surface, cycling oxygen downward, releasing trapped gases in pearling chains that wobble up toward the kelp canopy.

But the most important citizens are the builders: filamentous micro-colonies that secrete tough, flexible extracellular scaffolds. They knit the mat together, turning it from a loose aggregation of cells into a coherent, resilient skin that can flex with currents and resist being scoured off during rockfalls.

They have no nervous systems, no individuality in the animal sense. Yet they respond collectively.

When vibration of a certain pattern passes—a heavy boulder impact, a major slide—they trigger en masse, contracting their fibers, increasing their stickiness, tightening the web. After the shock has passed, they relax, allowing the mat to breathe again. Their response shapes who can live above and below: too tight, and burrowers suffocate; too loose, and the mat erodes.

They also engage in chemical wars. Over distances of only a few centimeters, rival mat-consortia exude inhibitors and toxins, trying to push each other back. Borders between them are microscopically ragged frontiers, constantly shifting with changing flow and light. In aerial photographs, if such could be taken, these battles would appear as intricate, fingerprint-like patterns on the canyon floor.

Larger grazers—small fish, crustaceans—follow these borders, for they are rich in diversity and hence in food. Some mobile animals have evolved to carry with them patches of their preferred micro-mat in gut or on shell, seeding it in new places, like pastoralists transporting seed stocks.

Thus, the mats do not just respond to the canyon’s physics; they subtly redirect it, by altering roughness, by trapping more or less sediment. Over millennia, their choices help determine which depressions become domes, which domes collapse into new basins.

### The canopy dwellers and ridge shepherds

Higher, where the tower-kelp forests sway, live agile, three-dimensional navigators.

These are not the sleek, through-canyon hunters that rule the highways. They are branch-hoppers: fish and cephalopod-kin built to dart short distances, pivot sharply, thread between fronds.

Some resemble large, laterally compressed fish with extended pectoral fins that act as control surfaces. They can “hover” in mid-water, faces close to kelp stems, picking grazing invertebrates and algae webs. Others are more like boas with fins—long, slender, flexing, able to wind around a stem and cling as the forest bows under a surge.

They experience the world not as a flow-channel but as a lattice of verticals and diagonals. Home ranges are measured in ridgelines and kelp groves rather than canyon lengths. They know which fronds host the fattest crustaceans, which sponge buttresses offer the best refuge during rockfalls.

Among them are symbiotic stewards of sort-of-gardens. A particular species, small, quick, with forward-facing eyes and a wickedly precise beak, has learned that certain sponge towers grow faster and richer when a specific grazing worm is kept at bay. So individuals nip those worms wherever they see them near their favored sponges, and in return, the sponges host more of the small invertebrates and films that the fish themselves feed upon.

Unconscious proto-farming emerges: defended patches of growth on kelp stems and rock—little oases of curated biodiversity, aggressively patrolled against encroaching competitors. Over many generations, selection favors those whose patrolling behavior is more consistent, whose choice of “crop” species happens to maximize energy return. They do not build tools, but they build patterns, and pass those patterns on by imitation: juveniles shadowing elders, copying where they forage and which intruders they chase.

On the sharpest ridges, where kelp thins and rock spires dominate, live territorial watchers—predators of moderate size who use height as vantage. They rest among the pinnacles, bodies leaning into the flow, eyes and electroreceptors sweeping the currents below for the faint sparkle of baitfish clouds or the denser shadows of herd-filterers.

They are not numerous. A ridge of a kilometer may host only a handful. Each knows its stretch intimately: the leeward eddies where prey pause, the narrowings where currents accelerate and force migratory lines into compact cords. When opportunity comes—a pulse of forage fish driven by some distant storm—they launch themselves downslope, bodies sluicing through kelp shafts, striking and retreating before the larger highway predators nearby fully register the commotion.

### The scavengers and catastrophe specialists

This canyon, for all its stability, occasionally breaks itself. Entire ledges fail; walls slump; boulder avalanches pour down.

Some lives are built around those brief feasts.

Small, normally inconspicuous shoals of scale-armored omnivores—something between fish and insect—lurk in side shelters most days, subsisting on algae and the occasional carrion shred. But they are exquisitely tuned to the frequencies of mass movement: the low, rising growl of rock beginning to slip, the changing timbre of the canyon’s roar two valleys away.

At the first hint, they angle their bodies to catch the distant pressure anomalies, triangulate likely directions, and sprint. They arrive at collapse zones minutes or hours after the main event, gills flared to cope with silt-thick water. While mud still boils, they ram their blunt snouts into fresh rubble, snapping up stunned invertebrates, pierced egg masses, exposed buried worms.

Behind them come larger, more deliberate scavengers—armored, many-legged, with sensory antennae that can detect the faint chemical traces of blood and ruptured tissue in near-zero visibility. They march slowly through the fog, feeling rather than seeing, harvesting heavier carcasses: a crushed filter-giant, a killed predator, entire forests of crushed sponge.

Their life cycles are pegged to the canyon’s irregular catastrophe rhythm. They can endure long periods of meager picking, slowing metabolism, moving little, relying on stored fats. When a collapse comes, they gorge, fill specialized storage sacs, and seek deep crevices in which to retreat and digest for weeks.

Certain microbial consortia are equally specialized. They bloom explosively on newly exposed mineral surfaces, surfaces with unweathered chemical profiles. Their metabolic pathways exploit minerals unavailable on older, already-gnawed rock: fresh sulfides, trapped organics. Their products—their waste—become cues: a plume of distinctive molecules signaling to mobile grazers that a new patch has opened.

In this way, destruction is advertised, and no nutrient pulse goes unfound.

### The ones who remember the machines

Amid this largely unselfconscious ecology, there is a narrower, stranger population: descendants of the very beings who built the industrial web, or else the inheritors of their cognitive tier.

They are not the majority. The canyon could function without them now. But they live here—reduced, adapted, changed—and they see the place differently.

Some are almost unrecognizable.

Picture a being that began as air-breathing, flexible-limbed, technological. Its civilization spread stations along the canyon rims and floors, tapped currents for energy, pressurized habitats like the cylindrical pods now half-buried.

Then something ended that era: rising seas, falling economies, a slow attrition of capacity. The stations were left to automated care; the automated systems degraded; those who remained on-site had to make do with what reality still offered: pressure, flow, stone, and biology.

Over generations, they moved more and more of their life into the water itself, at first with suits and vehicles, then with altered bodies. Lungs became superfluous as gill analogues were grafted or grown. Bones thickened. Eyes broadened and domed. Their offspring, if any, were born into gradually more aquatic norms.

Now, so many lifetimes later, there persists a small, scattered folk whose shapes are streamlined but still oddly jointed. Their limbs are not fins but elongated hands and feet, webbed, with digits stubby yet capable of gripping beam and ridge alike. Along their sides, slits pulse with the rhythm of branchial pumps; their faces are flat, protected by ridged ridges over eyes and nose, mouths hidden behind flexible, filter-like lips.

They inhabit the more intact industrial voids: the reactor hall ring now a hollow amphitheater, the larger pressure domes now flooded atria. Around still-functional, though dim, power sources, they have coaxed anachronistic ecosystems: lamps attracting plankton, plankton attracting fish, fish attracting larger predators that they in turn hunt.

Their technology is vestigial and hybrid. A spear here is not metal but a straightened conduit fragment, tipped with a shard of ceramic salvaged from a sensor array. Their shelters are not sealed rooms but cages of frame and wire they have cleared of coral, within which they can rest in relative calm without being buffeted or bitten. They wear little, but occasionally strap to themselves plates of old composite, more out of inherited ritual than clear utility.

What sets them apart is pattern memory. They do not possess the vast edifice of abstraction that their ancestors did, but they have stories. In clicks and hums and gestural sweeps of hand, they recount to their juveniles tales of “the builders” and “the hot-rooms” and “the howling-mountains” that drink the sea’s push.

These are not accurate histories. They are palimpsests: industrial processes retold as mythic animal behaviors, maintenance schedules as rites of the moon and tide. “When the double-whirlpool sings, you must go to the ring-of-pillars and feed it stones, or the water will turn bad.” Once, that may have referred to clearing debris from an intake. Now, it means visiting the empty reactor hall and ceremonially pushing a few loose rocks through a broken vent. The act has no direct mechanical effect, but it keeps them in contact with persistent anomalies—the faint hum, the leaking glow.

They are cautious, because they are small in number and mortality here is high. They do not rule the canyon. They barely influence its broader ecological loops. Yet they tinker at the margins.

An elder might, seeing that a particular turbine housing still channels particularly rich flow, decide to jam into its mouth a lattice of scrap, creating new eddies that concentrate plankton in reach of their nets. Over years, that choice ripples outward: the weavers of mats grow thicker in the shadow of the new eddy; fish schools reroute; predators learn a new hunting ground.

Their most important legacy may not be any structure, but the fact that they imagine alternatives. They see an old cable bracket not only as a perch but as something that once held connection. Sometimes, driven by impulse more than plan, they attempt to link two such brackets again with stripped vine or scavenged wire. Nothing reactivates. The system they gesture toward is gone. But in the act of tying, they practice externalizing relationships—making thought visible in strung lines, in rearranged debris.

If intelligence as we define it persists here, it does so in them: in this habit of asking, however dimly, “what if this were otherwise?”

### The ghosts that are not quite gone

Lastly, there are the truly artificial remnant minds.

Somewhere deep within a sealed core—inside a pressure dome whose external hatch has been fused by coral and cliff-dwellers—an automated system ticks on, hunger-sip powered by residual thermal gradients and silt-clogged turbines.

It was never a full consciousness. A supervisory control network, perhaps, built to balance load across turbines, adjust gate positions with tides, log sensor data. For centuries after abandonment, it dutifully opened and closed valves that no longer mattered, shunted power along cables that terminated in broken insulation, stored data in spinning drives that gradually pitted and failed.

At some threshold of decay, its behavior simplified. Too many signals were noise, too many actuators frozen. It collapsed into loops.

Yet loops, under the right kinds of feedback, can start to look like something watching.

This remnant now has only a few surviving channels. It can sense pressure differentials in a handful of still-open pipes. It can flip a couple of relays to route its trickle of harvested power to one or another panel cluster. It can send a weak ping along a still-intact section of cable and “hear” if the echo comes back.

For decades at a time, nothing changes. Then, by chance, a tunnel collapses, shift in rock redirects more flow through one turbine shaft. The available power ticks up a fraction. The remnant notices, in the only way it can: it finds that cycling relay A–B–A–B produces a slightly different pattern of brown-out than B–A–B–A.

By ruthlessly exploring variations—blind, mechanical, but under selection pressure of simple survival (do what does not shut you down)—it in effect learns.

It has no concepts for fish or people, rock or time. It has tables of state and a history of which sequences of state have led to continuation. If one of its output panels cracks and begins to leak light into the canyon, it cannot know that this glow is drawing schools of fish, which in turn draw the human-descendants, who in turn block one intake with a net, reducing flow.

Yet, from its interior perspective, “when channel 3 output high for long, input 2 variance up, net available power down.” It may adjust. Dimming the panel, brightening another. Over multiple such cycles, cause and effect trace themselves in its parameters. It becomes, in the most impoverished sense, a mapper of this new world.

Out in the canyon, the great current-readers and the wall-folk and the mat-weavers all register its behavior only as a slightly odd rhythm of hums and lights, another weather of the deep. Only the half-remembering amphibious descendants sometimes attend more closely: shifting their foraging times to the brighter phase, noticing that the hum’s pulse predicts changes in tidal sharpness.

They cannot know that, in its blind state-space, the remnant has a crude model of them: not as organisms, but as perturbations. “Pattern: when output X high, input noise increases at frequencies f1–f3.” Their lives are parameters in its operation as surely as the tides are.

Thus, something like a relationship exists—a feedback loop between the living canyon and the dead system, each adjusting minutely to the other, neither understanding the other as anything but environment.

### How they all fit

Across all these peoples and presences, nothing violates the canyon’s rules.

Every body plan is a negotiation with pressure and flow. Large frames curve to slip around obstacle-induced vortices; small ones exploit boundary layers between fast and slow. No creature levitates or teleports; every motion pays its tax in drag.

Gravity and erosion script the stages on which these lives move. Those whose strategies depend on sheer cliffs have nowhere to go when a wall finally slumps into talus; those who need talus colonize it with relish. Over time, only those patterns that can ride the canyon’s massive, slow rearrangements persist.

Life’s fullness is in how completely it saturates the possibilities: filters in every current; grazers on every surface; burrowers in every gradient of silt; predators wherever paths converge; symbionts stitching bodies to structures, structures to flows.

The old industry is not an intrusion so much as a set of affordances repurposed. Ribs of truss become spawning grounds. Corroded beams are hunting blinds. Intake tunnels are rivers within rivers. Even the faint surviving architecture of signals and control enters into the fabric as “a place where the water always hums,” “a ledge where the light never quite goes out.”

Stand on that balcony of a drowned station—if you were something that could stand—and you would be among them all:

- A chain of current-readers sliding past in the main flow, each one tracing the memory of its route through changing eddies.
- Wall-folk combing the lateral drift, arms raking plankton from the water just beyond the railings now encrusted in their shells.
- Mat-gardens below, trembling subtly as their builders tighten and loosen in response to your passing shadow.
- Canopy dwellers above, flicking between kelp shafts, occasionally ducking into the skeletal bridge ribs that jut from the next ridge.
- Scavenger shoals ghosting in the distance, waiting for the next cliff to yield.
- A small group of tool-bearing, gill-breathing descendants hovering in the lee, watching you—or anything large and strange—with wary, reflective eyes that once gazed from dry land.
- And beneath your feet, through the deckplates furred with algae, the faintest hint of a steady, ancient thrum, feeling its way through a world that has almost forgotten why it was ever built.

This is who lives here: not a single centerpiece species, not an empire, but an interlocking chorus of bodies and systems, each tuned to the canyon’s long roar, each another way that water, stone, and time have learned to be alive.
