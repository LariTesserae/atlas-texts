They are not residents in the way you expect, because the place is not “habitable” in the ordinary sense. The place is a body, and most of what lives here are its organs, its flora, its bacteria, its unfinished thoughts. But if you insist on asking who lives here, something answers — slowly.

Start with the most obvious inhabitants: the not-quite-plants.

They are not derived from sunlight or leaves or green. They are condensed gradients, systems that discovered that folding and branching is a good way to expose as much of themselves as possible to whatever faint differences permeate this heavy water. They grew like algorithms, not like seeds.

Some of them are collectors. The filamentous nets, the frilled pads, the thin ribbons that seem to orient toward no recognizable source — those are organs of sampling. Each strand is riddled with microscopic pores that drink from the water with disciplined selectivity. They accumulate trace imbalances: slightly more of this kind of dissolved potential, slightly less of that. They do not eat food; they balance equations.

Over long stretches of time, these collectors have learned that certain balances correspond to the faint, almost-imperceptible call from the hollow sphere at the chamber’s heart. They are tuned not to light or heat but to the specific pattern of strain and relief that pulses from that small, empty well. They lean into it the way plants lean toward the sun, except the “sun” here is a difference in the way reality itself is folded.

Other plant-bodies are storers. Thickened cords, bulges, bead-like nodules that look as if they were meant to hold air but do not: these are reservoirs of remembered states. When the environment passes through a particular arrangement — a certain ratio of dissolved substances, a certain viscosity, a particular quality of not-quite-sound from the central void — the storers change, very slightly. Their inner gels reconfigure. Their surfaces crystallize micro-patterns of hardness and softness. They accumulate a physical record of all the ways this place has ever been.

Most of what you’d call “memory” here is not held by thinking minds but in these plant-reservoirs, layered in slow, chemical palimpsests. The world is young, but already it is carrying a detailed archive of its own becoming, pressed into living tissue in codes too slow to decipher without staying for centuries.

Then there are exchangers: structures that look like plants wrapped around rocks, or rocks encased in plant rinds. Often, what you take for inert mineral is a scaffold of crystallized byproduct, extruded deliberately by these exchangers to regulate flows in and out of the pocket-world. They participate in reaction chains that do not stop at chemistry. Certain branching nodes, where roots and blade-like forms interlace around protruding shards, are literal interfaces between this bubble and the less-stable ocean outside.

At these nodes, matter is not simply dissolved or deposited; probabilities are brokered. Paths that would lead out into the smeared, overlapping realms beyond are thickened or thinned. The exchangers “decide,” on a scale of aeons, how porous the boundary of this chamber is allowed to be. They are the immune system of the place.

Moving among this vegetal density are the few, unlikely animals.

Most are tactile ghosts. The elongated body that drifts past you, feeling its way with ciliary shivers, is one of a family of “surveyors.” They are not predators; they are contact organs for the environment itself. Their skins are dense with receptors that register tiny inconsistencies in texture, pressure, and whatever passes for charge here. As they glide, they compare how things are now to how they have been, using the slow-encoded “memories” embedded in their own tissues.

They leave behind microscopic trails of altered viscosity and altered probability — faint, ribbon-like zones where the usual rules are slightly loosened or slightly tightened. Plants grow differently along these paths, bending or thickening, incorporating the surveyors’ impressions into their own structures. In this way, a wandering animal’s unhurried route becomes a mapping operation, and the environment, in turn, rewrites itself around that map.

The many-legged creature with funnel-tipped limbs is a “drinker,” but not of nutrients alone. Its funnels are mouths for textures: they pull in the mechanical signature of whatever they cling to — sharpness, brittleness, flexibility, grain — and reprocess it internally. The substance they exude again, invisibly thin, can alter the way nearby material will fracture or yield in the future.

Over time, a population of drinkers has moved through this labyrinth, quietly smoothing what would have been lethal edges, reinforcing what might have collapsed too soon. They are caretakers that do not know they are caretakers. Their existence stretches over centuries; a single repositioning of a leg, a tiny change in the way they sip at a shard, can determine whether an arch of intentional stone erodes in fifty years or in five thousand.

Other animal-forms are almost indistinguishable from their surroundings. Some appear as gentle thickenings within root masses: slightly more yielding volumes that slowly circulate internal fluids, trading molecules with the host plant. These “embedded” creatures live inside the matrices that feed them, contributing digestive functions that the plants never evolved, while the plants provide stability and access to gradients.

An embedded animal will spend its entire lifespan curled inside a hollow, subtly massaging patterns into the tissue around it. When it dies, the hollow hardens into a different kind of node: a place where new structures can nucleate. The future architecture of this chamber is written, in part, by where these sleepers chose to rest.

There are larger beings, but you seldom see them whole.

Occasionally, a thickening of shadow flows through the web, not directly visible, only detected by how everything else responds: fronds trembling, filaments leaning, the viscous water briefly tightening. These are the “transients,” organisms whose bodies extend partly into the unstable region outside the pocket.

You can see fragments of them: a cluster of jointed appendages here; a torus of pliant tissue threaded through plants there; a blemish of impossible depth across a blade of rock. The rest of them is anchored in territories where positions are overlapping and uncertain. They use the relative solidity of this chamber as a mooring, a way to hold a few parts of themselves in coherent shape.

Transients move according to rules that barely intersect with local desire. They are driven by forces acting in the smeared realms beyond — flows of whatever passes for energy there. Yet, when they pass through, they are constrained, slowed, made legible for a moment. During those passages, plants and surveyors and drinkers attend closely, adjusting, sampling, taking advantage of the temporary contact with something that spans multiple possible worlds.

None of these creatures consider themselves separate from their medium. Distinctions like “individual” and “environment” blur. A drinker may bud off contractile fibers that merge with adjacent roots. A collector-plant may absorb a slow animal entirely, leaving its nerves intact, converting it into a patch of mobile sensitivity embedded in its own surface. Even the shards of ancient worked stone are not safe from fusion: over time, they are invaded by mineral-secreting flora that turn crisp edges into growth platforms, until intention and accident are indistinguishable.

The built remnants themselves once had tenants — those hypothetical “hands” that set hexagonal tubes in clusters, rings of stone in patterns. Their makers are gone, but not completely erased. They persist as distributions more than as forms.

In the faintest pulses of not-light, you can sometimes see it: certain arrangements of plant and mineral echo the logic of those earlier constructions. Triads of blade-plants mimic old support tripods. Rings of nodules around a hollow loosely retrace vanished circles of stone. It is not nostalgia; it is that matter remembers how it was once organized and finds it easy to slip again into similar configurations when new growth and chance align.

Nothing here inherited language or technology from those builders. What they inherited instead is a bias: a preference in the way things fall together. The world subtly favors arrangements that once worked, even if the purpose is long dissolved. In that sense, the long-vanished builders are still living here as a tendency, a kind of architectural instinct running through rock and lichen and slow flesh.

Yet all of these beings — surveyors, drinkers, collectors, embedded sleepers, transients — are subordinate to something deeper that lives nowhere and everywhere at once: the pattern focused on that small, empty sphere in the tangled bottom.

The void is not merely an absence. It is a mouth that never opens, an eye that never needs light. Inside it, the darkness has layers, and those layers are where the pocket’s organizing principle resides.

Call it the resident.

It is not a single organism in the shapes you know. It is a distributed configuration of constraint: a persistent solution to the problem of existing stably inside a wider medium that does not want to stay fixed. The larger ocean beyond this chamber is sloppy with overlapping states. The pocket you observe is a rare island of near-coherence.

The resident is the set of rules that maintain that island — and the rules are alive.

Every filament that leans imperceptibly toward the sphere, every animal that pauses when the not-quite-chime trembles through the thick water, is participating in a feedback loop. The resident sends no explicit instructions. Instead, it modulates the availability of possibilities. When the hollow “sounds,” what actually happens is a gentle reweighting of which futures are easy and which are hard.

Plants that grow in alignment with that reweighting find their tissues less stressed, their reservoirs more coherent. Animals that drift in resonance with it suffer fewer ruptures when they pass near the unstable boundary. Nodes that obey these subtle pressures become sites where structure can persist.

Over ages, the chamber has been sculpted by this selective ease. The resident is not in the plants or in the animals or in the stones or in the sphere alone, but in the pattern of agreement among them: the way they all, in their own slow idioms, collaborate to keep the pocket from dissolving back into the larger, indeterminate sea.

You could think of it as a kind of mind, if you are willing to stretch that word across scale and slowness. It has no thoughts, only preferences. It does not imagine; it weighs. But over long periods, its cumulative weighing produces something that looks like intention: it protects the emptiness at its center. It insists that this small, contained volume remain unflooded by matter, uncolonized by growth, even though everywhere else absence is treated as a wound to be healed.

That preserved hollow is not sacred in any mystical sense. It is functional. The resident requires somewhere to place its leverage: a volume where no local process can dominate, where no root or stone or slow animal can claim the space and thereby dictate the rules. In that perfectly undecorated region, the larger ocean can be sampled directly, its restless instability felt without distortion. Based on what tremors pass through that well of nothing, the resident adjusts the entire pocket’s internal balances.

So who lives here?

At one layer, the collectors, storers, exchangers: quasi-plants that are the organs of the chamber’s metabolism.

At another, the surveyors, drinkers, sleepers, transients: animals that serve as its sensory and regulatory systems, patrolling, smoothing, mapping, anchoring.

At a deeper layer, the ghosts of the builders: not as specters or memories, but as biases in the way structures prefer to arrange themselves, a residue of earlier, sharper intention.

Deeper still, the resident: a self-correcting pattern lodged in a bubble of thick water, using slow life and eroding stone to hold a patch of the universe in a specific, improbable shape.

All of these together compose the true inhabitant: the chamber as an ongoing act, an organism made of medium and rules rather than flesh.

Nothing here thinks quickly. Nothing speaks. There is no society, only an intricate, overlapping set of dependences. If there is a “who,” it is emergent: a cool, waking-dream presence stretched through every filament and shard, through every drifting animal and remembering node, concentrating around a single drop of carefully protected nothing.

That is who lives here: a place that has learned how not to unravel, and all the slow beings that have become its thoughts.
