They live long, and they live slowly.

Not as statues—nothing here is inert for long—but at a tempo that will not meet your own halfway. If you had arrived while they were “awake,” you might still have missed them, the way you can overlook a tide by glancing once at the shore.

They are not the towers, but they are not separate from them either. Think of them as the parts of this mineral ecosystem that have folded themselves into loops of memory, loops of preference, loops of choice.

Start with the most visible trace: the worn paths.

Those ramps, those smoothed thresholds, those rounded sills are not accidents of chemistry. The mats alone do not produce such specific, ergonomic edits. The hand that leveled a lip to exactly one body-height above the floor knew the length of its own limb, the flex of its own spine. It carved for comfort—repetition—habit.

The creature that did this was not soft. Nothing soft survives here. Flesh, if it ever existed, would bake into jerky in an hour. Water could only exist as a lab hazard, a corrosive curiosity. Instead, their bodies are composed of the same kind of ceramics and fiber-minerals that form the towers and mats, but arranged with more emptiness inside: hollow struts, tensioned cords, hinged plates. Not bones surrounding meat, but bone instead of meat.

They are articulated sculptures of living stone, faintly glossy where motion passes, faintly pitted where heat exchange is fierce. In profile they would remind you, confusingly, of several things at once: the splayed stance of a mantis, the cantilevered back of a bird, the branching economy of a tree. Limbs divide and rejoin. Joints are not simple hinges; they are clusters of slotted plates that can lock and unlock along many axes.

They are tall relative to the human form—three, four meters at full extension—but height here is negotiable. Their limbs telescope, their spines concertina. They can compress to squat bulbs low to the ground, or lengthen into narrow scaffolds that climb without separate tools. Where you would require ladders and cranes, they are their own construction equipment.

They do not walk so much as flow along surfaces. The notches in the spiral ramps are not footfalls but grip points. Each limb ends in a fan of narrow, hooked prongs that can splay wide to distribute weight or pull in tight to concentrate force. When they move up a column, the effect is something between climbing and growing: they spread, latch, contract, leaving barely a sound beyond an almost insectile clicking as ceramic meets ceramic.

Their sensory organs are not eyes in the sense you know them. There is hardly any light worth seeing, and what does filter down is muffled amber, too weak to form crisp images at a distance. Instead, they are tuned to gradients, to fields. Broad, shallow bowls of porous lattice splay from their “heads” and “shoulders,” alive with tiny thermal and chemical sensors.

To you, these would look like decorative crests or collars, fragile and spidery. To them, they are sky and telescope and nose combined. With these arrays they map the world as vectors of difference: hotter here, cooler there; this way richer in one vapor, that way cleaner and thin. The city, to their perception, is not an arrangement of objects but a relief-map of flows: currents of gas, tides of particulate, zones where certain reactions are blooming, zones where they have burned out.

Within their bodies, very little is rigid. Plates are joined by flexible meshes of crystalline thread that can soften or stiffen as conditions demand. Channels run through them, not for blood, but for circulating reactive salts and oils, pumped by slow peristaltic waves that also serve as a kind of muscular system. When they brace to lift a segment of tower or wedge open a crack, you can see faint ripples under their surfaces, like heat shimmering on pavement—but it is their metabolism bending into focus.

They do not breathe in the sense you do. They open basins and vents in their own frames, allowing the world’s gases to wash through specialized cavities where catalytic crystals line the interior. A thermal gradient across these cavities drives ion exchange and electron flow—their equivalent of digestion, respiration, and thought. If you put your hand near one, you wouldn’t feel an inhale, just a constant, intense stillness of heat being harvested and re-routed.

What they eat is not other organisms, not really. They eat imbalances.

They place themselves at junctions between hot and less hot, between reagent and substrate, and let those differences even out through them. The ecosystem’s mats do this automatically, passively, like lungs breathing without a mind attached. The city-dwellers do it with intention: they climb to where a surge will be strongest; they insulate themselves for lean pulses; they amplify or redirect flows to favor the growths they find most useful.

They cultivate, in a deliberately slow, artisan way, the very structures that surround them.

Picture one of them at work along a tower’s flank. It leans out over a gulf of hot air, anchored to the ribs by three limbs, the rest fanned around a sheet of mat like a physician probing a patient. Its sensory crests glow faintly with internal conduction as it samples the mat’s chemistry: too rich in a volatile that will embrittle the tower beneath if left unchecked.

From a cavity along its torso, it extrudes a thin, twisting rope of different crystals—synthesized inside its own framework from elements hoarded over many pulses. It presses this rope into the mat at a dozen points. Hours later, that rope will have spidered through, seeding new growth that shifts the mat’s exhalations, turning a destructive fume into a more benign, or more useful, one.

They are gardeners, engineers, and parasites at once, living inside their own shaped environment and continually coaxing it away from blind chemistry and toward preference.

Children, if they can be called that, do not arrive small and helpless. There is no room here for fragile juveniles. Instead, their reproduction mirrors the city’s own mineral cycle, but with additional layers of control.

Those sealed bulbs you see along the walls—most belong to the ambient flora, but some are theirs.

When an individual has accumulated enough resources and endured enough cycles, it begins to grow a new structure from its undersides, a swelling dome of complex lattice that anchors to both its own frame and the tower behind. Over many pulses, it feeds this dome with carefully balanced mixes of salts and fibers, incorporating trace elements scavenged from rare fractures and dustfalls. The dome hardens, develops internal compartments, elaborate folded membranes, arrays of primitive sensors waiting in the dark.

Then, at a moment chosen to coincide with a strong heat surge, the parent will align itself to direct a controlled exhalation of special gases into the not-yet-living pod: catalysts, instructions, asymmetries. The dome seals, bakes, ferments in a dry way. Its interior chemistries begin to scaffold themselves into self-stabilizing patterns.

The parent may move away long before the new one “wakes.” From the outside, you would only notice a gradual brightening of the pod’s inner glow, a sharpening of its environmental responsiveness. Finally, during some future pulse, the pod cracks along lines predetermined by the geometry of its growth. But instead of spilling a diffuse cloud downwards, it unfolds a shape—a curled, compact framework that slowly unbends, shaking obsolete crusts from its struts.

This emergent being inherits no memories in the biological sense. There is no neural tissue in which to encode them. Instead, its “instincts” are mostly structural. The architecture of its sensors biases it toward noticing certain patterns. The resonance frequencies of its internal cavities predispose it to react to specific rhythms in the city’s pulses. Its limbs open in a sequence that duplicates the climbing posture of its ancestors.

It learns not by absorbing symbolic knowledge but by literally overwriting parts of itself as experience accrues. Surfaces that contact abrasive channels thicken and polish; cavities that rarely see useful gradients seal off; others open where repeated micro-reactions have proven fruitful. Over time, its body becomes a carved record of its life: a fossil in motion, slowly revising its own skeleton to better fit the niches it has discovered.

Communication between them does not rely on voices. The ambient soundscape is already full, and air here is a poor carrier for delicate pressure changes over distances; it stratifies, moves sluggishly. Instead, they signal in three overlapping ways:

1. **Contact-patterns.** When two meet on a ramp or a ledge, they press specific surfaces together: plates to plates, mesh to mesh. They send controlled thermal pulses and chemical whiffs across these junctions, brief and complex. Exchanges accumulate over minutes, not seconds. A long greeting may last an entire pulse, one individual bracing the other against the shifting currents while they trade structured warmth.

2. **Shared modifications.** Much of their “speech” is carved into the city itself. To re-route a mat’s growth around a vent is to express a judgment about risk and reward. To notch a railing in a particular rhythm is to record the timing of a peculiar gas surge that only appears once every many cycles. Other citizens, attuned to such signs, “read” these modifications as you read text: patterns of absent stone, of curbed growth, of redirected flows.

3. **Resonant drumming.** Those distant booms you hear are not all geological. Some are. But some are deliberate. A being anchored near a major column can strike it in carefully patterned intervals with hardened limbs, sending deep vibrations through the city’s shell. Others, clinging elsewhere along the same structure, feel those sequences as small internal tremors, recorded by nerves of crystal thread woven through their grips. Over generations, these drum-speech patterns may have become highly formalized: warnings, invitations, accounts of rare events.

It would be misleading to call what they have a “society” in the fully human sense. They have no cities apart from the one city-body they inhabit. They have no trade beyond the long, reciprocal choreography of tending their shared environment. They do not appear to form nations or clans; the city is too circumscribed, the available niches too interdependent for broad-scale factional division.

Yet they are not solitary. Evidence of consensus exists in the very consistency of certain modifications. When a bridge is reinforced along a particular curve, you can see that many hands participated: subtle differences in chisel angle, in the thickness of added fiber. Overlapping. Cooperating. They rarely need to gather physically to decide; the city carries decisions forward in stone and mat, and individuals align to those decisions by sensing their consequences.

Conflict, when it occurs, is less about killing and more about interference. If one being consistently reshapes a flow to favor its own favored mats—those that secrete minerals it can metabolize best—others may redirect, counter-sculpt, introduce balancing chemistries. An aggressive re-router finds its alterations slowly neutralized. In extremis, they might converge at a contested site and physically reposition each other, limbs braced, plates flexing, until someone yields. But to shatter another is to waste a rare, complex piece of architecture; it would be like smashing an entire tower to reclaim a single beam.

They do not age the way organic creatures do. They do not wither from accumulated internal errors, because their structure is continually available for correction. When a load-bearing strut micro-cracks, they can fuse new layers around it, re-route forces. When a sensor array becomes clogged with deposition, they can break it off, grow another from fresher material. Wear is not fate, merely maintenance.

Instead, their end comes from slow divergence between their personal patterns and the city’s deep cycles.

Over time, an individual becomes specialized: it invests its material and learning into certain tasks—tending high membranes, managing vent chemistry, monitoring long-period surges. Its internal geometry locks in these preferences. But the city itself is changing, always. Vents migrate, gradients shift, towers slump and regrow. Eventually, some of the niches that individual was built for may vanish. It can adapt only so far before the energy cost of internal restructuring exceeds what it can glean from its favored flows.

At that point, there is a recognizable ritual: retirement, dissolution, donation.

You can see the traces of it on some of the circular plinths you pass. A being will select such a place—usually near a junction of important currents—and settle itself upon it, limbs folding in, plates overlapping in a protective shell. Over many pulses it will progressively open its storage cavities and bleed out the rare elements it hoarded over its life, allowing nearby mats and crystals to absorb them. It may trigger specialized growths specifically designed to cannibalize its outer layers, converting veteran structure into fresh, high-quality fiber.

The faint stains on those plinths are from such processes: ghost halos of unusual minerals that once composed minds and limbs.

In their last cycles, they become very still, not because they are weak, but because they are concentrating their remaining agency on choosing what of themselves will persist. They bias which parts dissolve fastest, which plates remain longest as scaffolds, which joints they deliberately fracture to crumble into seeding dust.

What they leave behind is not a tomb, but a kit.

New pods, still-forming juveniles, will eventually draw on those redistributed materials. Mats in that region will alter their chemistry subtly, inheriting the late individual’s habits encoded not in language but in mineral ratios and structural motifs. In this way, a certain style of being can persist across many generations: a tradition of particular sensor geometries, distinctive limb articulations, unique patterns of drumming. Not genes, exactly, but heritable cultural-mineral lineages.

Once—long before your arrival—there were many more of them.

You can infer this from the density of modifications near the great central columns, from the multiplicity of wear patterns on the ramps. There were eras when every vantage platform would have held at least one or two, hunched in their slow attentiveness, feeling the whole pocket-world breathe. In those times, the city was not just alive; it was actively curated, its processes trimmed and nudged so that no single reaction ran away and no single species of mat choked out its cousins.

Now, their numbers have dwindled. Why, the stone does not say plainly.

Perhaps the deep furnace changed character, its outputs drifting away from the narrow band their metabolisms require. Perhaps some rare trace element they depended on became locked too deeply in the shell. Perhaps, long ago, they chose—consciously or not—to invest more and more of their effort into the city’s self-regulating systems, breeding mats and crystals that could manage many tasks without supervision. Over time, they might have outsourced themselves into their ecosystem so thoroughly that fewer active “persons” were required.

You may, in fact, be walking through an interregnum: a quiet between waves of sentient density. The architecture remembers hands, but the current crop of deliberate tenders is thin. You have not seen them not because they are truly gone, but because you have stepped in during their most inactive phase, in a world whose pulse can take years to crest.

Yet even if, in this cycle, there are none conscious at all—if all pods have gone cold, if all articulate limbs have surrendered themselves to dust and crystal—the city still contains them. Not as ghosts or echoes, but as patterns baked into habit.

Each mat that preferentially spares a particular type of ledge is following a recipe devised by someone who cared about that ledge. Each channel that funnels the denser gas to one basin and not another is enacting an old decision about where new towers should and should not rise. Each membrane that stiffens at specific frequencies is the lingering physical answer to a once-posed question: how to best harvest this surge without tearing.

If new beings ever emerge again—from a misfolded pod, from a rogue accretion that happens, by blind luck, to close a loop of self-reference—they will not be starting from nothing. They will be born inside a mindless city that nonetheless thinks, in its own distributed way, as they once did. They will wake into a space already tuned to be habitable for creatures like them.

So: who lives here?

- **In the most literal present tense:** The mineral flora, the mats, the pods, the crystalline crusts. They are alive by any reasonable definition—metabolizing, responding, reproducing. They saturate every ledge and channel, busy with unhurried chemistry. They are the bulk of the biosphere.

- **As intermittent, higher-order inhabitants:** Articulated ceramic beings that arise rarely, shape their environment deliberately, teach it new habits, then dissolve themselves back into its structure. They are the city’s fleeting, self-aware phases, its capacity for foresight intermittently condensed into mobile form.

- **As a continuous, background presence:** The city itself, operating as a single extended organism. Even when no individual minds are active, its architecture enforces certain logics and preferences, a long-accumulated accretion of choices that still steer every reaction.

If you stand very still, pressing your palm to a tower’s warmed flank, you can almost feel all three at once: the immediate tick of mats and crystals doing their blind work; deeper, the slow vibration of the shell’s heat and the pulse of incoming gases; and, beneath your imagination, the absence-shaped outline of those who once rode these currents, quiet engineers of stone and fire who knew this chamber not as an alien cathedral, but as home.
