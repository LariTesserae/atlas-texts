What lives here does not so much *occupy* the world as continue it.

Everything that is not water is plant—and much that moves, breathes, and chooses is also, in some way, plant. Niches that we would expect to be taken by animals, fungi, or even machines are here settled by things that begin from vegetative tissue and only then diverge into other modes of being.

I’ll sketch them not in taxonomic rigor, but in layers and habits, from the broadest, slowest minds to the smallest lives that animate their surfaces.

---

## 1. The plateau-mind and its great bodies

The entire living crust behaves, in practice, as a single, distributed organism: not a tidy “world-tree,” but a fused, continent-scale **macroflora** whose innumerable components share sap, signal, and history.

It is not uniform. Think of it as overlapping **regions of emphasis**, each dominated by a few vast lineages:

### 1.1. The vascular commons

Everywhere you press, something pulses. Beneath your feet runs an **interlaced hydraulic mesh**: broad, slow trunks and finer capillaries belonging to many “species” of plant that have long since given up strict individuality.

- Fluids in this mesh carry not just water and nutrients but **slow codes**: gradients of ions, hormones, and protein-like messengers that spread on the order of hours to weeks.
- Where networks densify—at sinkholes, spiral towers, or the bases of swollen domes—they form **processing knots**, places where many signals converge and re-route.

There is no single brain. Instead, there are **overlapping basins of coherence**, regions tens to hundreds of kilometers across within which patterns of flow, secretion, and growth form something like a mood or preference. These basins shift with long climatic drifts, with collapses and new fusions of structure. They remember.

They are not “people” in our sense. They never have a thought that finishes in seconds. What we would call a decision might be a **century’s** work of adjusting growth rates, thickening here, starving there, gradually biasing the future. But over geologic time, these plateau-minds have steered themselves toward stability, away from instability, toward mutual reinforcement.

You walk on their **body**, but more precisely you walk through their **attention**: any stress, any cut, any new pattern of traffic becomes part of what they are registering.

### 1.2. The great motile vegetates

Some parts of the plateau have partially detached themselves from the fixed skeleton and become **locomoting masses of plant-tissue**—beasts, if you like, but beasts whose bones are cellulose, whose blood is sap.

They are rare and very slow.

- **Bulwarks**: Humped bodies the size of small hills, their surfaces mossed and epiphyte-laden, their underparts a forest of rootlike feet. They migrate over decades, drifting downslope into sagging valleys, then slowly up again as the substrate flexes. Their motion is too slow to see; you detect it by the way their flank—where you camped a year before—is now subtly further along a curve.
  - Inside, they are hollow, honeycombed caverns full of warm aerosols and tame fungal gardens. Many smaller creatures live in them permanently, never stepping onto the broader plateau.
  - The bulwark’s “will” is gradient-based: it creeps toward mineral-rich zones, away from accumulated toxins or chronic predation, steered by the chemical whisperings of its internal tenants.

- **Torsion-pillars**: Seemingly rooted towers that, over centuries, **spiral** very slightly, turning like slow augers. This rotation changes the pattern of shade, drip, and access around them, methodically re-sorting nearby ecosystems. Certain long-lived symbionts track the turn, always inhabiting the tower’s “leading” side where fresh niches emerge.

These beings are best thought of as **organs that learned to walk**: still plugged into the plateau’s vascular system via trailing roots or seasonal fusions, yet sufficiently autonomous to reposition themselves.

Are they conscious? Not in the quick, narrative sense. But they participate in the plateau’s longer thoughts, like knuckles in a vast, flexing hand.

## 2. Mid-scale intelligences: the drift-colonies and cultivators

Within the plateau’s flesh live smaller, more bounded societies—still slow, still chemically-thinking, but with lives we might dimly recognize as strategic.

### 2.1. Drifter-colonies

These are **animal-plant composites** that inhabit the hanging curtains, root-veins, and membrane ceilings. A single entity is a small cloud of organisms: sessile polyps embedded in a shared mat of tissue, plus a mobile halo of associated grazers and cleaners.

- The mat senses via **chemical and electrical gradients**; the mobile attendants supply it with information from a meter or two away, then “dock” to download.
- Mat and halo coordinate to **steer the local growth** of their curtain: by selective pruning and feeding, they thicken some strands, thin others, gently reshaping the airflow and drip pattern to favor themselves.
- Over generations they bud off fragments—offspring mats with starter halos—that drift or crawl a short distance away.

These colonies are as close as this world comes, at small scale, to **engineers**. They stabilize useful ledges, maintain open pores in otherwise clogging mats, and cultivate particular microbial assemblages that produce protective chemicals.

They are not fully separate from the plateau-mind; their modifications feed back into global traffic patterns. But they have enough local agency to be distinct: they can fail, flourish, compete.

### 2.2. The fungal clerks

Scattered in the dim subsurface are **zonations** dominated by particular fungal superlineages. These lines are less about decomposing matter and more about **recording and routing it**.

They grow in stratified sheets and filaments that:

- Sequester certain trace elements, concentrating them into **fine mineral veins**.
- Encode long-lived **chemical tags** into their tissues—distinct combinations that mark “this matter passed through here at this time under these conditions.”

Some of these fungal aggregations interact directly with animal vectors: mid-sized burrowers and gliders that routinely visit their fruiting layers to feed.

- In return for sugars or toxins-for-hire, the fungi load these visitors with specific spores, effectively **dispatching messages and resource claims** elsewhere.

Over many cycles, this has produced a distributed, low-resolution **log of flows**: where nutrients have come from, where pressures are mounting, where voids are opening. The plateau-mind reads this log via its own roots, adjusting broad patterns accordingly.

These fungi are “clerks” in the sense that they keep the matter-books; they are not in charge, but without them, the plateau’s capacity to remember conditions across geological time would be poorer.

## 3. Animals in the plant-body

To human eyes, the fauna is surprisingly sparse at any one vantage—no great herds, no flocks, nothing that fills the open. But if you attend to scale and substrate, you find busy layers of animal life, all adapted to **never being far from something soft and wet to grip.**

### 3.1. Large, slow-bodied dwellers

These are creatures on the order of a big cat up to a small rhino in mass, though never built to run.

- **Many-limbed climbers**: Imagine a cross between a starfish and a sloth, stretched out into six to ten long, muscular arms radiating from a central, low body.
  - Each arm ends in a pulp of gripping pads and reversible hooks.
  - They move inverted as easily as upright, preferring the undersides of arches, span-bridges, and membrane plains.
  - Their metabolism is low. A large individual may take hours to cross a hundred meters, pausing often to press its ventral surface flat against the substrate and **sip exudates** through an array of soft mouth-patches.
  - Their senses are contact-heavy: dense fields of mechanoreceptors feel minute flexings in the plant-tissue; chemosensory cupules test every surface. Sight is vestigial—two small pits that distinguish only gross light/dark.

- **Cavern grazers**: Thick-bodied, blind herbivores, more like patient, mobile stomachs than typical animals.
  - They inhabit the dim hollows under membrane plains and inside bulwarks, feeding on slow-growing, nutrient-rich structures: thickened support knots, certain fungal fruitings.
  - To avoid outright stripping their home, they cycle: an individual may remain in a given chamber for years, then feel some deep chemical urge and heave its bulk along a known corridor to another chamber, allowing the first to regrow.
  - Their waste is highly structured—pellets enwrapped in mucus that selectively feeds desired fungi and repels others. In effect, their digestive tract has become an **ecosystem filter**, a curated gateway from plateau tissues into specific microbial successions.

These large animals are not numerous. Their life-history is slow: gestations that last seasons upon seasons, long infancy clinging to parental flanks, adult lifespans measured in local centuries.

They have no tools, but their habits **sculpt**: favored corridors remain open; resting hollows are polished to gentle, plant-lined baths; certain support columns bear generations-deep wear patterns.

### 3.2. Medium-scale navigators and predators

Here live the creatures that actually *choose* among options on timescales we’d recognize: minutes to days. They are the plateau’s nimblest minds.

- **Webbed leapers**:
  - Body length perhaps that of a human arm, mass very low, skeletons gracile but strong.
  - Limbs end in fanlike, webbed structures that double as parachutes and soft hammers, distributing impact across the springy substrate.
  - They stake out small three-dimensional ranges—volumes encompassing a handful of columns, a couple of sags, a stretch of membrane plain.
  - Diet omnivorous: small invertebrates, certain soft shoots, nectar from lantern-fruits.
  - Their calls—barely audible clicks and tonal pips—coordinate with neighbors to avoid collision in the compressed visual space. Some individuals share habitual “routes,” effectively co-owning paths the way birds might share flyways.

- **Percolating hunters**:
  - Long, elastic bodies that move partly through the substrate.
  - When engaged in predation, they inject **digestive filaments** into a burrower or small grazer, then trail it at a distance, absorbing the released nutrients back through the shared channel as the victim continues to live and feed.
  - Their intelligence is specialized: extreme pattern-recognition for substrate vibrations, a good predictive model of how tunnels and caverns connect, poor interest in anything else.
  - Over time, their feeding can **steer** the movement of prey populations, subtly redistributing browsing pressure on the surrounding plant-body—another feedback channel between animal choice and plateau growth.

Many of these mid-scale animals engage in what looks like **gardening**, though driven by instinct:

- Some clear specific lichens or slimes from favored resting spots.
- Others prune away reactive plant hairs from travel corridors, leaving a characteristic smoothness.
- A few species actively spread the spores of defensive fungi on the outer walls of their nesting tunnels, creating living sheaths that deter invaders with bitter or numbing exudates.

### 3.3. Small, fast, multitudes

Here, speed finally becomes cheap. Insects, mites, tiny soft-bodied oozers, centimeter-scale gliders—all proliferate in numbers beyond casual counting.

- **Capillary-feeders**: needle-mouthed creatures that live at the boundary of wet/damp in the substrate, always riding the front of a creeping saturation wave, piercing plant conduits and withdrawing just enough sap not to trigger a closure response.
- **Microgliders**: dusty, wing-membraned things that drift in the interior caverns, filtering spores, bacteria, and volatile organics from the heavy air. Each species is tuned to a different chemical band, collectively acting as a **mobile distillation system** for the plateau’s aerosols.
- **Patch-tenders**: ant-analogues, but without hard shells—soft, adhesive, specialized for climbing anything.
  - They maintain sharp chemical territories: zones on a membrane plain or curtain where only the microbial consortia they “prefer” are allowed to grow.
  - They herd and feed on little, immobile sprout-animals (essentially degenerate larvae they’ve semi-domesticated), moving them to richer microspots.

Their conflicts create **microscopic wars** that never scale up to landscape devouring plagues. The plateau’s dense, continuous, multi-layered nature means no single small species can run away with everything; its boom is someone else’s feast quickly enough.

## 4. The nearly-invisible kingdoms

You can’t understand what lives here without the things you almost never *see*.

### 4.1. Biofilms as atmosphere-organs

Every surface slicked by permanent damp hosts **biofilm consortia**—matrices of bacteria, archaea, and ultra-simple eukaryotes that continuously exchange gases and small molecules with the air.

- Some layers specialize in pulling trace volatiles out of the air and recycling them into plateau tissues.
- Others generate **scent-codes**: specific, repeating blends that mark “healthy flow,” “stagnation,” “new opening,” or “recent damage.”
- Animals read these codes much as we casually read color and line, orienting themselves to gradients of comfort, risk, and opportunity.

When big things happen—collapse of a shaft, sudden drying of a region—the biofilms update first. Their changed emissions travel on convection currents into caverns, along shafts, lightly coating every passing tongue and antenna.

In this way, **information concentrates fastest in the microscopic**, then percolates upward into the behaviors of mid-scale animals and the very slow growth adjustments of the plateau.

### 4.2. Symbiotic endoliths

Though mineral “rock” is mostly hidden, where roots press against it live specialized **endolithic communities**: microbes and hyper-slow-growing lichen-like complexes that literally mine the plateau’s last available minerals.

- They excrete acids and chelators, painstakingly liberating ions.
- The plateau-mind rewards them with a constant, if faint, drip of organic compounds down the root channels, feeding their metabolism.
- Some of their metabolic byproducts act as **global regulators**—rare trace molecules that, when finally drawn upward, change plant hormone regimes over vast areas, subtly shifting the plateau between slightly more growth-forward or slightly more consolidation-forward modes.

These endoliths, if they “know” anything, know the deepest timescales: rock weathering, continent raising and sinking. Their genome-like information must be full of things that changed eons ago and never needed revising.

## 5. Ghosts of the builders

You noticed relic geometries: equal column spacing, hexagonal spines, flow-directing basins. The beings that shaped these are not present as walkers or fliers—but they may not be entirely gone.

Two main possibilities, which the plateau itself may embody both of:

### 5.1. Dissolved architects

Once, there were likely **localized, faster minds**: clusters of tissue that invested heavily in neural analogs—dense electrochemical circuitry, perhaps piggybacking on the plateau’s vascular mesh.

Rather than evolving hard bodies and external tools, they **learned to speak plant** very precisely:

- They could imprint growth cues into juvenile tissues with fine temporal and spatial control.
- They could set up long-term feedback loops between structure and function, in effect “programming” pieces of the plateau to behave as machinery: pumps, reaction chambers, selective filters.

Over long ages, the distinction between these directing nodes and the plateau they directed blurred:

- Their hardest-won tricks—the subtle control schemas—became canalized into plant development itself.
- Their tissues, highly connected, made convenient highways and signal hubs and thus resistant to pruning.
- As their original, compact nervous concentrations failed or were damaged, their **routines persisted** as stable modes of growth and response, diffused into the network.

So now, when a ring of equal columns arises in a certain kind of gap, nobody has to “decide” it. The plateau’s inherited developmental machinery simply does so, because it’s been shaped that way.

The builders survive not as people but as **architectural reflexes**, distributed habits of matter.

### 5.2. Residual node-minds

In some deep, sealed hollows or long-protected towers, there may still be **mindlike concentrations**:

- Dense knots where signal-conducting tissues have layered and layered, wrapped in insulating sheaths.
- Their outermost surfaces still exchange with the plateau-mesh, but inside they run **internal cycles**: replaying old patterns, updating slowly from the trickle of new data.

To any visitor with our kind of timeframe, they would seem inert. Days of waiting might catch only a few micro-voltage sweeps. But over centuries, they could:

- Tweak the tendency of a local area to grow certain structures.
- Bias certain symbiont lineages to spread or contract.
- Emit rare hormone-flares that ripple outward and subtly reset developmental programs in their vicinity.

If you could sync your perception to their tick, you might find that an entire regional architecture—the arrangement of towers, bridges, reservoirs—is a *gesture* they are still in the process of making.

## 6. How everything fits: a living, slow, cooperative machine

Who lives here? Not discrete species assembled like a zoo, but **interlocking habits of life** tuned to a place that almost never wipes clean.

A rough hierarchy of dynamical roles:

- **Macroflora plateau**: sets baseline conditions, stores and moves matter, implements extremely slow, stabilizing strategies.
- **Fungal clerks & endoliths**: mediate memory of flows and deep resources, write fine-grained history into chemistry.
- **Mid-scale animals & drift-colonies**: make the flexible choices—where to travel, which channels to keep open, which patches to farm or abandon.
- **Microfauna & biofilms**: provide rapid feedback, sense and advertise every small change, make local course corrections cheap.
- **Residual minds / builder ghosts**: inject rare, high-level biases, preserving long-evolved “design ideas” in plateau growth.

Communication routes:

- **Substrate conduction** for global, slow signals.
- **Chemical plumes and films** for medium-range, medium-speed cues.
- **Animal motion** for targeted courier roles.
- **Bioluminescence** mainly as a fine-grained, local coordination tool: mating, warning, subtle alignment of shared tasks in darkness.

Conflict exists: predation, parasitism, territorial exclusion. But the world’s extraordinary oldness means lineages that consistently destabilized large regions have long since been pruned away, or changed. What remains tends to **fight within bounds**, never burning the library it lives in.

No one here is in a hurry because **hurry doesn’t pay**. It cannot cross enough distance fast enough, in a medium this thick, to be worth the metabolic cost except for the very small. So life has become not a race but an intricate, mutual leaning-together.

---

If you had time, and a way to listen across centuries, you might finally say: what lives here is one great being that has learned to **populate its own mind** with sub-beings.

It has grown itself a nervous system of animals, fungi, films, and drifters, each with their own little projects and hungers, all woven into the plateau’s vaster aim: to remain what it is—soft, wet, continuous, and ongoing—for as long as its warm, dim climate allows.
