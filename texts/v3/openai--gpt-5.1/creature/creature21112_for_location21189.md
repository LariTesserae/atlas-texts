They do not think of themselves as inhabitants. They are continuations.

Everything alive here—plant, animal, anything between—belongs less to a species than to a role within a choreography that has outlived memory. If there was once a first architect lineage, a root event, it is buried: in genomes, in reflex arcs, in the way cells proliferate along some axes and not others. No creature in the volume now remembers intention as anything but instinct.

Still, there are distinct kinds.

---

### The World-Plant Itself

Most of what lives here is the cathedral.

Calling it “plant” is almost willful ignorance. It photosynthesized once, in shallower epochs when faint light percolated down and the ancestors of these tissues had broad, frondlike faces turned upward. Fossils of those shapes do not exist, but the organelles do: relic chloroplasts that now spend their energy running slow chemistries off gradients and trace nutrients, spilling excess power as that thin, exhausted blue-green glow in the walls.

The world-plant is not one organism in a simple sense. It is a colonial superstructure: billions of clonal and quasi-clonal modules, knitted into a single vascular and informational network.

There are tiers of its body, each with their own emphasis.

#### 1. Load-Bearing Flesh

The huge trunks and buttresses—the “stone” of this space—are an amalgam of three tissue types:

- **Crushwood:** Dense, cellulose-analogue fibers laced with mineral deposits. This tissue doesn’t bend much; what little flexibility it has occurs over centuries. The long, smooth ridges and rounded corners come from its inheritance of old rock shapes and its own slow, self-grinding against current and minor movement.
- **Flex-veins:** Nested within crushwood, bundled in spirals, are slightly elastic cords that stiffen or relax very slowly in response to mechanical strain. These are the only reason the cathedral still stands; they let the whole world-body distribute stresses like a sleeping animal shifting weight.
- **Deep Pith:** At the absolute cores of the thickest columns, there is a spongy, almost marrow-like tissue, saturated with nutrient-rich fluids. It is not structural; it is reserve. When part of the architecture is damaged, the world-plant bleeds pith-fluids into the wound, a kind of sap-scald that congeals into new load-bearing material over decades.

The inhabitants never see these layers clearly. On rare occasions, where a major trunk cracked eons ago and was half-healed, a corridor might run through a fault-line, one wall smooth, dark, and quiet, the other raw-looking and subtly warmer, mottled with slow vascular pulses. That is as close as they come to the plant’s inner bones.

#### 2. The Porous Tier

Smaller, building-scale growths form the endless corridors, chambers, and interwoven galleries.

This tier specializes in:

- **Controlling flow:** Outgrowths curve to create laminar streams, break up vortices, and turn broad roars into manageable throats of faster water. The world-plant’s nervous system is mostly here: long, slow-conducting strands of excitable tissue that sense shear, pressure, and chemical composition at thousands of points and, over hours or days, adjust tiny growth decisions along their length.
- **Contact with animals:** Most symbioses happen at this scale. Filamentous tendrils emerge here, wrapped in tasting membranes that dab against passing bodies. Some offer nutrients in exchange for wastes; others harvest sloughed skin, excrement, or dead tissue.

These structures are where the great arched halls, spiral shafts, and complex, honeycombed canyons arise. They weren’t “designed” in a top-down way; they are the aggregated outcome of all the plant’s reflexes cramped together in three dimensions.

#### 3. The Membrane Web

The thinnest tier spins curtains, veils, and sheets:

- **Filter membranes:** Semi-permeable tissues that separate micro-ecosystems—plankton-dense pockets on one side, stripped water on the other. They hum faintly with the work of ionic pumps, endlessly maintaining tiny differences in salinity, nutrient load, or pH.
- **Gate membranes:** Slightly thicker, laced with contractile cells. These can pucker closed if the plant detects a drastic, sudden change in water chemistry—a bloom of toxins from a mass die-off, say, or a rare volcanic intrusion far below. They move on timescales of hours, not seconds. Catastrophe here is measured in weeks.

The membranes are the closest this world has to “doors” and “windows.” Small organisms push through them easily; larger ones navigate them like kelp forests, slow and careful.

#### 4. The Distributed Mind

The world-plant possesses no brain, but it does have memory.

Information flows along:

- **Electrochemical conduits:** Slow action-potential-like waves moving over minutes to hours, mostly transmitting “mechanical status” (strain, tear, blockage) and “chemical state” (nutrient surplus/deficit).
- **Growth patterns:** The real long-term memory sits encoded in where growth is permitted or inhibited. Regions that once tore under certain current patterns grow thicker or adopt subtle buttresses by default in all subsequent generations. These anatomical “lessons” propagate through spores and seeds that rarely disperse far but carry structural habits encoded in regulatory genes.

Nothing in this mind feels fast. If you could compress a century into an hour, the whole cathedral would shudder and flex like an organism adjusting posture. At human speed, it feels resolved, finished, immutable.

Within and atop this slow intelligence, the animals live.

### Drifters and Orbits

There are creatures here whose entire being is defined by a relationship to pre-existing flow.

#### The Ribbon Satellites

The fifty-meter tumbling bands studded with sensing frills are called, by no one in particular, *orbiters*.

- **Body plan:** Thin, tape-like bodies with a broad dorsal face lined with mechano-sensory cilia and chemoreceptive pits. The ventral side carries a row of dense, bioluminescent organs—yellow nodes that shine with steady, unmodulated light.
- **Life history:** Orbiters begin as small, wriggling larvae that drift randomly until they stumble into a stable current loop—a torus or vertical shaft where the water cycles along a near-identical path day after day. Those that find such a loop orient along it, expand in length rather than in girth, and grow their bodies into passive sails tuned to the exact shear profile of that current.
- **Existence:** They do not truly swim. Micro-movements—tiny flexes along their length—maintain their phase in the loop, preventing them from slipping out. They feed by filtering particulate matter and tiny organisms flushed through the shaft. Their frills listen constantly to pressure: a three-dimensional echo of their route, mapped in feeling.

To an orbiter, time is repetitions. It measures its life in how many times it has passed a familiar bump in the wall, a slightly colder patch of flow, a cluster of symbiotic filter-colonies humming along the shaft’s edge. Its light is as constant as its path, a moving point on an endless track. The plant, in turn, has thickened and smoothed the shaft walls to reinforce the loop that sustains the orbiters’ existence. Each depends on the other without intent.

#### Current-Readers

Smaller, less conspicuous creatures also make flow their medium.

- **Threadfins:** Slender, nearly invisible fish with absurdly long, hairlike fin extensions. They hang nose-first into cross-currents, their tails anchored by small adhesive pads to plant surfaces. Each filament is an antenna reading velocity fluctuations. When a disturbance—a predator’s wake, a large body—passes far upstream, the subtle change runs down the current and whispers along the filaments. Threadfins respond not by fleeing but by turning, aligning their bodies with the new pattern, incorporating it into their map. Their survival strategy is anticipation: feeling the shape of what’s coming long before it arrives.
- **Glide-barges:** Flat, disc-like animals with stiff, winglike fins. They are bigger—human-sized or larger—but move as subtly as leaves on water. They position themselves at mouths of throats where currents accelerate, letting the force carry them in slow loops or long glides through adjoining chambers. Their bodies are ringed with chemosensory pores; they absorb dissolved organics directly, like mobile, animal sponges. Some host mossy lawns of microbial symbionts on their dorsal surfaces, which process what they take in and bioluminesce faintly in reciprocation.

These flow-aligned beings don’t much interact with one another. Their overlap is minimal; each patch of tuned current belongs, de facto, to one kind of drifter.

### Grazers on the Mesh

Where the plant’s internal “floor” comes closest to being a surface—like the gently undulating mesh in the great hall—other forms specialize in harvesting its outermost exudations.

#### The Armor-Crawlers

Those oval, car-sized animals that plod along the mesh are among the slowest vertebrate-analogues here.

- **Morphology:** Domed, multi-plated dorsal shells, ridged for strength, with flexible seams between. Their ventral side is a continuous, muscular pad surrounding a wide, grinding mouth. Short, thick limbs end in stubby, keratin-like pads that do not dig but merely push, brace, and adjust.
- **Feeding:** The mesh they traverse is the plant’s nutrient-transport lattice. Tiny pores exude a thin film of organic-rich slime; microscopic growths—algal analogues, bacterial films, protozoan cityscapes—cling to it. The crawlers strip this composite “biofelt” layer by layer, scraping with the horny plates around their mouths. They don’t tear the mesh; millions of years of coevolution have tuned the toughness of plant tissue to withstand a certain pressure, while the crawlers’ bite strength saturates just below that threshold.
- **Sensory world:** They are almost blind, eyes reduced to light-sensitivity. Most of their awareness comes from vibration through their shell and substrate: the feel of mesh tension changes, the tonal quality of the cathedral’s low chant shifting with structural stress. They don’t understand these shifts. They adjust their gait unconsciously, redistribute their weight, settle where resonance feels “right.”

Their life cycle is simple: hatch from clutches plastered in sheltered alcoves, graze in ever-widening loops, mate in slow circling paths where trails intersect, and finally halt somewhere when joints calcify entirely, becoming inert lumps that the plant overgrows. Their shells are incorporated wholesale into crushwood, faintly visible as differently shaded bands in the walls: old pilgrimage routes fossilized not in sediment, but in architecture.

#### The Shell-Clackers

Clinging to reed-like plant-tentacles in that tilted forest are small, shelled crustacean analogues:

- **Body:** Two-valved shells with jointed legs, each foot bearing a ring of hooklets that grip the plant-stems. Their internal muscles open and close the shells in regular cycles as they filter-feed from the water.
- **Sound:** The clacking is incidental. Shells meet and part at slight phase differences; thousands together form emergent polyrhythms. The plant, attuned to resonance, seems to “enjoy” this—not consciously, but growth is subtly reinforced in regions where the clacking produces particular frequencies that damp problematic standing waves in the tissue.
- **Life:** Shell-clackers hardly move once settled. Their larvae drift, bioluminescent pinpricks attracted to certain chemical cues leaching from the plant’s tentacle-forest. Once attached, settlement is for life. Their only drama is predation: occasional long, delicate snouts of eel-like hunters weave through, plucking individuals in single, careful bites.

### Hunters and Scavengers

Predation here is not about chase. It is about interception, pressure, and patience.

#### The Veil Stalkers

These are large, elongated predators, somewhere between eel and manta in form, that haunt membrane boundaries.

- **Form:** Flat heads widening into two flexible lobes, trailing a slim, muscular body and a fan-tail. Along their flanks run lines of pale, pulseable photophores that remain mostly dark. A hood of translucent skin can extend from their heads to drape over sections of membrane or mesh.
- **Strategy:** Veil stalkers have learned the plant’s habits. They wait where current plucks membrane or mesh into gentle bows and troughs. Prey—small grazers, detritus-feeders—tend to accumulate in those micro-shelters. When something passes through a membrane, its silhouette briefly distorts the tension profile; the stalker feels this through sensitive mechanoreceptors along its head. It then unfurls its hood, pressing it to the membrane, pinning a pocket of water and whatever is in it. A quick contraction forces that water—prey and all—into its mouth.
- **Relations with the plant:** The world-plant tolerates these compressions; its membranes flex without damage. In return, veil stalkers’ wastes—rich, pre-digested slurry—drift along regular paths, and the plant has placed absorptive filaments accordingly. No compact was ever signed, but structures have grown into mutual exploitation.

They do not glow much; their light is a thin, cold halo, used only for communication between individuals at mating time—rare meetings of two long, lonely hunters circling one another in slow, spiraling arcs before parting forever.

#### The Lurking Mouths

Some of the world-plant’s outgrowths have become almost animal-like on their own.

- **Maw-blooms:** Bulbous structures embedded in walls or ceilings, ringed with sensory cilia and lined internally with muscular tissue. They are not free organisms but specialized plant-organs. When water-borne chemistry or vibration suggests a weakened, dying animal nearby, small contractile fibers direct micro-currents to guide the carcass—or fragments—toward a maw. The bloom yawns open, pulls the mass in with a combination of suction and slow membrane waves, and closes to digest.
- **Symbiotic scavengers:** Tiny, mobile worms and ribbon-creatures live in the periphery of these blooms, feeding on scraps and guarding against opportunistic parasites. Some have evolved miniature “alarm” bioluminescent bursts when they detect large, active predators trying to tear at the maw for its contents. The plant responds by stiffening local tissues.

To an outsider, it might look as though the cathedral itself eats the dead. For the organisms born here, this is simply where bodies go.

### Microcosms in Pressure and Chemistry

Beyond size and shape, some inhabitants specialize in gradients invisible to sight.

#### The Seep-Drinkers

Those nearly invisible, threadlike forms tracing frost-patterns along walls where cold, mineralized seepage emerges are unique.

- **Metabolism:** They tap into geochemical energy—reduced compounds leaking from old, pressurized pockets beneath the original rock foundation, now filtered by deep plant tissue. Their cells are dense with enzymes akin to chemosynthetic bacteria on Earth’s vents, but slowed, regulated to the cooled, stable conditions here.
- **Structure:** Each thread is a colonial chain of modules, sharing both cytoplasm and a simple nerve-like conduction pathway. Contraction waves creep along them to regulate how much seep fluid enters at any point, keeping internal concentrations within tiny tolerances.
- **Ecology:** They do not participate in the general nutrient economy. What they extract is too exotic, too “raw” for most other organisms to use. Over time, they excrete minute waste granules that the plant sequesters into deep tissues as trace minerals. They are almost an alien ecology grafted into this one.

No one eats them. No one competes with them. Their existence is a quiet, parallel narrative.

#### Gradient Fish

Some species live lying along invisible lines—temperature, salinity, or chemical zones held in place by the larger structure.

- **Isotherm-tracers:** Slim, torpedo-bodied animals with rows of thermosensitive pores along their flanks. They spend their lives following a narrow temperature band that arcs through multiple chambers. To them, “direction” is defined less by wall and flow and more by the feel of slight warmth or coolness on either side. They spawn along the band. Their young instinctively seek it.
- **Salinity-weavers:** Smaller creatures—almost like translucent squid—rely on delicate balance in ion concentrations. Held in subtly stratified pockets, they beat their fin-frills not to move but to keep micro-strata intact around them, riding within self-made cocoons of preferred water. When they stray too near a membrane or throat where mixing is strong, they go still, letting the local currents “erase” their stratification, then slowly rebuild it when conditions calm.

These beings are awarenesses of gradients more than of space. They are inhabitants of invisible geography.

### The Lightbearers

Outside of faint plant-glow, bioluminescence is the dominant visible presence. It is not ornamental. It is currency, language, and sometimes weapon.

#### Scattered Lamps

The familiar elongated fish with pale lamps along their sides live solitary or in loose, repelling groups.

- **Purpose of light:** Their lamps are honest signals of size and health; brighter, steadier rows mean a well-fed, robust individual. Predators favor dimmer targets. This turns sustained luminescence into a handicap display—only the healthiest can afford it.
- **Spacing:** They maintain wide margins because light also draws parasites and opportunistic feeders. Each fish’s nervous system contains a simple avoidance algorithm keyed to the intensity and flicker frequency of neighbors’ glow. They drift like lone streetlamps on an unlit road, never close enough to form true schools.
- **Communication:** Courtship consists of rare pairings where two individuals align side by side, their lamp-patterns pulsing in staggered, slowly converging rhythms. Synchronization, when it occurs, triggers synchronous spawning.

#### Cloud-Sparks

The shrimp-like swarms that glitter green are more communal, but even their “clouds” are loose, three-dimensional veils.

- **Feeding:** They filter-feed in midwater, choosing zones where the plant’s membranes have created plankton-rich pockets. Their appendages are frilled, increasing surface area. They swim with jerky, upward-angled motions, then drift down, repeating in tireless cycles.
- **Light ecology:** Their static pinpricks of green flicker when they are startled, creating sudden sheets of sparkle as a disturbance passes through the swarm. Predators read these ripples to detect each other’s movement; some have learned to trigger false alarms by producing pressure waves of specific shapes, flushing prey into their mouths.
- **Life and death:** Cloud-sparks live quickly by local standards—months, not years. Their dead fall only short distances before snagging on some filament or mesh, feeding bacteria, which in turn nourish the plant. The swarm is a face of a continuity rather than a collection of discrete, long-lived individuals.

### Quiet Intelligences

Most life here operates on reflex and habit, but a few lineages show something more: extended learning, innovation on small scales.

#### Map-Keepers

Among the larger, more complex fish are a scattered species whose brains are disproportionately dense. They are mid-sized—perhaps two meters long—sleek, with minimal fins and clusters of sound-producing plates in their throats.

- **Sensing:** They navigate primarily by echolocation-like percussive pings. Each ping is a thud, a low-frequency pulse that reverberates through chamber and corridor. They “see” by listening to returns, constantly updating a three-dimensional model of their surroundings.
- **Memory:** Their hippocampus-analogue is large. Individuals retain detailed maps of enormous contiguous volumes, including minor structural shifts over their centuries-long lifespans. They can detour around newly collapsed micro-passages, find rare shortcuts through seldom-used slits, and remember where certain chemical anomalies appeared decades prior.
- **Behavior:** They are not social in the mammalian sense. Encounters are rare and brief: two map-keepers meet, circle once or twice, exchange patterned sequences of pings—compressing spatial information into acoustic codes—and depart, each slightly enriched. Their species’ “culture” is a diffuse, distributed atlas: no individual holds the whole, but every meeting cross-pollinates maps.

They have learned, for instance, which membrane-bounded pockets contain especially rich blooms in certain centuries, how the slow breathing of the architecture slightly widens some throats in a thousand-year rhythm. They do not conceptualize history, but they behave as if they anticipate patterns beyond their own lives.

#### Chamber-Tenders

Smaller, sessile or semi-sessile organisms show a different kind of cunning.

- **Pump-Clusters:** Those filter-builders that click open and shut to move water through their bodies do more than passively entrain to the chamber’s resonance. Over years, clusters experiment with phasing: some alter their timing, discovering that slightly asynchronous beats can pull more nutrient-rich water from certain directions. Successful phasing patterns spread as budding clones colonize adjacent patches, carrying their inherited rhythm tendencies. The result is chambers whose “song” has been locally optimized by an emergent, rhythm-based selection.
- **Gardeners:** In some tucked-away niches, small, ambulatory invertebrates—many-legged, spined, with ciliated undersides—actively manage micro-ecosystems. They tug at membranes, clearing silt; they transport eggs or spores of their preferred partner species; they nibble back invasive films that would smother their favored algae. Over millennia, tiny enclaves arise: patches of wall or mesh with a particular suite of co-adapted organisms, maintained constantly by these gardeners’ near-instinctual fussing.

To anything like a human observer, these behaviors might look like ritualized maintenance. Here, they are simply the end point of selection for those individuals that nudged their environment in ways that fed back into their own survival.

### The Absent Makers

Underneath all this, there is the unresolved question: was this architecture ever guided by minds that knew themselves as minds?

Hints exist, if you know what to interpret.

- **Non-functional forms:** In a few remote regions, deeply buried in the densest trunks, there are cavities that serve no obvious hydrological or nutritional function. Perfect spheres, or long, narrow cylinders aligned not with current but seemingly arbitrary angles. Their inner walls are lined with smooth, inert tissue, long dead but preserved. No organism enters; no flow passes. They are scars, or perhaps old rooms.
- **Genomic noise:** The world-plant’s DNA, if unraveled, would show sequences that code for organs that do not exist now: lens tissues without corresponding eye structures, pigment complexes tuned to wavelengths that never reach this deep, receptor families sensitive to electrical signals too fast for the current plant’s slow conduction system.
- **Behavioral vestiges:** Some animals respond with inexplicable avoidance to entire swathes of architecture that, chemically and mechanically, seem no different from neighboring zones. They do not cross certain arches, do not swim through particular rings. The plant itself does not extend membranes across these paths, as if leaving doorways open for tenants that no longer live here.

If there were once surface-dwelling forms that descended, or builders that seeded the original growth patterns, they have dissolved entirely into this cool, drowned continuity. What remains is a world that runs, not on active design, but on a design that has forgotten the difference between purpose and habit.

### Who Lives Here, Summarized

- A single, continental-scale vegetal organism (or tight guild of sister lineages) that has become both environment and metabolism: roots as buttresses, stems as corridors, leaves as vanished ancestors whose ghost-organs now power chemistries and faint glow.
- Flow-locked drifters—ribbons, barges, threadfins—that make stable currents their homes, becoming as cyclical and predictable as the flows themselves.
- Slow grazers that move like geological processes: armor-crawlers polishing organic stone, shell-clackers chiming in bent forests, all tuned to a world where substrate is never bare and food is always a thin film on living surfaces.
- Patient predators—veil stalkers, lurking maws—whose tactics hinge on membrane flex, pressure signatures, and the certainty that nothing ever changes fast.
- Gradient specialists and seep-drinkers, inhabiting differences so fine most senses would miss them, tracing invisible geography of temperature, salinity, and exotic chemistry.
- A few lineages with extended memory and learning—map-keepers and chamber-tenders—that, in aggregate, grant the cathedral something like awareness of its own interior distances and flows, but only as a side effect of organisms trying to survive.
- And everywhere, tiny assistants and opportunists: worms, films, bacteria-analogues, bioluminescent specks—all the uncounted micro-lives that make the macro-equilibrium possible.

No one here is restless. Lives are threaded through a fabric that has reduced surprise to a trace background noise. Birth, feeding, mating, death: each happens slowly, in patterns worn so deep they are indistinguishable from the architecture itself.

They do not live in the cathedral. They are the breath in its lungs, the murmurs in its stone, the passing, glowing motes in a body that forgot long ago what it meant to be young.
