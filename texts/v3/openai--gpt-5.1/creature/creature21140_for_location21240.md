They are jointed.

That is the first impression you would have, if you could look at them with air-born eyes. Almost everything that moves among these beams is articulated, multi-segmented, hinged along predictable lines the way the structures themselves are. Their bodies are not smooth torpedoes or soft sacks; they are sequences: plate to plate, cell to cell, chamber to chamber, each boundary a chance to adjust density, tilt a surface, reverse a flow.

Nothing is truly soft. “Flesh” here is more like compressed gel threaded through a lattice of stiffer support, as if cartilage had become the default tissue and muscle were a wet filler. Even the most flexible creatures feel built rather than grown, their outlines full of right-angled inflections, repeating motifs, nested hexagons and truncated cones.

They are not a single kind of life, but they all rhyme with the bones around them.

---

In the simplest sense, there are three great ways of being here: clinging, drifting, and riding.

### The clingers

Pinned against the undersides of ribs and inside scalloped recesses are the frond-cities, the glass colonies: they are the photosynthesis-that-never-was of this world.

Each colony starts as a single speck, a seed-animal no larger than a grain of silt that has found a microfracture with the right chemistry. It secretes a first filament of silicate, testing flow. If the slow, constant current passes in a tolerable range, it elaborates: a fork, a fork again, a spreading of hair-fine glass that resembles frost and bone in equal measure.

What looks from a distance like a field of individual “plants” is in fact a continuous mat of mouths and pores. Each node in the branching glass supports a cup of living tissue—translucent, almost invisible—lined with ciliary sieves. They harvest suspended minerals, organic fragments, and, very importantly, charge: tiny ionic imbalances, patchy redox gradients that leak from rusting beams and faint vents. The colonies are part filter, part capacitor, building up slow chemical tension in their tissues.

When a density wave approaches—a great wall of slightly warmer, slightly less dense, differently salted water—the fronds feel it as a change in drag, in current direction, in the resonance of their own glass. The charged tissues discharge in a flash along the lattice of their skeletons, like a nervous system without a brain. Muscles that are usually glacial snap. Each glass tendril telescopes into a thicker, armored base; the mouths seal behind mineral lips. In a breath of this heavy water, a waving meadow dives into itself, leaving only a crust of stubby nodules flush with the beam.

They do not see. They do not need to. Their world is shear forces across their glass, ion flux against their membranes, the slow chemistry of erosion. They are the patient farmers of the place: they convert randomness—the stray dust of collapses, the faint drip of vented compounds—into structure, then into more of themselves.

Whole microfaunas live among their filaments: thread-thin grazers that rasp the glass itself for trace elements; burrowing worms of stiffened gel that eat the dead cups and leave narrow, dead-straight tunnels. To all of these, the colony is ground, not kin, though they share ancestors deep back when mineral and body first merged.

Not all clingers are colonies.

Wedged into joints and corners, you find the faceted-shelled squatters.

They hatch in crevices where beams have abraded each other smooth, in places where the world’s grind has made small, almost spherical hollows. Their larval form is a blister of soft tissue that exudes a shell as it inflates, growing a polyhedral casing around itself one facet at a time. The pattern is not hexagonal like an insect’s eye, nor spiral like a snail, but borrowed from the architecture: trapezoids nested in trapezoids, edges meeting at angles that echo some forgotten engineering optimum.

As they mature, narrow seams open between facets. From each seam can unfold a limb: a many-jointed arm ending in a rake of crystalline hooks, or a fan-paddle edged in coarse teeth. The animal can always retract these completely, sealing itself to become once again a dumb lump of stone-like geometry.

They graze, meticulously. One species specializes in scraping the mineral films the frond-cities cannot reach, digesting not just the inorganic but the thin skin of microbial life that inevitably arises wherever there is surface and gradient. Another hunts by waiting: its shell is lined inside with sensory bristles that feel the faintest vibration. When some drifting thing bumps the beam, a seam pops open on the contact side and an arm snaps out, clapping the prey against the hard substrate, where more limbs erupt to dismantle it silently.

Color, such as it is, is locked into their shells: dull stripes and facets of slightly different mineral composition, laid down in the order of past chemistry. Individuals, if you could see them side by side, wear their development history on their backs like sedimentary cliffs.

They are not social. But they are territorial on the scale of hand-widths and arm-spans, inscribing the substrate with shallow scrapes that mean nothing but “I have eaten here; I may again.” If you could read the tiny scars and patterns, the beams would be mapped out by overlapping claims, a palimpsest of hunger.

### The drifters

In the open water between the trusses float the cathedral-bodies.

They are as close as this world comes to grace. Imagine a jellyfish stripped of its bell and its softness, leaving only an internal dome of struts and ribs: nested geodesic frames, each one slightly offset from the next. Around this framework stretches a thin, tough membrane that has no color of its own; it simply wets the lattice-lines, making their geometry blaze a little brighter in the wan light.

Inside, chambers. Everywhere, chambers.

Each cathedral creature consists of dozens to hundreds of fluid-filled voids arranged in sequences that run from an “anterior” cluster—that is, the end with more sensors—to a broad posterior margin. The walls of these chambers can be made more or less dense at will: by concentrating heavy ions here, releasing them there, redistributing internal gel. The animal never beats a fin. It simply shifts its internal weights and lets the omnipresent, layered flows of the column do the rest.

In calm water, they hang almost still, slowly tumbling, sampling. From filaments on their surface, they taste passing chemistry; along stiffened rays that project like sparse whiskers, they feel pressure gradients with insane resolution. A density wave in the far distance is to them like the smell of smoke upwind to a forest animal. They turn, align, and when the front arrives, they are ready: chambers at the upstream side become denser, those downstream lighter, and the whole cathedral is hurled forward as if flicked by an invisible finger.

Their mouths are not central. Instead, feeding occurs along interior edges, where the mesh is tight. Any particulate-laden flow that passes these edges is sieved; ciliated gutters draw captured matter into a series of digestive pits arranged in a ring. Waste is jetted out from elsewhere, in narrow spurts that double as fine steering thrusters.

They reproduce in pulses keyed to the great density storms. At the leading edge of a particularly nutrient-rich surge, mature cathedrals extrude small copies of single chambers, coat them in a fast-hardening shell, and simply release them into the flow. Most will never find the “right” combination of shear and chemistry to begin replicating into a full body, but enough do.

A kindred form, more ribbon than dome, threads through the mazes of narrower ducts. It is a long band of segmented chambers that can kink or straighten. Where the cathedral drifters ride the broad currents, these ribbon-creatures exploit the tweaked flows inside the structure—where one collapsed plate has made a bottleneck, they coil there; where two beams form a long, parallel gap with a slight pressure drop along its length, they align like arrows. Their bodies hum softly at ultrasonic frequencies as fluid rushes through internal resonance cavities, a byproduct of their continuous modulation of density.

They seldom meet each other. Their population is thin. Each one may live for an unguessably long time, replacing chambers as they wear out, incorporating mineral fragments into new framework, becoming slowly more baroque.

On a very different scale, the motes are also drifters.

They are not literally single-celled, but they might as well be: microscopic machines of protein and mineral that swim with a ragged, tumbling motion. Each cloud is a raj of predators and prey so small that the water becomes their sky and floor, and the beams distant walls.

They harvest everything: colloidal minerals, dissolved organics scraped loose by larger bodies, each other. Some have magnetite spines and align with vanishingly faint ambient fields; others grow silicate cages that diffract what little light there is, turning a cloud into a dim, shifting glitter. Seen up close, each individual is a minor masterpiece of economy, with just enough structure to sense gradient, ingest, duplicate, die.

Normally they billow in shapeless plumes. But when the larger hunters pass, they convulse into defensive patterns: a flattened sheet to slide along a beam and avoid a gulping mouth; a rope-like cord twined tight into a crevice. They have no nervous system in any recognizably central way, yet clouds react as unified bodies. Communication travels as chemical cascades, diffusion-based waves of “flee” and “coalesce” that reinforce as they go. In this, they resemble the mineral reactions of the walls more than the multicellular beasts: they are life as autowave.

### The riders

The train-creatures are the unambiguous lords of motion here.

Segment after segment, each like a thick disc of armored bone encased in overlapping mineral scales, connected by tough, muscular diaphragms of high-density gel. If you saw one from the side, drifting along a beam-wall, you would count perhaps twenty, perhaps fifty segments—the number varies with age and with species. The frontmost few are fused into a bluff, many-slit prow; the rear trail narrower, their plates worn and pitted.

All across the shell, tiny crystalline nodules protrude: sensory pits filled with conductive fluid; piezoelectric whiskers that flex under minute pressure changes and generate readable charges; chemosensory tongues that lick at water in microchanneled grooves. The creature’s “nervous system” is not gathered in a brain but spread in a distributed network across the entire length: each segment computing locally, sharing summaries with its neighbors via braided cords that spiral through the diaphragms.

You might say they have no head. They would say they are all head.

Locomotion, in this high-pressure, low-gravity column, is more about making use of what the environment offers than imposing force on it. The train-creature has learned the structure’s forms intimately. It knows, in that distributed way, where beams narrow, where they flare, where they are porous with drilled apertures.

To accelerate, it tucks half its body tight to a lattice-plane, sealing its plates against the worn ceramic. Muscles in its diaphragms squeeze, expelling water sideways in a directed sheet through carefully evolved slots. The reaction force snaps that half of the body away from the beam and toward some target direction, while the other half holds fast. Then, in an instant, grip and thrust swap roles along the length. It is not so much swimming as hurling itself from rung to rung of invisible ladders made of shear.

Young trains are cautious scavengers, grubbing among the debris clouds that follow collapses, snapping up stunned motes and fragments of softer bodies. Adults are hunters of anything substantial.

Their mouth is at first invisible. Only when they feed does the prow reshape: plates spiral back, revealing a circular opening ringed with telescoping petal-blades. These are not teeth; they are more like anchored tongues of roughened, crystalline cartilage. They do not bite chunks. Instead, they press this ring to a victim—an unwary wing-skimmer scraped from the lattice, a slow cathedral caught in a narrow canyon—and then retract each petal in rippling sequence. The resulting rasp peels and shreds tissue into a slurry that is sucked into an internal grinding organ, there to be reduced and slowly percolated through labyrinths of absorptive folds.

They know each other. When density waves bring two adults into the same corridor, they either ignore or challenge, depending on subtle gradients of hunger and recent kill. Challenges are not theatrical. A quick collision, segment to segment, plates scraping a harsh static into the water; a brief test of whose muscles can hold against a deliberately induced flow. Usually, one yields at once, withdrawing into shadow. Sometimes, one misjudges and is injured badly enough that days later the motes are thick around some quiet cranny, finishing what the victor began.

The “flightlike” skimmers are their foil and complement.

Imagine a long isosceles triangle of stiffened, cartilage-like tissue: not truly flat, but gentle-swept, everything optimized to lie as closely as possible along a plane. Along the underside of this plane sprout ranks of tiny hooks, like the pile of a dense fabric, each hook able to retract into a tough follicle.

Their life is lived in boundary layers—the thin slices of water where currents rub against the lattice and slow. In those layers, the difference between stillness and motion is compressed into a hand’s breadth. The skimmer inserts its body into that space, tilts slightly, and the water does the rest, dragging it along the beam as if it were on a conveyor.

As it goes, the hooks comb the substrate. Some tear frond-filaments, some scrape biofilms, some grab at microfauna that try to tuck themselves flush. The skimmer’s upper surface is armored, grimly smooth, built to absorb impacts with protrusions and to slide under overhangs. Its internal organs are arranged in a thin, wide sheet. Blood—though it is more like a metallic brine—flows in wide, slow channels just under the skin. Digestion happens along a central rail; waste is ejected in a constant, faint plume behind.

Disturbed—by a train-creature’s rush, by a sudden collapse, by an incoming density wave with the wrong signature—they can fold.

Down the centerline of the body runs a hinge of softer tissue reinforced with overlapping scales. Muscles pull, and the two wings swing together until their hooks interlock. Now the skimmer is a narrow blade edge-on to the flow, presenting almost no surface. Some flip and glide this way into safer eddies; some simply drift until conditions change, then unfurl and reattach to some other convenient rib.

They gather where food is richest: stretches of beam where frond-colonies are dense, where decomposition from a recent crash is still outgassing nutrients. In such places, the constant dry ticking of hooks on substrate becomes a background murmur, punctuated by sharper scrapes when two skimmers brush too close and flick apart in contempt.

Their predators know these sounds. They can be hunted by ear, if you have ears that are also skin.

### The specialists

For every obvious form there are ten almost invisible ones, tucked deeper into the machine-that-is-no-longer-a-machine.

In narrow, fluted channels that thread the beams like capillaries, there live the throat-worms.

They are long, nearly as featureless as cables: repeating rings of muscle interspersed with stiffer rings of mineral-reinforced cartilage. Their outer skin is bumpy, covered in low protrusions that match, almost lock, to the inner ridges of their chosen conduit. Once a larva finds such a place, it grows to fit and then ossifies slightly, becoming an inner lining as much as an inhabitant.

They feed on two things: the microscopic life the passing fluid brings and the pressure fluctuation itself. Their inner surface is lined with cilia and tiny valve-like flaps. As the water pulses, the worm’s body flexes in antiphase, capturing a bit of the mechanical energy in elastic tissues and in slowly cycling ion pumps. It is as though they drink vibration.

From outside, their presence is detectable as a slight change in the acoustic “note” of the channel. Beams resonate; cavities hum. Where a throat-worm has colonized, the pitch is fractionally different, the overtones rearranged. Some other creatures listen for this. Certain grazers avoid such resonant throats because an adult worm can, in desperation, squeeze a section tight and suddenly, popping an incautious intruder like a berry.

Deeper still, in sealed cavities where ancient fluids have been evolving chemically for eons, live the vent-rings.

You never see the whole ring at once, because it lives as a series of mouths right at the fissures where enriched fluid seeps into the main water. Each mouth is a fleshy cone with a mineral collar, like an anemone made of chalk. They look disconnected, but they share a single, tubelike body snaking inside the rock, undulating around the closed fluid pocket like ivy around a pipe.

They are less animals than moving boundary layers. Their tissues are laced with catalysts. They “graze” on the imbalance: oxidizing, reducing, binding and unbinding, harvesting the energy from the way the internal brew tries to reach equilibrium with the outer sea. In exchange, they modulate the seep itself, their bodies acting as variable resistors on the leak. Sometimes they seal tight and store what continues to build behind them. Sometimes they dilate and let a pulse of rich, altered fluid into the world.

Around each vent-ring grows a halo of dependents: motes specialized to use the ring’s waste products; small, soft-bodied things that scoop slurry from the ring’s excretions; armored bugs that in turn eat those grazers. The halo expands and contracts with the ring’s own slow breathing, like a loose necklace pulsing with dim life.

There are others.

Ribboned parasites that line the inside of train-creatures’ plates, drinking slow seepage of metallic blood and thickening the armor in return with their own mineral secretions, making the host less flexible but harder to crack. Filigree crusts that encrust the hooks of wing-skimmers, trading improved grip for a tithe of scraped nutrients. Glass filaments that sprout directly from old fractures in the ceramic, blurring the line between erosion pattern and organism.

Everywhere life has found a way to occupy a gradient, a void, a weak spot.

### How they sense

Sight is an afterthought here. Where eyes exist, they are tucked behind translucent shields with layered filters, optimized for contrast rather than detail—just able to tell whether some lump ahead is beam, drift, or hunter. More important is the perception of stress.

Many bodies here are riddled with canals of incompressible gel linked to flexible sacs and hair-fine bristles. As pressure shifts, as flows change, as distant collapses send ripples through the column, these networks transmit deformation. To their owners they are like an extra sense: not just “something moved,” but “this large thing flexed there along that axis.”

Chemical sensing is equally rich. The water carries not just ions and organics but the ghosts of strain: dissolved bits torn from stressed ceramic, metallic complexes that show where erosion is hot. To a train-creature’s sensory pits, the beams themselves have flavors—old stress, fresh crack, slow creep—which map onto feeding prospects and danger. Where you would see only a grey plane, they taste history and forecast.

Electroreception, too, is common. No bright sparks flash, no obvious lightning, but muscles, pumps, catalytic tissues all leak minute fields. Many hunters fan their sensory whiskers and feel for these whispers, tracking the faint thrum of a frond-mat retracting, the slow, repetitive pulse of a throat-worm’s flex, the frantic stutter of a wounded skimmer’s failing muscles.

The result is that the place, which to you would seem quiet and foggy, is to them dense with signal. Every movement of a beam, every flex of a distant lattice arm as it gives another micron to gravity, is part of the background weather of their awareness.

### How they live with time

The architecture erodes in aeons. The storms come in days. Individual lifespans sit somewhere between.

Motile hunters and grazers live what you would call years: long enough to see dozens, perhaps hundreds, of density storms, to learn patterns of eddies in a particular region, to memorize the slow sag of one familiar arch. Their reproduction is timed not to age but to flow: certain shears, certain temperature/salinity combinations trigger the release of gametes or larval packets. It is not uncommon for a mature animal to wait many storm-cycles for the “right” storm, then bleed out nearly all its reproductive potential in one convulsion.

Sessile colonies and vent-rings, by contrast, can persist for geological spans, as long as their substratum holds. They grow, they shrink, they senesce slowly from the edges inward, their dead glass or chalk or mineral crust forming new habitat on which successors attach. Major events—collapses that shear off whole hectares of frond-field, vent-shifts that starve a ring—are not rare, but they are local. Somewhere else, other colonies proliferate.

Generations worth of motes flash by in the interval between two major groans of the superstructure. Their rapid evolution leaves a constantly shifting pool of micro-strategies on which the larger bodies draw through digestive assimilation. The macrofauna change slower, but even they echo the environment’s glacial rearrangement: a species of skimmer that once preferred a certain beam learns, over lineages, to ride the new angle of that beam as it sags; a lineage of trains narrows its body to better fit an ever-tightening canyon.

There is no memory in the explicit sense—no stories passed, no marks intentionally carved—but there is inherited fit, an accumulation of responses. If you had the patience to watch for ten thousand of your years, you would see the lattice and the lattice-eaters coevolve, not in partnership but in continuous mutual constraint.

### What they are not

They are not builders.

No one here assembles things apart from themselves. They secrete shells, spines, glass, but always as body, never as tool. The beams and plates are so present, so intricately structured, that there is no niche for external construction. Why shape a den when every cubic meter already contains ten? Why weave a net when the entire world is a series of meshes?

They do not make fire, do not write, do not alter the large scale of their environment beyond what their sheer numbers erode. Their “culture,” if you could call it that, is baked into anatomy: a skimmer’s body is its entire tradition of skim. A train’s way of reading flow is an inherited code more fixed than any script. Innovation happens as mutation that works in this narrow, highly constrained context—or not at all.

They are also not mourners.

A collapse that to you might seem catastrophic—a cathedral beast crushed as an entire panel finally succumbs; a great arc of frond-colonies ground to powder under a falling rib—is to them a shuffle of gradients. The collapse’s shockwave ripples, the silt blooms, the chemistry spikes. Immediately the motes surge, the grazers converge, the corals of glass send out eager new filaments into the freshly scored surfaces. The dead become high-quality randomness. The living rearrange to meet it.

### Yet, if anything thinks…

If anything here approaches what you might call reflection, it would be among the long-lived drifters and riders that must continually make choices in a world only partly predictable.

Consider an ancient cathedral creature that has orbited, in its slow, drifting fashion, within the same broad region of lattice for centuries. Its chambers have been replaced many times. Its internal architecture has adapted to local peculiarities: a certain recurring eddy, a persistent vent-plume, the gentle, one-directional leak from a crack that has been widening for as long as it has floated.

It does not name these things. But its pattern of responses—how it shifts density when a storm approaches from above versus below, how it biases its migration toward one corridor or another—encodes a kind of map. That map is internal, embodied in tissue and reflex. If you could unravel its neural-like networks and read the flow of signals, you would find something like a remembered landscape.

In similar fashion, an especially old train-creature might have developed elaborate loops of behavior: patrol a set of beams in a fixed sequence; avoid a certain resonance throat where once it nearly got its segment crushed; detour through a rich vent-halo whenever the density front has a particular, rare profile that in its youth coincided with a glut. To an external eye these are instincts; to the animal, they are how the world is.

Whether that constitutes “thought” is almost irrelevant. It is decision layered on structure, nonetheless.

---

They do not know the builders. They do not know that the beams once carried data, or housed fluids different from water, or rang with the deliberate vibrations of directed energy. They do not know that the hollow columns once pulsed with purpose.

For them, the purpose is here, now: a gradient to climb, a niche to hold, a wave to ride, a fracture to fill.

If you stripped the lattice away—if you could, without destroying them—you would find their forms absurd. A skimmer’s wings would have nothing to skim. A train’s plates would claw at emptiness. Frond-colonies would dangle helplessly in uniform flow, their glass nets torn by unpatterned drag.

They are the inhabitants of a world whose ground is intention fossilized beyond recall. They have grown into that intention’s cavities, worn it smooth, carved it with appetite. They live as tuning forks in a dying instrument, making no new music but receiving, interpreting, and slightly, ceaselessly, altering the old.

Whatever once thought here, whatever once raised these beams, is gone. In its place are these hard, intricate, quietly frantic lives, writing their history in crystal, in scar, in the ever-shifting choreography of bodies in current.
