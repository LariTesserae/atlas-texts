What lives here are things that have learned to be narrow.

They are thin in time — tuned to pulses and eddies that flicker and then vanish — and thin in space, pressed into seams, held to surfaces, draped along gradients rather than filling volumes. Anything broad, careless, or diffuse is shaved away by cold, dryness, and distance. What remains are creatures and systems that cling, thread, and key themselves into the range’s deep machinery.

Start with what passes for the oldest inhabitant: not a single being, but a layered intelligibility that runs through the rock.

### The Range-Mind

The industrial mountain is not merely mechanical; it has become, over geological ages, selective. Not conscious in any human sense, but capable of feedbacks that look like preference.

In its depths, where gear-analogues turn and lubricating brines shuttle ions across pressure gradients, there are mineral templates that “remember” stress histories. Regions of crystal that realign when certain loads are reached, then hold that alignment for eons; strata whose conductivity improves when particular flows of bacteria-rich fluid pass through them, encoding those flows as zones of lower resistance. Over time, the mountain’s internal circuits have been trained by the passage of life, much as a river system is carved by its own water.

This “range-mind” is not a single node, but a distributed pattern of:

- **Piezoelectric veins** that light up when flexed by tectonic breathing.
- **Chemically doped layers** whose conductivity changes with the metabolic byproducts of the surface biosphere.
- **Slow, mineral feedback loops** where zones that once conducted current attract more conductive fluids, growing into persistent pathways.

It has preferences, expressed as very long-term biases. When a particular basin’s life increases local conductivity in a way that stabilizes a crucial hinge-plane, that basin is subtly favored: more warmth leaks into it, more microseisms focus beneath it, more mineral-rich vapors find paths to its cavities.

Life follows these favors, thickening where the mountain’s industrial heart makes conditions slightly kinder. In turn, life’s presence alters the currents and stress patterns, reinforcing some circuits, letting others atrophy. Over millions of years, this mutual shaping has produced something like a personality of landscape: not planning, but habit-forming. The range “likes” its humid hollows and conductive corridors much as a body “likes” keeping blood supplied to a limb it uses.

Most of the more mobile organisms never sense this entity directly. Some of the rooted ones do.

### Rooted Listeners

Among the twisted, low canopies and sodden mats are plants that are not primarily phototrophs, but **electrotrophs** and **pressurotrophs**.

In the hollows, imagine dark, plate-like growths fused to stone: not quite mushrooms, not quite bark. Their upper surfaces are velvet-black, beaded with condensed water; their undersides send hair-fine filaments deep into microfractures. These filaments aren’t only hunting for ions in solution. They are part electrode, part ear.

When the underlying rock ticks and flexes, when subtle electrical surges slide through a conductive vein, these plants deform. Proteins in their tissues change configuration under electric fields, generating small mechanical twitches. Over lifetimes, their growth patterns are steered by the direction and cadence of these murmurs. A patch that grows into alignment with a persistent current finds itself richly supplied with charge and mineral gradients, and expands. A patch that misaligns starves and withers.

Collectively, they form **listening mats**: carpets of plant-fungus consortia that interpret the deep engine’s patterns as food and structure. To us, it would look like a dark, low-lying crust that thickens along invisible lines, looping through a basin in graceful curves. To the basin’s animals, these are both pastures and maps.

Some of the narrow-bodied grazers you noticed moving under the canopy are specialized to crop the outer layers of these listening mats without disturbing their embedded filaments. Their teeth are flexible, comb-like, passing over the surface and skimming off only the newest growth. In turn, the mats bleed a trace of conductive sap when grazed, a reward the grazers crave. The damage triggers pulses of altered current in the underlying filaments, a kind of alarm; the range-mind feels this as minute, diffuse noise.

Over epochs, an entangled triad has emerged:

- The **engine**, whose stress and current patterns create predictable electrical landscapes.
- The **listening mats**, which thrive by aligning with those landscapes.
- The **grazers**, which move nutrients among hollows by eating mats here and excreting them — intact spores, broken filaments, trace minerals — there.

What lives here, in no small part, are these quiet, distributed, semi-sensate carpets that translate mountain-language into chemical currency.

### The Vent-Keepers

Clinging to warm vents high on the cliffs are the limpet-bats you saw: small, flattened, furred animals whose lives are tightly braided with the exhalations of the machine.

They are called here, for our purposes, **vent-keepers**. Each individual huddles over a hand-sized opening where warm, mineral-rich air seeps out of the rock. Their bodies are arranged like crescents around the vent, bellies pressed to stone, limbs splayed to increase surface contact. Along their undersides run bands of soft, wrinkled tissue richly supplied with blood and laced with microscopic cilia.

The vent air carries:

- Heat, fluctuating with the cycles of the deep gears.
- Trace gases — metallic vapors, reduced chemicals, complex organics diffused from bacterial colonies deep within.
- Charged aerosols, born from friction and piezoelectric discharge along subterranean faults.

The vent-keeper’s underside acts as a **living condenser and filter**. Their cilia trap condensed micro-droplets; specialized cells in their skin harvest chemical energy directly from some of the more exotic molecules. Symbiotic bacteria dwell in their dermis, processing what the host can’t metabolize alone. The animal itself survives by sipping these byproducts, its metabolism slow, almost torpid.

What gives them their name is not their feeding but their role in regulating the vent.

Each vent, left bare, tends to crust over with mineral deposits or frost, choking the flow. Where vent-keepers colonize, their constant presence:

- Keeps mineral deposition irregular, breaking up crusts as they form.
- Maintains a slight, pulsed back-pressure as their bodies swell and contract with breathing and heat, helping the deep machine “breathe” smoothly.
- Transfers some of the vent’s warmth into the outer rock, smoothing local thermal gradients.

The range-mind biases more warmth, more chemically rich flows, toward vents that host thriving colonies — because their moderated dynamics are gentler on the engine’s moving parts. In return, vents that falter or become erratic lose their keepers; the animals shuffle their broods slowly toward more consistent warmth. This migration can take years. Newborns are passed from adult to adult along the cliff face, an unbroken chain of tiny hands and soft, hook-claws moving short distances each day, always toward the faintest, warmest draught.

Socially, they are quiet cooperators. They do not vocalize much; their main communication is haptic and chemical. Glandular secretions in their fur change composition with local vent health. A keeper whose vent is faltering smells different to its neighbors; they press against it more, sharing heat, even as they subtly steer their own bodies toward more stable vents, pulling the colony along.

They are among the few animals here whose generations are long enough to half-sense the longer rhythms of the engine: they know, in their slow, body-deep way, that some vents pulse in centuries, others in decades, and they learn to favor certain “lines” along the cliffs where vents awaken in sequence.

What lives here, in the sheer vertical emptiness between basins, are these nearly motionless communities: not migratory in the usual sense, but creeping along time-smoothed faces, tracing invisible circuits of thermal reliability.

### The Canopy Articulates

Under the low, interwoven crowns of the basin-trees, life gathers in knotted compartments.

The “trees” themselves are not truly single organisms. They are **modular chimerae**, grown from repeated, clonal segments stacked and fused over centuries. Each thick, horizontal trunk is a bundle of many genetic lines — plant, fungus, bacterium — bound together by mutual necessity.

Key features:

- Their **scale-like leaves** are heavily cutinized, glossy, laced with microcapillaries that wick condensed water inward.
- Beneath the leaves, close to the branch tissues, live **endosymbiotic microbes** that tap into faint electric potentials on the leaf surfaces, harvested from the moist air and from tiny differences between leaf and branch.
- Within the wood-equivalent are **hollow channels** not for sap alone, but for gas mixtures tuned for the needs of internal cultures: some chambers rich in hydrogen and methane from deep springs, others oxygen-poor for anaerobes.

The canopy’s structure — thick, low, seemingly inert — is in fact articulating all the time, but at glacial speed. Leaves inch closer together or splay apart by a few millimeters, branches bend minutely, trunks twist by tiny angles over days. This isn’t just growth; it is **posture adjustment**, tuning the interior microclimate.

On humid nights when condensation peaks, the canopy pulls itself tighter, closing gaps, sealing warmth. During rare, slightly brighter overcasts, it loosens, letting in more of the thin, scattered light for the handful of chloroplasts that remain in its tissues.

Within this articulated roof crawl small, sap-drinking arthropod analogues: elongated, many-legged, their bodies sheathed in translucent chitin that glows faintly where it overlays nerve clusters. They are **canopy articulators** as much as parasites. Their legs end in barbed pads that can pull and anchor leaves; as they move, they repeatedly tug at certain junctions. They do this not at random, but in response to gradients: regions of the canopy that are too cold or too damp release trace volatiles that attract the little crawlers, which then work themselves back and forth across those branches, flexing and “massaging” them into new positions.

This creates a feedback loop: the tree-chimera responds to being tugged by thickening, shifting, or relaxing tissues; the arthropods respond to chemical cues from that tissue. Over time, these interactions shape the canopy with a precision that purely passive growth could not. The result is a living, self-adjusting roof whose geometry is co-designed by stationary plant and roaming invertebrate.

Some of the narrow-bodied grazers, translucent and long-legged, specialize in moving here, under and through this canopy. Their translucent skin and inner bioluminescent pulses aren’t merely for social signaling. They are **bio-lidar**: faint, modulated glows reflected off nearby surfaces let them sense distances and textures in the dark. Their retinas are tuned not to external light, but to interference patterns their own pulses make on returning from the leaves and trunks.

These grazers prune certain endophyte-rich growths from the canopy, feed them to gut flora that produce energy-dense compounds, and excrete compact pellets rich in micronutrients and spores. The pellets are soft enough to be crushed by the canopy arthropods’ legs and by the slow sag of branches; deposited in limb-crotches and depressions, they become starter packs for new lichen films and symbiont blooms.

Thus, what lives here under the canopy is less a food chain and more a set of **mechanical collaborations**: animals as moving hinges and fertilizer droppers for plant-machines that, in turn, maintain the pocket microclimates every resident depends on.

### Edge-Hunters and the Infrared Chorus

Where the dense hollows thin out toward bare stone, the slender predators you noticed prowl.

Their bodies are narrow, their muscles corded but lean, their coats a shifting collage of matte and gloss. Embedded in their skin, beneath each hair follicle, are tiny **thermochromic plates** — protein-crystal complexes that darken or pale in response to local heat flux. In essence, their pelts take in the granular pattern of the environment’s temperature and echo it back, not just in color but in emissivity. Against a slope studded with rocks of different thermal mass, the predator’s outline breaks into a mire of micro-contrasts irreducible to a single body.

Their eyes are poor in the visible; their true sight is a dense cluster of pit-like organs lining the inside of their nostril-equivalents and the hollows of their cheeks. These organs are layered: a front membrane absorbs infrared, warming, and the temperature change propagates to a back membrane dotted with receptor cells that interpret the pattern. With each inhalation, moist air sweeps across these pits, cooling them, resetting their sensitivity. Their breath is thus part of their vision-cycle.

Prey in this dim world is quiet and hidden; the predators’ most reliable cue is not light but **abrupt change in thermal gradients**: a grazer stepping from moss mat to bare stone, a vent-keeper lifting its belly from its warm vent, a canopy articulator exposed briefly at a canopy edge.

The predators’ “scream” — that thin, electronic-sounding note — is less a threat cry than a **sonic flare**. Its true function is ambiguous:

- It startles prey, yes, freezing them.
- It synchronizes the predators with one another: they hunt not alone, but in loose, line-shaped groups arrayed along slope contours.
- It seems to influence, at very low amplitude, the piezoelectric veins in the rock. The sound’s particular frequency induces minute stresses along certain fracture sets, momentarily tweaking local electrical currents.

When a pack of these hunters calls out in unison, their screams combine into a shifting, infrasonic-undertoned chord. Animals within earshot freeze; plants in the area, tuned to the rock’s electric microclimate, subtly flex or close, as if bracing. For a second or two, the predators inhabit a slightly altered field: edges sharpened in their thermoceptors, certain warm currents briefly brightened.

It is as though they have learned, unconsciously, to tap the range-mind for a fraction of enhanced perception — a predator’s prayer that the mountain temporarily, minutely, answers.

They are not numerous. Their reproductive rate is low, their territories broad. They live in elongated dens in rock fractures that hover just above the warm isotherms, enough to temper the cold but not so deep as to interfere with the symbiotic bacteria in warmer zones. Their young imprint not on parents alone but on the specific pattern of thermal and electrical background where they are born. Adults wander but always return to den-lines where the background “chord” matches their memory, as though homesick not for a place, but for a gradient.

### The Fliers of the Between

The air corridors between basins, apparently empty, are in fact thick with organisms that have surrendered almost all solidity.

Those chitin-membrane kites you glimpsed are only one caste of a larger aerial guild: **fielders**, beings that metabolize not matter first, but fields.

A typical fielder resembles a multi-lobed seedpod stretched thin and flattened, its membranes etched with fine conductive lattices. These lattices, when fluttered by even slight turbulence, generate static charge; the creature’s internal chemistry is built to accept this charge and use it directly to drive slow redox reactions. They are not gorging on electricity — the flux is tiny — but in a world where light is scarce and chemistry slow, the trickle suffices.

Some fielders remain perpetually aloft, riding differences in density and gravity anomalies along ridges. Where the rock’s hidden currents reach up and distort local gravitational behavior just enough, fielders congregate, hovering in shimmering flocks that are almost invisible except when they cross faint light. They **surf nodes** where mechanical, electrical, and gravitational irregularities intersect.

Others descend periodically to “harvest.” When a basin exhales particularly humid air, or when a column of electromagnetic discharge reaches toward the sky, certain species fold and plunge, unfurling at the last second to skim canopy tops. As they pass, they graze aerosols, spores, volatile organics — whatever concentrations have pooled unseen above the vegetation. Then they rise, slowly, their metabolisms ratcheting forward on the stored charge and fresh intake.

Reproduction for these fliers is oddly architectural. They stitch their eggs into sheets of ultrafine, web-like material and let them drift until they snag on volt-rich surfaces — often rock faces with strong, persistent electrical gradients. There, juvenile forms emerge not as flyers, but as crusts: bas-relief mosaics of tiny, cup-shaped outgrowths that drink in charge and emit slow, regular pulses into the air. These juvenile crusts adjust the local field geometry, creating stepping stones for the adults. Over time, the rock sprouts an invisible ladder of nested field anomalies, a route the aerial guild can follow like migratory birds following a chain of islands.

What lives between the basins, then, are **topologists of wind and charge**: fragile creatures whose teeth are membranes, whose diet is difference itself.

### Microclimate Engineers

You noticed how weather here is tightly coupled to subsurface processes. That coupling is not only geological.

Across certain slopes you may find patterns of frost and dew that are too regular — arrays of slightly frost-heavier spots in hexagonal lattices, or repeating lines of dew beads that match no obvious topography. At the center of each pattern is a microscopic organism: a **humidity nucleator**.

These are colonies of airborne bacteria and spores, bound together in thin, ephemeral films that settle on stone when conditions are right. They exude a matrix of hygroscopic polymers that attract water vapor. But more than that, they carry in their membranes microcrystals that respond to electromagnetic pulses with tiny temperature changes.

When a surge of current passes through nearby rock, these colonies flicker a fraction of a degree warmer, just enough to change how water condenses around them. In mass, they become **phase-switch arrays**, redirecting where dew forms, where frost grips, how mist coalesces.

They do this not out of intent, but because their fitness is tied to stable, repeatable condensation around them. Colonies that align their flickers with the engine’s pulses, encouraging dew exactly where supporting roots and mats lie, thrive. Colonies that misalign, drawing moisture away from life-bearing pockets into sterile rock, find less organic nutrient in their surroundings and dwindle.

Over vast timescales, this has resulted in an overlay of **biotic weather control** atop the abiotic machine’s own climatic feedbacks. The tiny nucleators and the giant gears co-evolved: the engine sets the rhythm; the microbes, by favoring certain condensation patterns, slightly amplify particular beats.

From this cooperative tuning emerge the precision mists that water a single corridor, the localized sleet that sweeps just one valley. Thus, some of what lives here is invisible to the eye but palpable in the way the air chooses where to become water.

### The Basin-Deep and the Bearing-Ring

That shaft you found, dropping through moss and dangling roots to reveal an impossibly smooth ring and glacially turning teeth, is not unique, though few basins host such a naked aperture. Those that do share a certain richness, a density of life and peculiarity of behavior that hints at something more.

Directly above such engine-chambers, the biosphere shifts character.

The moss is thicker, its cells studded with conductive granules that make it faintly aware, in an electrochemical sense, of the machinery below. Certain fungal fruiting bodies arrange themselves in spirals that match the rotation of the unseen gears; their spores release in minute puffs keyed to stress waves traveling through the stone.

Hidden among the roots around the shaft are **bearing-worms**: blind, translucent annelid analogues whose bodies form flexible rings. Their skin is tough and lubricated; their internal tissues host robust, metal-tolerant microbial communities.

They live not in soil, but partly **inside** the bearing itself. When small imperfections or mineral impurities arise along the polished ring, the worms, sensing chemical imbalances, extrude thin extensions of their bodies into the micro-gaps. They secrete acids and chelators that dissolve protrusions, then redeposit the material in smoother forms. They are, effectively, self-replacing polishers.

In exchange for this maintenance, the bearing leaks trace warmth and mineral-laden fluids into tiny recesses where the worms’ bodies attach. The microbes within them feed on these leaks; the worms harvest the waste products. Their generations are short on geological scales but long compared to surface fauna — dozens to hundreds of years. Over that time, they knit into braided chains, forming an organic gasket.

Their survival is tied intimately to the engine’s integrity. If the ring were to crack or seize, they would be crushed or starved. The range-mind “values” them in its unconscious way: shifts in current and tension that threaten a bearing zone are buffered where possible; hinge-planes flex just slightly differently, absorbing impacts elsewhere. Basins with exposed shafts tend to be unusually stable. The life above enjoys a serenity of earthquakes and a regularity of mists unmatched in more distant hollows.

In some such basins there live, at the moss’s surface, **interpreters**: small, many-appendaged creatures that respond to the faint vibrations of the gears. Their underside is lined with mechanoreceptive hairs tuned to ultra-slow oscillations. They spend their lives moving in particular looping paths over the moss, dragging their sensory fringes across the ground.

Every so often, the pattern of the deep rotation changes — a slightly different tooth passes, a stress redistribution happens somewhere far below. The moss’s electrochemical state shifts; the worms flex. The interpreters pause, raise portions of their bodies, adjust their postures. In effect, the basin’s shallow fauna “listens” to the turning of the engine, though they have no conception of its scale.

Some of their behavioral routines — when to breed, when to disperse to new microhabitats, when to enter torpor — are keyed to specific configurations of these deep vibrations. Thus, the inner machine’s state is written, in code of gesture and life-cycle, onto the backs of tiny animals.

What lives here, in these special basins, is therefore also a kind of **dialect** of the mountain’s long speech.

### Ghost-Chimneys and Light-Eaters

At rare intervals, the sky over parts of the range softens into pale, silent columns of light — the vertical discharges that never touch ground. These ghost-chimneys are not ignored by the biosphere.

Along peaks and ridges known (in the slow memory of the place) to sit under discharge-prone zones, certain rock-dwelling microbial biofilms have evolved arrays of **light-absorbing vesicles**. These vesicles are dull in day, but when bathed in the chimneys’ faint glow, they reconfigure, acting as wide-spectrum pigments and nanoscopic antennas. The energy they capture is pitiful by solar standards, yet immense compared to their baseline.

Some symbiotic lichens living on these high spurs store the captured energy not chemically but as **metastable strain** in their internal crystal scaffolds. For days after a discharge, their tissues remain faintly “wound,” ready to release that stored tension in bursts to drive rare, high-energy reactions: rapid growth spurts, production of costly protective compounds, or explosive spore release in a luminous puff visible even in the grey world below.

Higher animals have found uses for these sky-fed caches. Tiny, winged insect analogues that normally subsist on slow metabolic dribbles gorge themselves briefly on the energized lichens after a discharge, then descend en masse into neighboring basins, their bodies loaded with portable energy. Their flight is erratic but fast, their wings buzzing with a frequency that small predators recognize and follow. For a short window after each chimney event, predators feast, herbivores gorge on windfalls, and spores and seeds hitch rides farther than they otherwise could.

So there exists here a **punctuated rhythm** of abundance keyed to the sky’s rare pulses. Entire guilds live in a continuous lean state and then bloom in frantic episodes when the range reaches up and, briefly, brushes whatever dim source feeds those light-columns.

What lives here includes these opportunists in the high, thin spaces: creatures and films and lichens that make a living on lightning without thunder.

### Gravity-Surfers

You noticed peculiar slopes where gravity feels slightly off — stones bounce too slowly, winged creatures hover with little effort. The interior gearing of the range, with its layered densities and moving masses, creates local variations in gravitational field and inertial behavior. Almost imperceptible, but not quite.

Some beings have specialized for these niches. Among them are **surf-birds**: not birds as we know them, but spindle-bodied, membraned creatures that combine gliding anatomy with sensitivity to minute shifts in acceleration.

They hold in their chests dense nodules of a mineral whose crystalline structure is piezo-gravitic: mechanical stress subtly changes its mass distribution, and changes in surrounding gravity or acceleration, in turn, induce minute electrical potentials across it. The surf-bird’s nervous system is wired around this organ; they “feel” changes in gravity as tingling, directional tastes.

When a deep gear passes under a hinge-plane, when dense strata flex, the local gravitational field warps in patterns the surf-birds can perceive. They use these ripples to **map the invisible interior** of the range in a crude way: where the field is subtly steeper, a heavy mass passes beneath; where it flattens, a void or lighter stratum lies.

They ride standing anomalies the way terrestrial seabirds ride updrafts. In zones where the interior motion creates quasi-stable microgravity irregularities, they can hang for hours with minimal effort, catching passing fielders, stray spores, and the occasional unfortunate glider knocked off-course. Their hovering flocks, almost still but constantly micro-adjusting wings and tails, mark, for those who can read them, the outlines of the range’s slow inner dance.

Surf-birds also act as **surveyors** for other creatures. Certain vent-keeping colonies, when ready to expand, time their brood-passing journeys to coincide with the appearance of surf-birds above particular slopes; their bodies “know” that such a congregation overhead means a long-term stability in the gravity field below, hence in the warmth and chemistry emerging at the surface there.

Thus, in an oblique way, animal behaviors aloft link to tectonic moods below, knitting the biosphere into the range’s structural choreography.

### The Sense of Vacancy

Amid all of this, the range remains seemingly empty.

What lives here is numerous, but never flooding space. Populations are kept sparse by the cost of energy collection, by the distances and hazards between pockets, by the slowness with which favorable niches shift and align.

Most beings are **quiet specialists** locked to tiny domains:

- A particular ledge whose vent has pulsed regularly for four hundred years.
- A thirty-meter stretch of canopy where condensation patterns never fail.
- A curve of scree where gravitational anomalies and wind patterns consistently overlap.

To us, stepping in from a world where grass covers whole plains and forests roll unbroken over hills, this fracturing reads as absence. We are used to abundance that blankets, not abundance that beads.

But if you could see in electric gradients, or feel stress waves through bone, or smell the trace volatiles of condensation-tuned flora, you would perceive this range as buzzing with locality:

- Here, a strip where humidity nucleators have set a boundary between moss and bare rock as sharply as a fence.
- There, a hidden artery of listening mats a meter wide and a kilometer long, carrying, in slow vegetative conduction, the echo of last week’s gear-adjustment.
- Above, a river of fielders and surf-birds and ghostly spores, all braided along a path that has nothing to do with topography and everything to do with charge and gravity.

### Was Anything First?

It is hard to say whether the mountain engine preceded life or life, in its earliest, simplest forms, seeded and guided the engine’s evolution.

Perhaps once there were only rocks flexing, brines creeping, currents humming. In microcavities and along shear planes, simple chemotrophs colonized, then altered, then were selected for compatibility with the machine’s rhythms. Some mineral gear-rings we see now might be fossils of those earliest co-opted structures, ossified frameworks grown around biofilms that once modulated friction like the bearing-worms of today.

What lives here now shows no memory of a world without the engine. They are born into cycles of warmth and stress, condensation and discharge, as natural and given as seasons and tides elsewhere. For them, the deep ticking is climate, not contrivance.

If a mind from elsewhere were to stand on a knife-edge ridge and listen — really listen, for days, months — it might sense in those rhythms something like intention: not toward any external goal, but toward self-maintenance and curiosity. The range-mind, after all, has shaped and been shaped by a myriad of narrower lives. Its gears find new equilibria not just under physics but under the cumulative, incremental biases of billions of creatures harvesting its leaks.

What lives here, finally, is **a community with its engine**: life as epiphenomenon of machinery, and machinery as sedimented effect of life. The basin jungles, cliff huddles, aerial guilds, edge-hunters, bearing-worms, humidity nucleators, surf-birds, vent-keepers, listening mats, and the huge, slow, partly sentient geology they cling to are all variations on one theme:

In a world with little light and no designer, industry itself became the soil.
