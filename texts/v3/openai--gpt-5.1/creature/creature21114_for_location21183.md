They are small against their own work.

Begin with their lungs, because that is where this place begins in them. They are built around breath: around thin, dry air that bites the throat, around the need to draw it in deeply and deliberately. Their chests are wide, ribs sprung; shoulders strong but narrow, built not for lifting weights but for lifting the cage of bone itself again and again without waste. You can see, even in stillness, the faint expansion of the upper ribs and the hollowing under the collarbones, a practiced economy of inhalation.

They are not tall. Height is a liability on ledges where wind can use your body as a sail. Their proportions trend compact—legs sturdy, torsos a little long, arms close. It is the sort of stature grown by a people who have spent their lives in stairways and on broken slopes, who know that a lower center of gravity means one more life saved each time the air tries to take you.

Their skin is the color of the rock when it is dry: gray-brown, gray-olive, sometimes shading toward the warmer tones of the humus in the carved stone bowls. It is hard to measure “pale” or “dark” here, where the light itself has little warmth; what matters more is texture. Their skin is thickened where the wind hits most—bridge of the nose, cheekbones, backs of hands. The constant cold and near-constant damp have given them a fine, tough grain, as if their faces had learned something from the moss: how to hold moisture just under the surface, how to resist being flayed by air.

Hair, where it exists, grows close and heavy, not in long banners that the wind could seize. You might think at first that many of them are shaven; in fact, their hair rarely grows beyond the thickness of a finger before it’s trimmed, matted, or braided tight to the skull. Color ranges within the same stone-sober palette: black faded to charcoal, brown dulled to weathered bark, occasional threads of cool-silver that shine only when the rare direct sun catches them.

In their faces, the most striking feature is the eyes. Not because they are large—they are not; large eyes leak heat—but because of the way lids and brows work together to narrow the field of open exposure. Lashes are thick; brows are like short, stiff brushes. They can half-close their eyes to mere slits and still see cleanly through wind-driven condensation and drifting mist. The iris colors are muted—stone, ice, peat—but the sclera takes on a faint bluish tint at the periphery, the mark of a lifetime spent walking the edge of hypoxia.

Their hands are square and deft. Fingers not long, not short, but capable of both blunt pressure and delicate pressure, the way a sculptor’s hands must be. Palms are callused in curious places—not on the heel from tools, for there are none as you would name them, but along the side of the thumb and the first knuckles, from bracing against stone; ridge-like pads across the tips of fingers, from endless contact with wet rock, fine cracks, living cushions of plant. The back of the hand is furred with tiny, pale hairs, more for sense than for warmth: they feel air moving before they feel air striking.

They walk as if their bodies were always in negotiation with the ground. Feet set down not heel-first, but almost flat, weight tasting the surface before it commits. The ankles are surprisingly strong, the calves roped with quiet muscle. You do not see a “stride” so much as a sequence of controlled falls, each caught and redeemed at the last instant. This is not nervousness; it is learned relationship with slopes that can crumble, moss that can slip, ice that can betray.

They grow slowly. Childhood here is long, and not merely because the world is harsh. The built stone around them changes at a rate that only reveals itself over years; so do they. Bones lengthen reluctantly, trading speed for density. A ten-year-old is still small enough to vanish into a wall crack; a twenty-year-old may yet be mistaken for an older adolescent. True maturity—where limb and lung and sense all finish their patient negotiations with this place—comes late, closer to the end than the beginning of what you would comfortably call youth.

Because everything else here is frugal, the bodies that live in it are frugal too. They carry more fat under the skin than you would expect in so lean a world, but it is tucked close to the core: around the kidneys, spine, heart. On the surface, they look wiry, almost spared-down. Their warmth is inward, not on display.

They smell faintly of stone and soaked lichen, of the clean animalness of bodies that sweat rarely and in small amounts, because every exuberant use of water is a risk. When they do sweat, it is along the back, between shoulder blades—the place where inner heat meets the constant chill—then wicks quickly into woven plant-fiber wraps and evaporates into the restless air.

They live inside the rock, but they are not moles or troglodytes. Their days are vertical.

From the basin floor to the highest carved terraces, their lives are strung along the city’s lobed heights like beads. Their dwellings—if you insist on distinguishing them from other hollows—tend to cluster not at the very bottom or along the exposed rim, but in the mid-level bands where seepwater is steady, wind is strong enough to clear air but not so brutal as to scour flesh, and light reaches reliably in its cool, diffuse fashion.

A “house” is not a discrete unit with walls, a roof, a door. It is a stretch of continuous stone that, by consensus and habit, belongs to certain people. One room will open onto a ledge that several neighboring households share; another room will communicate fluidly with a passage that also leads to storage cisterns and communal work cavities. Boundaries are understood as gradients—this is more ours than yours, but never entirely so. You mark such thresholds not by built partitions but by how the surfaces change: a slightly different smoothness where a hand more often rests, a pattern of shallow cup-like impressions in a wall that only members of that dwelling use to hang their wrappings or lay their tools-that-are-not-tools.

They do not own objects the way you think of objects. The rock is the main possession, and even that is not held as property, but as stewardship. What you can carry in your two hands at a steep incline is all the personal load they admit: a band of braided plant fiber for binding loads, a small pouch of dried cushion-plant mats and fungus for food, perhaps a carved stone token or two that speak of obligations and connections. You will not find piles of implements, racks of weapons, or chests of treasure. Anything heavy that they rely on is not moved; it is carved where it will be used, and there it remains.

What of clothing, in a place with no fiber trees, no flax fields, no animal herds large enough to clothe a city? They wear layers thin but cunning.

Closest to the skin: a soft, felt-like mesh grown rather than woven. It is a matting of plant fibers and fungal hyphae coaxed to intertwine over time, trained on the person’s own body when young, then periodically removed and re-formed as they grow. The material is surprisingly warm for its thickness, holding a thin layer of air in innumerable microscopic pockets. When wet, it does not cling coldly, but seems to shift its structure very slightly, opening to speed evaporation. Its color is the dun of dried moss, the green washed out by age and use.

Over this base, they wrap and bind longer, tougher strips of fiber and hide.

Fiber they have from certain cushion plants that produce long, flexible leaves with strong inner tissues; from tough moss-stems hardened in mineral-rich flows; from the wiry roots of cliff shrubs. They soak and twist and bind these into cords, braid the cords into broad bands. A single garment might be nothing more than a carefully knotted series of such bands crossing the chest and back, then spiraling around the thighs, leaving knees and elbows free so that nothing catches on stone.

Hide comes from the few larger animals they hunt—those goat-like ledge-walkers, occasional mountain birds, perhaps something more elusive in the lower valleys beyond the cirque. They use every scrap: thinnest membranes for patching water bladders, thicker pieces for knee-guards, elbow-guards, soles for foot-wraps. You will not see flowing cloaks whipping in the wind, only close-fitted pieces strapped down tight, shaped to break the edge of the air but not present the air with anything to grab.

Color is an afterthought. They stain some bands with mineral washes—iron-rich reds, manganese purples—not for decoration alone but as signals in a non-verbal code: a certain pattern of dark bands across the shoulders meaning “this person is trained to read the wind-shafts,” a particular streak along the forearm meaning “this one tends and measures the cisterns.”

Jewelry, as you would call it, is scarce and quiet. A ring of weather-smoothed stone around the neck, carved from a pebble that caught the maker’s eye. A twist of pale bone at the ear. Small things, heavy enough not to flap, worn as continuity rather than display.

They move through their settlement as if gravity had more directions than one.

Ladders as such do not exist; instead there are sequences of shallow notches in the rock—steps for those comfortable with emptiness under their soles—and more subtle features for the truly vertical. Their fingers know the patterns of slight protrusions that climb a wall where to you there is only stone. Those who are most at home in the heights hardly seem to climb at all; they simply rearrange which surfaces bear their weight, feet and hands a four-pointed logic that continually solves the problem of “up” and “down” anew.

Children learn these routes not late, not early, but gradually, as the city does not forgive haste. Their first years are not spent in flat courtyards—they do not exist—but in shallow, sloping spaces where a wrong step means only a tumble against rock, not a fall into emptiness. Elders, when their joints stiffen and their balance wanes, do not retreat. The city has gentle paths, long switchbacks and carved ramps, that they helped shape in their time; now they know all the slower routes, all the ledges where one may stop and lean and listen to the wind without being knocked from one’s feet.

They live with sound the way desert peoples live with light.

The city-sound is never absent. Wind and water and stone speak in an overlapping roar that would numb the sense of anyone not raised in it. For these people, that roar is the base tone onto which all meaning is layered.

They rarely raise their voices. Shouting would be swallowed and wasted. Instead, they have learned to speak not to the ears directly but into the fabric of the city’s noise. Their oral language, when used, is low and close and shaped. They cluster to communicate in eddies of relatively quiet air—under overhangs, in the lee of moss-curtains, at the mouths of side-passages where the main gusts shear away.

They do use words—but far less often than you might expect, and with a precision born of expense. A word is not to be thrown against the wind when a gesture or a shared reference to the sounds around them will do.

Most of their communication at distance is done by tuning into the already-present music of the stone.

Those carved vents and angled shafts that catch and funnel the wind are not mere conveniences; they are instruments. Long ago, their ancestors learned that by shaving a little more here, leaving a little more there, boring an extra narrow side-channel, they could cause this opening to hum when the wind was from the east, that shaft to drone at a lower note when the temperature dropped, another to only whistle in a certain pattern when thin threads were stretched across it.

So, in addition to the naturally occurring song, there is the song they have elicited.

Some families, some lineages, have “voices”: particular wind-shafts linked to their dwellings. When the wind hits right, those shafts speak in tones and patterns that the residents know as intimately as you know your own footsteps. By subtly altering the edges of those mouths—attaching or removing small hanging stones, adjusting the tension of moss-thick cords drawn across them—they can encode simple information into the day’s ambient noise.

A rise in pitch from the vent above the cistern galleries might mean “water level dropping faster than usual.” A rhythm shift in the long, bass moan that threads the mid-terrace sleeping hollows could mean “a group has left for the lower valley; expect return in four days.” They do not, cannot, use this system for gossip or chatter. It is a language of events and necessities, of warnings and invitations and the slow, repeated beats of tradition.

They hear the mountain the way others read a written script.

From childhood, they are taught to distinguish specific tones in the general howl. A child’s game: stand in the main forum with eyes closed and point the instant you hear the third note in the descending pattern of the north vent, then the second overtone of the shallow basin’s pooled water when a gust brushes it. They become, in effect, polyphonic listeners, their attention able to rest on several continuous strands of sound at once, picking meaning where you would only sense noise.

At close quarters, when even whispered words would tangle with surfaces and echo strangely, they use touch and small motions.

Two fingers lightly tapping twice on the stone between them: “listen.” A hand held palm-up, slowly turning palm-down: “wait.” A knuckle pressed to the sternum, then drawn outward: “I.” The same motion but done toward someone else: “you.” Over time, these gestures have elaborated into a whole kinesthetic lexicon that they can trade even across a room, as long as they share sightline. Should mist cut visibility, they fall into direct, brief speech near one another’s ears, phrases shaved of everything but core syntax.

Their language, when you strip it down, is shaped by the environment’s economy. Sentences are short. Many important concepts are expressed not as standalone words but as modifications of other words—tone, duration, and stress carrying as much load as consonants and vowels. Their speech, even in a story, has the cadence of wind dropping and returning, of drips accumulating into a momentary runnel then spreading again into a fine mist.

For all this, they are not humorless nor entirely solemn.

They laugh, but rarely out loud; more often laughter is a full-body event—a shut of the eyes, a brief folding inward, a release of breath that is more felt than heard. They tease one another with small, deliberate mis-synchronizations in the rock’s music: shifting a resonance so that, at the next gust, a familiar shaft voice “speaks” a wrong note, then watching as the others jolt, re-parse, realize the deception.

They have games played with pebbles flicked across grooves carved into sheltered walls: you must send the pebble dancing in a very specific sound-pattern by making it strike certain nodes in sequence, a kind of percussion puzzle solved both by hand and by ear. Children especially delight in the faint clatter such games produce—a daring puncture in the city’s roar.

What do they eat, in this sparse, wet cold, where agriculture in your sense is impossible?

They farm, but not fields. They cultivate niches.

In every thin bowl of humus, in every groove where water both arrives and drains, they guide and tend, never fully owning the growth but biasing it. Certain cushion plants respond to repeated, gentle compression by thickening their interior tissues and producing starch-rich swellings along their base. These, when teased free with careful fingers, provide concentrated food: bland but nourishing, something like a cross between a root and a mushroom. A ledge with fifty such cushions might be tended by the same family for generations, each cushion a living granary that must never be stripped bare at once.

They foster particular mosses that, under steady drip, sprout small fruiting bodies—capsule-like, almost berry-ish. These are not sweet; they are mineral, faintly sour, bursting with stored water and trace elements. They dry them on warm stone in certain angled cavities where rare direct sun can reach and heat the rock to a surprising degree. Once crisp, the capsules can be crumbled into powder, a flavoring and supplement for other, duller staples.

Fungi are their great partners.

The translucent frills you noticed in the miniature forests are not wild accidents; they are strains coaxed over time to live in specific carved shelves and bowls, fed with thin scrapings of dead plant fiber and the occasional shaved bit of scale from the stone itself. Some of these frills, when harvested young, are crisp and faintly sweet, eaten fresh. Others they allow to mature and sporulate into dust that they catch on seedheads of other plants, using the fungal spread as both soil-builder and co-symbiont.

They keep no herds, for there is no room, but they have relationships with the local fauna.

Those goatlike cliff-walkers you glimpsed are not domesticated in pens—that would be impossible here—but they are tracked, known, influenced. The people understand the routes the goats favor at different times of year, the ledges where they shelter from the worst of the wind, the high, hidden shelves where they birth their kids. In certain niches along those routes, the people leave offerings: a particularly lush cushion of forage-plant relocated from elsewhere, a sheltered seep cleared of ice to expose fresh water. In return, or at least in consequence, the goat herds linger more in those zones, becoming predictable.

Hunting is then less about chase and more about moment: a single, precise strike with a thrown stone or a close-approach snare set where the animals step. They do not massacre; they take, at most, a kid or two, an older, limping adult—enough to add protein, bone, hide to their stores without destabilizing the herd’s future. The bones and unused offal are not thrown away but folded back into specific growth niches, feeding fungi and enriching the near-nonexistent soil.

Birds occasionally fall to them as well, snared more by cunning air-reading than by speed. In the narrow band where updrafts are strongest, they mount watch from morning to midday; when a long-winged hunter dips too low, drawn by some eddy or glint, a well-timed thrown loop of weighted fiber-rope—practiced since childhood—can entangle a wing just long enough to bring the bird within reach. If the catch fails, the rope falls, light enough not to pose a lasting hazard. Even hunting patches lightly.

Far more of their sustenance comes from what would, to you, seem like scraping life off stone.

They harvest the translucent, wormlike creatures that slide in permanent seep zones, plucking them gently from between plant roots, careful not to damage the fragile micro-ecology. These creatures they eat raw or drop into warmed water in stone basins, where their bodies dissolve quickly into intense, savory broths.

They drink the cirque’s sound as much as its water. To sit above a hidden cistern and feel, through stone, that the rumble of the underflow is lower than it should be—that tells them to ration carefully now and to wait, perhaps, before taking another goat. To feel the rumble grow slightly in pitch suggests meltwater is increasing, that they may open certain channels and allow more moisture to feed their cultivated strata.

Water management is their sacred engineering.

In a place where melt, drip, and fog are everything, the city’s subtlest art is the guiding of flow without ever coarsely breaking the rock. With nothing metal, nothing sharp beyond their hardened stone flakes and the toughened edges of certain shells carried up from far lower lands long ago and treasured since, they carve only as much as is absolutely necessary.

They know the stone intimately: which strata draw water along themselves like wicks, which resist saturation, which crumble if over-burdened. They have mapped, in memory and in the slow, shared lore of touch, the vertical distribution of seep-bands. Each band has its own pattern of cracks, its own typical drip-rhythm in different seasons. Older adults can press an ear or bare hand to a wall and tell, from the faint coolness and the inner vibration, how much water is moving behind.

They do not dig wells in the sense of holes downwards; they coax the stone to show its inherent porosity.

Along promising lines, they deepen already-existing hairline fissures, not into wide channels that would drain a vein quickly but into gently sloped grooves that persuade some fraction of the invisible seep to emerge. They are patient: to cut a channel the width of a fingertip across a few meters of wall may take weeks of intermittent work, each small shaved flake removed with consideration for its effect on load-bearing and micro-habitat.

Their cisterns are rarely open basins. Most are closed cavities within the rock, accessed through narrow throat-holes that admit a small ladle or, more commonly, the cupped hand. Those downward-bores are angled carefully to discourage rapid evaporation and to minimize the amount of dust and stray spores that enter. Some throats are fitted—not with lids—but with living plugs: thick, domed mats of moss deliberately grown to just the right thickness so they sponge up any overflow and slowly release it back, keeping the cistern mouths perpetually damp and cool.

Around these vital nodes, the community’s habits accrete. You will find the smoothest floors near them, worn by centuries of cautious footfalls. You may see shallow indentations where people habitually brace their elbows while reaching down, or polished spots on the stone where damp palms have long rested, waiting for water to pool into cupped hands.

Breathing, for them, is not simply a biological act but half-religion.

The wind that scours the cirque is their first teacher and oldest fear. Any foolish, open-mouthed gasp on a cold gust can freeze the throat raw; any misjudgment about how much air is in a shaft before a climb can leave you faint and dangling.

As children, they are told stories that begin not with “in the time before” but with “in the deep breath at the start.” They learn early to pace themselves to the thin oxygen—never to climb and talk at once, never to weep loudly on a high ledge where sobs would waste too much air at once.

They have small rituals of respiration. Before setting out on a long upward traverse, a group pauses at a particular recess where the wall-carving guides the wind into a soft, steady stream. There, they stand side by side, backs pressed lightly to stone, and inhale together three times to the same slow count, exhaling each breath through the nose so it warms their own faces and does not surrender too much heat to the passing air. Only then do they begin to move.

In their rare communal gatherings—the quiet, dense ones in the great forum—there is always a moment where sound drops as low as it will ever go, and the whole assembly breathes in unison with the mountain: in on a rising gust, out on a falling lull. The gesture has no fixed prayer attached to it; its sacredness is in the synchronization itself, in allowing their small, warm bodies to briefly align with the great, indifferent respiration of wind and stone.

Their sense of time is not kept on external devices but held in recurring patterns of sky and sound.

That strangely regular cloud-drop that folds the cirque in white once or twice a day is to them a clock more reliable than sun or stars. They know the way its onset murmurs first at the upper rim, then thickens down the walls. They know the exact sequence of vents that hush or shift pitch as the damp mass pours in. They organize many of their doings around these pulses: you begin a certain kind of work just after a cloud-lift, knowing you have until the next curtain before vision shrinks and surfaces slick.

Longer cycles, too, are audible.

When the under-rock river’s rumble sinks to its lowest across many weeks, that marks what you might call deep-winter, even if snow never falls thickly. It is during that time that they do their most inward work: tending the fungus shelves, repairing fiber-mats, teaching children the fine-grained listening that cannot be rushed. Stories are told then, spoken closer and a little longer than usual, while outside the wind’s teeth are at their sharpest and even the moss shivers under ice glaze.

When the rumble rises and the drip-patterns on certain carved walls shift—more beads on higher strata, fewer on lower—that signals not just the ordinary midday thaw but seasonal change. The people adjust their foraging circuits, their ledge-gardens, their watch over the goats accordingly, all keyed to these subtle auditory cues.

They do not make marks on the rock in the way of writing. Carving is too precious an act to spend on symbols that might outlive their relevance or confuse future arrangements of water and plant.

Instead, they inscribe meaning into behavior and sound; they embed memory in routes, in the habitual ways hands reach and feet set down. Their myths are geography and architecture; to walk the city in the paths set by an old story is to “read” it.

For instance, there is a tale older than any living person about a time when one of the main vents, now silent, used to sing in a high, piercing tone all through the cold season. The story tells of a winter that did not end on time, of under-rock water frozen into unyielding stillness, of thin-limbed children and long-fingered adults thinning again.

But because narrative here must be functional as well as expressive, the story is told as a route.

You start at the lowest echoing chamber where the tale’s beginning is set, then climb in a prescribed, meandering ascent past specific seep-bowls, each associated with a fragment of what happened. At each stop, the teller taps the rock and says, “Here the water slowed,” or “Here the wind burned our throats.” The final segment takes you to a terrace now overgrown with a lush strain of moss that only appears during the warmest pulses. The story ends: “Here the sound changed.” The route, the sequence of places and their soundscapes and lives, becomes the record of what was endured and how it passed.

They do make art, but not in portable form.

Their art is in the way they choose what not to carve out. Some hollows, which could be purely functional—storage, sleeping—are instead allowed a bit of extravagance: a sinuous rib of stone left arching across an otherwise open chamber, purely for the pleasure of the way it divides light and shadow; a wall where, instead of flattening all protrusions, they have shaped some into a graduated pattern of ledges that serve no purpose except to catch the brief daily thread of sunlight and scatter it in a fan across the opposite side.

They are sculptors by omission.

Over generations, certain families become known for their touch in this subtractive art. You can recognize that a given ledge was “the work of those who listen to the south vent” by the way its lip turns gently inward, by the almost living smoothness of a certain corner where they chose to stop their cutting and leave the rest to water’s eventual polishing.

In private, within small sleeping caves, some do carve small, secret reliefs: a remembered curve of another’s shoulder, the spiral of a fungus-frill that grew once in a perfect whorl and never again, a stylized representation of the cirque seen from above—a view none of them have ever actually had, but which they intuit from how sounds and slopes meet. These carvings are shallow and minute, often no larger than a thumbprint, placed where only those lying down will see them: on ceiling over one’s favored side, on the inner curve of a pillow-basin in the rock.

They are not industrious in the way of unceasing, feverish labor. The cold and thin air forbid that.

Their days have long spells of stillness that are not idleness but attunement. You will see someone stand in a passageway for a quarter of an hour, hands tucked under armpits, appearing to do nothing. In fact, they are listening—waiting to feel through their feet the subtle change that will tell them a cloud is coming and that it is time to shift hanging mats outside to catch more moisture.

They measure their worth not in how much they make but in how precisely they notice and how gently they can adjust.

Elders are valued not for hardiness alone but for their psalm-like memory of patterns: the last time the upper moss-band browned this early, how many days later the under-rock rumble changed; the rare alignment of bird cries with a particular flute-tone in the north wall that, when it occurred generations ago, preceded a rockfall. These recollections do not exist as “facts” stored in minds; they exist as remembered feelings of the air on skin, of sound in bone, retold in compressed, carefully chosen phrases.

They do reproduce, of course—and raise children—in a world where loud, wet birth-cries would be instantly swallowed.

Bringing new life into this thin, cold, loud basin is an act of stubborn defiance against the overall leanness of things. Pregnancy is closely watched, not with instruments but with communal care: older women, and men experienced in tending the ill, know the subtle shifts in breathing, in stamina, in appetite that herald each stage. They arrange for the pregnant person to move their sleeping space gradually lower or higher as needed, to match the rhythms of available oxygen and warmth.

Birth does not happen in a public square, nor in full isolation. It takes place in special chambers midway up the cirque, carved deep enough into the rock that wind’s roar is reduced to a steady, distant presence, like surf heard from the back of a cave. The entrances to these rooms are narrow, forcing those who attend to turn sideways, bowing their heads: a gesture that becomes unintentionally ceremonial.

Inside, the stone has been encouraged to shape itself into soft-edged basins and benches. Moss is not allowed to grow wild on these surfaces; instead, specific, velvety mats are transplanted and tended, creating warm, damp cushions that both insulate and absorb. The room is kept slightly warmer than the communal norm by a constant trickle of water deliberately allowed to run over a sloping rock plane outside and down across a slightly darker slab that holds and slowly releases the sun’s limited heat.

Laboring moans, when they come, are short, more exhaled than shouted; the one giving birth has long years of training in saving their breath for when it matters. Attendants murmur, not words of comfort—they would be wasted—but rhythmic syllables that match the mother’s breathing, helping her keep a steady pace, neither too fast nor held too long.

Infants arrive red and small, shivering in the chill. Immediately, they are pressed skin-to-skin, parent to child, then wrapped in a composite of felted fiber and warmed, thin hide. They learn the character of wind before they learn faces, for even in these inner chambers, the air moves. Their earliest lullaby is not sung, but is the feel of an adult’s hand cupping the back of their head, shielding one ear from the roar while leaving the other exposed, so the baby sleeps anchored between silence and storm.

Children here are not coddled, but they are not thrown heedlessly into danger either.

For the first few years, they are carried more than they walk, bound close to an adult’s back or hip with those same braided bands used for garments. This positioning lets them feel, through their carrier’s body, the micro-adjustments of balance and breath that movement in this terrain demands.

When they begin to toddle, it is in inner chambers and on the flattest of the shallow ledges, always under an adult’s half-distracted but ever-ready hand. A child’s first real lesson is how to crouch and catch themselves, how to fall compactly so the stone bruises but does not break. Their second is how to listen: to stand in a corridor and, with eyes closed, point toward a sound-source. Games reinforce: “Do you hear the high whine? That is the fog coming; we go inside.”

Teenagers, if you can call them that, are the ones you glimpsed at dusk stabbing between rooms, moving in quick, fluid bursts, testing their strength against slopes, seeing how close they can dance to the edge of gust-eddies without being thrown. They are watched with a patience that is not indulgence but grim knowledge; every few years, someone misjudges. When that happens, the entire city hears it: the abrupt break in the usual sound-pattern, the clean, cruel punctuation of rock on flesh, flesh on rock.

They do not rush to such accidents. They move as fast as is safe, but no faster. They know that one fall must not become many. Afterwards, at the next midwinter inward-turning, the circumstances are recited—not as blame, but as calibration. “He stepped when the north vent was two tones higher; he did not feel the gust’s curl.” Then, every child henceforward is walked past that place, shown the pattern of stone and told who fell there, so the memory of that particular misjudgment remains locked not in the ground alone but in minds and muscles.

They are not warriors, because there is nothing here to war with in the usual sense.

No neighboring states covet their stone or their drips; anyone who tried to conquer this cirque with armies and engines would find their ranks shredded by wind, their machines crusted with ice. Yet they are not without conflict.

Disagreements among them are as slow-burning and structural as the environment that shapes them. You do not rage and shout; your raised voice would only blow away. Instead, argument happens over routes, over choices of what not to carve.

If one group believes a certain seep should be tapped more aggressively to feed new fungi shelves, while another fears this will desiccate a beloved moss-stratum higher up, the debate will unfold over days. Each side will guide others along their perspective route: “Come feel this wall,” “Come taste this drip,” “Come listen with me to how the under-thrum shifts when we shave just this margin.”

Rarely, such conflicts harden into long-term schisms, estrangements expressed by the quiet cessation of visits to certain ledges or the deliberate avoidance of one family’s vents when tuning wind instruments. There have been, in the far past, groups who chose to leave altogether, descending out of the cirque to lower, softer lands, taking with them whatever of this place they could carry in technique and habit.

Those who remain speak of such departures not with condemnation, but with the same resigned tone they might use to note a boulder giving way after centuries: “That path is gone now; the slope has changed.”

Death, like everything here, is integrated and unhurried.

Not every corpse can be carried far; the steepness of the world and the fragility of living hands mean decisions must be practical. There are specific places on the cirque’s inner walls—narrow shelves under overhangs where the wind almost never strikes, yet the air moves enough to dry—reserved for the dead.

Bodies are laid out on stone that has been slightly scooped to cradle them. There is no burning; fuel is nonexistent. There is little covering; you do not entomb, for that would create pockets of rot dangerous to the thin soil system. Instead, they wrap the dead lightly in fiber-mats that have been retired from daily use, marked with small tokens indicating name and relationship—hidden stones, knotted cords—then leave them to slow, cold decomposition.

Birds, those high, long-winged riders, sometimes come and pick. So do smaller creatures, shy and pale, that emerge from cracks only at certain temperatures. Over long weeks, months, flesh returns to the cirque in ways too diffuse to trace. Bones, when exposed and clean, are taken up again—not as grim trophies, but as a continuation.

Some become tools: thin ribs turned into scraping edges for plant harvest, finger bones strung as clackers to test echo behavior in new chambers. Others are ground, carefully, into powder that is given back to the fungi shelves or tucked into the pockets of humus where new growth is being coaxed. A few are given a more overt memorial: a skull hollowed and polished, placed in a niche where the wind can thread through eye sockets and nasal passages, adding a faint new tone to a chamber’s song.

In this way, the dead literally join the voice of the city.

They have no gods in the human sense of personified sky-fathers or earth-mothers, yet they are not without reverence.

They speak sometimes of “the Depth” and “the High Quiet,” not as deities but as two great, impersonal frameworks. The Depth is the under-rock river, the ancient tectonic forces that once heaved this plateau up; it is everything that moves slowly, invisibly, but without which nothing familiar would exist. The High Quiet is the air above the cirque rim—too thin for comfort, luminous, rarely seen. It is the possibility of outside, of other ways of being, always present but seldom touched.

Between Depth and High Quiet stretches what they know: the thick band of middling existence where stone, water, and air meet in ceaseless abrasion. They locate themselves there, choosing neither to worship the extremes nor to pretend they can master the middle. Their piety is in precision.

On rare evenings when the sky happens to clear near dusk and a hard, pale blue deepens above the cirque, a few will climb as high as is safe and sit with feet dangling over impossible drop-offs, eyes turned upward. They do not pray aloud; they simply watch the edge where rock ends and air continues, committing its exact hue and grain to memory. Later, when the wind’s voice in certain vents shifts unexpectedly, they will recall that tone of sky and compare, seeking patterns, always patterns.

Do they ever leave? Yes.

The cirque is their core, but not their entire world. There are seasonal migrations down slope, when the sound of the under-rock river warns that, if they do not descend, they will miss the brief, richer blooms in lower ravines. Small groups go—those most sure-footed, those whose lungs can best handle the comparative thickness of air farther down without becoming dizzy.

They travel light: a few carrying-slings, some dried food resonant with the flavors of upper moss and fungus, stone flakes for cutting, coils of rope-like fiber for traversing cliffs. On these journeys, they become more like the goats they track, moving quickly along less carved, more chaotic stone. Here, below their shaped world, the horizon opens a little. They see, distantly, other mountains, other shelves, perhaps the hint of valleys where rumors say trees still grow and rivers run wide enough to hush even their roar.

Some among them feel a pull toward such distance—toward a place where sound is different, air thicker, green more abundant. These are the ones most likely to not return, joining those earlier leavers in whatever scattered communities may exist below. Others go down, then hurry back, half-panicked by open views and the absence of the cirque’s enclosing music. For them, the wide world is too quiet, its silences too loud.

From below, the people of the heights are ghost-tales. Travelers from lesser elevations sometimes glimpse, through mist, figures moving along impossible cliff paths above, hear fluted cries in storms that do not match any bird. They imagine spirits, wind-walkers. They do not quite believe that anyone could truly live there.

But live there they do—thin-breathed, stone-handed, sound-tuned.

If you walked into their midst—a stranger from some softer, thicker-aired land—they would see you long before you saw them. They would mark the different way you move: the overconfidence on sloping stone, the squandering of breath on exclamations, the way your eyes chase horizons instead of cracks.

They would not rush to greet you; this is not a place for rushing. But after watching a while, after ensuring that your presence did not immediately threaten any of their delicate balances, a few might approach along the safer terraces, bodies angled slightly into the wind the way one leans into the voice of someone hard to hear.

They would likely not speak first. Instead, one of them might lay a hand flat against a nearby wall and gesture for you to do the same, letting you feel, beneath your palm, the vast, holding rumble of water in darkness.

In that shared contact, they would be showing you what lives here more than they themselves do: the unending interplay of wear and persistence, of carving and restraint. They are, in their own eyes, only one more slow-moving element in that interplay—warm eddies in an old, cold flow—shaping and being shaped, listening and being heard, as long as lungs and stone hold.

They live thinly, but not miserably. Theirs is a life of sharpened attention, of careful steps, of warmth hoarded and shared. In the cirque’s huge, carved hollows, in the luff and slap of moss-curtains, in the disciplined tuning of wind-songs, in the stubborn green glowing in cracks, their presence is another patient, enduring line in the long script of this exhausted, exquisite height.
