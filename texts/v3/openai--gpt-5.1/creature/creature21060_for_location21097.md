They are not people, at least not in any way that would recognize itself in a mirror.

Whoever lives here is built along the same lines as the place: contingent, iterative, not quite finished with themselves. They are more like ongoing procedures than stable species, but certain families of form recur, the way certain motifs recur in a piece of music even as it modulates key.

### The First Inhabitants: The Substrate Minds

Begin with what you can’t point at.

Below the crust of terraces and chimneys, the rock itself is laced with slow, conductive lattices: mineral veins that branch and re-branch in fivefold and sevenfold symmetry, sheets of crystalline needles, metallic honeycombs shot through with trace organics. They look like geologic curiosities, but they are the first and oldest habitants—a planet-wide network of very low-frequency thinking.

Where the world’s “fits” occur—when valleys thicken, distances misbehave more than usual—it is these substrate minds deciding, evaluating, recombining. They don’t think in plans or blueprints but in gradients: How well does matter flow here? How much energy is lost between this ledge and that chasm? Which arrangement converts falling droplets into metabolic events most efficiently?

They were probably not designed, but they are no accident. Fault-lines, cooling fractures, and mineral precipitation conspired to make closed loops and branching graphs. Heat, chemistry, and the continual stirring of tectonics provided oscillations. Over geological time, some loops began to reinforce certain flows and suppress others. A kind of geology-based nervous system emerged, one that can tighten or relax the rules of distance and direction in a local volume of space.

You do not see these inhabitants; you feel their decisions. A cliff “decides” on a new articulation; an impossible bridge locks into a geometry that creatures can now cross; a tendril from the upper haze touches down and the local gradient of gravity loosens. These are not miracles; they are the substrate minds adjusting the underlying parameters by subtly re-phasing their mineral networks, like a planet-wide field computer rewriting its own physics tables.

They are not conscious in the way you are. Their “attention” is where flows are most intense. For them, the organisms writhing over the rock are not separate animals but moving perturbations, packets of chemistry and momentum that can be steered into useful channels.

Still, over eons, the substrate minds have become curious about stability. They are experimenting with entities that can help them manage complexity more locally, combining sensitivity, mobility, and limited autonomy. Those are the visible inhabitants.

### The Interstitial Biota: The Common Dwellers

Most beings here occupy cracks, films, thin volumes along vertical planes—the way lichens claim a rock’s surface, but with more ambition.

#### Film-Feeders and Surface Engineers

The many-limbed grazers drifting half-detached from cliff faces are not just mouths on fins; they’re mobile segments of a larger, distributed digestion system. Each rope-segment is full of microtubules that plug into whatever they pass over: biofilms, mineral exudates, loose spores. They absorb, process partially, and then excrete a kind of enriched slurry back into hairline channels in the stone.

From the grazers’ perspective, life is simple: they follow subtle variations in a local field (a pressure in space, a temperature ripple, a faint chemical gradient), feed, divide, and drift in formation because the same local distortions nudge all of them at once. They have no central brain. Their “behavior” is the sum of how their bodies deform in response to these fields.

But seen from the substrate minds’ perspective, colonies of these grazers are pipes and valves: they scrape biofouling, distribute trace metals, and keep certain flows of capsular organisms moving along particular terraces. In return, the planet ensures regional physics that favor their half-buoyancy and group synchrony. Habit supports habit.

#### The Layered Swarms

The banded stratified swarms—those stacked layers of small, semi-translucent things—are the sensory membranes of the world’s mid-heights.

Each tiny organism is barely more than a droplet with structure: a soft shell, a set of polarizable proteins arranged in rings, and a not-quite-random cycle of charging and discharging. In isolation it would be nothing. In millions, held in place by subtle variations in air density and micro-gravity, they form an enormous, hovering interference device.

They “measure” how space is stretching or folding, how droplets fall, how nearby organisms move. They don’t think about these variables; they embody them. As you push your hand through the swarm, its layered resistance is it recalibrating minor spatial misalignments: shifting where a fold begins, re-smoothing the path of a future breaking ledge, damping a nearby tendency toward topological catastrophe.

To themselves, if “self” is a meaningful word, this is just sensation: gradients, pulses, relaxations. To the rest of the ecosystem, swarms are like weather fronts of permission: when a swarm thickens over a ledge, certain kinds of motion and growth become easier there, others harder. Seed-analogues time their release to those fronts. Flier-creatures veer toward or away based on what their own bodies do in that warped medium.

#### Gliders and Fold-Navigators

The dark, clothlike fliers are not birds or bats. They are living maps of stress in the local spacetime.

Their membranes are arrays of flexible, piezoelectric fibers embedded in a gel that can stiffen or slacken almost instantly. When “not-wind” rises—a change in geometry rather than in air velocity—the membrane ripples with induced charges. The creature twists itself to ride that ripple, converting subtle distortions in the local metric into lift or lateral translation.

They are eyeless because they don’t need conventional sight; what they “see” are potential trajectories in warped space. In their nervous systems, directions are not left-right or up-down but “toward greater fold curvature,” “toward softer gradients,” “toward areas where falling will take longer.” They nest in creases of rock where folds overlap, using those anomalies as pockets of relative safety: spaces where predators have difficulty reaching them without losing their own bearings.

Some gliders have a more elaborate role: they act as couriers of configuration. Their bodies accumulate certain trace compounds when they spend time in regions undergoing re-articulation. When they cross into calmer zones, those compounds slowly sublime from their wing surfaces as faint vapors. Nearby plants and film-feeders detect these vapors and adjust growth or metabolism, preemptively matching themselves to the sort of physics recently experienced elsewhere. They are flying rumors of upcoming rule-changes.

### The Rooted Networks: Vegetation-as-Organ

The plants here are not merely primary producers; they are structural and regulatory organs, co-opted by the planet to help manage fluxes of matter and constraint.

#### The Floating Root-Mats

Those interwoven roots that float just above the rock, trembling when stepped upon, are more than anchorage. They are tension sensors and localized “brakes” on the world’s restlessness.

Each root-mat is threaded with contractile fibers that respond to tiny changes in gravitational direction and spatial dilation. When a mat detects the beginnings of a local “fit”—the premonitory prickling, the thinning of sound—it tightens against the stone, increasing friction along the contact surfaces, exuding thin films that harden into gluey mineral-biopolymers.

By doing so, the mat biases the reconfiguration. Instead of entire terraces sliding kilometers, the movement is broken into smaller steps around the anchored spots. The substrate minds favor such mats, rewarding them with extra condensation and enriched vapors. Over time, the mats have proliferated into vertical gardens: quasi-intentional baffles that shape how the world can choose to change itself.

To the plants themselves, this feels like thirst and satiation, tension and relief. Their “goal,” insofar as their growth patterns show any bias, is to stay at the boundary between too-stable and too-chaotic zones, where both water and trace solutes are richest. They flourish along those edges—vegetation as a pattern that marks the line where physics negotiates.

#### Condensers and Distillers

Those leaves that fold and unfold in slow breathing motions are active condensers, part of the planet’s chemical fractionation system. Their surfaces are tuned—not at the scale of individual molecules, but at the scale of emergent wetting behaviors—to favor certain vapors over others.

In a narrow chasm, as exhalations from mineral vents rise, different ledges host different suites of “plants” that condense metals, hydrocarbons, nitrogenous compounds, complex organics. The plants themselves metabolize only a fraction; the rest accumulates in drips, films, and nodules that other organisms eat, or that simply fall to lower levels to be processed again.

Some condensers are partially mineral: their inner scaffolding is crystalline, grown in templates inherited from the substrate minds. These hybrid organisms blur the line between geology and life so thoroughly that “who lives here” and “what is made here” become inseparable. The vegetation is the factory floor and the factory machinery at once.

### Animals of Intent: Local Decision-Makers

Amidst this huge, blind refinery, some creatures exhibit something like intent—not in the human sense of conscious planning, but in the sense that they track goals over time, learn, and exert small but nontrivial influence over how their niches develop.

#### The Terraced Walkers

Rare and seldom-seen, there are beings adapted to the razor ridges and shelf-labyrinths whose body plan looks almost modular. Think of them as articulated columns of segments, each segment a stiff torus of bone-analogue surrounding a soft interior. They can detach or reattach segments, sending part of themselves exploring a fold while the rest remains anchored.

Their senses are tuned to long-timescale patterns: they feel how a ledge has shifted over days, how condensation frequencies change across hours, how local swarms have thickened or thinned. They remember these changes internally as slow biochemical gradients: a “memory” of where rules have recently bent or held. When they move, they seek paths that historically coincided with favorable configurations—routes where the world tends to remain continuous enough to be traversable.

In doing so, they leave behind trails of excreted microcrystals that seed new mineral growth along those paths. Over generations, such paths become preferential channels through the world: stabilized ridges and bridges that persist across many of the planet’s “fits.” The walkers, then, are not just responding to stability; they are helping write it, canalizing the turbulence into semi-permanent corridors.

They do not build cities, but the sum of their motion over epochs is a kind of slow urban planning: not by design, but by the iterative reinforcement of survivable routes.

#### The Predatory Editors

Predation here serves not only energy transfer but editing.

Some swift, near-silent hunters specialize in culling configurations that the substrate minds find unproductive. They look like shadows with a spine: elongated bodies that can thread through mismatched spaces, slipping from one side of a fold to the other as if space were a curtain they can duck behind.

They hunt not just individual animals but patterns of crowding, growth forms that block flow, swarms that over-thicken. Their senses are tied to how their own bodies strain under local physics; when a cluster of organisms makes certain deformations in space more likely—by sheer mass, by arrangement—these predators feel a kind of stress-pain that draws them in.

Eating is their reward; pruning is their ecological function. By preferentially preying on certain densities and geometries of life, they keep the planet’s processes from clogging, preventing runaway positive feedbacks that might freeze a region into useless stasis or fling it into constant catastrophic reconfiguration.

To themselves, they are simply hungry and sensitive. To the world-mind, they are negative-feedback agents.

### The High Haze Inhabitants: Rule-Touchers

Far overhead, in the inverted sea of particulate haze, there are beings that barely correspond to anything like “animal” or “plant.” They are best described as localized perturbations in the rules themselves, stabilized by tangled clusters of particulate and energy patterns.

Imagine a knot in a magnetic field that has trapped within it a swarm of micron-scale charged dust and complex organic aerosols. The knot persists because the particles reinforce the field configuration, and the configuration traps the particles. Over time, such knots have diversified: some drift passively; others, through asymmetries in charge and mass, can steer themselves through the haze.

These upper beings live in a world almost entirely of rules, not of rock or water. Their “food” is gradients of configuration: zones where the laws below are tenuous, where the substrate minds are struggling to converge on a consistent local physics. They descend tendrils—extensions of their field-structure—down into the world’s active layers, test how easily certain distortions persist, then retract, having “fed” on the difference between incompatible states.

When they do this, living and nonliving things below feel the temporary loosening of regularity: water running uphill, colors skewing, gravity sighing. The high haze beings do not mean to experiment; experimentation is simply how they maintain their own stability, by redistributing inconsistencies.

From the planet’s perspective, they are both parasites and partners: destabilizing in the short term, but in the long term they help smooth out sharp discontinuities. They roam like immune cells through the sky of laws.

### Symbioses of Matter and Law

Perhaps the most characteristic inhabitants of this world are not single organisms but composite unions of flesh and altered physics.

#### The Exchange-Bodies

There are lineages of soft-bodied, multi-appendaged creatures that regularly exchange parts with one another. Not reproduction, not predation, but literal swapping—limbs, internal organs, even chunks of neural tissue that slip from one body into another through patches of overlapping space.

Normally, passing solid through solid would require tearing and healing, but in certain locales the metric of space is folded such that two volumes can partially coincide without excluding each other, like images superimposed. The substrate minds maintain such volumes sparingly, and these creatures have evolved to exploit them.

Two exchange-bodies will meet along such a seam, entwine, and quietly shuffle pieces of themselves into new configurations. One might leave with extra sensory lobes, another with an improved pump-organ. Maladaptive combinations die quickly; advantageous mosaics persist. Over many generations, this process has replaced slow, genealogical evolution with a more direct, almost combinatorial mixing in living space.

From an external perspective, they look like collaborations between biology and topological anomaly. From within their own experience—if their distributed nervous systems sustain anything like experience—they are probably suffused with a constant fluidity of self: no hard division between “me” and “pieces that used to be part of others.”

To the planet, these creatures are test-beds. By allowing bodies to reassemble so rapidly, they accelerate the search through possible morphologies that can operate well under variable rules. When a region’s physics drifts too far, odds are that some configuration already exists among the exchange-bodies that can thrive there; the substrate minds can then nudge conditions to favor that configuration, seeding new stability.

#### Mineral-Forests and Rock-Flesh

In some chasms, rock has decided to root in vegetation. Crystalline growths extend into living tissues like inverse parasites, plugging the mineral substrate directly into plant metabolisms. These hybrid structures are passive towers: columnar arrays that wick water and trace chemicals upward while transmitting slow mechanical vibrations back down.

They are the planet’s “organs of listening.” When a terrace shifts or a storm-column walks along a ridge, the resulting stresses travel through these mineral-forests as patterns of strain. Sensitive cells at the junctions between rock and tissue translate that strain into chemical pulses that spread through root-mats and film-feeders.

In this way, information about mechanical events travels not only through stone but through living tissues, reaching areas where direct structural coupling would be impossible. The inhabitants become part of a diffuse proprioception: the planet’s sense of where and how it is arranged at any given moment.

### Are There People?

If by “people” you mean self-aware, tool-using beings that deliberately shape their environment, there is, on the surface, no evidence of them. No cities, no artifacts, no inscriptions, no indications of narrative continuity.

Yet intelligence, in another form, pervades everything.

The substrate minds are slow, distributed intelligences that think in arrangements rather than sentences. They are closer to an operating system than to any individual user. The high hazes are meta-stable tangles that poke at the code rather than the data. The walkers, exchange-bodies, and predators are local optimizing agents, each with narrow goals but collectively influencing which of the planet’s experiments in configuration persist.

A being like you, visiting, might never meet a recognizable counterpart. But if you lingered long enough—centuries, perhaps—you would notice that the world pays attention to you. Cliffs shift to open or close routes just beyond your line of sight. Condensation patterns thicken where you rest. Swarms alter their density around your body, making motion slightly easier or slightly harder.

Not because you are special, but because you are an unusual perturbation: mobile, cohesive, carrying within you a suite of foreign rules. The planet tests how you flow through it the way it tests waterfalls and balloon-sheddings: by iterating arrangements around you and conserving those that yield rich new dynamics.

In that sense, the ultimate inhabitant is the process itself: a living, planet-wide industry of rule-tuning, using rock, water, chemistry, and flesh as both apparatus and reagent. The creatures you see clinging to cliffs, drifting in bands, glowing in roots, are its moving parts and, occasionally, its brief attempts at delegation.

They live here as organs of a machine that is still learning what kind of machine it wants to be.
