They are not gone.

They have simply retreated into dimensions of habit that don’t intersect easily with an outsider’s glance. The chamber you stand in is their outer lung, their exposed membrane. They do not live here any more than you live on the surface of your own skin.

They are small for a tool‑using people—shorter than you by a head, bone‑light, built to spend most of their waking hours pinned between forces: the pull of gravity, the shove of the air. The proportions you would call “graceful” are, for them, defensive. Nothing broad that the wind can catch, nothing tall that presents a lever. Limbs are more strut than column: long, narrow bones laced with trabecular webs, built to bend and resonate instead of snap.

Their silhouettes would read as humanoid if you reduced them to line drawings. Heads, torsos, four primary limbs, capable of bipedal walking when necessary. But all of that is a ground plan, not the final form. The true form is in the surfaces, the added structures, the way their bodies have bargained with the rules of the place.

Skin, to start with, barely resembles what you expect. Up close it looks almost matte and granular, like a ceramic with a fine grog. It is not true ceramic, but a sheath of keratinous plates embedded with silicate grit, laid down incrementally from within. They do not have hair in the mammalian sense; the closest analog are stiff, sensor‑dense filaments that lie flush until needed, then cant outward a fraction of a millimeter. Under your fingers, if you were allowed to touch, they would feel like extremely fine sandpaper warmed by slow blood beneath.

They breathe much as you do—gases in and out—but their nostrils are not inviting openings. They are narrow, slit‑like seams under a complex of bony baffles and cartilage screens. Breathing is partisan work here: filtering, slowing, untangling the roar enough that useful differentials—of composition, of density, of temperature—can be teased out. Their faces are mostly mask: high cheek structures that flow into orbital ridges, a nose that is more ridge than protrusion, jaw hinges set deep behind layers of tendon that serve as dampers.

The eyes are an adaptation you would recognize and not trust. Each iris is rimmed in a dark, light‑absorbing ring, but the sclera is nearly the same faint, dusty color as their skin. The corneas are overlaid with permanent, clear shields etched with micro‑ridges: not eyelids exactly, but living goggles that break up glare, scatter brightness, and route a portion of visible light away to buried photoreceptive patches in bone. Blinking is slow and infrequent and involves more of the eye socket than the lids—like a shutter deep under glass.

Ears, as discrete flaps, have been mostly surrendered. Where you expect a pinna there is a smooth, shallow depression edged with stiff, short filaments; the true hearing organs hide under the skull, coupled directly to bone via lattices that register air‑borne sound only as a secondary sense. Their primary acoustic sense is through their entire skeleton. They feel the place, constantly.

If you watched them at rest, you might notice tiny, rhythmic expansions in their ribs that do not match breathing. Those are internal tensioning systems: muscles and ligaments pre‑loading and relaxing structural members so that the whole body sits slightly off resonance with the chamber’s dominant frequencies. It is possible to be shaken apart here. They spend energy to avoid it.

Hydration is the invisible obsession of their biology. They do not sweat; they cannot afford it. Every exhalation passes through finely divided, mucous‑lined cul‑de‑sacs where water vapor is stripped back out of breath and wicked inward again. The entire digestive tract runs under partial vacuum relative to the outer air, to discourage evaporation. Cells hoard bound water like treasure, tethered into long chains of organic molecules that will not easily sublimate away.

Thus, their blood is viscous and slow, a syrup threading through a double system: one circulatory network for what little fluid they can keep, another for a class of mobile, gelled salts that transport not only ions but mechanical energy, stiffening or softening internal structures in response to vibration. You could say they are partly hydraulic, partly piezoelectric. Tension and charge move through them as information.

They are omnivores because they cannot risk specialization. However, “food” for them includes categories you might call inert: mineral foams, porous ceramics seeded with slow‑release compounds, processed plant matter whose main virtue is a particular micro‑architecture. They eat texture as much as chemistry, acquiring materials that can be re‑arranged into their own cartilage, bone, and protective plates.

When they do take in living tissue, it is often their own cultivated biomass: the armored vines, the blistered leaves. These they process in two steps. First, communal shredders—wind‑driven grinders like those you saw above the terrace—pulverize structural components into graded powders. Second, internal gut flora, themselves descended from carefully domesticated environmental microbes, unlock bound organics. They waste almost nothing; feces are sparse, dry skeins of consolidated grit that often go straight back into construction.

Their hands are the most familiar thing about them. Four digits and a thumb, each much slimmer than yours, each ending not in a nail but in a tapered, dense pad of chitinous lamellae. The pads are both tool and sensor: textured enough to rasp, sensitive enough to read the vibration profile of a beam or the flow of air in a crack. Their grip strength is disproportionate to their mass; tendons anchor down the forearm almost to the elbow, letting them hang for hours without fatigue.

They are not fast, not in the impulsive mammalian way. Continuous high wind makes running dangerous and jumping suicidal. Their movement lexicon is about commitment. Before they cross a bridge, they read it—press fingers to the support points, lean, feel how the structure hums against the ambient roar. A step is taken only when they are satisfied that the frequencies will not add destructively. When they climb, they distribute weight across multiple points, often using elbows, hips, even the side of the head as bracing contacts.

This slow decisiveness is mistaken for caution if you don’t understand that the alternative is structural failure of self or city.

They are clothed, though much of what they wear looks initially like extra skin or exoskeleton. Clothing here is instrumentation and buffering. Outer garments are woven from flattened plant fibers and cured resins into flexible plates that overlap like fish scales—each plate precisely tuned in thickness and size so that, en masse, they break up and diffuse particular bands of vibration. The resulting coats and leggings, close‑fitting and matte, are less about modesty than about making a moving body acoustically invisible to the environment.

Over joints you see softer masses: multilayer wraps filled with powdery gels, micro‑baffled pockets that protect cartilage from long‑term micro‑shocks. Around the throat and jaw, a variable collar that can cinch or flare to control how much of the external roar makes it to the delicate machinery of the inner ear and voicebox. In the brightest zones, they wear hoods lined with thin, semi‑transparent leaves grown specifically for their light‑filtering lattices.

Their tools are minimal in diversity but extreme in refinement. The absence of metal has not prevented them from reaching a fine craft; it has focused them.

Cutting tools are composite: blades of vitrified plant silica or carefully fractured stone, their edges mirror‑straight at the microscopic level, mounted into grips of resin‑impregnated wood. Drills are often wind‑powered—slender, suspended torsion rigs that spin abrasive tips into stone in slow, relentless cycles, the operators merely adjusting pressure and feed.

The most distinctive implements are their “listeners”—devices that might look like fans or antlers at first glance. Arrays of thin, tuned slats mounted at the ends of handles or on body harnesses, which convert particular frequency bands into tactile signals. Carpenters, gardeners, and navigators carry these everywhere, using them to feel shifts in the flues’ behavior, impending failures in beams, subtle changes in the chemistry of the air.

Their language began as a compromise with the noise and evolved into its own rich domain. Laryngeal sound is possible but secondary, reserved for closed spaces, emergencies, or intimate conversation in buffered alcoves. The everyday, public language is an intricate mix of:

– Hand and arm signs, most of them low‑amplitude movements close to the torso, wasting little area to the wind.
– Whole‑body orientation: the angle of the shoulders relative to major flues, the tilt of the head, the subtle arching or softening of the spine.
– Manual contact: finger sequences tapped out against another’s forearm or back, coding nuance through rhythm and pressure.
– Tool‑borne cues: quick shakes of a listener slat, short arcs traced with a drill handle, little flicks of dust that catch the light in particular ways.

There are vocal elements here and there, tonal bursts at frequencies that ride atop the roar without interference. But their emotional register is more likely to be expressed as a change in stance or timing than as a change in volume. Anger sharpens movements into staccato vectors; affection slackens joints, lengthens transitions, invites synchronized balance.

Because balance is always shared. In a world where the air itself pushes with the force of a slow collision, no one truly stands alone. You brace together, hook ankles into the same crevice, tension a shared line across a gap. Collective poise is the basis of trust.

They live mostly below the chamber you have explored, in tiers stacked like segments of a buried shell. The upper bowl is infrastructure: light, air, nutrient grinding, plant cultivation, maintenance of the great flues’ throats. Below, in ever deeper rings, are the quieter domains.

Immediately beneath the gardened platforms lie the work levels. Here the sound is still immense but structured, bled through baffles and tuned chambers so that distinct pulses can be discerned. Workshop pods cluster around the downshafts of major supports, using them as both anchor and conduit. One set of artisans tends the flue vanes: replacing abraded edges with carefully grown plant‑stone laminates, adjusting their angle to keep the overall instrument from slipping into destructive harmonics.

Others are “shapers” of living beams. They plant cuttings into template frames—simple jigs of dead wood or stone that dictate the path of growth. Over years, the plants thicken around these forms, fusing into load‑bearing members that are both structure and membrane. Shapers mark their progress by feel and by a local dialect of taps and scrapes: a language of material feedback.

Adjacent to these are the nutrient foundries. Remember the channels of dust you saw above? Their terminus is here, in great, perforated vats of layered granules. Workers kneel along their rims, hands plunged into the powder like potters into clay. Except they are not mixing; they are listening. Through their tools and skin they read how the slow winds percolate, whether the resistance to flow is correct, whether the fines have clumped, if some unexpected condensation event has altered the micro‑geography of channels. With small, precise motions—adding coarser grit here, opening a new perforation there—they maintain the invisible rivers that feed the roots above.

Deeper still are the dwellings. You might find them surprisingly intimate compared to the open articulation of the city’s visible structures. Rooms here are small and low‑ceilinged, sometimes no more than nested alcoves hollowed into thicker structural ribs. Every surface is textured: fine ridges on walls to diffuse noise, shallow basins on floors to collect dust and prevent it from drifting. Color comes from selectively grown lichenous films and carefully inlaid shards of iridescent leaf glass—not as spectacle, but as orientation cues in low, glareless light.

Common features in every home:

– A “quiet wall” where baffles and resonant slats are tuned to cancel or at least heavily damp the dominant environmental tones, creating a localized dead zone. This is where infants are cradled, where the elderly sit when the cycles of the outside roar crest too harshly.
– A personal array of listeners and tools, often heirloom pieces passed down and slowly re‑tuned as the city itself evolves. These are displayed not centrally but within easy reach of habitual handholds, so that they can double as supports during tremors.
– A small garden fixture—nothing lush, often just a cluster of specially bred, slow‑growing plants whose main value is subtle, scentless vapor exchange. The chemistry of their exudations is keyed to the family’s specific microflora, reinforcing immune systems in a place where traditional pathogens are rare but opportunistic dustborne extremophiles are not.

Families, as much as anything is neatly bounded here, tend to be small, extended horizontally rather than vertically through generations. Lifespans are long by the standards of such a harsh niche; slow metabolisms and careful risk management buy them time. Childbearing is rare and ritualized, not because of scarcity of resources alone but because new organisms must be carefully “tuned” in early development to the current song of the city.

Infants spend their first seasons in specialized cradles that are part musical instrument, part protective pod. Long, slender vanes around the cradle transform ambient roar into a complex but softened vibration pattern fed into the child’s bones. Elders check and adjust these vanes constantly, recalibrating them as cycles in the void above shift. In this way, each new body grows up with its internal resonances matched to the present reality, not to the remembered conditions of their parents’ youth.

Community life orbits around thresholds between loud and less loud. There are shared chambers where the sound is allowed to rise to near‑surface levels, used for training, ritual acclimation, and the testing of new structural designs. There are also deeply buffered hollows—“sinks”—where the roar drops to a distant, almost hypothetical presence. These latter are used sparingly. To them, quiet is not a default; it is an altered state.

In the sinks, you find practices that from the outside might read as meditative or religious. Individuals or small groups sit anchored to the same spine of stone, eyes closed, feeling the minute pulse of their own hearts and the tiny clicks of tendons—sensations normally masked. Here they negotiate with their own bodies: loosening chronic tension accumulated from constant bracing, recalibrating posture, sometimes simply rehearsing memories of movement in air that is not trying to push them off the world.

That word—religion—does not map cleanly to their interior life, but they have something you would recognize as cosmology. They inhabit, in their own understanding, a throat.

The bowl city is one dilation in a much longer passage, or so the oldest narratives insist. Above is a widening into blinding diffusion; below, a narrowing into crushing, silent pressure. The flues are not engineered from nothing but harnessed from a pre‑existing wind, a great current connecting domains they can never occupy.

Origin stories speak of an ancestral line that did not always live in the throat. Some say they came from above, from a land of true light and softer air, driven downward by some overbright catastrophe. Others say they rose from below, fleeing a place where even dust was squeezed to glass. The stories disagree on direction but not on the fact of migration along a gradient of survivability.

What lives outside now, beyond the plates in the ceiling and the stone cutoffs of the flues, is not personified in their myths. The void is neither god nor demon; it is a field, a condition, an adversary to bargain with rather than defeat. The “city that endures by becoming a precise, humming part of its environment” is not metaphor to them. It is identity.

They name themselves in ways that translate poorly, but one thread recurs: words and signs for shaping, for guiding, for trimming excess without stopping flow. A plausible rendering might be “those‑who‑tune.”

Their core cultural virtues reflect that:

– Calibration over conquest. Success is measured in degrees of reduction of destructive resonance, in the smoothing of cycles, in the longevity of beams. A great builder is one whose works fail gracefully centuries after they die, not one whose works never yield.
– Attentiveness to slow variables. Where you might be impressed by immediate spectacle, they respect those who can perceive a drift over dozens of years in the pitch of the roar, a subtle thickening in the dust’s grain, a faint blueshift in the overhead plates.
– Shared burden. Any structure you brace against for support is also braced against you. Responsibility is literal. Interdependence is not a moral ideal but a physical constraint transposed up into ethics. To neglect a joint that another uses is a kind of violence.

They do not practice agriculture as something separate from architecture. Growing beams, nutrient mazes, light‑modulating foliage, and edible tissues are all aspects of the same ongoing project: to create surfaces and spaces that endorse their kind of life. They are, in a sense, gardeners of stability.

Their fauna co‑inhabitants—the shard‑flat gliders, the spindle sparks, the ambiguous crystalline films—are neither pets nor vermin. They are tolerated participants in the same experiment. The gliders, for example, are observed and occasionally guided by simple interventions (a new ledge here, a deflecting vane there) because their collective choice of roosting sites reveals micro‑eddies in the airflow that the people cannot feel directly. The spindle things clean root mazes of clogging particulates; the crystalline smears, if indeed they are living, are watched because their slow creep records the deep cycles of flux across years and decades.

Predation, in the classical sense, is rare inside the bowl; energy flows are too thin and precious. Conflict arises more often over information—over differing readings of the void’s intent, over proposed adjustments to flue angles that might alter the city’s long‑term drift. Their quarrels are not about territory but phase.

They are not static. Over the centuries, they have undertaken projects on scales you are only beginning to infer from the architecture.

Some of the oldest, thickest supports in the bowl are heavily overbuilt relative to current needs, their lower segments scarred with obsolete mortises and abandoned climbing paths. These were once part of a previous configuration, when the city took a slightly different shape, directed the wind in a different pattern. As cycles in the environment above shifted—perhaps in intensity, perhaps in composition—they responded not only by tweaking vanes and adding baffles, but by gradually re‑basing the entire city: trimming away old supports, coaxing new growth beams into alternative load paths, allowing certain sections to sag and others to rise.

This slow, generational renovation is almost continuous. Right now, while you imagine the place as complete, a crew somewhere in the deeper levels is likely hollowing out a new channel, gently starving a cluster of roots so that their inevitable death will free a cavity exactly where a future expansion will require it. In that sense, what lives here is not a city as a static noun, but city‑as‑verb. The bowl is a time‑thickened process.

Do they ever leave? Rarely, and never far. The nearest “outside” for them is not the true void but adjacent chambers in the same network of throats: side passages where the conditions are marginally more or less survivable. Small, specialized teams—encased in extra layers of baffle‑garments, trailing lines back to the main bowl—venture out when something in the roar suggests a change upstream or downstream.

They go to read the walls of the ductwork: erosion patterns, accreted films, chemical crusts that only form under certain temperature and composition regimes. From these traces they forecast the coming centuries. Will the pressure gradient steepen, threatening to strip their gardened flues bare? Will some distant, enormous shift choke the wind to a stuttering, unpredictable surge? Their planning horizons are long because the inertia of their infrastructure is immense.

When they return from such forays, they carry not trophies but samples: fragments of unknown dusts, chips of differently stressed stone, slivers of plate from the ceiling’s underside captured with daring hooks. These make their way to yet another subterranean class of specialists—analysts whose craft is nearly invisible but fundamental. In low‑noise rooms studded with instruments that are half geological, half biological, they subject every sample to batteries of listening tests: how it rings when struck, how it fractures, how it alters the song of a tiny test flue.

Out of these gentle violences they compose strategies. Add a baffle ring here. Shift that column’s top brace by a thumb’s breadth. Start, three generations from now, to grow a new support in that slow, awkward gap. The inhabiting species, from the outside, may look precarious. From within, they feel surrounded by an enormous, ongoing plan.

In the mythic register, the outside—the broader system they inhabit—is sometimes framed as a long, low note, unbroken, sounding from before they can imagine and continuing past any conceivable future. They are harmonics upon it: temporary, local patterns riding on a greater wave. Death, in that frame, is not a falling into nothing but a de‑tuning: the bones no longer catching the right fractions of the world’s song, the personal pattern dispersing back into the general roar.

So: who lives here?

Thin‑boned, dust‑skinned people whose bones listen more keenly than their ears do. Gardeners of armored plants that drink vapor and light instead of water. Mechanics of air, living in the lee of a void that could scour them away if they mistuned a single immense instrument. A culture that builds with roots and ash in order to tame pressure and noise, that raises its young in cradles tuned like resonators, that speaks with hands and stances in a world where speech is ordinarily drowned.

They are not visible in your first, dazzled survey of the bowl because their life has flowed inward, into the deeper ribs and shaded hollows. But the place holds their outline everywhere: in the wear on a ladder rung, in the finely adjusted angle of a vane, in the exact thickness of dust in a groove. The city is their collective body turned inside out, the sum of a long conversation with a hostile continuum.

They live by accepting that they are not separate from the roar but one of its chosen shapes, for now.
