They are a people of edges and waiting, of patience pressed thin.

They are small in the scale of their world—smaller than the knots that fuse two root‑bridges, smaller than the scars on a plate where a storm once tore. But that is only size, and size means almost nothing here.

They call themselves nothing that translates cleanly; whatever word they use means both “those who cling” and “those who listen.” If you needed a name, you might call them the Hangers, or the Rhizome‑folk, or simply the People of the Underlight.

They live where things meet. They do not trust open spans of plate, those wide, rippling plains that can rise or buckle without warning. Instead, they build in the crotches of converging columns, in the angles where one laminate presses against another, in the shadowless refolds of the world‑body where tension is complex and change is slower.

Their bodies are wiry, thick‑jointed, built for three dimensions. Gravity here is unreliable in its details; they respond by treating it always as an adversary. Their centers of mass are low. Thighs and forearms are dense with compact muscle; fingers and toes are long, each ending in a trio of callused pads that can mold themselves to slick or fibrous plant‑surfaces. The nails are not claws—there is nothing to dig in to except the living flesh of the world itself, which they avoid injuring—but the nails are narrow and strong, good for finding micro‑ridges, good for slipping under a membrane seam and peeling it carefully back.

Their skin is pale, almost the same pallor as the world’s tissues, but patterned with subtle, irregular bands of denser melanin in greenish and grayish tones. Against plant‑stone they vanish unless they move. Under the ceiling’s shifting radiance, they are outlines more than figures: a soft darkening at the edges, much like the non‑shadows that objects cast here. They have learned to look like their environment, not by elaborate camouflage but by simply not interrupting the light more than it already is.

Their hair—when not bound—is a hazard. Loose filaments catch condensation and cling to adhesive pores on leaf‑curtains, so most cut it short or twist it into tight cords. Some weave into these cords the desiccated veins of small plants, both as ornament and as insurance: if a bridge fails and they hang by the hair‑cords snagged on some passing tendril, they prefer those cords to have some elasticity.

Their eyes are large but not luminous; they are built for contrast, for perceiving minute shifts in hue and texture. Where a surface slightly bulges from internal pressure, where pores dilate, where a faint green tinge indicates a metabolic phase change—these are things they must see from far away. The iris shades are mostly muted—from storm‑violet to sap‑brown—but the pupils contract into odd vertical pinpricks when the ceiling flares bright and dilate into loose, lopsided circles when it dims. Peripheral vision matters more than focus here. Dangers are big and slow and come from the edges of the world, not the center of your gaze.

They are light‑boned, but not fragile. Bone, for them, is a compromise—sturdy enough to brace a leap across a suddenly opening crack, light enough that a fall into the air‑void sometimes ends not in shattering but in being caught by some hanging net‑root. Many survive such falls clinging half‑conscious to the underside of a random filament, their hands cramped into claws around a damp, gently throbbing cord of tissue, until their clan can lower a retrieval tether.

They grow slowly. Childhood is long. In a world where new pathways appear and old ones vanish across a few cycles of ceiling‑light, knowledge can’t be rushed. The young go where the older already know how to read the mood of the world. They remain tied, literally, to older bodies—thin braided lifelines knotted around waists and shoulders, running back to a mother, uncle, elder—the “Anchor.” No one moves alone at first. One learns with one’s whole body pressed to membranes, feeling the tiny subvibrations of distant growth, the faint, pulsing shudder that announces a convulsion hours in advance.

Their sense of danger is not keyed to predators’ roars or the crash of breaking waves; it is tuned to small discrepancies. A droplet that quivers at the wrong frequency on a vein’s edge. A plate that resists pressure a little less than it did yesterday. A faint discord in the ever‑present hum that rides through the tissues, the plant‑world’s distant, continuous work.

To surface people, their settlements would look temporary, precarious. To them, each is an argument against entropy.

They choose pockets where two large growths meet—a ceiling‑column and a horizontal plate, say, pressing together to leave a wedge of space that breathes in slow, regular expansions as the plant‑body flexes around them. They read these cycles carefully. If the breathing is too strong, the space will close; if too weak, it may never stabilize. Somewhere between, there is a safe constriction: an opening that will adjust and adapt without crushing.

Within such a pocket, they persuade reality.

They dry layers of naturally shed membranes into tough, flexible sheets, then laminate them with fibrous pulp and sap that hardens into a kind of bioplastic. These become walls, rafters, sling‑floors. They stitch them into the host structure, never nailing, never driving anything rigid deep into living tissue. They loop and knot and weave. Pressure is spread, edge effects softened. A well‑made dwelling feels to the host plant not like a wound or foreign object but like another fold of its own skin.

Doors are slits aligned with existing stress lines. When the plant swells, the slit opens; when it contracts, the slit narrows. The people augment that with cords and counterweights, learning to time their movement through narrowings that would crush an inattentive visitor but merely brush the shoulders of one who knows the rhythm.

They cultivate more than they build.

Every surface can be coaxed. Over generations they’ve learned that if you scratch a membrane in cross‑hatched patterns and dab it with a mash of certain micro‑plant tissues, it thickens, amorphous at first, then taking on the lattice of your scoring. Do this along the lip of a treacherous drop and, in a few cycles, you have a padded, grippy curb. Do it along the underside of a narrow arch and weeks later you have a handhold ridge.

They select rootlets and side‑shoots as other peoples select saplings. A slender tendril that grows toward heat can be slowly trained into a curve and then a loop; given time, you can persuade it to spiral around a dangerous void, forming a living staircase. Once it has thickened past a certain diameter, you can prune the feeding tips (with careful, apologetic cuts) so it stabilizes at that size instead of crushing its own usefulness by overgrowing.

They garden their host less as if it were soil, more as if it were an animal’s behavior.

Sometimes they pinch off the beginnings of a membrane, forcing fluid to reroute, encouraging a parallel growth that might bridge a gap more safely. Sometimes they bruise a too‑eager root, slowing its advance so it does not dislodge an important shelf. There is a theology in this: not of dominance but of compromise. They say, in their wordless songs, that the Great Body is young and hungry and blind in many places. It does not notice them unless they provoke its displeasure. Their art is to steer the blind giant’s fingers a little, for mutual benefit, without letting it feel that something small is directing them.

Food, here, is both abundant and hard to reach.

Everything is plant, but not all plant flesh is edible. Much is fibrous, saturated with bitter, defensive alkaloids. Much is too alive—the equivalent of biting into an artery. They have fine taxonomies of taste and aftereffect. A sweet, crisp ridge‑tissue turns the tongue numb; good for pain, bad in a climb. A spongy inner rind near certain columns is delicately sour and, if fermented in bladder‑membranes for a few cycles, becomes a stable, tangy paste rich in slow energy. Tiny nodules that grow where three veils intersect are almost always safe, unless they bear the faint purple sheen indicating they share flow with a toxic column below.

They farm the rafts that form on rare free‑water pools. They will travel days along precarious bridges to a known depressive basin, watch as films of micro‑plant congeal on the surface of a new pool, then leap and paddle onto those quivering mats like hunters on sea ice. There, knee‑deep in cold, slick tissue, they cut blocks of the micro‑plant sheeting—tissue that is both leaf and root—and bundle it into slings. Back home, they press these into shallow hollows prepared on inner walls, irrigating with condensation captured in hanging membranes. The sheets root, thicken, form a draped garden of edible, easily regrown flesh.

They keep other things.

Mobile lichen‑bundles, left undisturbed, become denser, their filamentous coils thickening. The people take the young, before they’ve developed full wandering autonomy, and tether them on short, twisted cords within controlled areas. Fed with scraped scrapings of inedible plant‑crust, the lichen‑creatures extrude chemical exudates that can soften tougher tissues or ferment pastes more thoroughly than simple waiting. They are livestock not for meat but for biochemistry.

Leaf‑thin gliders, the humidity swimmers, are not domesticable in the conventional sense, but the people have learned to herd them with plumes of odor and artificial humidity gradients. In lean times they will set up great, translucent curtains of parasitic frond just beyond their settlement, dripping carefully infused condensate that smells like nutrient vents. Gliders come, in slow, graceful swarms. The people harvest a few, gently, taking only the outer membranes (which regrow if left a rim) and releasing the rest laden with the colony’s spores, spreading their gardens to other regions.

There are almost no predators, at least not in the teeth and claw sense. What kills them is movement and absence: a bridge that wasn’t there, a ledge that is suddenly gone, a water pocket that fails. Thus, their main technology is not of weapons but of maps and warnings.

Maps are not flat. Their world is too kinetic for that. Each clan maintains a Room of Paths: a hollow within a core structure where strands of dried plant‑fiber hang from the ceiling in layered curtains. Each strand is a route between major anchors; its knots mark features: a squeeze, a sloping plate, an unreliable membrane, a storm‑scar. New information is added by tying in fresh offshoots, old routes are cut away and laid in a heap to be studied, lamented, remembered in cautionary tales.

Children learn to read this room with eyes closed. They trace curves and intersections with their fingertips, imagining the feel of each. When they eventually travel those routes in reality—the odd sway of a rope‑bridge grown slack with recent rains, the spongy give of a scar‑grown handhold—they recognize them as sensations already in memory. The path is imprinted in flesh before it is ever walked.

Warnings travel by vibration and light, not volume.

A clan on one side of a vast crease in the world might learn, by patterns of returning hum and a subtle deepening of the ceiling’s color, that the other side has lost someone to a sudden convulsion. There is no running to help; the distances are too great. But they can adjust. They can avoid their own cutting and building in that region for a few cycles, letting the giant plant complete whatever adjustment it has begun without added stress.

Within a local network, they communicate by playing the world like a drum. A child on watch might sit with bare back pressed to a load‑bearing column, humming slowly through their teeth while tapping with their heels in a simple, repeating rhythm. This pattern transmits along the fibers. A watcher in another pocket, a day’s climb away but connected by the same structural “bone,” feels that pattern against their spine. If the rhythm changes—three quick strikes, stop, three quick strikes—everyone touching that column knows: storm sign. If instead the child hum stretches long and then is punctuated by an abrupt silence, the receivers know: fall sign; someone is lost.

Their language—spoken, when it is—is sparse, low, and rich in consonants that can be made with a mostly closed mouth. Ordinary conversation is one person’s breath against another’s cheek, or a murmur shared with someone while both of you hang upside down from a branch, legs hooked. Longer discussions, philosophical musings, myth, are not spoken at all; they are sung as low pure tones into the fabric of their dwellings, recorded as micro‑tension changes in the membranes.

Elders claim they can still hear the conversations of long‑dead ancestors in the hum under their fingers if they are quiet enough. That may or may not be true; what is real is that these humming “texts” train the young to recognize structural voices: the feel of a healthy column, the dangerous stillness of a region about to shear.

Their stories are not about conquest. There is nothing to conquer. They tell, instead, of cleverness and timing.

One tale, murmured often to children learning their first solo paths, is of the clan that saw the ceiling bruise and the columns stiffen long before any storm came, because one girl noticed that the droplets along a certain ridge did not fall in their usual tiny avalanches but clung too long. They moved early, dismantled their homes, shifted as a group along a risky, infrequently used membrane corridor. When the convulsion came, their old pocket was crushed flat and mashed into new growth. They survived not through strength but through interpretation of a small, quiet wrongness.

Another is darker: of the pair of siblings who, angry with an elder’s refusal to allow a shortcut through a dangerous root‑maze, went at night and scored the bases of certain stabilizing tendrils to show that the elder’s caution was misplaced. They woke to the groan of an entire shelf sagging. No one died, but the clan’s main food‑garden slid out of reach, its supporting plate slumping into a gap that now yawned too wide to cross. For years they lived lean. The siblings grew old repeating to every child that skill without respect is just a longer fall.

Socially, they are neither hierarchical nor equal in the simple sense. What they respect is anchorage. Those who know best how to attune themselves to the host plant—the “Listeners”—hold a peculiar authority. They are not leaders in war or trade, but their word about when to move, when to cut, when to leave a region is law in practice. Disobeying a Listener is like insulting the ceiling’s light. You can do it; the world will not thunder down a curse. But something in the fabric between people and place goes slack.

Family is fluid, tracked not primarily by blood but by shared rope. A “household” is everyone currently bound into the same network of safety lines and routes: whoever sleeps in the same main pocket, cooks from the same lichen‑ferment, shares the same vibration‑messages. Children grow up remembering not “mother” and “father” but “the three who held my line the first time I crossed that wide gap.” If a bridge root shears and one half is lost, those on the surviving side become, from that day, something like kin to each other. They are the “Those Who Stayed,” bound by a loss no one else can quite feel.

Their dead rarely leave bodies. A fall into the deep is irretrievable; a crushing by slow, implacable plates leaves little to gather. Instead, they ritualize the points at which a person departed: the scar in a membrane where a handhold tore free, the notch in a step where someone’s weight shifted wrong. These sites are marked by small arrangements of dried veins, knotted in patterns that encode who, when, how. Over time, the host plant grows over them, incorporating the knots into its own skin. Ancestors literally become texture.

They are poor in metals, as you noticed, and treat them with a mixture of awe and suspicion.

The thin, dark flakes they harvest from root‑torn mineral pockets are rarely shaped into tools for ordinary use. Their favorite cutting edges are instead teeth of hardened phytolith and silica‑rich tissue set into bone or dried‑wood handles. Metal is for anchors: hooks hammered and then pressed deep into dead wood‑like cores where growth has ceased but strength remains. A metal hook, once set, becomes a sacred point: a suretie that the world’s flux cannot easily erase. Children are tied to such hooks on the night of their first independent sleep; knots are rechecked three times, hands pressed to the metal afterward to feel its illicit chill.

In their art, such as it is, they work mostly with absence.

They carve the glowing ceiling not to leave their mark but to puncture it in precise patterns, creating tiny variations in brightness. Stand directly beneath such a carving and you may see only an irregular constellation of darker spots; move thirty meters to the side and strips of dimmer light align to form simple figures: a long arc that means “safe ridge,” a pinwheel that means “old convulsion site,” a stick‑body with many arms that means “here we drew up a drifting raft and made it home.”

Some of these carvings are so old that no one recalls their makers. Their meanings have been reinterpreted; a once‑literal diagram becomes a mythic star‑sign. The people below rearrange their lives according to those half‑remembered glyphs; in turn, the plant’s growth is altered by the small, directed harms and reinforcements they focus around those symbols. Over centuries, human intention and vegetal metabolism write over each other again and again, creating feedback loops neither quite controls.

They are not alone in intelligence.

Somewhere, deep in that interlocking rhizome, there is a distributed processing of signals. It is not a “mind” in the human sense; it does not crave, remember stories, imagine what‑ifs. But it senses flows, optimizes pressures. It detects, however faintly, the patterns that the people impose: the repeated scoring along certain stress lines, the tendency for bridges to appear between specific distances, the persistent depletion of certain nutritious tissues and the introduction of certain new gardens in their place.

In response, it experiments.

In one region, it begins to sprout ladder‑like fungal shelves more often, as if having “noticed” that whenever such shelves emerge, the little heat‑sources and exudate‑scars nearby increase. In another, it thickens certain columns in advance of any obvious mechanical need, having “learned” that these nodes are repeatedly stressed. To the people, these feel like gifts or omens. To the plant‑body, they are nothing more than local optimizations of flow and structure—but, taken together, they begin to resemble a dialogue.

The people lean into this on purpose. There is a role among them, older than their oldest step‑scars: the Temptor. Not a priest, exactly; more a negotiator with something that doesn’t know it is being negotiated with. Temptors will go to a precarious edge or an under‑supported junction and begin to “teach” the plant with inducements. Shallow cuts in a particular pattern, alternating with feedings of rich slurry from fermented gardens. If the plant responds by thickening in a way that pleases them, they reinforce, bringing more food, carving faintly similar patterns nearby. If it reacts by withdrawing, becoming glassy and brittle, they back off and mark the place as taboo.

Over generations, the world’s macro‑structures in areas where Temptors work become subtly different: more amenable to predictable bridge‑formation, less prone to sudden, unforeshadowed collapse. The people attribute this to the success of their teaching; the plant has simply adapted to a reliable source of extra nutrient. But the effect is the same: zones of semi‑cooperation, the closest thing this underworld has to “civilized lands.”

The people’s weirdest kin are not products of their own bodies but of the margins where they and the plant meet in more direct hybridization.

Sometimes, a child is born with skin patches that never quite keratinize, remaining translucent, veined, faintly luminous from captured ceiling‑light. Such children can press those patches to certain membranes and feel not just vibration but fluid flow. They know, without conscious analysis, when a vein is under dangerous pressure or when a region is starving of condensate. They often become the best Listeners, revered and protected. Some live to old age. Many do not; their sensitive patches are vulnerable to infection, and their own growth sometimes goes strange, sprouting harmless, leaflike ridges that itch and must be constantly abraded.

Less human are the “Walk‑veins”: long, threadlike aggregates of plant tissue that have, in some regions, adopted a quasi‑independent locomotion, coiling and uncoiling, seeking out nutrient gradients. The people dislike them. They climb where they should not, pull on structures that were stable. But a few bold souls have experimented with guiding Walk‑veins, training them along desired paths by regular applications of exudates, much as one might train a creeping vine on a trellis.

A successful Walk‑vein path is extraordinary: a self‑repairing, living rope that thickens where worn, that can, if one end detaches, slowly grope its way back to anchor. One can imagine a future in which the people and these semimobile structures co‑evolve a more intricate partnership: moving settlements that ride on slowly advancing root‑rafts, whole clans that never settle in one pocket long enough for it to fully heal around them.

They do dream of elsewhere.

There are stories, whispered half in fear, of an Above and a Below.

Above is the impossible: beyond the glow‑shell ceiling, somewhere past the translucent matter that lights their world. It is an idea more than a place. Children ask what feeds the light; elders describe a greater, colder emptiness where brightness is not a tissue but a direction, and where shadows are sharp. Some say the Great Body sends roots up to drink that light; others say Above is dead, and their world is the only living skin clinging to a fossil remnant. No one they know has ever gone there. Yet the ceiling carvings, their oldest ones, speak of directions in which the light’s quality shifts in long, slow cycles. There are those who think that, given enough patience, one could follow that gradient to a thinning of the shell.

Below is nearer, in concept. From certain vantage points, the air falls away into what seems like bottomless dark speckled with dim, slow flickers: emissions from deeper growths, maybe, or the sparks of friction in places where the plant‑body grinds against something harder. Sometimes great sheds plunge down there, old plates and columns sloughed off in storms, and the people watch their slow fall until mist absorbs them, imagining them landing on a second world of soft decay. There are tales—almost certainly fabricated—of individuals who survived such a fall by clinging to a slab, living for years on the rot of its surface, before the Great Body grew up around them again and delivered them, aged and wild‑eyed, to some other light‑field.

Between those unreachable extremes they inhabit the middle stratum: not too near the ceiling, where violent growth is most intense, not too near the unseeable “floor,” where pressure and weight must be beyond any bone. They find this middle good enough. Full of change, yes, but change they can sometimes forecast.

They are young, biologically and culturally. Their oldest clans remember, at most, the reorientation of a few major regional plates, the death of a couple of enormous gliding beasts, a handful of really catastrophic storms. Their myths do not go back to world‑making or cosmic ages; they begin at the scale of “when that column used to be there instead.” Still, in their songs and carvings, you glimpse the beginnings of something longer: an intuition that they are riding the early moods of a colossal being that will, one day, settle.

Some of their elders fear that settling.

They imagine a future in which the Great Body thickens and stills, its plates knitting into a solid, woody shell, its luminous ceiling dimming as whatever fuels it is exhausted or rechanneled. In such a world, their skills—reading tension, predicting convulsions, moving with growth—would be obsolete. The safe pockets would close; the open spaces would fill; the quiet would become a different kind of silence: the silence of completion.

Others long for it. They picture terraces that last for lifetimes, gardens that do not slough away, bridges that do not need constant coaxing. They are willing to surrender the romance of precariousness for the stability of an aged world. That debate—whether to pray for calming or to dance for continued restlessness—threads through their low, vibratory hymns.

For now, though, they inhabit the interval.

They move slowly through the brightness, their breath steaming faintly in the cold, their hands and bare feet finding holds in the ever‑moist flesh of their world. They sleep in pockets that flex around them, listening to the distant thunder of plates cracking and fusing. They eat gardens growing on the skin of a living continent and drink from droplets that have never known sky.

They are neither masters nor parasites. They are symbionts in progress, a nervous system in miniature learning to anticipate the twitch of an unformed giant.

From a distance you would not see them. Their bridges are threads across abysses; their steps are scars on scars. Only if you came very close—pressing your own ear to the damp plant‑stone and humming—would you feel their presence: tiny, organized disturbances in the vast, slow pulse of the world, a counter‑rhythm of soft, deliberate signals insisting that here, at least, something small has intentions.

Who lives here? Those intentions do.

Those who have made a home of unfinished growth. Those who hang and listen and hum their meanings into the flesh of a still‑forming world, hoping that, in the long run, it will remember them kindly.
