They are not people in the way you expect, but they are also not anything simpler. They sit at a scale where insect, amphibian, and primate impulses all braid together.

### Bodies

They are soft rather than carapaced, but not naked. Think of a slender salamander that has learned to stand upright, then shrink it until one could lie comfortably across the width of a human thumb. Their limbs are long for their size—good for spanning fibers, bracing between stems—and end in many-jointed fingers tipped with small, dark, keratin-like hooks. The hooks are not claws meant for tearing flesh; they are pitons, meant for moss and root tissue, so that a body never loses purchase on the always-damp, almost-slick world.

Their skin is not smooth, but a fine, velvety nap that holds a film of water like fur holds air. Under a lamp it shows mottled colors—greenish-brown, grey, sometimes faint rust tones—patterned in blotches that echo the host plant’s patchwork of bruises, scars, and lichens. The pattern is not purely genetic; over a life they accrete stains from the things they handle, from the fungi they sleep against. The old are darker, less variegated, as if slowly lacquered by their work.

Eyes are large, forward-facing, lustrous black. They see well in almost no light but poorly at any distance; this is a world of near-vision, where things beyond arm’s length are suggestions and movement more important than form. Around each eye, a fringe of short, flexible spines rises and falls subtly, altering the way condensing mist and stray droplets slide from their faces. When they are startled or intent, these spines flare almost like an eyelash crown.

Their heads taper gently into a short muzzle, nostrils slitted and easily sealed. Inside is a mouth heavy with textured pads and a modest number of teeth—some needle-fine, for gripping chitin and worm-flesh, others broad and rough, for rasping plant tissue and fungus. Tongues are dexterous, narrow, prehensile tips capable of tasting films, lifting filaments, cleaning tools.

They breathe through small lateral vents as well as through mouth and nose; in stagnant pockets of air, you can see these vents flutter. Their chests are narrow, but their bellies swell and contract with a visible elasticity, adapted to meals that come irregularly but richly when they come.

A tail balances them. Not a reptilian lash, but a flexible, muscular cable, half again their body length, feathered at the tip with tendrils of sensory filaments. These feel vibration in root and film: the murmur of a predator tunneling, the slow slide of a big drop preparing to fall. In moments of quiet, they rest their tails against the substrate like an extra set of fingers, reading.

Across the back and shoulders, especially in adults, patches of skin thicken into low, flexible plates—callus grown from a lifetime of carrying fibers, bracing under ledges, wedging into tight traverses. Some individuals embed small, hard foreign bodies there—a flake of bone, a shard of bark—as if making themselves into composite tools.

Sex differences are subtle. Perhaps the birthing sex carries a slightly broader pelvis and deeper body cavity; perhaps the other grows slightly larger sensory crests. But in this scale and this light, social roles have long since overwritten simple dimorphism. Children learn whom to follow by smell and by the timbre of a voice, not by silhouette.

They are not alone in their own skins. On them live symbiotic films of bacteria and microscopic algae, tolerated where they do no harm, rubbed away in tender spots. In a few places—the inner forearm, the hollow of the throat—the adults cultivate tiny luminous colonies on purpose, using special oils to keep them alive. These glow faintly, a private, low light for close work, and a sign of adulthood: controlled bioluminescence on the body, not just in tools.

### Senses and speech

Sight is near, but hearing and touch are broad.

They hear in the low register: the creak of root, the thud of drip, the faint scrape of many feet on moss. Their ears are not external flaps but subtle membrane-plates along the jaw and skull, each with a crown of delicate bristles. When they listen, they still their bodies, half close their eyes, and those bristles tilt as reeds in a slight current.

Their world is textured. Every surface is read with fingers and tail, the way you might read braille. Children are taught early to know the difference between safe scabbed-over scar-tissue and the treacherous slick of a fresh wound that may tear wider under weight.

Smell is intricate. They map their city as a cloud of overlapping scents: this neighborhood always faintly sour from its fermentation pods; that one resinous from sap-storage; this path lined with the musky marker-glands of sentries. Individuals carry their own blends—family-smells made from shared bedding, diet, the particular molds of their dwellings. Strangers are obvious; kin are a warm, almost edible presence.

They speak softly, with lips close to one another’s auditory bristles. The language is built to die quickly in the saturated air: clicks against the back of the throat, tongue-taps on palate, breath-squeezes that barely travel. Consonants dominate; vowels are short and damped. To call farther, they drum with fingertips or tools on roots and membranes, sending pulses through the plant-body. Those patterns are language too: simple, codified rhythms for danger, for gathering, for the presence of something new in the water.

They also use light. A swiftly covered and uncovered lamp-cup, a hand moved over a patch of glowing fungus, the tilt of a bioluminescent throat-patch—these become signals in transit-ways where voice would risk attracting the wrong attention.

### Origins and place in the web

They did not arrive from outside as we would think of it; they crystallized in place out of older lives.

Once, this hollow would have been just another moist pocket: home to soft-bodied grazers, blind burrowers, film-skaters, a small ecosystem feeding on what the plant exuded. Among those small things, some line of omnivores learned not merely to take advantage of the plant’s wounds and structures, but to influence them.

Perhaps their ancestors chewed out nesting cavities and noticed that the plant grew scar-tissue that made the cavity stronger. Perhaps they stored scraps in a hollow and found that the rot attracted new food. They began to stay put, to think of a particular bowl of water and its surrounding terraces as theirs.

Over many generations, the cleverest, most cautious, most manipulative of these creatures survived better in such hollows. They learned to reproduce less often but invest more care: tending eggs or soft, blind infants in the safest crevices. They learned to teach, because here knowledge of where not to step, what not to harvest, mattered more than tooth or sprint.

And so, slowly, a culture condensed, as a droplet condenses on moss.

They are still bound into the food web. Larger things eat them if they can; smaller things are eaten by them. Their dead feed fungi that may in turn feed their grandchildren. But they have changed the ratios. They have become ecological engineers at a scale where the movement of a root by a millimeter over a season is a civic project.

To the plant-body, they are parasites and healers both. Their pruning prevents strangling tangles and rot pockets. Their waste adds nutrients where roots are thin. Their constant presence may stimulate thickened growth at stress points, making the hollow more durable. Somewhere, in the diffuse net of the plant’s slow awareness—if such a thing exists—the city might register as a pleasant itch, a place of activity around which tissues quietly strengthen.

### How they live with one another

They do not live as solitary pairs but in clumps: kin-clusters of five to twelve, often related by blood but sometimes by adoption of the lost or the useful. Each cluster claims a dwelling-node—a hollowed root-burl, a fused tangle of stems—plus a section of the nearest path and some share of adjacent storage-pockets.

Within the cluster, work is distributed by life-stage more than sex. The oldest, with worn hooks and dulled senses, become listeners and rememberers. They spend hours pressed to walls at the edges of the city, feeling for the distant stir of danger, and longer hours talking to the young in low, rhythmic narratives about “the time when the ceiling opened” or “the year the water warmed and the bowl turned sour.”

Adults in their prime are the shapers and foragers. Their days are a sequence of careful excursions: out along the monitored routes to scrape edible film, set traps, check fermenting caches; back in to mend ropes, coax growth, train seedlings against braces. They move in pairs or trios, never alone except on the most necessary, most honored scouting tasks.

Children move in eddies between these patterns. Small, quick, and given a certain tolerated freedom within circumscribed safe zones, they learn by trailing adults, repeating tasks at reduced scale: weaving small cords beside great ones, tending pet grains of luminous algae in broken seed-husks as practice for community lights. Play is mimicry—acting out predator attacks, plant-flexes, rescues—and it is never entirely without function. Even a game of “drip-dodge” under a leaky overhang teaches timing, balance, how to read the quiver of a forming drop.

Clusters bind themselves together into larger consortia—what you might call neighborhoods or lineages. These are marked not by territory alone but by shared practices: one may be famous for the particular pattern of its braided vine railings, another for its especially bitter protective fungus strains, another for a distinctive humming rhythm used in work-songs. Alliances and rivalries exist, but prolonged open conflict would doom them all in so small and perilous a world. Their disagreements are expressed in controlled ways: who may harvest from which section of a bloom first, who has the right to maintain a certain arterial path, whose elders’ stories are trusted about an impending shift in the plant.

There is hierarchy, but it is shallow. At the apex in each consortium is not a war-leader but a “rememberer of edges”: the one who knows most acutely where the city can stop and the plant must be left to itself, where pruning and harvest are safe, where they tip into damage. Deference accrues to those who have repeatedly been right when predicting slow changes: the thickening of a wall, the opening of a new seep.

Above even these are a few whose role crosses all clusters: those who go upward.

### The upward ones

They are rare: slender, long-limbed individuals, often born small and restless, chosen young by certain telltale behaviors—a fascination with the faintest drafts, a habit of staring toward the dimmest light-leaks. They are trained to climb the densest, narrowest routes toward the outer world.

Moving outwards is lethal without knowledge; pressure changes, toxic molds, abrupt cold, and the jaws of things adapted to truer open air wait beyond. Upward ones travel in pairs at most, heavily burdened with cord and hooks, carrying carefully padded seed-pods in which they hope to bring back dirt, stone specks, novel fungi, detached fragments of other plants. Some never return.

Those who do come back altered. Their skins bear different stains, new scars from unknown teeth or ice-sharp plant edges. Their stories become a powerful but gently handled substance—a kind of cultural acid. They speak of a space so vast that drip no longer matters, where wetness falls from above in dense veils; of brightness so intense that one cannot look at its source; of growth that is not tightly layered but spread flat over unfathomable distances.

Most listeners cannot really grasp this. Still, those stories shape their cosmology. They know now that their “sky” is not the end, only a thin lid. Above is a larger throat, and above that, rumor says, more.

Pragmatically, the upward ones bring seeds for new symbioses: molds that harden into better barriers, algae that glow in more useful colors, fibers that braid stronger. They may also bring chemical traces—salts, minerals—that subtly change the metabolism of the host plant where the city touches it. Over generations, the city thus imports the outside world in microcosm, grafting its influence into the mossy throat.

### Food, work, and technologies of coaxing

They are opportunistic omnivores with a keen awareness of cascading effects.

Fungus forms a staple: a dozen kinds, each with its own texture, taste, and temperament. Some are mellow white mats that can be scraped repeatedly from the same patch if never fully stripped; others erupt suddenly as thick, soft towers that must be harvested all at once before they spore and rot. The people have names, taboos, tiny songs about each. One always leaves three ridges uncut on a certain prized bracket fungus, for example, or “it will not grow back next water-turn.”

They cultivate films. Along certain sheltered paths, you see deliberate stripes of particular algae, protected from overgrazing by strands of thorny, inedible moss tied over them—living larders to be scraped in thin, nutrient-rich sheets when other sources run low. In some inner chambers, they maintain sealed fermentation pods: hollowed nodules stuffed with plant scraps, insect shells, meat offal, then plugged with waxy secretions. Weeks later these are opened carefully to reveal rich, sour masses used as seasoning, medicine, or bait.

Animal prey is taken mainly by traps and gentle weapons. Nets fine as hair are set across micro-channels; any small swarm drifting through will tangle, then be collected in a swift folding motion. For larger creatures—slugs, beetle-things, small burrowers—they use deadfall gates of stiffened leaf, triggered by a filament; or sticky resin smeared on attractive substrate. Only in emergencies do they stalk something as dangerous as a burrower directly, and then always with cooperation: one group above to drum and distract, another below with sharpened tooth-blades ready to slash gills or soft segments when it surfaces.

Their tools look fragile to us, but are subtle at their scale. A common implement is a multi-pronged comb, carved from the spine of some once-encountered larger beast, used to roughen root surfaces, scrape films, or tease apart tangled fibers. They have tiny shovels made from thick beetle wing-cases, used to excavate pith or shape drainage channels. The rarest tools are stone slivers: razor-sharp, impervious to rot, passed from hand to hand for generations, their edges maintained with loving skill.

They understand fire only as smolder. A true flame would flash and die in the heavy air, if it started at all. Instead they make slow, hot embers: bricks of dried fungus and plant fiber, lit with great difficulty from stored coals and then nursed. These give off little light but comforting warmth and enough heat to desiccate, to char, to alter. In fire-bowls they dry certain poisonous mildews into harmless flavors, blacken root-burl interiors against insect infestation, bake thin films of sap into a glassy sealant. Their gatherings cluster around these dull glows, more for heat and scent than for illumination.

Their greatest technology is time. To bend a root into a bridge, they begin when it is hair-thin, tying and weighting it with care, returning day after day, week after week to adjust. A child may start a split-vine railing and become an adult before it is fully thickened and reliable. Some family projects last longer than a single lifetime; the initial planter is long dead before the structure reaches its planned curve.

### Belief and meaning

In a world where plant-time and animal-time are visible together, their thought is steeped in both.

They do not worship the plant-body as a god in the human, separate sense. It is too immediate, too plainly cause and effect. Damage it here, and a ceiling collapses there. Feed it here, and a new knob of growth appears in weeks. It is more like the ocean to an islander: encompassing, dangerous, generous, utterly indifferent. Still, they speak to it. When they cut deeply, they press their foreheads to the wound and hum low patterns meant to “call the healers”—their name for the microscopic and animal symbionts that clean and close. They tell children that the plant “listens in slowness”, that it remembers harm and help both, and that negligence accrues the way rot does: invisible at first, then suddenly everywhere.

They mark time in changes rather than in counted days. A story might begin, “Before the second narrowing of the west throat…” or “In the year when the bowl’s current turned colder.” Personal ages are expressed as associations with structural projects: “I was born when the high bridge was still a single vine,” says an elder; “I took my naming when the double-root above the nursery sprouted its first shadow-leaves.”

They have myths about origins, but these are not creation ex nihilo. Instead they revolve around migration: once, long ago, their ancestors lived in another throat, which dried, or rotted, or was torn open by something vast. A fragment of that old hollow, seeded with ancestor-clusters, was carried by flood or falling log to this current host. Their rituals of memory involve tiny acts of re-enactment: pouring a thin stream of water from one bowl to another, scattering a handful of dried moss clumps from a high ledge to watch how they lodge.

Ethics, for them, are ecological. The worst crime is not killing another—they accept that as sometimes necessary—but creating a vulnerability that persists. To dig an unauthorized tunnel that weakens a support, to overharvest a fungus that keeps a certain rot at bay, to seal too many vents and cause a pocket of stale, sickening air—these acts mark one as dangerous to everyone’s survival. Such offenders are sometimes exiled to the periphery, where risk is constant; more often, they are kept under intense watch, their movements always accompanied, their free choice curtailed.

They have taboos about complete darkness as well. Certain deep pockets where no light ever glimmers, where even slow creatures seldom pass, are considered “closed mouths” of the plant—places that are not to be awakened. Children are told that to bring light into such a pocket is to call something older than root and water, something that eats the memory of paths. Whether that is literal or metaphor, the effect is practical: there remain untouched refugia of microbiota, reservoirs the city has not yet altered.

### Neighbors and commensals

They do not stand at the top of their food web. In the spaces between clusters, other intelligences hover.

There are nodules of fungus that, over years, have become quasi-organized: great mycelial brains the size of a fingernail that regulate their own blooming, direct nutrient flows along invisible threads, even alter their bioluminescence in patterned pulses. The people have noticed. Certain shrines are simply places where they bring carefully curated scraps—shell, sap, meat—to a particular fungus-knot that, in return, blooms when most needed or subtly inhibits the growth of competitor molds nearby. They call these knots “listeners-in-the-dark.” It is not clear whether true communication occurs, but generations of reciprocal habit have built a relationship.

Some of the city’s pests have adapted, too. Tiny mite-like grazers that once only ate algae films now specialize in nibbling the cords and bindings the people use to train plants. Whole dynasties of these mites exist at the edge of the city, feeding on the infrastructure that shapes infrastructure. The people wage a constant, careful war here: not to exterminate (for even these mites have their predators, whose loss would be dangerous) but to contain. They use repellant scents, confuse the mites by adding decoy cords of inedible fiber, introduce parasite wasps that keep their numbers in check.

There are also creatures they have turned fully to their own ends. Soft-bodied grazers, not much brighter than a worm, live in pods near most dwellings. Fed scraps and allowed to graze controlled patches of mold, they in turn produce a steady trickle of waste rich in nutrients and, in some breeds, faint cold light from gut symbionts. Children are often set to tending these, learning responsibility and, incidentally, keeping them from going feral.

Then there are the burrowers, the great terror. Through long, bitter experience, the people have mapped rough territories of individual burrowers by feel: this quadrant of the plant thrums with the deep, slow heartbeat of one; that region went silent last year and might now be vulnerable to colonization by a new, hungrier one. They have learned some of these beasts’ rhythms: when they rest nearest the surface, when they dive deeper. Raiding parties sometimes go out not to kill, which is nearly impossible, but to irritate: planting bitter nodes, seeding irritant fungi, drumming patterns that the burrowers find uncomfortable, nudging them to shift their tunnels away from critical supports.

### How they see their own city

To them, the city is not a set of “buildings” but a pattern of favored curvatures and remembered stresses. A home is where the root bulges just so underfoot, where the overhead mat gives a certain soft echo when tapped, where the smell of family is most dense. A market—or its equivalent—is not a square but a knot of crossing paths near a distinctive cluster of luminous sacs, where one always hears a certain mix of voices and detecting scents.

They map mentally—not in two dimensions, but as a braid of verticals and horizontals, each tagged with stories. “The path that used to run under Elder Mar’s fall-trap” remains named thus long after Elder Mar and the trap are gone. Directions are given in terms of events and senses: “Go until the drip-sound doubles, then turn where the root skin feels like old bark, then climb toward the sour smell until you see green-light like frog-gut.”

They know that their city is small. Upward ones remind them; outward scouts, who foray down rare tunnels into neighboring hollows, return with tales of other pockets where no city lives—only animal swarms and wild fungus. They imagine, vaguely, that there may be other clusters like theirs elsewhere in the same plant, dim cousins who have never met. In some stories, two such cities eventually grow toward one another, their coaxed architectures causing a collision of growth-fronts, resulting in conflict, exchange, or tragic mutual collapse.

But they also know intimately that “small” here is deceptive. Within the circumference of a human hand, an entire civilization sustains itself, worries, tells stories, plans three or four slow seasons ahead. Every surface is both home and hazard; every new drip, every unusual silence, might foreshadow some change around which lives will have to bend.

### What it is, to be them

To inhabit this moss-throat city is to hold three timescales in your body at once.

In the tremor-sensitive tip of your tail, you feel the immediate: the next drip, the approach of a predator, the slight flex that means a root has shifted under your weight. Your muscles are always ready for a small, precise adjustment, a hook of claws into a safer crevice.

In your hands and tools, you feel the mid-term: the project that will take days of scraping before a path is stable, the fungus bloom that must be harvested over several nights lest it rot in place, the young vine that must be visited and re-tied each time you pass.

In your stories and rituals, you hold the long: the memory of when water once rose to swallow a third of the city and then receded, leaving a new stain-line and altered ecology; the wisdom that a path that seems too reliable, too unchanged, for too long may actually be thickening toward a dangerous fracture; the knowledge that your great-grandparent began the training of this very buttress you now trust with your weight.

You live in a cool hush, your breath always faint against the thick, oxygen-rich air, your voice always near another’s ear. You are never truly alone; above, beneath, within the root you cling to, other lives crawl, flow, pulse. You know in your bones that your city does not stand apart from them but is one more patterned concentration of hunger and fear and cooperation.

And still, within that, you carve out something astonishing: continuity. You raise children who recognize the same curve of moss-ledge you loved as a child. You sleep in a darkness that is familiar, punctuated by the same slow, comforting drip that marked your parents’ nights. You pass on a stone flake or an old comb to someone with your scent in their skin and your stories in their head.

In a room-sized basin inside a throat of moss, under a sky of wet tissue and distant grey, they live: not merely surviving but insisting, quietly and with great patience, on a shared shape to their days.
