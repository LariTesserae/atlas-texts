They live inside the margins, in the tolerances and misalignments. Nothing here is centered on them. They are what persists in the slack left over when a machine this large cannot be perfectly tight.

---

## The First Inhabitants: The Ones Who Built

They are not present, but their absence structures everything.

Whoever made this place thought with hands. Their intelligence is all joint and lever, mass and counterweight. They did not have calculus, but they could look at a cliff and see how to hinge it.

You can reconstruct them imperfectly from how the world fits their bodies and forgets yours:

- **Reach and stance.**  
  Control levers are placed too far apart for human shoulders, spaced for arms half again as long, or torsos that twist more easily than ours. The carved grip patterns are deep, like gouges for thick, blunt fingers that needed help to hold.

- **Height and eye-line.**  
  There are walkways—now choked by later machinery—whose parapets come somewhere between your chest and neck, neither comfortably human-waist nor giant’s knee. Inspection slots in housings are at a consistent vertical level: you must crane your neck, or climb onto something, to look through them. These openings are framed with wear, as if faces once leaned there for long minutes, watching the grind.

- **Strength and limitation.**  
  Everything is over-built. A single lever, once mobile, is balanced with counterweights so precisely that someone relatively weak could have shifted a thousand tons with steady effort. That someone did not have precise metal tools: the lever ends are socketed around knotted loops of rope, their bindings thick with redundant wraps and wedges. A people intimate with failure, planning for strands to fray, beams to crack, and yet wanting the motion to continue.

You never find language. No carved pictograms, no embedded tablets. If they recorded instruction, they did it in the positions of stones and the lengths of rods: the running machine itself a memory, the pattern of cycles their script.

If they had faces, those faces are gone. What remains is their thought-style: an insistence that any principle simple enough to hold in the mind should be large enough to reshape a horizon.

At some point, they receded—or were replaced by their own work. The mechanisms now run far beyond what hands alone could manage, cycling on stored tensions and locked-in triggers. The builders might be buried within it, or on a different layer of this strange planet’s constructed geology, or extinct.

Whatever they were, this place is their fossilized intention.

---

## The Current Dominant “Life”: The Slow Mineral Weavers

The true occupiers are not animals. They are patterns that ride water and stone.

The thin crusts and films you see at first as dirt or scale are not passive deposits. They are active reactions, self-advancing arrangements of matter that exploit the machine’s gradients.

Think of them as three overlapping “species”:

### 1. The Lattice Crusts

These are the hexagonal nets that webcrete in seams and along still, cool edges.

- **Structure.**  
  Each hexagon is a microcell: concave, thin-walled, its edges enriched in metals leeched from the water. Within each, faint branching ridges replicate, fractal-like, as if building scaffolds for something yet smaller.

- **Behavior.**  
  They seed where vibration is strong enough to circulate water in microcurrents but not strong enough to tear them off. They advance by dissolving and re-precipitating the substrate, loosening a grain here, laying down a wall there. When touched, they sacrifice the outer layers, shattering into dust while a subtler pattern endures deeper in the crack.

- **Sensing.**  
  They have no nerves, but they respond to changes in pressure and chemistry: new seepage with a slightly different metal ratio causes them to branch toward it, old dry zones are abandoned, allowed to erode back to bare plate. In aggregate, they behave like a slow, distributed nervous system for the built geology, mapping out where the machine leaks, where stresses accumulate.

Over centuries, if undisturbed, they can reshape a seam into a channel with regular ribs and grooves—functional drainage, accidental art.

### 2. The Climbing Films

The translucent skins that move “uphill” along damp walls are more opportunistic.

- **Physics as metabolism.**  
  They exploit ion gradients between metal-rich trickles and buffered surfaces. Their very membrane is a semi-conductor; as water passes through, tiny charges build, driving the migration of charged clusters along the film. This drift is their version of blood flow.

- **Motion.**  
  They creep not by muscle but by differential expansion: absorbing ions on one edge, stiffening that side, relaxing the other. To you, they are nearly still; to themselves, they are constantly surging in glacial ripples.

- **Ecological role.**  
  They detoxify. Where runoff would otherwise corrode channels or clog drains with harsh precipitate, these films incorporate the harmful ions into their matrix, thickening and curling into draped folds. When a rare shock of heat or mechanical abrasion destroys them, they release a dust of charged flakes downstream—spores of pattern, each ready to seed a new film where conditions are similar.

They keep the machine from choking on its own chemistry. They are repair mechanisms that were never designed, grown entirely from the physics that governs the place.

### 3. The Warm-Fissure Corals

At the faintly warm cracks, the most intricate forms arise.

- **Morphology.**  
  Delicate, fluted calyxes out of glass and metal oxides, layered like the innards of a shell. On their surfaces, nanoscale pores array in regular whorls, spacing tuned to the oscillations of the surrounding vibration.

- **Function.**  
  They are resonant collectors. Vibrations in the structure focus standing waves in the fissures; these corals grow at the nodes, capturing and dampening specific frequencies by flexing. The energy they absorb slightly heats them, which in turn drives microconvection in the seepage water, enhancing nutrient flow.

- **Feedback with machinery.**  
  Where they flourish, certain harsh harmonics in the machine’s motion are reduced. Rumbles become smoother, fewer high shrieks. They have, unintentionally, tuned their environment: the machine shakes them, they blunt the shaking. If smashed in a surge, the harmonics return until new growths re-establish.

Taken together, these mineral weavers are the landscape’s new nervous and skin systems. They do not think, but they adapt; they do not move in the way animals do, but they migrate and compete for gradients. They occupy every niche the machine’s roughness creates.

## The Animals: Fringe and Fugitive

Living tissue is a minor experiment here, a thin overlay on mineral life. The animals are few, small relative to the machine, and shaped by constant threat.

### 1. The Seam-Burrowers

You saw one as a moving knot in a crack. It has no front in the mammalian sense; it is a torus that has forgotten it ever had a hole.

- **Body plan.**  
  Continuous rings of articulated plates around a compressible core. Each ring can twist relative to its neighbors, giving the burrower the ability to corkscrew, flatten, or arch itself.

- **Feeding.**  
  Its mouth is not at an end, but as a band: a few successive rings in which the inner surfaces are lined with micro-teeth and chemosensitive patches. As the band passes through a seam, it rasps off mineral film, ingests slurry collected by capillarity, and passes it inward along ciliated grooves.

- **Circulation and excretion.**  
  Inside is a pressurized gel that doubles as blood-equivalent and structural brace. Metabolic products accumulate in vesicles embedded in the plates; periodically, the burrower pauses and sheds a ring, which dries and cracks into the pellets you sometimes find crumbled in seams. These pellets are rich in concentrated metals—fertilizer for the crust lattices.

- **Sense and navigation.**  
  It hears through its body, feeling resonance differences as it passes beneath various mechanisms. It can detect ahead, along the seam, where vibration becomes too intense—a crush zone—and changes course by gradually biasing twist in its rings. It never sees; eyes would be liabilities where rock and plate meet.

Across the valley, millions of them constantly polish and widen the machine’s innumerable microgaps. Their small work accumulates into larger-scale drainage channels and expansion spaces. In a way, they prolong the machine’s functional life while feeding entirely from its byproducts.

### 2. The Overhead Radials (“Girdle-Crawlers”)

Those disk-like things that flowed along beams are as close as this world gets to arboreal primates.

- **Anatomy.**  
  A central, shielded core—layered cartilage and fibrous bone—ringed by six to ten flexible limbs. Each limb ends in a wide pad consisting of thousands of microscopic hooks embedded in a soft, slightly adhesive matrix, letting it stick to rough composite or oily metal alike.

- **Locomotion.**  
  They move in a rolling gait: three or four limbs detach, arc ahead, find purchase, then pull as the opposite set release. The body never hangs from one or two points; redundancy is their life insurance in a hostile lattice.

- **Diet.**  
  They graze on the climbing films and the youngest mineral corals, not by scraping them bare but by biting out strips, leaving enough matrix for regrowth. Their saliva carries enzymes and ions that encourage faster replenishment, ensuring that their food source recovers. In return, they excrete compact nodules rich in reprocessed metals and organics—the pellet-like masses you saw fall and shatter. The films and crusts colonize these fragments eagerly, gaining trace nutrients rare in this largely inorganic environment.

- **Reproduction and sociality.**  
  They are solitary or travel in pairs at most. Their eggs—few, large—are pasted to the undersides of beams above reliable mist sources, encased in porous shells that wick and filter water for the embryos. Hatchlings are miniatures, already capable climbers, compelled by instinct to memorize, through motion, the vibration-patterns of safe routes.

- **Sensing.**  
  They see poorly; their eyes are ocelli tucked into creases on the shell’s upper surface, good only for gross light differences. Most of their “vision” is tactile-auditory: limb pads and ventral ridges are packed with mechanoreceptors. They know beams by how they sing under load.

Girdle-crawlers inhabit a 3D maze where a misstep can throw them into pulverizers or crush zones. Those that live long are virtuoso readers of resonance, moving in a hidden choreography with the machine.

### 3. The Piston-Watchers

Those glimpsed clusters of limbs in wall recesses belong to a different lineage: thin, planar creatures adapted to hide within and across moving gaps.

- **Form.**  
  Imagine a many-legged mantis flattened into a sheet. The core body is a narrow ribbon; from it sprout pairs of stick-like limbs that can fold completely against the ribbon or extend into bridging struts. Their skin is matte, the same dark composite hue as the machinery, patterned with striations that align with common chisel marks—perfect camouflage and a form of visual mimicry in a world that barely uses sight.

- **Ecology.**  
  They feed on burrowers, ambushing them at specific chokepoints where multiple seams converge near a piston’s berth. A watcher will map, through repeated observation, the exact timing of a piston’s stroke, then dart across the gap in the safe interval to press its ribbon-body flat along a crack where burrowers must pass. When one comes, the watcher’s limbs wrap and pin, injecting digestive enzymes through fine barbs.

- **Intelligence.**  
  They are among the sharpest minds here, but their intelligence is narrow as a knife. Everything revolves around timing, path prediction, and memory of vibration sequences. They “think” in beats and intervals: this rumble, then this knock, then safe; that screech, then this rising roar, then danger.

- **Communication.**  
  Rarely, two watchers meet at a contested seam. They communicate by drumming patterns against the wall with synchronized limbs, modulating the local resonance in specific ways. These exchanges are short, establishing dominance or mutual arrangement—who hunts which interval. They have no language in our sense, but they trade temporal maps: you take the third pulse after the long stroke, I take the first two.

They are relicts in the sense that they hold onto a niche that was probably more favorable when the machine’s cycles were different. Some of their carefully timed routes lead now to dead, frozen pistons, to old corridors entirely filled in. They continue to return, drumming frustration into unyielding stone.

## Microfauna and In-Betweens

Between burrowers and crawlers, smaller things exist, barely animals, not quite mineral growth either.

- **Slurry-sifters** lie in the sumps beneath the ribbed basins, like mats of living hair. Each filament is a threadlike organism that intercepts fine particulate from water, using the chemical energy locked in wear-dust. They knot into patches, then peel off as sheets that float briefly in hidden cisterns before adhering somewhere new. When a basin overflows, some are flung upward, briefly visible as drifting, slick veils in the churning water before being dragged back down.

- **Spark-gnats** congregate near those rare white flashes from deep discharge slots. They are not aerial insects; they are motes of metal-laced gel ejected as vapor and recondensed in the cooler airflow. Charged, they ride electromagnetic eddies like dust on wind, occasionally haloing around arcing points. As their charge dissipates, they settle in quiet corners, a fine conductive dust that seeds new mineral films.

These entities blur the line between chemistry and organism. They are short-lived, perpetually forming and dissolving, yet their populations are stable enough on the long scale to factor into the place’s balance.

## What It Means to Live Here

For every inhabitant, survival is a problem of **rhythm and refuge**.

- **Rhythm**, because the machine’s cycles are the only constant. The safest spot can become lethal if a cam’s profile wears down a fraction, changing timing. Any being that endures must build its behavior on the recognition of long, repeating sequences, and must be able to notice when a sequence slips out of true.

- **Refuge**, because nothing soft can withstand the main flows. Life clings in the unplanned spaces: hairline cracks, undersides of supports, stagnant pockets in the plumbing where turbulence never quite reaches.

In this sense, the mineral weavers are the primary inhabitants: they infiltrate the structure itself, becoming part of it. The animals are transients, riding on that quiet colonization.

No creature here rules. The machine does not notice them. It is not predator, not god; it is environment, foundational and unconscious. It will crush a lineage as easily as a single individual, not out of malice but out of momentum.

## How the Machine Has Changed Them—and They, It

Over long time, life and mechanism have reshaped each other, unintentionally.

- The **burrowers’ constant polishing** has turned once-tight joints into efficient micro-drainage. As a result, catastrophic plate buckling from trapped seepage is rarer now than in the machine’s early epochs. An engineer, if any remained, might commend this “maintenance” without realizing it was the byproduct of feeding.

- The **climbing films and corals** have altered wear patterns. By targeting certain frequencies, they’ve smoothed out destructive resonances that would have fractured beams. Entire sectors of the lattice owe their continued existence to these crystalline “parasites.”

- The **girdle-crawlers’ grazing and pellet-dropping** transport crucial trace elements—carbon, nitrogen, complex organics—across otherwise sterile spans of machinery, seeding new mineral growth in places the raw water chemistry would not support. They have inadvertently diversified the ecologies of films and crusts.

- The **piston-watchers’ predation** keeps burrower density in check in certain chokepoints. Without them, some seams would be over-widened, jeopardizing the precise fits on which larger assemblies depend.

If the extinct builders could see their creation now, they would find a machine still recognizably theirs, yet subtly, pervasively modified—self-patched in ways they never engineered.

The place is no longer just a tool. It has become a host.

## The Human Place (If You Stay)

If you remained here long enough to be more than a visitor, you too would become an inhabitant shaped by it.

You would learn to walk with your knees slightly bent, tuned to the most common ground shocks, so your joints ride the tremors instead of fighting them. You would breathe in phrases between thunder-strokes, timing movement with lulls and impacts.

You would come to navigate by **sound maps**, associating clusters of knocks and grinds with landmarks:

- The triple-boom-then-screech where the misaligned cam drives a reluctant press.
- The slow, surf-like whoosh of a deep cistern emptying, marking an unseen basin.
- The glassy clatter that means overhead chains are swinging free—a warning to stay clear.

Light would be less important than the tiny changes in pressure on your skin, the way mist swirls to reveal hidden drafts. The faint ozone tang of an impending discharge slot would be as vivid a signpost to you as a road sign is aboveground.

If you lived long enough to raise children here, their lullabies would be patterned after the long cycles; their stories would personify certain recurring noises as beings—distant, unfelt gods of the thud and roar. They would know, by half-conscious calculation, just how much time lies between the rumble underfoot and the hammer’s fall across the valley.

Your species would join the others in the only real task shared by all inhabitants: tracking the machine’s moods, finding slack when tension spikes, and nesting in the interstices of a world forever assembling itself.

---

In the end, what lives here are:

- The **ghosts of the builders**, present only as intent frozen into levers and beams.
- The **mineral ecologies**, sheets and lattices and corals that treat the machine as soil, plumbing, and skeleton.
- The **few small animals** that read vibration like script and move with exquisite caution through a place that never looks down.

Nothing in this valley is central, nothing finished. Life, like the machinery itself, exists in a permanent interim: iterative, adjusting, surviving on the thin difference between crush and clearance, between flood and trickle, between darkness and the dull, failing glows in the cracks.
