They live here the way marrow lives in bone.

Not as tenants, not as visitors, but as the content of a container they long ago grew around themselves. Nothing that moves in this bowl imagines a world without walls, any more than your gut flora imagines the sky.

They are not one species, and they are not many species in the way you know that phrase. They are a web whose threads have thickened until they’ve become load‑bearing.

You could start with the substrate‑makers.

These are the original masons, if anything here can be called original. They secrete the hard, spongy tissue that forms buttresses and vaults; they extrude the fibrous mats that carpet terraces; they swell and stiffen into ribs that bridge hollows. They don’t remember when they were small. Their bodies run in long trains through the walls, through the floors, through the columns: pale cables of composite flesh that branch and merge like nerves.

You rarely see them whole. You see their mouths: circular, lipless openings that dimple a surface like pores, dribbling a slow ooze that sets into structural foam. You see their growth fronts: smooth, bulged arcs where a corridor has been thickened, the inner face glossy and new, still crisscrossed with fine veins that will later recede. Inside each wall, inside each arch, there are cylinders of slow heartbeat and peristalsis. They are the body of the city.

They do not think in any way you would call thought. They follow gradients: of tension, of crowding, of humidity, of faint chemical traces left by past stress. Where load grows high, their tissue secretly thickens. Where flow is needed, it thins. They are the reason the landscape never quite collapses, only sags into new shapes. Anyone that crosses their surfaces leaves signals; they respond over decades.

Interleaved with these are the breathers.

They are the lungs of the bowl: enormous, static sacs that rise as bulges in ceilings or domes in side chambers. Their skins are ribbed, each rib a band of contractile fiber that tightens and relaxes with implacable slowness. When many of them happen to align, you feel that deep shudder through the ground — the basin’s mass inhaling or exhaling.

They open not to the outer sky but into networks of narrow tubes. The tubes run down into the wetter depths and up toward the thin, high cracks. At one end they draw in comparatively fresh air from vents that sip the outside; at the other they stir the interior, pushing stale exhalations into spaces where bacterial mats and chemical films strip out waste gases and turn them back into food.

The breathers are blind, deaf except to pressure waves. What they “know” is the global rhythm. They expand more on days when passing storms drive denser air into the vents. They slacken in long clear spells when the outside wind thins. Over lifetimes they thicken or shrink, modulating the overall humidity and the base tone of the bowl’s thunderous breathing.

Around and upon these bulk organs live the grazers of flow — soft, frilled animals that sit with their faces toward whatever trickle of strengthened breeze leaks from the nearest breather’s ducts. They are shaped like open hands, like anemones engineered for air instead of water, each finger or tentacle fringed with cilia that sift the aerosol stew: spores, shed scales, bacteria, bits of film. Their waste falls as fine, rich dust into the corridors, to be licked up by other mouths.

The water‑keepers are quieter still.

You meet them as those oval sumps cupped in soft tissue. Their visible parts are simple: a ring of spongy lip; a shallow basin, elastic and damp. Below that, though, is depth — stacks of alveolated organs reaching down into the fabric of the bowl, every surface tuned to slow exchange.

They hoard when the bowl is lucky, when mist and rain thread themselves through canopy cracks and trickle down special conduits to their mouths. They swell, their inner tissues engorged, membranes relaxing to cradle more liquid. In lean times they give it back: capillary forests inside them push water up thin strands that weave out along corridors, emerging as that ubiquitous slickness. Their control is not centralized; each cistern listens only to its own stretch and osmotic whisper, yet in aggregate they keep the bowl from ever going bone‑dry.

They are tended, in a sense, by things that don’t know they are tenders: sponge‑eaters that nibble away dead outer tissue so new absorbent layers can grow; burrowers that tunnel between the cistern’s deeper chambers, occasionally linking them, allowing water to be shared. A cistern that is too isolated shrivels; one opened to too many hollow spaces floods them, rotting their supports. The population of burrowers rises and falls against that balance, an accidental engineering.

All around, layered through, are the scrapers and ruminants — the reworkers.

On any given wall, if you run your hand along it, you’ll feel tracks. Smooth swathes where broad mouths have passed; roughened patches where a former layer has been gnawed down to its fibrous core; islands of untouched, slightly firmer tissue either too new or too old for most tastes. The city cannot grow clean; it would choke on its own production. These animals keep it porous.

One major type is the plate‑creeper: those flattened disks with gripping undersides. They feed by grazing. Their lower surfaces ooze a slurry of enzymes; their upper skins, armored in tough, desiccation‑resistant tissue, are mosaics of sensory hairs. As they slide, the film they leave behind is rich with broken‑down proteins, half‑digested fibers. Fungal threads and bacterial films blossom in that wake, and within hours a secondary wave of smaller feeders moves in. Behind the plate‑creepers, surfaces become more sponge‑like, less solid, opening new pores where beneficial microbes can lodge and where structural growth can later thicken.

Plate‑creepers also “talk” in ways that matter at the scale of the environment. The pheromones they leave in their slime alter how the substrate‑makers grow behind them. Heavy traffic in one direction subtly biases new growth to form ridges and load paths aligned to that direction. Over generations, the city co‑sculpts its own boulevards not with intent but with mutual rutting.

Beneath your feet, weaving through the mats, are the filigree miners. They are narrow, translucent things: somewhere between worms and threads, their bodies mostly digestive tract and reproductive buds. Individual miners don’t travel far. They oscillate, really: stretching, contracting, pushing their blunt ends into soft substrate, drawing backwards the way a needle pulls thread. They digest what they can and leave behind a looser weave. Their collective effect is to keep the mats aerated, to keep decay in the slow, fermentative zone where energy is wrung fully from falling husks and droppings.

Viewed across a few square metres, their traffic makes the ground shudder in those very slow waves you saw in the “moss,” as if the floor were breathing on its own. It is breathing, in a sense: the miners’ motion pumps air and water through the mat. Above them, micro‑ecologies ride on the quality of that breath.

At larger size scales, the reworkers become landscapes unto themselves.

Those low, shuffling hills — the furred, many‑legged giants — are mobile farms. Their outer coats are not hair in your sense but dense, radiating forests of symbiotic filaments: photosynthetic plates at the tips, chemosynthetic colonies in the middle strata, decomposer webs near the skin. They carry their own “pastures” with them, harvesting the meagre light that drops through rents in the canopy and the chemical gradients that exist between their warm cores and the cooler air.

Their legs — usually twelve or more — are blunt, jointed columns sheathed in toughened tissue. Where they plant them, they crush. Where they drag them, they till. Their feet exude slow rings of nutrient gel. Wherever a giant has lingered, the ground afterward is a darker, richer brown, colonized quickly by fast‑growing films that other creatures rush to graze.

Giants are not solitary, but they do not herd. They move according to their own cycles, following very old scent paths that loop the bowl in months‑long circuits. When they happen to cross, they communicate by pressure and sound: low grinding bellows transmitted through the ground, rhythmic side‑presses of flank against flank. They swap hitchhikers in those moments. Entire lineages of smaller animals and symbiotes have radiated into the niches on a giant’s body surface: hollow‑bodied insects that drink sweat; filter‑feeders that live in folds that channel air; even thin‑shelled, leaflike organisms that unfurl from the giant’s back in brighter zones, then curl and sink into crevices when the light fades.

Older giants are more architecture than beast. When they die — which they do not often, and not quickly — they do not fall in any dramatic way. They slow, they park in some shallow depression whose outlines were themselves shaped by their passings, and over years their limbs stiffen. Their core sinks, their back slumps, their symbiotic coat becomes continuous with the surrounding mats. The substrate‑makers notice this mass of nutrient‑rich bone‑equivalent and grow around and through it. Many of the bowl’s pillars are, deep in their hearts, the layered, mineralized skeletons of these walkers.

Moving where the giants rarely go, there are the canopy trawlers.

Picture a thing like a many‑mouthed manta, but designed for climbing instead of swimming. A broad, thin body whose edges are split into dozens of stiffened fingers, each ending in a hooked pad. The underside is a carpet of sucker mouths; the top is studded with stiff, hair‑like antennae that read air current and vibration. They hang from the undersides of arches, from the beams of high vaults, draped like living tapestries until sound changes — a prey‑wave arriving in their private frequency band — sends a ripple through their bodies. Then they surge, releasing and catching, folding and unfolding, moving with eerie grace along the ceiling to position themselves over some denser patch of flyer traffic.

When they hunt, they descend like parachutes, their margins tightening into a bowl. Air and all it carries is funneled inward. Tiny flying things — spores, gnats, larval specks — slam into mucus and are swallowed. The trawler’s interior is a maze of slowly rippling folds, each fold lined with ciliated grooves that sort what enters: usable from inedible, large from small. Waste is expelled upward, in fine jets that trace away and fall as an invisible nutrient rain.

They are not the only predators, but they show the general rule: even the hunters are also processors, recyclers, makers of new gradients for others to ride. No mouth here is “purely” for killing.

Of the multitude that fly, some are merely dispersers — messengers for genes and spores — but a few kinds are as central to the basin’s self‑maintenance as any wall.

One such is the pulse‑bee, though it neither hums like a bee nor bothers with petals. These are fist‑sized fliers, rotund central sacs ringed with six to ten thin, translucent wings that shiver so quickly they’re blurs. They home not on light or smell but sound: each generation imprints on particular frequency chords that correspond to certain tissues’ resonances.

Substrate‑makers, breathers, cisterns — all of them sing, just beneath audibility: not intentionally, but because of the way their materials vibrate as they contract and swell. Pulse‑bees triangulate on those songs. They visit the mouths of walls, the stomata of lungs, the lips of cisterns, drinking secretions and depositing bound packets of genetic material, microbial consortia, cellular fragments. Over evolutionary time, they have become the slow, stochastic mixing mechanism that stops any given part of the basin from becoming a truly isolated clade.

Their own reproduction is tuned to the basin’s macro‑rhythms. They lay their eggs deep in quiet pockets, in old, muffled hollows where the sound is almost comfortable. Larvae fatten on stored gel and the faint thrumming that seeps through the walls, timing their metamorphosis to long rises and falls in the overall sound field — which in turn track multiyear cycles of moisture and outside cloud cover. In a glut year, pulses of adults flood the air, cross‑fertilizing the basin’s slow organs with rare vigor.

Below, in the damp hollows, there is a different world of swimmers and seepers.

For them, the “air” is the interstitial water lodged in mats and cistern margins. Their bodies are ribbons and ellipses, flattened to move between fibers, fringed with cilia or feathery appendages that double as gills. Some glow faintly — not for spectacle, but as incidental leak from slow, deep chemical reactions inside them.

They are the ones that finish what everyone else has started: the final degraders. They take in the small leftovers — the half‑rotted pellet, the thin smear of ancient protein in a forgotten crevice — and strip it to mineral forms. They excrete ions and salts directly into the tissues around them, to be sucked up again by substrate‑makers and filaments. Their lives are short and fast by the basin’s standards, populations booming and crashing in response to each new small flood of dissolved food from above.

If you looked closely enough in their wet, dim world, you’d find predation there too: tiny, translucent blades that slice through slower forms; sucking mouths that attach and hollow. But on the scale of the whole, they are less distinct actors and more like specialized organelles in a huge, distributed stomach.

Out toward the higher ridges, where the built canopy frays and thins, there is a different guild again: the edge‑dwellers.

They alone, among this multitude, experience something like weather. They know sudden drafts and the occasional stab of cold; they know the brief harshness of direct, unfiltered light when a hairline crack aligns with the sun. Their forms, correspondingly, are tighter, harder, more sealed.

One is shell‑like: a low, domed body that hugs ridges where condensation is least certain. Its outer layer is mineralized, cracking into plates as it grows, plates that can flex slightly on underlying ligament‑tissue. A ring of muscular “lips” around its rim can press it flat to the substrate, sealing in its internal atmosphere when the outside air thins or dries. When conditions suit, it loosens, lifts slightly, and walks on dozens of paired cilia, grazing thin biofilms that only grow in these dryer, brighter leaks.

Another is almost lichenoid: a composite spread of fungus‑plant‑animal that lays itself flat on cracks where real starlight filters. It appears inert when you watch from afar, a crust at most, but under magnification it is a forest in miniature. On its surface, microscopic spires trap light and direct it to deeper photosynthetic layers. Beneath those, slow animal cells patrol, ingesting intruding dust‑life and defending the colony against overgrazers. At its base, fungal wefts anchor it to the underlying architecture and trade nutrients with deeper mats. It is one of the few organisms here that truly “wants” the outside light; its presence at a crack subtly biases surrounding growth to keep that crack from sealing completely.

The edge‑dwellers, without knowing it, keep the basin’s envelope from becoming absolute. They enforce a slow, dynamic leakiness: a few shafts of honest sky, a few drafts of cold, a few infusions of the chemistry and radiation that originally allowed life to explode in the open air.

Among all this, it is tempting to ask: is there a central mind?

There is none in the sense of an individual, no single great controller nestled deep in the bowl, directing flows. Yet there is more here than blind accretion. Patterns, once stumbled into, are preserved and elaborated, as if the whole has learned.

For instance: the strange, regularity in the sound.

To your first impression it is cacophony, but the residents, born into it, are shaped by it. Many of the mid‑scale animals — the plate‑creepers, the winged dispersers, the slow predators — each emit their own contact calls, feeding murmurs, mating signals. Over eons, the basin has selected against those whose voices too closely overlap. Being lost in the general roar is death for any animal that must attract a mate, call a larva, warn kin.

So each lineage has carved out its own little auditory biotope: a band of frequencies, a characteristic rhythm, a timing pattern that falls on the troughs of others’ peaks. The background hum — the deep beating of breathers, the omnipresent rustle of miners, the thunder of giants’ feet — sets the framework, and upon it is layered a complex polyrhythm of lives. The result is not music. It is something closer to a huge, emergent signal space.

Young animals are imprinted with these bands. A flyer navigates by how its wingbeats interfere with the standing waves of certain corridors. A predator feels for the faint, off‑grid jitter of a herd of small grazers whose collective chewing pattern is their only signature. The walls themselves, elastic and strong, transmit and filter these waves, and over generations their material properties are selected to favor some transmissions and dampen others, simply because animals that live in tissues with useful acoustics reproduce better.

There is no conductor, but there is feedback. A patch of architecture that resonates in an especially clear way with the calls of some successful animal will, over time, tend to be colonized by more of that animal, whose excretions and microbe cloud in turn nudge substrate‑makers to maintain or even emphasize that resonance. Very slowly, call and corridor elaborate each other. The city becomes sonically as well as physically tailored to its inhabitants.

Thinking of it that way, you might say the “architects” here are not the big visible builders, nor any imagined central intelligence, but the loops themselves: habitual circuits of cause and effect that have become effectively immortal.

Loop: rain patterns outside → rate of condensation at canopy → growth of photosynthetic plates → abundance of flyers that feed on their saps → population of sweep‑predators → deposition of nutrient pellets on mid‑level terraces → thickness of mats → distribution of miners → aeration of substrate around a cistern → its ability to buffer drought → survival of substrate‑makers nearby → integrity of that section of the bowl’s rim → how much mist is trapped next season.

Loop: edge crack widens by chance rockfall outside → lichenoid composites colonize → their need for light subtly inhibits closure → more light fuels slightly more pulse‑bee larvae housed in that region’s shallow hollows → more adult pulse‑bees cross that sector each cycle, cross‑fertilizing edge‑dwellers and substrate‑makers → architectural tissues there come to have somewhat different mechanical and acoustic properties → certain flyer species preferentially route their migrations along that rim → their droppings seed different symbionts on giants who occasionally climb that high → those giants’ routes through the basin alter, dragging the pattern further.

These loops can tangle into what feels, from far enough away, like a coherent self.

If there is anything here deserving the term “they,” not as a plurals of bodies but as a unit, it is that super‑organism: the eons‑long accumulation of rhythms and feedbacks that has filled a mountain bowl with a living roof and a humming depth.

To its own constituents, there is no such concept. The plate‑creeper wants only its next stretch of moist film; the pulse‑bee its next resonant mouth; the miner its next line of half‑rotten fiber. Giants amble, cisterns swell, breathers sigh, each along grooves worn by ancestors that never called this “home” as something separate. They are organs and tissues, each with its own reproduction, its own evolutionary branch, locked into each other by the simple fact that none of them can exist elsewhere.

There are no gods here, no priests, no languages in air. There are only bodies, stacked and laced, wet with one another’s waste, living inside each other’s architecture. If you ask what lives in this constructed bowl, one answer is: everything. Another, held at an angle, is: one thing, in many, many pieces, endlessly remaking itself out of its own residue.

It is not immortal. Outside, glaciers and winds still plan. A shift in climate could dry the passing clouds, could starve the cisterns, could thin the breathers until more and more of the roof cracks and the harsh outside sky tears in. On some eventual day — not tomorrow, not in any span your bones would recognize — this entire envelope may desiccate, crumble, slough back down the slopes as dead sponge and fossil honeycomb. Rock will stand bare again.

Until then, this is how the place lives: as a held‑breath between stone and sky, a cavity so full of animals that “environment” and “inhabitant” have ceased to be different words.
