They live long, and they live slowly.

Not individually—an ordinary life here isn’t centuries long—but as a continuity, a lineage threaded through a city that never entirely stops growing. The inhabitants think of themselves less as “citizens of a place” and more as participants in an ongoing organismal project: a town that is also a body, a set of habits that is also a species.

You notice them first as scale. The city is intimate, yet it is not built at human height by accident. Doorways cut and re-grown, balconies, stairwells threaded through vertical membranes: they are all sized for something around your own dimensions. Shoulders brushing living walls, heads passing not far beneath ribbed ceilings—this is a city made for people who move upright, who expect a handrail at their ribs, a floor at the length of their stride.

Their bodies fit the climate that shaped the basin. They are built for cool, dry air that sometimes condenses but seldom floods. Skin or outer covering tends toward the matte, the non-reflective—tones that would blend into lichen and fibrous plant-rind: olive, dust-brown, bark-grey, old copper. There is little naked shine on them; gloss is reserved for deliberate things: tools, lenses, conductor-plates.

They are not hulking. Mass costs water; bulk radiates heat you must work to conserve. Most adults are lean and corded, with that unassuming economy you see in people who walk a great deal. Hands are strong and supple; fingers often long, stained from plant-sap and composite-dust, nicked by a lifetime of careful cutting and joining.

Their faces show something of their relationship with light. Lids a little heavy, brows or supraorbital ridges slightly pronounced, as if to shade eyes in a world where the sky is wide and the canopy thin. The eyes themselves are attentive but not darting; they are used to watching slow things. When they wait for a reaction from the architecture, they are timing in minutes, hours, weeks. That patience settles into their expressions. They do not fidget much. When they are still, they are very still.

Hair, where present, tends to be worn short or bound close—not out of militarism but to keep it from catching on the textured, living surfaces. Loose fibres drift, cling, colonise; they are not cavalier with any filament. Some go further: keeping heads shaved, or weaving hair with fine, washable cloth to keep the city’s microflora out. Colours vary, but there is a mild preference for whatever matches the surrounding palette: dusty blacks, muted browns, faded russets—shades that do not stand out starkly against green-brown walls.

Their clothing is grown as much as it is sewn. Base fabrics derive from the same plant-stock that forms the city’s bones: tough, fibrous sheets pressed thin, then softened and layered. From a distance, their garments read like quiet, practical uniforms: tunics that fall mid-thigh, wraps and jackets in simple cuts, leggings or close-woven trousers. Closer, you see elaboration. Surfaces are subtly ridged where conductive threads are woven in; seams hide capillary channels that wick sweat toward evaporative panels. Many garments are slightly thicker along the spine, the sides of the ribs, the forearms: built-in padding for the places most likely to press against living walls and ladders.

Colours rarely shout. Dyes favour the same muted spectrum the lichens have claimed: grey-greens, off-whites, ochres, soot. But patterns are everywhere, just constrained to small scales. A cuff will carry a band of delicate, almost mathematical motifs, echoing the fractal arrangements of certain leaf veins. A collar will be embroidered with diagrams of fluid-flow through a branch-cross section, stylised into something that reads as decorative to the uninitiated.

They move with the building, not against it. In a corridor that subtly narrows because the humidity-damp blades have folded in, they turn sideways without needing to look. When a walkway has taken on a new, slight tilt over a year, they adopt a slightly different gait, as one does on a ship. Children learn early how to read the city’s micro-postures: a faint tackiness underfoot that means a surface is still thickening, a shallow bulge in a membrane that signals internal pressure and warns against leaning too hard.

Work here is not separated cleanly into “skilled” and “unskilled” in the way many machine-cities insist on. Almost everyone learns, at least in outline, the three intertwined literacies: Plant, Metal, Water.

Plant is first and pervasive. It means knowing which tissues will thicken under stress, which will retract if cut along the grain, which nodes respond most strongly to localised heat or moisture. Children’s first lessons do not use chalkboards; they use a patch of low, responsive ground-layer. They stand in bare feet and feel how the city maps their weight, how a sustained pressure evokes a slow, radially spreading firmness as fibres reorient. An adolescent rite is to coax a simple thing from the architecture: a handhold in a difficult corner, a new step on a steep slope, a bulge of seating grown in a favoured meeting-place. Success is not judged by speed, but by how gently the intervention coaxes rather than forces.

Metal is the second literacy, acquired in the quiet factories and under the ribbed arches where pipes run. It is not a culture of big, roaring engines, but of careful fits and tolerances. They take apart old machines as much as they build new ones, studying the wear patterns on bearings, the pitting on impellers exposed to misty air. A good machinist here is someone who can fit a rigid component into living tissue and have both survive the next decade. Welds are made with double attention: to the crystal structure of the joint and to the plant’s eventual attempt to grow around, under, through whatever has been inserted.

Water is the third literacy, and the most revered. To be fluent in Water is to understand the flows you cannot see: the looping, layered systems of storage bladders, capillary channels, pressurised conduits within sap and steel alike. Those who tend these systems walk with a particular gravity. They are the ones who can read a faint change in the murmur beneath a floor and know a valve three strata down has stuck. In their hands, valves and reservoirs are not just infrastructure; they are organs in a semi-domesticated beast.

They do not worship the city, but they also do not treat it as a neutral backdrop. Their language (whatever its sounds) is laced with verbs that attach agency to the substrate. A bridge does not “wear out,” it “forgets its span.” A support does not “fail,” it “retires its effort.” When two blocks lean together over years until their membranes fuse, they are said to “marry.” When a dead section is composted in place and drawn back into root and rind, it “returns its story.”

They build shrines, but they are small and local. One may be a niche in a wall where an ancestor coaxed a difficult growth; the niche now holds a handful of polished stone from the exposed plain, a fragment of an obsolete machine. Another is under a lightwell that has never once failed to bring in the exact, cherished angle of morning sun; there, people leave thin plates of glass etched with phenomena only visible in that beam. None of these places demand worship. They invite recollection and a kind of technical gratitude.

You might expect an ecology so tightly husbanded to produce a culture of harsh scarcity, of strict, brittle rules. Here that severity is present, but tempered by time. They have learned that rigidity snaps where flexibility bends. There are customs rather than laws, reinforced by deep habit.

One of the oldest customs is the Rule of Cut and Wait. No one makes a major incision into the architecture alone. At least three must observe and agree on where, when, why. The first cut is always partial, followed by a period of watching: an hour, a day, a season, depending on the scale. They see how the living tissue answers. If it recoils violently, exuding sap or changing colour, the plan is rethought. If it thickens around the wound in an accommodating way, more is asked. This is not superstition; it is risk management, reified into ritual.

Another custom is the Silence During Waterfall. “Waterfall” is their term for a controlled overflow, when stored water is deliberately released to flush certain channels, clean microflora, equalise pressures. These events are scheduled with a regularity that reflects the basin’s climate. During them, people nearby keep voices low, footsteps careful. Not out of fear, but in acknowledgment that this is the closest thing the city has to a raised pulse, a visible systole. Children are brought to feel the vibrations underfoot, to hear the deep, transient rush beneath the usual soft sounds.

Their society is not large. The city’s scale would not comfortably hold millions. Instead, it carries, say, tens of thousands arranged in dense but breathable clustering. They divide themselves not primarily by wealth or strict district boundaries, but by Lineages of Practice.

A Lineage is a long, braided association of families, mentors, and apprentices around a shared speciality. One Lineage might tend to the high platforms and sparsely-leaved crowns—experts in load distribution and sky exposure. Another might specialise in the low, cool halls where condensation seethes, maintaining the delicate balances that keep moulds and slimes in check while harvestable microflora thrive. Yet another is devoted to the factories: keepers of stamping rhythms, guardians of ancient dies whose exact feedstock tolerances have been tuned across generations.

Lineages intersect constantly; no group is isolated. Children often grow up inside one tradition, then apprentice or partner into another, tangling the strands further. Arguments between Lineages are frequent and considered healthy. They dispute over where to reinforce a sagging span, whether to retire an old machine-chapel or refurbish it, how aggressively to coax the city’s edge outward toward a promising, slightly more humid patch of plain. These disputes are rarely settled by votes. Instead, they are settled by experiments, scaled carefully. A test cut here, a pilot growth there. The results become gossip, then precedent, then embodied wisdom.

They govern lightly, improvisationally. There is no towering administrative quarter, no ruling palace. Coordination happens through Councils, but these are temporary, convened around particular questions: water allocation during an unusually dry sequence of days; re-routing a major pipe when a derived organism has unexpectedly thickened into its path; managing an influx of rare, prolonged mist that could allow for a wave of growth if timed right. Council membership is not fixed. It tends to include respected practitioners, skilled water-readers, and at least one person adept with the city’s sparse but integrated sensor-net, which gives them non-biological data—temperatures, flow rates, stress gauges—in flashes of quantified clarity.

There are names among them for people who straddle the boundary between the long-slow and the short-urgent. Call them Mediators of Time. These are not mystics, but they have a feel for how long the city takes to respond versus how long a human crisis can be borne. They are the ones who argue either for patience (“wait three more cycles; the supporting buttress will have caught up”) or for decisive intervention (“this growth direction will not change in time; cut now, fix with alloy, teach the tissue a new habit”). They are rarely young, but they are not always old; they are those who have lived through at least one slow disaster and one fast one and learned the difference.

You might imagine such a society would fear buried things absolutely. Their avoidance of underground pipes suggests it. But the deeper truth is subtler: they distrust what cannot be inspected, cannot be watched adjusting. Transparency, visibility—these are more than practicalities; they are ethics. A pipe must ride the underside of a root-arch so anyone walking by can see if a joint is weeping. A data-line runs along the outer face of a wall, not inside it, so that a hand can trace it and feel if stray growth has begun to pinch.

When they must go below the walking level—as they do to tend storage bladders, root-knots, and deep conduits—they go with ceremony, marked by harness and light and the presence of at least one recorder whose job is not just to oversee safety but to remember what was changed. Old incidents have taught them that the city forgets in its depths. A closed cavity, once modified, will grow over scars until their origins are obscure. So they pair every descent with a story: spoken aloud, recorded in text or etched diagrams, and sometimes impressed as patterns of micro-scars into near-surface tissue above, leaving a hint that something beneath is not as entirely organic as it appears.

Death here seldom goes entirely into the air. Bodies are not burnt; fuel is too precious, and smoke unwelcome in soft lungs, vegetal or human. Nor are they laid under soil, for there is almost no true soil within the knot of the city, only woven plant. Instead, the dead are offered to specific, consecrated digesters: structures grown for the purpose of breaking down complex organics without letting them diffuse toxically. These digesters are halfway between organ and institution. They are warm within, layered with specialised microflora adapted to human tissues. They are fed plants too, and sometimes machine waste, but never mixed on the same day as people.

Families visit the digesters, but with a quiet, matter-of-fact tenderness rather than wailing. They place the wrapped body on a living shelf, whispering last words, then withdraw. Over days and weeks, the digester processes, strains, and re-allots the material into the city’s nutrient loops. A portion is often reserved, directed consciously toward some new growth: a bench in a communal space, a support for a lightwell. People will sit on or under that new structure and speak of the one they lost. “Her shadow is here,” they might say. They do not pretend identity persists, but they honour that particular reconfiguration of substance. Every so often, when a bench fails or a support must be cut back, they mark and preserve a fragment physically, as memento: a ring of densified fibre, a small plaque. Thus, memory is not solely cognitive; it is anchored in wood-like flesh.

Their festivals are few and measured. No riotous, constant carnivals; nothing that would strain the city’s quiet, elastic rhythms. But they do mark certain thresholds.

When the first mist of the “lean arc” comes—a predictable sequence of drier seasons—they hold the Closing. People gather in those broad, shallow halls where condensation used to seethe thicker. Lamps are dimmed. Humidity-sensing fronds are watched as they slowly pull back from their full, generous arcs to tighter, conserving postures. A storyteller will recount a year when the Closing came and did not end when expected, and what that taught them. Children are allowed to sit right against structural membranes and feel how, over the hours, their slight softness sharpens, the skin becoming more taut, more glossy. It is a lesson in limits: here is how much we can ask from our reservoirs before we begin to harm the slow life we depend on.

Conversely, when an uncommon, generous sequence of storms passes—when the basin is gifted with more water than models predict—they celebrate the Opening. The city itself participates. Peripheral structures that had been kept in a low, dormant state are allowed to swell and differentiate. Certain long-debated expansions are finally attempted: a bridge extended, a canopy widened, a new shaft coaxed through previously uncut mass. People walk the edges of the plain under light drizzle, boots kicking up wet dust that rarely exists long enough to experience. They plant seeds there. Not of wild plants; those have long ceased to be factor in this scoured basin. They plant experimental strains, the results of their horticultural lineages’ long thought: variants that might root deeper, capture more, change the equation of what the plain can support. Their sense of hope is slow and long-angled, but it is not absent.

Technology, in their hands, feels modest, almost shy, but carefully honed. They have no tall, glaring screens dominating plazas; light is functional and kindly. Communication rides on threaded conductors and low-power transmissions that do not interfere with the plant’s sensory network. Much of their data-gathering happens through hybrid nodes: living tissue embedded with clusters of mineral sensors, the whole thing reading moisture, tension, microvibrations.

Those who know how to interpret these nodes’ outputs—in small, staccato patterns of light or quiet, tonal beeps—are valued like weather-readers once were in agrarian cultures. They sit under a lightwell, back against a lichen-mapped wall, fingers absently tapping along the city’s slow pulse while they listen to reports: this trunk’s growth has stalled; that storage bladder is a quarter low; these high platforms have taken a new load from an unplanned growth. Their work shapes policy and maintenance more than any distant ideology.

Children are not sheltered from this. They are trusted with small, reversible tasks early. A ten-year-old might be sent to trim back exploratory rootlets that have begun to interfere with an external overflow groove. An adolescent might be assigned to watch a valve, noting its creaks over a day, learning the difference between healthy oscillation and incipient failure. In such a small population, competence spreads quickly and visibly. Pride is taken not in flashy individual achievement, but in elegantly solved collective problems: a new way to brace an arch that requires half the metal; a pruning technique that encourages desired growth without triggering defensive retraction.

They do, occasionally, know fear. Storms seldom linger, but in remembered history there have been anomalous events: a season when winds picked up and howled across the basin much harder than the ancient buttresses were prepared for; a rare, violent downpour that threatened to overwhelm the city’s absorption capacity. Stories of those times are carried forward as warnings, almost like myths—but always with engineering embedded.

Of the great wind, elders will recall how a particular line of platforms, grown with too much eagerness into a tall, thin sweep, began to oscillate dangerously. The city’s infrasonic thrumming rose to a pitch that made people nauseous. Mediators of Time debated: encourage the plant to thicken, which would take months, or cut sacrificially, removing mass and changing resonance immediately at the cost of decades of painstaking growth. They chose to cut. For weeks afterwards, you could see the raw, hacked edges, the wounded architecture oozing sap and slowly scabbing over. That scar is now an honoured hump in the street, a memory of choosing damage now to avert greater damage later. Parents bring children there to talk about hard decisions.

Visitors are rare. The plain is not welcoming, and the city is not visible from great distance; it lies low, a dark knot in a pale bowl. But the basin is not entirely alone on this world. Somewhere beyond the horizon are other attempts at habitation, other bargains struck with harsher or kinder terrains. On long timescales, small, infrequent caravans or delegations make their way here, be they human or otherwise.

The people of the plant-city treat strangers cautiously but with curiosity. They know that their way of life is precarious in its own, stable fashion: it depends on specific climatic patterns and a long-descended suite of co-evolved organisms. They are protective of those relationships. They ask invading seeds to be scrubbed away; they require visitors to pass through low, waiting halls where spores, dust, and foreign microflora can be assessed and, if needed, quarantined. Yet once those thresholds are negotiated, they are generous with information. They will explain their cut-and-wait rituals, display cross-sections of structural tissue, hand over seedstock of plants that are safe to share with other biomes.

In turn, they are hungry for tales of other strategies. To hear of a city that tunnels deep into rock, trusting buried things implicitly, fascinates and unsettles them in equal measure. To learn of a place that floats or a culture that lives almost entirely in machines without live substrate prompts long, evening discussions under the dim, greenish lights.

Their art leans heavily into what their lives already orbit: growth, time, quiet machinery. You find songs whose rhythms mirror the slow beating of pump-chambers, whose verses chart the historical thickness of a key load-bearing beam. There are tactile sculptures carved from old, retired plant-masses—pieces that preserve the complex internal architecture of a once-living strut, displayed so that fingers can trace the paths sap once took. Some musicians play with the body of the city itself, setting up resonant loops between high, flexible members and carefully timed percussive strikes, making the architectures hum at once more audibly, a temporary chorus.

There is narrative art in abundance: tapestries woven with threads dyed to represent moisture gradients across a decade; long, folding books printed with diagrams of certain hallways at five-year intervals, showing how openings have moved and ribs have thickened. Children’s picture-stories often feature personified structures: a shy buttress that takes too long to grow, a proud bridge that has to learn to accept a metal brace. These are not naive tales—they are instruction smuggled into play.

They are, in sum, a people who have learned to make their ambition congruent with slow forces. They do not lack drive; they plan, tinker, improve. But the scale at which they measure success is rarely single-year, let alone daily. A good decision is one that, when you stand at the basin’s lip and look back in twenty years, you can see written in the slight, steady reconfiguration of that greenish mass: here a platform broadened and did not break; there a corridor deepened and did not choke. Their heroes are not conquerors, but those whose negotiations with sap and steel left the fewest regrets.

They live in that knot of lichen-softened growth as both gardeners and tenants, mechanics and children. The city holds them and is held in turn. Each day, as dew almost burns away, they walk soft paths that remember their footsteps, listen for drops in quiet halls, and attend to the engines whose task is not domination but translation—turning human-scale intention into plant-scale change.

In the long quiet between storms, in the cool breath of the basin, they are what a place like this produces when given centuries: a culture of attentive reciprocity, settled without being stagnant, humble without being resigned, moving at the measured pace of structures that can afford to think in tree-years and machine-cycles at once.
