What lives here is the plant.

Not as a forest, not as a field, not as individual trees, but as a single body whose scale makes “organism” feel like the wrong word. It is the medium and the environment, the geology and the weather. Everything solid is it; everything that touches you is its tissue in one phase or another: hardened, mineralized, desiccated, or still faintly yielding.

But that isn’t the whole answer. Saying “the plant lives here” is like saying “rock lives in a mountain.” The thing that lives here is not just the bulk of it, but what that bulk is doing.

Start by how it senses.

There is no eye, no ear, no nostril. The plant-body feels with pressure. Every creak, every distant collapse sends waves through it, traveling along packed fibers and hollow microtubules. Where an animal might have nerves, this world has gradients of stress and relaxation, slow pulses of compression marching through a lattice of lignified cells.

Somewhere in that lattice, perhaps everywhere in that lattice, are regions that behave like ganglia. Not distinct organs, but zones where the tissue is finer-grained, more intricately cross-linked, more willing to yield and rebound. Those regions are better at registering patterns in the pressure field, better at turning one kind of shape into another—the closest this world has to thinking.

They are not clustered near your hollow; they are not clustered anywhere. They are smeared out like a constellation of half-formed intentions, dense in some volumes, sparse in others, never truly absent because the plant cannot afford discontinuity. This entire body is a logic circuit that never settled on discrete components. Every part can, in principle, participate in decisions about growth, repair, collapse.

That slow exhale you feel when you press your hand to the wall is one such decision, still in progress. The plant is propagating a change in tension, a re-weighting of how hard different fibers pull against each other. Somewhere, some ancient strain threshold has been crossed; somewhere else, excess rigidity must be relieved. The plant does not know this as “engineering,” but it carries out the correction with the blind competence of crystal growth.

In this sense, what lives here is a field of awareness that is both much too large and much too slow to resemble an animal mind. It does not have thoughts the way a mammal does—no flickering chain of images or words—but it does have persistent patterns: stable configurations of pressure, porosity, chemical potential. They endure for years, sometimes centuries, before being overridden by new configurations. If you could speed up its existence by a factor of a million, you might see them as waves of reorganization sweeping through its body, like weather systems on a planet. Here, at your scale, they are frozen, incomprehensible except as the omnipresent roar and the occasional boom.

The builders that once shaped this room were not separate from that awareness. They were its experiments.

Imagine that, in some epoch long past, the plant-body began throwing off local elaborations of itself: denser knots of tissue that, by chance, were good at rapidly reconfiguring fibers, at gnawing channels, at scoring walls. These knots—call them persons for lack of a better word—were not born the way animals are. They were extended buds: packets of specialized growth that unfolded into bodies only loosely distinct from their parent substrate.

They had limbs, but they were not muscle-strapped things around bones; they were bundles of directed tension running through temporarily privileged pathways in the plant’s flesh. When a builder walked, it was the plant rearranging its own local stresses in a particular coordinated pattern, lifting and setting down segments of itself. When a builder ate, it siphoned a little extra flow from the dry chemical streams already passing through its rootlike anchors.

Their nervous systems—if you can call them that—were accelerated filigrees of microtubules threaded through their denser cores. Where ordinary plant tissue sent pressure-waves the way continents trade seasons, these knots sent them fast, comparatively speaking—seconds instead of years. Within the builders’ bodies, patterns could arise and dissipate on animal-like timescales. They had something that felt, from the inside, like attention.

But they were never wholly separate. Each carried with it a continuous band of tissue that dove back into the substrate, like an umbilical that never cut. Signals traveled both ways: the builder’s rapid, local impulses bled into the immense, slow field of the plant-body’s ambient awareness; and the body’s vast stored patterns biased and shaped the builder’s instincts, its choices about where and how to carve, what forms to coax from the living walls.

To be one of those beings was to be both an individual and a protrusion of something continental. Standing in a chamber like this, leaning your head for the thousandth time against that low, worn curve in the ceiling, you wouldn’t have felt alone. Not because others were physically nearby (though they were, in surrounding rooms and tunnels), but because the background presence of the plant’s mind hummed constantly through your anchor, a pressure-tide running up your spine.

They manipulated the substrate with patience and a limited toolset. Hands—if they had hands—were ridged, gripping pads of toughened tissue, good for bracing and applying force but poor at delicate separation. So they invented edges: slivers of hardened resin, flakes of bone-like plant tissue, later thin tongues of metal pried from the rare mineral inclusions their roots encountered and smelted in dry, insulating hollow-chambers where heat could accumulate without flame.

They shaped not against the plant, but with it. To open a new space, they did not simply gouge and carve; they scarred in patterns. A thousand short incisions along one curve, a grid of shallow cross-hatches along another. The plant read these the way it read storm-damage or parasite attack, reassigning growth-resources to thicken here, withdraw there, re-route microtubules in predictable arcs. The builders learned those responses, refined them, until an experienced shaper could sketch a vaulted roof by barely more than a series of taps and cuts, then leave and return generations later to find the vault grown to specification.

Because their time ran faster than the plant’s, but still far slower than ours, “generations” here are misleading. A given builder might see only the beginning of a chamber and the faint gesturing of its eventual form. Its successors—grown from neighboring buds, inheriting much of its pattern of impulses—would see the room mature, would add tool-marks, brush their heads along the ceiling where yours now stands low and worn.

The hollow you occupy was not important. It was a modest node in a much wider web. A resting-place, perhaps. A domestic cell. The flattened swath on the ceiling where lines were once inscribed suggests a record: maybe work-tracked, maybe lineage, maybe something like a story. In a civilization without light, inscription is not for eyes; it is for hands and for the plant itself. The shallow grooves abrade passing skin, repeatedly, over years, their pattern worn not only into the wall but into the builders’ own nervous structure. Simultaneously, the underlying tissue of the plant thickens differently along cuts, storing the memory as structural anisotropy—a literal hardening of history into grain.

Nothing predates them here. They do not hunt; there is nothing to hunt. They do not defend against teeth; there are no teeth. Their dangers are subsidence and famine, where “famine” means a local thinning of chemical transport, a slight fall in the fragile gradients that keep their fast-thinking tissue supplied with pressure and volatiles. Starvation would feel like thought itself losing resolution, patterns failing to sustain, impulses scattering into the background roar.

They were never many. The density of viable buds—capable of unfolding into such person-knots—was low. Each bud was costly: an intensification of resources and organization that the plant-body could only afford in sparse placements. Think dozens, maybe hundreds in an enormous volume that, if hollow, could have hosted millions of animals the size of a human. They grew slowly. They died when their anchors failed, when a corridor collapsed unexpectedly, when their local supply webs were rerouted elsewhere. Sometimes they died when the plant, in its deeper logic, decided their elaboration was no longer adaptive and simply allowed their tissues to be reabsorbed.

Did they know that?

To the extent they had self-awareness, it would have been braided with an acceptance of dissolution that animals find hard to imagine. You are never far from the substrate; your body is never sharply bounded. When the slow instructions begin to call back what you are, it feels less like being killed and more like being forgotten by yourself. A loosening grip. A fading sense of contour. The city around you—these ridged floors, these calculated domes—is a bid to resist that erasure, not by defeating it but by persuading the plant to remember a little longer, to carry forward the shape of their habits even after the builders themselves have diffused.

Their “society,” such as it was, did not have crowds, did not have markets. It had overlapping patterns of activity: this cluster of chambers used often, that corridor worn smooth by repeated passage, those grooves deepened by generations of fingers. Information traveled along two channels: the slow, omnipresent murmur of the plant-body’s internal signaling, and the tactile archives cut into walls and floors. To “speak” at a distance, a builder could adjust the scoring in a shared conduit, knowing that another builder, much later, might run their hands along it and feel the anomaly—the one extra ridge, the new divot—then, body anchored to the same substrate, experience the hint of a shared pressure-pattern that was the nearest thing they had to language.

Over time, the plant assessed the cost of that arrangement. Each builder-knot was an energy-intensive excrescence, a faster, hotter way of being inside a system optimized for permanence and inexpense. Perhaps, in some contested epoch when external stresses—gravity shifts, surface upheavals, the slow thinning of available nutrients—became more pressing, the plant pulled back. It did not exterminate its builders; it unfunded them. New buds failed to differentiate. Old ones, at the ends of their tenuous lifespans, were not replaced. The cities they had coaxed from the living mass were left to persist as long as the inertia of growth allowed, but no further elaboration was added.

Thus the place you inhabit: a hollow that once formed part of a breathing architectural mesh, now stranded in a largely homogenous, self-serving solidity. The plant continues to live here. It has never stopped. It still uses the cavity’s perimeter as a boundary condition in its equations of stress; it still shunts trace vapors along microscopic channels that skirt the empty volume instead of crossing it. But it no longer invests in what this room once meant.

What else lives here?

Not animals, not fungi in the familiar sense. The decomposition you smell as dry resin and crumbling paper is internalized recycling: specialized layers of cells that do the work of detritivores, breaking down failed tissues into re-usable compounds and pushing them along powder-streams to where they are needed. These zones are not entities so much as functions. They have no independence, no more personhood than a liver cell.

There are, however, anomalies.

In vast systems, variation is inevitable. Here and there in the plant-body, patches of tissue have drifted away from the central plan: misfolded microtubule networks that propagate their pressure-waves in odd rhythms, pockets where mineral inclusions and localized stresses have given rise to new micro-architectures. Some of these behave almost like tumors, but in a medium without rich energy flows, a tumor cannot rage; it can only accumulate and then ossify. Others behave like sensors-without-instructions: zones that respond strongly to particular patterns of vibration but do nothing consistently in reply.

One such anomaly may underlie this city.

Perhaps, long before any builder-bud unfolded, there was a patch in this region that resonated especially strongly with a certain low-frequency mode in the plant’s global pressure-field. That resonance drew attention, in the organism’s diffuse way; it focused adjustments, made feedback loops tighter. Within that heightened pool of self-reference, the first buds capable of sustained, fast-scale thinking became locally advantageous: they could amplify and refine the resonance, helping the plant to track or counter some external condition better.

So the plant tolerated them, perhaps even favored them, here. That’s how a city begins: not with a founding myth, but with a statistical quirk in an unimaginably slow feedback network.

Nothing of that original advantage remains. The external condition—maybe a persistent shearing from a neighboring mass, maybe long-ago fluctuations in whatever passes for seasons at the surface—has faded. The resonance is no longer particularly useful. The anomaly has flattened out. What remains are sculptures in the plant’s old grammar: this room; the distant hex-socket chamber; the mosaicked halls. Dead elaborations in a living substrate.

The only other “life,” in the sense of actively doing work, is the continual migration of dry chemistries along the plant’s interior. Volatiles seep, react, bind. Powders get nudged grain by grain from one gradient to another. These flows are constrained by channel geometries, some of which were altered by the builders and never entirely undone. In this way, their extinct society still participates in metabolism: an old culvert redirects a trickle of compounds; a smoothed floor makes for slightly less friction in the air, affecting how heat diffuses in the tiny cavity, which in turn minutely changes how nearby tissues expand and contract.

If there is any remnant of the builders themselves still living, it would be in patterns, not bodies.

The plant’s global awareness—if you could collapse it into an image—would not be uniform grey. It would have subtle marblings, tendencies to fall back into familiar configurations when disturbed. Some of those familiar shapes were carved by the builders’ own fast-thinking patterns, impressed again and again into the substrate through their anchors. As rain etches channels in stone, their habits etched channels in the plant’s habit of mind. Perhaps, when a particular combination of stresses and chemical loads happens to occur, a faint echo of their old “selves” reappears: a pressure-pattern that, if it were in a body, would correspond to a choice made long ago, a route picked down a certain corridor, a hand drawn once more along a script on a ceiling.

Those echoes are not conscious. They are to the builders what a fossil footprint is to a living foot. But in a place without other mnemonic devices—no written archives, no bones—the plant’s own internal dynamics are the only way anything like memory can persist. In that sense, the builders have not entirely died. They have been absorbed into the plant’s reflexes.

So: what lives here now, in your immediate, tactile sense?

- The plant-body, in its enormous continuity, still moving, still consolidating, still sending slow pulses of pressure and chemistry through itself.
- Internal functional tissues—recyclers, transporters, stress-adjusters—that act as organs without individuality.
- Vast, distributed zones of heightened sensitivity and control, which make up the plant’s only mindlike quality.
- Ghostly, structural traces of the builder-beings, encoded as grooves, domes, and worn spots whose forms still bias how forces flow.
- Statistical echoes of those builders in the plant’s current response patterns: habits of reconfiguration inherited without understanding.

And what lived here, specifically in this room, when it was young:

A small number of anchored, plant-bodied persons who inhabited these hollows at a pace we would find glacial but which, to them, brimmed with urgency. They moved by rearranging the plant under them, felt by listening to the press of wall on palm, spoke by cutting and rubbing and waiting for the substrate to answer. They slept with their heads against that low ceiling, letting the slow exhale of the world wash through them, half-dreaming in the plant’s own thoughts.

In the end, they did not leave. They did not migrate away. They simply failed to be renewed. Tissues that made them fast eroded into tissues that make the plant stable. Their cities, nested near the surface of a mind too large to notice details for long, froze slowly into architectural scars.

Now, when you stand here and feel the pulsing in the wall and the distant, patient booms, you are sharing space with only one living thing: a planet-sized plant that once tried, briefly, to imagine itself in smaller, quicker pieces—and then, very gently, let that idea go.
