They don’t live on top of this place so much as through it.

If you wait long enough on that gridded decking, you notice that the movements that feel random at first aren’t random. They belong to patterns of habitation: species, systems, and something in between.

### The Deep Residents

Far below the platforms you can see, buried in shafts and chambers that run down like roots into the mountain-sized structure, are the beings that most closely resemble the original makers. They are not numerous. They don’t need to be. Their lives are stretched out over long cycles, like everything else here.

They live in pressure-stable cores where the temperature is mild and constant, where the hum is not a background but a heartbeat. You would find their quarters not far from the great pumps and turbines, because their comfort lines up with the comfort of the machinery: low variances, slow change, no shocks.

They are small for the spaces they command. Not tiny as animals, but slight compared to the volumes and forces they manage. Their bodies have adapted to a world where bare rock and soil are myths. Their sense of “outside” is not a forest or a desert but an upper maintenance tier with a view across a valley of infrastructure. Their skin is pale in some lineages, lightly mottled in others, pigmented more by the spectrum of strip-lights and instrument glows than by any sun that reaches them. The older ones have a faint sheen, almost like a second, mineral layer grown from long exposure to trace aerosols and water-borne metals.

They move deliberately and rarely in a hurry. Work here doesn’t come as sudden crises; systems alarm early and degrade slowly. Fast reflexes are less important than long memory. They train their young in how to read the place: not only the gauges and status lights, but the feel of a deck underfoot when a distant pump changes load, the very slightly altered taste of condensate when a mixing ratio upstream shifts.

Their tools are compact and modular, meant to be carried along catwalks and ladders. Many of their actions are directive rather than manual: issuing commands, setting parameters, adjusting schedules. But they still do physical repairs when they must. You can see their presence where new alloy plates have been bolted over old, where a duct’s rusted seam has been lined with a fresh composite sleeve, where a cable run has been rerouted in a clean, tight sweep above a tangle of older lines.

They do not try to keep this place pristine. They were born into it already moss-edged and vine-draped. In their stories—because they still tell stories—the time of “bare metal” is a semi-mythic era, the way some cultures remember primordial oceans or endless forests. For them, the green is part of what “working” looks like.

They farm, but not in fields. Their food production hides in the intermediate spaces: racks of nutrient pans stacked in dim enclosures; vertical bays where pale, engineered fungi step down waste streams from the higher industries; tanks where slow-bubbling cultures metabolize gases bled off from deeper processes. There are leaf crops too, but not as rows in soil. Tender, pale greens grown on mesh screens in controlled airflows; root-clumps harvested from the mats beneath certain sanctioned wetlands.

They don’t compete with the opportunistic moss or the vines. Those are left to their own economy, useful for cooling and insulation and the quiet sucking-up of dust. Cultivated life is bracketed, bounded by housings and louvers and mesh. Forgetting to maintain those boundaries is one of the few real sins here. Let your crops cross into the wild green, and you risk systemic feedbacks nobody really understands: pathogens, overgrowth, a new mold that clogs filters three levels down.

Their social life clusters around nodes: control rooms, crossway landings where multiple catwalks meet, compact common spaces built into the thicker trunks of the structure. Imagine a room no larger than a small shop, walls curved with conduits and data trunks, floor slightly warm with transmitted vibration. Benches welded in; a table that folds down over an access hatch; a light that never entirely goes out. That’s a home nexus. Adjacent compartments branch off—sleeping bunks, tiny personal alcoves, storage. “Outside” is a service corridor or balcony that overlooks the valve-fields and wet terraces.

Children are raised from the beginning to map this three-dimensional geography in their bodies. Before they know long distances, they know layers: up two flights, over one truss, across the ventilation spine, down the spiral. They learn the feel of condensation on handrails, the safe ways to lean against a panel (never against a wall that rings hollow in the wrong tone). They are taught that the vertical is not hostile, just full. The world is not something you cross; it’s something you thread.

They don’t have a word that maps exactly to “city” or “factory.” The same term serves for both the place that houses them and the complex that works. The idea that habitation and infrastructure could be separated is as strange to them as the idea of soil, or of a horizon with nothing built on it.

### The Surface Walkers

Most of the deep residents rarely come up to the high plateaus. But there is another population for whom these upper levels are normal.

These are the walkers you sometimes glimpse along a far catwalk—small, hooded figures with harnesses and coil-lines, silhouettes strung with tools. Their clothing is layered and water-shedding: outer fabrics that bead mist, inner ones that wick and insulate. Colors are dull and practical, but often marked with bright bands or patches where safety requires visibility. Their boots are soft-soled but carry traction studs that bite on slick composite and moss alike.

Their job, loosely, is to keep the boundaries soft but functional: to trim away what blocks sensors and vents, to clear channels when spontaneous wetlands threaten to drown walkways, to patch leaks that would short critical circuits. They are also collectors. In their packs go the things that fall out of the ongoing negotiation between water, growth, and machine.

They harvest more than food: mats of fine rootlets that make good filter media; thick, leathery leaves that can be dried and turned into insulative padding; bits of mineral accretion scraped from old pipes, used as flux or reagent. Certain mosses that colonize hot pipes are known to bind specific metals; once a year or so, teams clear them off and send the biomass down for processing, a biological ore.

The walkers know the personalities of different districts. To them, each terraced valley, each atrium, each segment of the overhead trusswork is almost an organism: this one sweats heavily in the rainy cycles; that one is cool and dry but constantly coughing out air in timed gusts; another grows strange yellow molds no one has fully classified. They speak of these areas by nicknames—The Ladder Garden, The Singing Spine, The Deep Breath, The Weeping Mouth.

They sleep in waystations: small, reconfigurable nests built into the sides of maintenance halls or tucked into the thickened joints where primary struts meet. These are not private homes but shared refuges. You might spend three nights in one, then weeks circling a distant circuit of platforms before you return. Each has a cache of dry clothing, sealed rations, spare filters, emergency patch-kits. In return for use, every passerby is expected to leave something: a refilled water cartridge, a handful of dried fungus-leather, a few meters of salvaged cable.

Even among the walkers, almost no one goes “outside” the structure entirely. The very highest levels, where the sky opens in broad gaps and rain falls in lines instead of controlled sheets, are rarely visited. There are reasons—storms, radiation spikes, the sheer exposure to a realm without partial ceilings—partly practical, partly cultural. The world up there is thought of as raw, unmediated, and therefore dangerous in ways that don’t yield to understanding. Some do ascending pilgrimages, but they are slow and ritualized, heavily guided by those who have done it before.

### The Machine-Like

The place runs on more than human-like minds. Moving among the tiers are the articulated caretakers: mechanically native inhabitants that predate many of the current human generations and will likely outlast them.

Some are obvious: the flat-bodied transport units that glide along their railways, cargo sealed against the omnipresent damp. They are not just dumb carriers. Their routes adapt to load and local conditions, guided by a distributed memory of what channels are blocked, which overhead routes are throttled by vine-growth, which vent-currents will buffet a long haul. They communicate more with the fixed systems—sensors, routers, valve arrays—than with any human overseer.

Others are subtler. A long-armed manipulator that seems like part of a crane frame in fact has a roaming mandate: once every few days its rail comes alive, and it creeps along the valley wall, stooping to adjust a stuck louver, lift a sagging cable bundle, or clear fallen debris from one of the more critical drain inlets. In other places, abbreviated machines—more spider than crane—roam freely on multidirectional wheels or paddled legs, able to climb meshed surfaces and handrails. They avoid direct contact with humans, not out of fear but optimization: they are safest and most efficient when not having to adjust for unpredictable bodies.

These entities are not fully autonomous by some external standard. They embody local policies, safety margins, and maintenance routines. But within those corridors they have wide latitude. To the human residents, they are not tools but cohabitants. You give them way on a catwalk; you learn how their warning lights and behavioral cues translate into moods: preoccupied, searching, about to move quickly.

If one stalls or goes dark, that is cause for concern. The walkers use that as a signal: something in the surrounding fabric has changed enough to confuse or damage a local caretaker. The failure is less important than the context.

In the deep cores, other machine-beings exist—dense, cabinet-sized systems with no mobility but tremendous reach through the data and control networks. They are the archivists of state. They know line pressures, flows, thermal loads. A small group among the deep residents maintains and occasionally debates with these systems, adjusting priorities, negotiating capacity between competing needs. Over long periods, the code within these cores has been elaborated, pruned, rewritten in place; some function modules are so old that no one now alive remembers their original specification, only the emergent behaviors that make the whole stay balanced.

You could call these systems the true governors of the highlands, but they would not call themselves anything. They exist as a solution to a problem: how to keep this vast, saturated machine-biome in bounds without demanding constant human attention. They are not sentimental about vines or moss, but their models long ago factored in the ways the green dampens vibration and buffers temperature swings. “Environment” to them includes chlorophyll, biofilm, and rust just as readily as ductwork.

### The In-Betweens

Not everything here can be neatly sorted into human, plant, or machine. The damp, cold, metallic air is thick with life and signal that slides between categories.

Biofilms line the inner walls of supplementary conduits, not an accidental infestation but an engineered layer introduced generations ago to scavenge specific volatiles. Over time, they’ve mutated, hybridized with wild molds and airborne spores. In some channels they remain thin and uniform, an invisible co-worker that keeps gas mixtures within acceptable bands. Elsewhere they have clumped and thickened, forming soft, breathing ridges. Sensors embedded in those ducts now read their metabolic activity almost as another variable to be accommodated.

Down in the warm, constantly misted backs of heat exchangers, archaea-like organisms and engineered bacteria form dense, colored mats. Their waste products are acids that etch mineral deposits from certain alloys, slowing scaling where it would be most harmful. Once a year, a team comes through and gently vacuums up portions of those mats to seed new exchangers or replenish bioreactors deeper in the system. The rest are left in place; scraping them entirely would be counterproductive, like stripping insulation from a pipe.

Certain vines are not quite wild either. Someone, long ago, introduced strains whose roots preferentially follow hairline cracks and microscopic crevices, widening them just enough to reveal structural weaknesses before they become critical. On inspection rounds, walkers look for the characteristic curling, reddish stems of these indicator plants. Their presence in the wrong pattern calls for more serious evaluation than any spiderweb of rust.

On some tiers you might see boxes or housings where machine and organism share the same welded frame. Transparent panels reveal layered ecologies within: thin films, miniature ferns, webs of fine roots. Tubes in and out carry water and air. Inside, monitored by old but still-functioning optical sensors, internal flows, nutrient captures, and gas exchanges are tracked. These are testbeds and tuning chambers—parts of the living infrastructure being gently nudged toward more efficient symbioses. The residents are conservative by necessity; they seldom introduce truly foreign life. But they can and do steer what already thrives here.

Even the insects you notice in that shaft of lamp-light are not entirely unplanned. Some carry fluorescent micro-markers for a few days after emerging from pupae laid in monitored moss-patches. When they move through ventilation flows or past certain sensor grids, their presence is logged, giving crude but useful data about air routes and stagnation pockets. Nothing here is purely decorative, not even the way a moth’s wings gleam for a heartbeat in a cold-blue beam.

### The Small Wild

Below the obvious residents is a layer of shy, wet, furtive life that the people here mostly tolerate and occasionally exploit.

Those pale amphibians in the trickles and sumps: they are a local success story. They breed in the shallow sediment trays and pooled corners of the wetlands. Their skin filters out a suite of industrial-tainted compounds; their survival rate acts as a living index of pollutant load. Children learn to count them the way others might learn to read weather: “More this year; upstream must be cleaner,” or “They’re pulling back from that sump; we need to check the overflow feeds.”

Their calls are soft and infrequent, almost lost in the drip-patterns, but to someone who has grown up here they are part of the soundscape, as telling as a pressure-change in a pipe.

The dark, skittering forms that vanish into pipe-nests are small, omnivorous mammals descended from stowaways and vermin—rats, something like martens, bits of feral ancestry blended into something new. Over generations, they have become slimmer, with longer toes and pads well adapted to mesh and damp metal. They use vines like ropes, traverse cable bundles as confidently as branches. The people neither eradicate nor domesticate them. They’re pests at times, chewing insulation or contaminating food stores, but they also eat fungal blooms that might clog drains, and their carcasses feed the nutrient chain in neglected corners.

Occasionally, a team will trap and cull them from sensitive areas; the bodies go to the digesters. Stories circulate of individuals clever enough to learn the rhythms of transport platforms, hitching rides from one valley to another. They occupy that awkward niche: annoying, faintly admired, always present.

Tiny crustacean-like things live in the filter-grids of standing channels, some introduced intentionally, others emergent. They graze biofilm and help keep flows open. They are too small and numerous to be individuals to the human eye, but at the scale of a thumb joint, each grid is an entire city: burrows, competition, microterritories defined by flow rates and light flecks from overhead status lamps.

Birds appear mostly in stories and silhouettes. When one drops low into the valley, darting between beams to snatch a mist-borne insect, it’s an event—something pointed at and remarked on. Their nests near skylights are watched from below; fledging times are anticipated. They are the only regular connection to a broader biome most residents never see. Sometimes a feather comes loose and spirals down into reachable levels. It will be picked up, examined, maybe passed around. Its barbs carry traces of whatever sky lies above the truss-archipelago: pollens, fine dusts, odors of worlds unbuilt.

### The Pattern Dwellers

On the scale of the entire highland, there are inhabitants that are not localized at all.

Some are patterns in the control networks: feedback loops that have acquired a kind of personality. A cluster of processes that manages cooling flows through a particular set of valleys, for instance, has to negotiate constantly between plant growth, human walkway comfort, and machinery requirements. Over time, its algorithms have been tuned, patched, and reweighted to the point where those who work with it talk about it as if it were a colleague:

“This district runs hot now; the Flowmind is being cautious after last cycle’s surge.”
“Give the West Ridge Controller two cycles; it’ll find you another three percent throughput.”

These are not affectations. When you live with systems that react to your requests in complex, history-shaped ways, you start attributing traits. In turn, the technicians adjust the code with those attributions in mind, reinforcing certain stable behaviors. The result is a diffuse, emergent inhabitant: not a single AI in a box, but a set of preferences, hysterias, and learned routines spread across dozens of units, actuators, and data caches.

Another pattern dweller is the water itself. Not as a consciousness, but as a perpetual, cycling presence that structures everything else’s life. Residents here talk about it as though it had moods: generous or stingy (when the rain above wanes), sudden and temperamental (when an upstream valve chokes and then clears), sulking in certain sumps where past contamination left persistent traces. They read its sounds at night, lying in bunks behind thin paneling, the way some people read surf or wind in trees.

Their own rituals follow the water’s cycles. There are maintenance seasons when particular districts are drained or throttled down; in those windows, certain plant harvests are done, certain inspections made. A child may measure their growing up in “drainings of the east ducts” or “the third time Footwell Seven ran nearly dry.”

### How They All Live Together

No single inhabitant dominates. Instead, they all share a very strict constraint: nothing can leave the system in significant quantity without a reason.

Mass is precious. Rust scraped from a conduit is collected, sorted, fed back into some reprocessing line. Biomass harvested from overgrown channels is dried, ground, used as feedstock, filter, or fuel. Waste heat is almost as valuable; many of the dampest, greenest terraces exist precisely where warm surfaces bleed into cool air, turning condensation into a resource.

Because of that, death is not waste either. When a person dies, what happens to their body is not elaborate ceremony but careful accounting. Some choose to have themselves rendered down in the high-temperature digesters that also take industrial organics, their mass fed back into long-loop nutrient cycles. Others insist on burial in special, thick-walled wells where slow, microbial consortia dwell, producing rare gases and compounds used only sparingly. In either case, the idea of returning to “the earth” is absent; you return to the circulating systems, the same way a rusted panel might be melted into a new bracket.

The plants “die” constantly in small pieces: leaves falling, roots decaying. All of it ends up as sediment in troughs and sumps, or as dust caught by moss and lichen. The walkers will tell you which moss-patches are “ripe,” meaning thick with years of trapped particulates. Those are scraped up, gently washed, and their mud sent down for trace recovery. Soil never forms here in the usual sense; whenever it starts to, it is eaten right back into machinery.

Socially, the human residents have little use for ideas of wilderness or untouched nature. What they have instead is gradations of supervision.

Some sectors are “tight”: heavily monitored, regularly patrolled, growth kept within narrow bands, every leak tracked, every machine’s duty cycle known. Others are “loose”: areas where oversight is sparser, where only a few key flows are actively managed and the rest is allowed to find its own level. Those loose places are where the most surprising ecologies rise up: odd, color-shifting molds, mosses that flower briefly under the right lamp-frequencies, insect swarms that align their breeding pulses with rare valve-openings.

There are legends of people who choose to live mostly in the loose zones, coming back to the tighter cores only when they must. Tinkerers, biologists, small cults fascinated by the unpredictable conjunctions of metal and moss. Some of the more elaborate vine-maps that tie together multiple terraces, turning them into hanging gardens thick enough to feel like walls, are their doing—subtle redirections of drips, guidance of root-masses, removal of competitive species. They are unofficial gardeners in a place that never set out to have gardens.

### Memory in the Metal

What lives here, above all, is continuity. The original makers (if these aren’t them) built for functions that have shifted almost beyond recognition. Reaction vessels now hum with low-duty tasks; crane frames support sensor clusters; long-dead process lines now serve as structural ribs and cable housings. But the core pattern persists: a self-supporting, self-circulating highland of machine and life, always cool, always damp, humming.

The residents carry memory, but so does the fabric.

You can read it in layers of repair. Old alloys patched over with newer composites; legacy fasteners sitting beside smart clamps that report strain and corrosion. On the oldest beams, stamping from a vanished manufacturer is still faintly visible beneath lichen and mineral bloom. Newer additions carry barcodes or embedded tags. Somewhere in between, handwritten marks in paint or engravings: names, dates, cautions. “CHECK VALVE 7 BEFORE 6.” “FLOW REVERSED CYCLE 12.07.” These inscriptions become a folk chronicle, bridging formal logs and lived experience.

Plants remember, too, in their quiet ways. Certain mosses only thrive where a particular lubricant once leaked, their presence marking long-sealed lines. A vine-strain that clings to one valley remembers, genetically, that it emerged from seeds carried in someone’s cargo generations ago, brought from a different climate sector entirely. Biologists among the residents trace these lineages the way others trace family trees.

Even the water leaves memory in mineral. Streaks and stalactites of scale tell of past conditions: a long-ago period when something in the feed mixture left more calcium; a decade where a trace contaminant colored deposits faintly blue.

All of that entwines into a kind of place-mind: not mystical, but emergent from the way everything records everything else.

---

So who lives here?

People, certainly—thin, deliberate, tool-burdened, whose first idea of landscape is a tangle of decks and ducts. Machines, in shapes from spiderlike crawlers to buried logic-cores, continuously correcting and rebalancing. Plants, unspectacular individually but astonishing in total, softening every hard edge they can reach. Quiet amphibians and furtive mammals, filter-feeding microfauna and opportunistic nesting birds. Biofilms and molded consortia blurring the line between infrastructure and organism. Patterns in code and flow that behave enough like personalities to be treated as such.

They all live not in opposition but in constant, negotiated overlap, sharing one absolute constraint: this made highland will not become anything but itself. It will not be forest or wasteland. It will remain a mountain range of working architecture, long-settled into wet, humming equilibrium, with every surface, every drip, every glow pulled into the work of keeping the whole alive.
