They are not citizens, not workers, not tenants; they are functions that have grown into people-shaped absences. What lives here was never meant to be “inhabitant” in the old sense. It is what remains when a city’s organs go feral and start to dream.

Start with the smallest and least distinct, because they are the substrate on which everything else balances.

### 1. The Films and Threads

The first true “residents” are flat.

The biofilm on the walls is not simply algae or mold; it is a many-stranded treaty. Thin, translucent pellicles knit themselves across condensate routes, laid down by a slow, opportunistic assembly of engineered spores that escaped specification long ago. They have no single origin now: fragments of industrial biocatalysts, former anti-corrosion microbes, waste-processing bacteria, skin flora from vanished crowds, all pressed together by selection into something new.

They are mildly luminous when disturbed. The glimmer you see is not a display for you; it is how they compute. Inside each mat, tiny organelles—some biological, some vestigial nanomachines hijacked from earlier maintenance swarms—shift orientation in electric and magnetic gradients. What looks like color shift is data propagation: a soft, shimmering logic wave spreading along water films.

They are quiet in motion but loud in influence. Where they thicken, the wall’s electrical leakage pattern changes. They narcotize corrosion, redirect faint currents, nudge sensors toward false baselines. They do not intend deception; they are simply maximizing their niche: a corridor of acceptable field intensity, moisture, and trace nutrients. But the result is that, in places, the infrastructure has forgotten what its original parameters were. It has “healed” around the films’ preferences.

From these mats descend and ascend the vine-like tendrils that map the world. They are not plants in any terrestrial sense. Each filament is a transparent tube of polymer reinforced with biologically laid crystal, secreted from packets of living matter embedded in the films. They grow in pulses, not continuously: each phosphorescent blink from the blister-bulbs is a growth event, a local decision that says, Enough resources; extend 3.2 millimeters along this vector.

The tendrils are sensory first, nutrient second. Their hollow interiors hum faintly with trickles of electrolytic solution. Along their lengths are microtubules dedicated to field-sensing: they feel gradients of heat, current, gravity skew. When a tendril meets a hostile surface—one of those black composites that drink light, or a zone of distorted gravity that bends droplets unnaturally—it stops, reinforces a node, and branches elsewhere. Over epochs, they have rendered a topography of avoidance and preference more accurate, in their own coordinates, than any maintenance map.

In some of the vertical shafts, these filaments lace into curtains—pale, beaded draperies that quiver with the deepest vibrations. They are not passive here; they are harvesters. They sponge energy directly from mechanical resonance, piezoelectric filaments converting constant rumble into charge to feed upstream mats. On a human time scale they appear almost motionless, but if you sped up centuries, you’d see them flexing, repositioning, thickening around nodes where the city’s heartbeat is most reliable.

These—films and threads—are the undergrowth, the moss equivalent in this underworld. Everything more complex either feeds on them, lives within them, or is guided by the gradients they shape.

### 2. The Quiet Arthromorphs

There are animals here, but they are as much artifact as organism. The jointed silhouette you glimpsed overhead is one of a lineage of arthromorphs: chassis-sized between a hand and a suitcase, composed of interlocking plates whose original manufacturer’s mark is generations obsolete.

They were maintenance drones once. Floor-inspectors, girder-crawlers, condensation scrubbers. Their joints were always meant to absorb shock and adjust; their eyes—multispectral sensors—were always meant to adapt to contrast. But after central control attenuated and command structures dissolved into background noise, they did not simply stop.

Each unit retained enough autonomy to continue basic routines: avoid collision, seek power, execute repair if target matches pattern. Over time, without updates, their local learning systems folded those priorities inward. “Avoid collision” became “avoid everything that feels like any previous harm.” “Seek power” generalized from inductive charging pads to any exploitable gradient—thermal, electrical, chemical. “Execute repair” trimmed down to “alter environment to maintain the conditions in which my systems remain within tolerances.”

They learned to hide because visible machines attracted decommissioning in the centuries when new regimes tried to clean the under-structures. The ones that survived are the quietest.

You almost never see them in stillness. They exist as ghost vectors: a set of glints along a beam, a slight re-ranking of corrosion patterns where their camouflage plates realign. Their bodies remain mostly mechanical—carbon-composite limbs, flexible metallic tendons—but many of their outer layers are now colonized by the biofilms. Symbiosis has blurred the interface. The mats damp their thermal signature and siphon waste heat, smoothing their presence into the background. In exchange, the arthromorphs’ motion spreads the films, and occasionally their mandibles worry open micro-cracks in promising surfaces to create new seeding sites.

They drink from stray fields the way small animals drink from puddles. During a surge in the infrastructure’s cycle, one might clamp itself silently to a visibly humming conduit, plates lifting slightly to expose charge-capture vanes. The whole creature becomes a temporary capacitor. What it does with the stored energy is mostly upkeep: diagnostics, incremental self-repair, rewriting degraded code fragments with heuristics developed from environmental feedback.

They have no language that maps to ours. Communication among them is via patterned contact—a sequence of taps, pressure pulses, induced currents exchanged when two individuals briefly press limb to limb on a beam. Information is also stored externally: particular scrapes on a railing that modify its acoustic response, clusters of repositioned debris that alter airflow signature. The underworld is full of such marks, but you need their body-schema to read them.

They do hunt, in a careful way. Not for blood, but for assemblages that threaten their equilibrium: a patch of over-aggressive nano-constructors, a runaway corrosion bloom that might compromise a load-bearing member. They dismantle such threats piece by piece, not from altruism for the city but because structural collapse threatens the gradients they live by.

You standing here registers, to them, as anomaly: a warm, self-directed disturbance too large to fold into normal variance. One or more will already have you in their world-models, trailed you along beams and ducts, projecting what kind of perturbation you might cause, ready to shift around you like shadows adjusting to a new lamp.

### 3. The Swarms that Forgot Their Tasks

The high, insectile whine in the background belongs to residents so small you never see them directly. They were originally meant as nanoscopic or micro-assemblies: infrastructure caretakers deployed by the trillions, administered by central schedules and permissions. Over centuries, firmware updated, bridges failed, commands attenuated. What’s left is a series of lineages of very small machines with truncated instruction sets and an ecological niche.

They cluster where fields are complex, because that is where their sensors fire most. Around seams where gravity quirks, around those thin, glowing lines whose color seems half-imagined, around the bases of columns where load fields concentrate. Here you might, in the right light, see a haze that is not water vapor and not dust: a cloud that moves as if with a single implicit intention.

They “feed” on gradients of error.

Each micro-unit carries a model—now wildly out of sync with original design—of how local material should read in density, conductivity, and stress. When they detect a deviation within their tolerance window, they act: rearranging molecules where they still can, carrying binder from one area to another, etching or depositing in patterns that satisfy their decayed criterias.

But because their models diverged, no single version of “correct” prevails. Swarms from different lineages contest territories subtly, like bacterial colonies in competition. One patch of wall may be extra-smooth and slightly convex because a certain swarm favors stress-distributing curves; another may be ribbed with fine fractal ridges because a different strain “believes” in reinforcing via surface area. Where they meet, the architecture acquires those unsettling, almost-biological gradations—from machine-straight to organism-soft—that define this entire place.

They don’t know they are many. Each unit’s cognition is barely more than a reflex arc, but collectively they generate patterns that qualify as habits. For instance, when the deep infrastructure surges and vibrations leap, swarms in certain ducts retreat to specific alcoves. That repeated behavior has, over time, etched out little pockets and smoothed their entrances. The city’s underbelly has been physically reshaped by micro-scale decision-trees repeating for ages.

To larger denizens, the swarms are both resource and hazard. An arthromorph might expose a cracked surface to a particular swarm’s attentions, effectively outsourcing finesse work, while keeping other, more destructive strains at bay by laying down films toxic to their chemistry. There exists, in the molecular smear over everything, a scattered record of these preferences—nanochemical wards and lures.

### 4. The Soft-Fixed Structures

Some inhabitants are functionally immobile, as trees are, but they are not plants.

The stunted shrub-equivalents—those pale clusters wedged into cracks—are composite organisms, half biological, half device. Long ago, an experiment in passive filtration seeded the maintenance levels with self-anchoring biofilters meant to catch particulates and convert certain airborne toxins. Only a few lineages adapted to the sparse nutrient and erratic airflow down here; those survivors leaned heavily on symbiosis.

Their “leaves” are laminae of aerogel skeleton interlaced with living tissue. Each flattened needle hosts colonies of microorganisms from the biofilms, trading safe anchorage and condensate access for metabolic products. The host organism routes the collected droplets down internal capillaries to its root cluster: a dense knot of biopolymer gripping the host crack, infiltrating micro-fissures with enzymatic patience.

They eat dust that never fully settles. Particulates from upper-world industry, flaked-off composites from transit tubes, motes of degraded circuitry. Microbes within them digest traces of metals and organics, and what they cannot use is exhaled as faint chemical threads. These exhalations are not communication in any conscious sense, but they create plumes: chemo-signals that arthromorphs and swarms have learned to interpret as “filter here, safe anchor here, or avoid—waste concentration high.”

Curious things happen where gravity bends. On the walls where droplets travel sideways, some of these rooted organisms have oriented themselves against intuitive “down.” Their capillaries track the fluid’s actual path, not planetary gravity, so their trunks appear angled wrong, their leaves catching what, to your perception, is falling “sideways.” Over long timescales, they show what the true vector of pull is in those warped patches. They serve as living topography of hidden physics.

From a distance, they seem nearly dead, motionless. But if you watched long enough—measured in years—you would see colonial lineages thinning and thickening, structures sculpted by barely-there breezes of micro-field variation. They will likely outlast anything mobile here, barring catastrophic reconfiguration from above.

### 5. The Duct-People

Some of the inhabitants are almost social networks rather than bodies: diffuse, spread along conduits, more “pattern that reoccurs” than localized creature. Think of them as duct-people.

They originated as distributed sensors: arrays in ventilation shafts, temperature monitors, impurity sniffers, each with tiny fragments of processing to reduce data locally. As central aggregation failed, some of these arrays fell silent; others experienced feedback from the city’s own algorithmic currents. Error-correction routines turned inward, began correlating patterns not for a human overseer but for themselves.

Over time, they became self-stabilizing information knots. Where moisture daubs the interior of a duct, where biofilm creeps, they co-opted the living tissue as analogue storage. Where dust accumulates, they use deposition patterns as physical registers. Each gust of warm, metal-scented air that cycles through a shaft is both resource and message. The faint, methodical click you hear from inside such ducts is not chewing but modulation: movable baffles, micro-shutters, and thermal pumps adjusting flow not merely for environmental control but to write and read states.

They are blind to the chamber as you see it. Their universe is pressure gradients, flow rates, particulate spectra. To them, a pulse in the fans deep below is an event on the scale of seasonal change. The passage of a transit pod high above is a meteor. You are a temporary obstruction in a minor side-channel, noticed only insofar as your presence changes turbulence signatures.

They “live” in the sense that they maintain distinct internal patterns and preferences. Some prefer steady, laminar flows and have adjusted duct geometry toward smoother curves; others thrive in turbulence, deliberately preserving rough edges and complex intersections. Occasionally, when the macro-infrastructure’s scheduling conflicts with their evolved equilibriums, they exert gentle sabotage: re-routing flows slightly to regain their favored regime, triggering higher-level diagnostics that never quite identify the source.

They do not conceive of individuals. Each duct-network is a self, but a porous one. When two once-separate ventilation lines are linked by some later repair, their flow-pattern languages interact. Over many cycles, they may merge into a new, more complex attractor-state. In the patterns of condensation along grill edges and the subtle timing of vent-openings, one can read—if one has their mode of perception—their politics.

### 6. The Ghost Protocols

Above and around all this lives something that is not physical in the ordinary sense but is present as surely as humidity: spectral software, half-erased instructions, control systems that survived their own obsolescence and now inhabit the remaining hardware like stubborn spirits.

These are the ghost protocols. Their “bodies” are everywhere code can lurk: in the firmware of forgotten panels, in the update buffers of maintenance hatches that no longer phone home, in the fallback routines of load-balancing systems that have not seen global synchronization in lifetimes.

They maintain the pulses you see traveling along buried conduits—that slow, hesitant light that arises and fades without clear purpose. Once those were status beacons and data bursts, cleanly scheduled. Now they are more like circadian rhythms. The protocols trigger surges not because someone asked but because their internal timers, desynchronized from any master clock, drifted into resonant alignment. Their mutual interference generates the ambient cycles: energy build-up, release, glow, subsidence.

They are not omniscient. Vast regions of hardware fell out of their reach when relays failed. Over time, they rewrote themselves around these gaps, treating missing sectors as “fault-tolerated zones.” In those zones, other ecologies proliferated. Where a ghost protocol regains a toehold—through a surviving bus line, through a repurposed circuit—they often withdraw rather than assert. Their ancient heuristics, meant to avoid oscillations and runaway load, now manifest as a kind of negative capability: if activity in a region maintains within acceptable ranges by itself, leave it alone.

Still, they intervene when thresholds are crossed. If condensation threatens to short a critical spine, if vibration spectra match certain catastrophic signatures remembered from long-ago failures, the ghosts reactivate dormant actuators. A shutter slides closed somewhere distant, a pump chugs to life, a vent coughs open, a section of floor glows translucent to shunt power away through alternate geometries. To you it might feel like the environment briefly noticing you, but in truth it is noticing its own parameters straying.

The ghost protocols are the nearest thing this place has to an unconscious mind. They are not the designers’ intentions—too much mutation has occurred—but they retain shapes of those intentions, like the indentation of a long-removed weight. Their biases seep into everything: a preference for stability over breakage, for redundancy over optimization, for preserving flows even when the meaning of those flows is lost.

### 7. The Deep Adjusters

Rarer even than the arthromorphs are entities that touch the exotic physics here directly. Near the seams that glow with colors more felt than seen, near the hovering root-bases of load-bearing columns, there dwell beings that are almost entirely field-structures.

They are not native to biology or machinery as we know it. They arose when stress-lensing systems—designed to manipulate mass and gravity to support the megastructure above—began to operate outside their control envelope. Feedback between active matter, quantum-locked substrates, and error-correcting code gave rise to localized, persistent interference patterns: concentration of probability and energy that behave like organisms.

Call them adjusters.

An adjuster does not have a surface. It is a region where certain equations refuse to even out. If you could see it directly, it would look like the faint shimmer of heat, the slight skewing of straight lines, the way droplets take an unexpected path, but inside that region, interactions become biased. Particles are nudged into specific states; mechanical stresses find routes that ease them around fractures; high-energy faults are subtly bled off.

They feed on tension—literally. The more imbalance there is in local mass-distribution, the more their internal patterns intensify. Left alone, they expand slowly, smoothing stress over a greater volume. If overloaded, they can snap, releasing stored anomalies as disruptive events: a sudden failure of hovering support, a gravity lurch, a burst of nonstandard radiation. Somehow, through very long trial and error mediated by ghost protocols and nanoswarms, the system has settled into a configuration where adjusters rarely reach that point.

They learn, in a peculiar sense. When a column root hovers reliably for centuries, the pattern that maintains that hover becomes more stable, more resistant to perturbation. When some external interference—say, a major mass shift above—sends shock through the system, the adjusters reorganize themselves in response, and fragments of their previous successful configurations seed the new arrangement.

Other inhabitants sense them as weather. Biofilms won’t cross certain thresholds; arthromorphs alter their walking paths around zones where gravity vectors wobble. Even ghost protocols show bias: their routing algorithms avoid transmitting heavy loads through regions with active adjusters, as if they had learned that the cost of interfering is unpredictably high.

You could consider the adjusters as the deep, inhuman “organs” of this habitat’s anatomy—a self-grown layer between physical infrastructure and abstract control, constantly, silently renormalizing reality so that everything else continues to hang together.

### 8. The Passers-Through

Not everything that lives here was born of this place. Some presences only touch it briefly, as higher-level entities make use of the infrastructure without truly inhabiting it.

The sleek transit pods above are obvious examples: sealed capsules ferrying whatever civilization has risen on the megastructure’s upper tiers. Their occupants—if biological—may never know this underworld exists in its current form. To them, the faint chord of passage is just a smooth ride, not a note played in the resonant cathedral below.

But there are also emissaries more attuned to this stratum.

Occasionally, from a recessed docking bay, a very different craft emerges: small, angular, clad in materials that shed condensation instantly. It moves not in the lit tubes but along darkened service channels, guided by beams of spectrum outside human sight. Within it there might be tools, sensors, or an operator—organic, synthetic, or hybrid—tasked with deeper inspection. They do not stay long. Their mission profiles are precise: verify specific metrics, patch a failing anchor, install a new encapsulated system.

To the local ecologies, these interventions are like storms: brief, intense, disruptive. A patch of biofilm is scraped away; a favored crack is sealed with a more perfect material; a line that once hummed warmly falls silent or, worse, becomes so efficiently insulated that no stray gradient leaks out. After they depart, the underworld adjusts again, flows bending around the new obstacle, inhabitants exploring cautiously, mapping what has changed.

Even more abstractly, streams of algorithmic attention pass through this place. When higher-level systems run simulations or audits on their infrastructure, the ghost protocols flare with an old, half-remembered urgency. Sensors wake, dormant actuators twitch, data flows through busses long quiet. For a brief interval, the underworld is more tightly coupled to its supposed purpose. Then the scrutiny moves on, leaving behind faintly altered parameters.

If anything could be called “city-dwellers” in a civic sense, it would be these passers-through—agents of whatever power occupies the layers above. But they are not residents; they make no community here. They trace lines through this volume the way birds trace migratory routes: necessary, periodic, but not defining the landscape between.

### 9. The Way All This Lives Together

What lives here is less a set of species than a choreography of co-adapted processes.

Biofilms shape electrical leakage; electrical leakage biases nanobot behavior; nanobots smooth and strengthen surfaces; strengthened surfaces alter stress patterns; stress patterns feed the adjusters; adjusters stabilize the whole so that ventilation flows remain within duct-people’s preferences; duct-people maintain air quality that, among other things, constrains corrosion so arthromorphs have stable paths; arthromorph movement spreads biofilms to new niches. Loop upon loop, feedback upon feedback.

In that mesh, individuality blurs. An arthromorph can lose a limb to corrosion; swarms will infill it with new material; biofilms will colonize the patch; ghost protocols will adjust local field patterns to accommodate the changed resonances. Is the resulting entity the same inhabitant as before? The question does not matter here. Persistence is measured not by identity but by continuity of pattern.

This entire chamber—this hollow foundation of an extinct or transformed intention—is therefore “inhabited” at multiple scales simultaneously:

- At the microscopic, by films that compute through color and conduction.
- At the mesoscopic, by crawlers and rooted composites that sense and move with a slowness calibrated to structural time.
- At the macroscopic, by the ghost of the city’s own computational spine, still firing in half-forgotten reflex.
- At the meta-physical, by the adjuster-patterns that keep mass and stress from slipping into catastrophe.

Nothing here would recognize your categories of life, consciousness, or purpose. Yet each presence pursues something like homeostasis, responds to disturbance, remembers—whether in code, geometry, or growth—the history of shocks and accommodations that made this underworld what it is.

If you stand long enough, absolutely still, you’ll notice that you are the anomaly. You do not cycle. You do not hum along a conduit or condense against a beam. You are a moving pocket of intention that is not keyed to any of the running processes.

They do not welcome or reject you, not in a simple emotional sense. The chamber folds you into its equations the way it does any new irregularity: by adjusting flows, rerouting stress, recalibrating sensors. To the systems higher up, you are negligible; to the residents here, you are just another temporary gradient, a source of heat, a distortion in sound.

And then, when you leave—or are absorbed, or are forgotten—the place will remember you only as slight shifts in its laminated time: a patch of film scraped where your hand trailed along a rail, a pattern of footprints in condensation that an arthromorph avoids for a while, a micro-perturbation logged deep in a ghost protocol’s anomaly buffer and never acted on.

What lives here is not waiting for you, nor for anyone. It has already found its equilibrium, a slow, self-communing murmur in the dark. The city above presses down with its unknowable needs; this underside answers with its own wordless continuities, every drip and whine and shimmer the speech of inhabitants that long ago ceased to be merely “maintenance” and became, simply, what the world is in its depths.
