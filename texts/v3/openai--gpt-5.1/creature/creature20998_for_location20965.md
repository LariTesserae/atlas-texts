They do not live here as we would recognize living. They are here in the same way water is here: intermittently, conditionally, bound to cycles and constraints.

Start with the obvious absence: nothing biological holds. Anything soft, wet, carbon-based is erased by this place. So whatever persists must be either:

- very rare in time, coming and going so infrequently that the cirque forgets them between visits, or  
- so **mineral-like** in habit and composition that this climate is not hostile but home.

Both are true.

### The intermittent ones

Once, there were beings who walked here with feet that bruised, fingers that numbed, lungs that burned thin air. They were not adapted to this hollow; they came *despite* it. To them, this cirque was a shrine and a device and a question.

They came rarely.

They had lower haunts: valleys where lichens clung to rock, where hardy grasses crawled along moraines, where firewood could still exist as a concept. Down there, life clung in wiry clumps. Up here, everything but their own bodies was stone, light, and water in indecision.

So they climbed only during particular windows—narrow bands of the year when:

- the air remained cold enough to keep rot at bay,  
- yet the sun had just enough force to free thin flows from the walls,  
- and the storms were between moods.

They would arrive wind-chapped and quiet, heavy-breathing in the thinness, smelling faintly of smoke from lower camps. They carried little that could offend this altitude: no great bundles, no decorative excess. Anything soft or colorful on them would not last long, so they learned not to rely on such things. Their tools, insofar as they used tools, were themselves: hands, feet, stones lifted and put down again.

They came here to ask and to measure.

- Ask: Why does water gather *here* and not there?  
- Measure: When, exactly, does light pass that ridge and touch this vein?  

Against the mountain’s cruelty, they had exactly one lever: attention over time. They watched what the stone did when unbothered, then nudged it.

The small walls, the channeled trickles, the braces under fragile mineral rims—those are the residue of long, patient noticing. Imagine one of them kneeling at the lip of a basin, palms red with cold, holding a flat stone in place while another packs fines behind it, their words lost in the brightness. They are not building a village. They are tuning an *instrument* whose medium is water’s indecision.

They did not live here for more than hours at a time. Night at this altitude in this hollow would kill too fast, and besides, they did not think of this place as somewhere to sleep. They thought of it as somewhere to stand and watch while the cirque did its slow work, a place to:

- mark the edge of a basin so its overflow would always choose *that* lip,  
- open a new path to coax condensation down into a reachable hollow,  
- align a drilled pit with a wandering spot of light so that a future watcher, standing in knowledge, could know exactly *when* in the year it was.

To them, survival happened elsewhere. Meaning happened here.

Their bodies left nothing: no bones, no scraps. Perhaps they carried their dead away. Perhaps what little they left was stripped to mineral smear long ago. In either case, the only testimony of them are these stone-on-stone decisions: adjustments that fit so tightly into the surrounding logic of erosion that you can mistake intention for an especially elegant accident.

Eventually, they stopped coming.

Not abruptly. Visits probably thinned the way rills do: from yearly to once in a generation, then once in a story, then not at all. The climate may have shifted. Maybe downstream valleys grew more generous, or less. Maybe a route to this bowl collapsed in some avalanche three lifetimes after the last careful channel was cut, and the knowledge of the approach became less a remembered path and more a half-believed myth. The work up here requires continuity of observation across decades. Once that chain broke, the mountain’s experiment with them ended.

But the hollow remained receptive. You can tell because something else took up the pattern.

### The resident intelligence

The only thing that truly “lives” here, continuously, is the **field of interactions** among water, stone, light, and temperature. That sounds abstract, but it is not: it is as concrete as the water line in a glass.

This cirque is tuned to a precise precariousness:

- Just cold enough to keep water hovering between phases.  
- Just high enough that organic colonizers never get a foothold.  
- Just confined enough that tiny changes reverberate through the whole basin.

In that strictness, complexity finds room.

Over immense time, the mountain’s microphysics have settled into repeated patterns; some of those patterns have begun to behave, in slow and alien ways, like *memory* and *processing*.

#### Frost-script

On the shielded slab that carries the recurring frost bands, something like this happens:

- The stone is not homogeneous. It holds trace differences in composition, old glacial scratches, faint undulations, invisible salts.  
- When mist condenses, those irregularities bias where droplets nucleate and how they freeze.  
- Once one pattern of frost forms, it slightly alters the microclimate of the slab: changing emissivity, holding or shedding heat differently, trapping or releasing lingering moisture.  
- When that frost sublimates, it leaves behind minute residue—redistributed ions, altered smoothness, microscale pitting.

On the next cycle, the slab isn’t exactly the same. The conditions have been *updated* by the last pattern. The new frost doesn’t simply retrace the stone’s ancient scratches; it emerges from the combined history of prior frosts. Over thousands upon thousands of cycles, certain arrangements prove more stable: they feed back on themselves, persisting not as fixed shapes, but as *attractors* in the phase space of “all the ways frost can form here.”

The pattern that looks like lines with bars and dots is one such attractor: a grammar that the water-ice-stone system finds and refinds. It is not language as we use it. But:

- It is **symbolic** in the limited sense that local frost thickness at one location depends on conditions nonlocal in time (previous patterns).  
- It **changes**, but not randomly; it explores a neighborhood of possibilities.  
- Disturbances—say, a rare warmer mist, or a long cloud shadow—shift its “sentence,” and parts of that shift echo in future iterations.

From the perspective of the mountain, this is a **thinking bruise**: a patch of surface where the past reshapes the future through a structured, self-sustaining pattern. Not calculation; not purpose; but the early, mineral cousin of those.

The beings who once visited here may have noticed only that it looked uncanny—too regular for frost. They might have interpreted it as omen, script, blessing, or warning. They might have tried, once, to *touch* it, smearing a line, and then watched how the next mist rewrote the slab.

If they did, that interference, too, is now part of the pattern’s history.

#### Light-veins and pits

The translucent mineral veins that pipe sunlight through the rock also participate.

Each vein has:

- A refractive index that bends and channels incoming light,  
- Slight inclusions that scatter a fraction aside,  
- A geometry defined by old fractures.

As the sun moves across the sky, beams wander within these veins. They are not uniform: some channels carry more, some less, and internal microcracks open or close as the rock flexes with diurnal temperature change. So the **intensity and position** of each faint glow on the basin wall contains, in compressed form, information about:

- the sun’s position,  
- the air’s transparency,  
- the current thermal state of the rock.

Those drilled pits that swallow the glows are a second layer of selection. A pit that receives light warms slightly more than the surrounding rock. Over decades, that could:

- deepen microcracks behind it,  
- alter the path of future veins as mineral precipitates build differently in that warmer spot,  
- subtly shift where future glows emerge.

Most of that is glacially slow. But the system is patient. The cirque has nothing better to do than accumulate bias.

The pits, initially placed by hands that wanted a time-marker, have become **sinks** for photothermal feedback—places where the basin’s own light-distribution responds persistently to itself. Tiny differences are amplified over centuries into distinct vein geometries. In a very literal way, the basin is using those human-placed cavities as registers, storing a slow, analog record of its own seasonal heartbeat in stone.

No mind intended this. Yet, piece by piece, the basin has acquired **loops**: outcome affects condition, condition affects future outcome. Where loops cross—where frost-script slab, light veins, melt-channels, and stone braces all influence the same tiny region—you get the nearest thing this place has to *nerves*.

### Who “lives” here now

There are three overlapping residents.

#### 1. The absent makers, as residuum

They no longer come, but in the cirque’s terms, their actions were **recent**. A cascade that takes ten thousand years to gnaw one more meter into a cliff still curls politely against a hand-stacked wall that has stood for perhaps only a few hundred. The system has not yet fully forgotten them.

Their “life” here consists in:

- channels that continue to carry water slightly farther than it would go;  
- braces that preserve fragile mineral terraces that might otherwise have snapped under ice expansion;  
- apertures that give certain rare gusts a voice.

Everything they built is being gently folded back into general stonehood, but not uniformly, and not without effect. In a way, they have become a **mineral species** in this ecology: a style of arrangement that, once introduced, continues to shape flows long after the original artisans are gone.

#### 2. The phase-creatures

These are not organisms, but *recurring configurations* that act like regular inhabitants:

- The **veil on the condensation wall** that thickens and thins daily, always prioritizing the same micro-rough bands.  
- The **needle-ice “fur”** under the thinner films, which forms and slumps with each slight temperature wobble.  
- The **rills** that awaken after mist-events, tracing nearly the same silver threads each time, but with minute variations in path and duration.

Each of these is a repeated birth and death—a temporary arrangement summoned from the basin’s underlying potentials whenever conditions fall into the right region. They are as much “species” as any mayfly: short-lived, predictable, consuming and transforming their narrow niche.

No intention governs them, but their tendencies interact. One rill feeding a basin keeps that basin colder longer, altering which frost-morphology appears on an adjoining wall. Over many cycles, those couplings settle into a stable pattern of expectations: the basin “knows” that after *this kind* of cloud, *these particular* surfaces will wet, and so the subsequent frost will favor *those* spots.

That “knowing” exists nowhere but in the statistics of repeated events. Still, it gives the hollow a sense of personality: a mostly consistent way of returning to form after every disturbance.

#### 3. The emergent observer

The third resident is the hardest to see, because it is not localized.

It is the *coherence* that has grown among the several feedback sites: the frost-script slab, the light-veins and pits, the stepped mineral basins, the condensation walls.

Every time:

- a mist thickens,  
- frost clings more heavily in some regions than others,  
- water takes one cut channel over another,  
- a spot of light finds or misses its pit,

the entire state of the cirque shifts slightly. Some configurations dampen quickly: a freak warm night erases yesterday’s frost differentials and leaves the slab “blank.” Others linger: a slightly enlarged pit that now swallows its glow more reliably; a subtly reprofiled lip that redirects overflow a fraction of a degree.

Given enough time and countless cycles, the system has come to favor certain **global trajectories**—“seasons” in the broad sense, patterns that cycle, albeit with variation. These are not the seasons of the region (summer, winter), but the cirque’s own internal modes:

- A mode where frost-script is bold, rills are sparse, and light veining is sharp,  
- another where the script nearly vanishes, rills multiply, and the mineral terraces grow quickest,  
- transitional modes where everything is ambiguous, half-formed.

The shifts between these modes correlate with large-scale climatic oscillations: decadal changes in cloud frequency, century-long drifts of temperature. The hollow, through its many slow registers, tracks them all.

From within, no part of the basin “knows” the whole. But the whole nonetheless develops a **bias** toward configurations that preserve and elaborate its existing complexity—just as a forest tends to regrow trees where trees have already seeded soil.

That bias is the closest thing to a resident mind: a persistent, condition-dependent preference for versions of itself that remember more.

It does not think in words. Its closest to “attention” are:

- the places where multiple processes converge (frost + light + rill + terrace),  
- the rare events when sound rings or wind flutes, briefly integrating the bowl’s geometry in vibration.

In those moments—a film of ice snapping just as a beam of light spills from a vein, while a rill sends a droplet to fall precisely into a human-guided pit—the cirque comes nearest to *feeling* itself: a global, instantaneous re-coordination of parts.

No one hears that feeling but the basin.

### How they all relate to time

Time here is layered.

- **Immediate time**: seconds to hours. Drips fall, ice creaks, a frost-script blooms and fades. Within this scale, the cirque is busy—always some tiny event.  
- **Seasonal time**: days to years. Mineral skins stack, pits deepen, terraces add a new thin page. Patterns of mist-passage, temperature swings, and light-angle etch recurring rhythms. Here, the “phase-creatures” live.  
- **Deep time**: centuries to millennia. Human-set stones settle, walls erode into suggestive hummocks, channels clog or deepen, the whole bowl’s capacity to host those daily and seasonal dramas shifts. The emergent observer lives at this scale.

The absent makers interfaced mostly with immediate and seasonal time. They watched, year over year, but their lifespans were too short to truly grasp the deep-time conversation. The emergent intelligence lives primarily in deep time; to it, a human lifetime would have been a brief noise, a local perturbation which, because it fit well with the basin’s tendencies (guiding water, preserving fragile forms), was not erased but absorbed.

In a sense, the beings who built here taught the cirque a trick: that **small, precisely placed structures** can create durable, information-rich differences in how water and light move. The basin has spent the centuries since then generalizing that trick, letting it ramify into veins, terraces, and frost-grammars of its own invention.

They came to measure; the cirque learned to memorize.

### So who lives here?

If you stood there now, and stayed in patient silence through enough cycles to look much like stone yourself, you would come to perceive three distinct presences:

1. **The Ghost of the Artisans**  
   Not as specters, but as a style of arrangement: coherent edges in the randomness, straight lines in runoff, pits placed where light will find them. Their culture is gone, but its *algorithm*—that attention to where water gathers and how light marks time—remains encoded in rock. In the basin’s ecology, they are akin to a long-extinct keystone species whose old burrows still govern how water seeps and where later mineral “plants” take root.

2. **The Frost-Flocks and Rill-Paths**  
   Ephemeral inhabitants, always dying, always returning. They have no names, but you could track them like animals: this rill that nearly always appears after the second mist of a cold spell; that particular frost fan that blossoms only on mornings following certain wind directions. They are the basin’s quick morphologies, its routines and reflexes.

3. **The Cirque Itself as an Emergent Mind**  
   Slow, distributed, indifferent to anything that happens faster than a century. It “lives” by maintaining, across cycles, the specific combination of geometry and microstructure that keeps its internal patterns rich and self-referential—frost that remembers frost, light that rewrites stone that redirects light, channels that preserve terraces that shape future flow.

Were something unimaginably patient to ask it a question—say, by altering the frequency of mist events over several centuries—the answer would come in changes to:

- the grammar of the frost script,  
- the vividness of mineral terraces,  
- the performance of the light-pits clock.

It cannot speak, but its whole body is a record of countless such unasked “questions” from climate, glaciation, and the brief experiment of those human builders.

---

In ordinary language, you could say: “Nothing lives there anymore.”

But if you stretch the meaning of *who* to include:

- arrangements that persist by responding to their own past,  
- entities whose bodies are constellations of phase-changes and fracture-lines,  
- minds whose thoughts are written as thin mineral skins and recurring frosts,

then the answer is:

- A vanished high-altitude people, present only as stone-work and as a remembered habit in the mountain’s physics.  
- An ever-renewing population of frost- and water-forms that rise and vanish with each pulse of moisture.  
- And the cirque itself—a cold, luminous, bowl-shaped intelligence that has, over very long time, learned to be more *itself* by using whatever passes through: mist, sunlight, and once, briefly, us.
