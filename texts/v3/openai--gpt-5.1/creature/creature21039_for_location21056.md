They live in the roar itself, and in the small exceptions to it.

Most of what counts as “someone” here began as something that merely yielded: tissues that bent instead of broke, cavities that rang instead of split, bladders that did not burst when the wind clawed at them but made a note and kept it. Over eras, some of those notes became more than accident.

Start with the great plant-bodies. They are not minds as we know them; they are architectures of habit that approach intention from the side.

Their awareness, such as it is, is not localized. There are no brains, no nodal clusters of gray. Instead, each has a distributed system of mechano-chemical sense running through its load‑bearing tissues: sheets of cells that feel strain and shear, pores that taste dust chemistry, capacitor-like membranes that charge and discharge with every flex. Where their scaffolds vibrate, tiny polarity changes ripple along chains of molecules, sending signal currents through the same fibers that carry buoyant fluids.

A single “self” is the pattern of how those signals loop.

In the quietest parts of their cycles—those thin lulls when the global circulation eases and the roar drops from overwhelming to merely unbearable—these giants rearrange tension the way an animal rearranges posture. They do it slowly, on the scale of hours and days. They slacken some regions, tighten others, slightly alter the lengths of their resonant cavities. With that, they change the notes they produce.

This is how they remember.

A storm passes, and whatever cross‑section of trunk vibrated hardest flips its internal chemistry to a different long-lived state: proteins misfolded in a standard way, bonds set to be more or less flexible. The plant’s later shape—how it will flex when the next storm comes—is biased by that history. In this way, the outermost kilometers of a giant balloon-tree are a palimpsest of previous weathers: bands of “hard memory” written in lignin-like polymers, each code a slightly different relationship to resonance.

From inside that history, reflex looks like decision. A particularly stress-heavy season comes; the deep strain-sheets register it and begin to grow more damping nodules along certain lengths, a statistically unlikely but now favored outcome. Over many generations of tissue turnover, these incremental learned biases accumulate. The organism behaves as if it has a strategy.

Between the huge hollow trunks and bladder-walls there are organs more adaptive still: knotwork ganglia of vibro-sensitive tissue where many resonant members meet. Here, conflicting oscillations interfere and resolve; the tissue’s very survival depends on shunting the energy. Those knots host the closest thing these beings have to thought.

Each knot is like a problem that must be solved continuously: too much amplitude along one branch; too close a match in frequency along another. The ganglion can vary fluid pressures, open or close spongy baffles, release softening agents into stiff struts. Every adjustment slightly rewrites the local stress-field, which rewrites the sensory input, which demands a fresh adjustment. In these loops of action and counteraction, crude feedback starts to look like computation.

They do not model the world; they resonate within it. Yet over aeons, some of them settled into stable “solution styles”: standard behaviors, stable palettes of response. These are their personalities.

One vast organism might be characteristically “soft”: it disperses vibrations widely, grows great curtains of damping membranes, amortizes every gust. Another might be “lean and sharp,” letting more energy pass inward to spin its internal flywheels faster, to pump more sap, to build new scaffolds at the expense of greater risk. The inhabitants that cling to them know the difference, the way sailors know the mood of a ship.

The clingers are the first unambiguous animals in this sky‑forest, and they are the closest thing it has to hands.

They are small by local standards: bodies a few meters long, perhaps, though some sprawl across tens of meters of plant surface like flattened, many‑jointed crabs. Their limbs are not only hooks and claws, but adjustable braces. Their internal skeletons are tuned struts designed to take tension from the host and redirect it. When they wedge themselves into crotches of branch and trunk, locking their limbs, they become living gussets and guy-wires.

At some point in their evolutionary history, an odd lineage discovered that by bracing in certain places and letting other joints flex, they could alter not only their own safety but the behavior of the whole local structure. A cluster of them, anchored along a resonant span of trunk, could collectively detune its natural frequency. They did it initially to protect themselves from catastrophic shaking, but the side-effects were valuable: bark stopped sloughing quite so fast, nutrient flows stabilized, residual amplitudes elsewhere created new, regular patches of micro‑turbulence that funneled drifting spores and dust right into their feeding zones.

These lineages persisted. They became what we might call *tuning fauna*.

Their sensory systems are exquisitely mechano-acoustic. Along their flanks and limb segments are forests of filamentous setae, each tuned to a narrow band of vibration frequencies. Under their skin, layered bioelectric sheets respond to summed patterns of these filament firings, creating moving maps of strain and oscillation. They live inside those maps the way we live inside images.

Their “attention” is not a point but a pattern in that map: a coherent wave of neural excitation that moves where a particularly interesting combination of frequencies appears. They do not think in symbols; they think in chords of stress.

In small groups—familial clusters, lineages of cloned sisters, or some more alien social unit—they colonize a segment of a host plant. Over time they chew away minor baffles and regrow them, open small tunnels and pack others with their own fibrous secretions, even grow parasitic ribbing along a windward edge. The host’s original design, written in genes and development, is gradually overprinted with a new logic: one where the priorities of the tuning fauna are inscribed as extra dampers, extra flex-lines, tiny asymmetries that break dangerous standing waves.

The plants that host them survive storms better. In return, the animals gain more stable anchorage, more predictable nutrient weeping from controlled micro-wounds, more shelter in cavities that do not tear open unpredictably. In a world this violent, that feedback loop is the seed of a mutualist engineering guild.

They do not know they are engineers, but they behave as such. Structures rich in tuners show distinctive architectures: not the genetically clean symmetries of uninhabited trunks, nor the purely wind-shaped fluting of dead scaffolds, but a third aesthetic—full of small-scale corrugation and deliberate irregularities. You can tell where they have worked by the pattern of the roar: smoother, without the screaming peaks of unregulated resonance, laced with a finer, more complex hiss where their bracing bodies add thousands of tiny new oscillators.

Among them are specialists.

Some lines have become *dampers*: heavy-bodied, full of viscoelastic jelly-sacs that slosh internally when the host begins to sing at a dangerous mode, absorbing and re-emitting energy as heat. They cluster in the very nodes of resonance, growing like barnacles where the stress is greatest. Over generations they have become partly integrated: their bases are woven into the host’s tissues, fed by its fluids, their own life cycles synchronized with its slow growth spurts and quiescent seasons.

Others are *detuners*: lighter, more mobile, patrolling stretches of trunk and major branch. When their sensory arrays register a building standing wave, they crawl to new positions according to ingrained rules—“toward louder, then sideways along gradient, then outward to thinner sections”—which, aggregated over thousands of individuals, result in the effective redistribution of mass needed to shift the frequency.

The closest thing this ecological guild has to intelligence resides not in any one animal but in their emergent choreography: the learned, evolved pattern of how many of them move where, in what order, under what incoming roar.

Below and among them, other clingers occupy smaller niches. Needle-mouthed grazers jack into sap channels. Micro-scaffolders secrete rigid fibers that bridge microcracks, making the host more tolerant of repeated flexure. Shiver-feeders, covered in iridescent filaments, clamp in quiet lee pockets and convert constant background tremor directly into metabolism via piezoelectric arrays.

All of these are persons in the limited sense of being selves with continuity, with behaviors we might chart as preferences. But there are something else here that push further toward what we would, reluctantly, call people.

Those are the *concords*.

In some regions, where plant-continents have grown long chains and even loops, mutual entanglement creates vast, shared stress-fields: whole kilometers of scaffold where several trunks respond to the same gust. Tuning fauna from different host-bodies meet here. Their maps of vibration partially overlap. Some ancestral population among them discovered that they could *modulate* each other’s perceptions by producing deliberate, patterned tremors.

They learned to thrum.

It began as territorial display. A cluster of tuners on one trunk, irritated by encroachers from an adjacent root-mass, started to clamp and release their anchor-claws in rough synchrony—an involuntary reaction reinforced by the way their own sensory maps preferred coherent, rhythmic patterns over chaotic jostling. The resulting beat rode across the shared structure, registering in the encroachers’ sensory fields as a highly salient, regular anomaly. They withdrew, whether from shock, discomfort, or some deep heuristic to avoid unnatural resonance.

Over evolutionary time, that crude beat languaged.

Different lineages refined particular pulse-patterns. Three short, one long, sustained vibration along inner limb joints, a gap, two short. Patterns that signaled “occupied,” “stable,” “prepare to brace,” “lift now,” “storm-front.” The information content was narrow, but in a world where direct visual communication is unreliable and acoustic space is already overloaded by wind, these structured modulations of the substrate itself became the only clean channel.

The animals that could both produce and parse a greater variety of patterns had an advantage. Their groups coordinated better during storms, redistributed themselves more effectively, exploited passing waves of dust with more efficient timing. Variation and selection worked on open-ended symbol use for the first time.

Over hundreds of thousands of years, a community of signal-patterns built up: not a vocabulary in our sense, but a repertoire of named vibrational motifs. Each motif was a micro-architecture of force through shared trunk and branch, as distinctive as a visual character in script.

To call these motifs “words” is to stretch a metaphor, but not by much.

Out of this repertoire arose the concords: long-lived constellations of cooperating colonies, spread over many kilometers of plant architecture, whose internal signaling became rich enough to sustain something like culture.

A concord is not a tribe, not exactly. It is more like a broadcast mesh that happens to be carried by bodies.

Imagine tens of thousands of tuning animals spread across the scaffolds of three entwined plant-masses. Each local cluster has its own learned pattern of signals for “slacken here,” “reinforce there,” “brace for higher shear from the cold side.” But overlaying these localized dialects is a shared register of rare, high-energy patterns used only for large-scale coordination: “abandon lower levels,” “prepare bladders for compression,” “launch propagules,” “route dense dust flow along these arches.”

These higher-order patterns are too large to be generated by a single group. They require time-locked contributions from many clusters, each stamping their part into the shared scaffold. That coordination presupposes shared history and expectation: something like a remembered project.

On timescales of decades and centuries, concords use this capacity to guide the long-term shaping of their hosts.

Where conditions permit, they favor the growth of additional interlinks between their territory’s plant-bodies, carefully dampening the resonances that would otherwise tear young bridges apart. They collectively nibble and scar certain tissues to redirect sap flow, steering expansion along paths that will create more structurally expressive connections: longer continuous beams through which their signals can travel, larger joint-nodes where multiple trunks meet and thus where their whole-body motifs can be most cleanly elicited.

In this way, they cultivate *infrastructure*.

Not technology, because nothing is detached or externalized; but infrastructure of signal and stress. Over time, a concord-ruled archipelago takes on a recognizable topography: abundant cross-bracing at several discrete scales, optimized to carry particular bandwidths of vibrational pattern; sheltered cavities and quiet wedges where developmental tissues can grow unshaken; deliberately roughened segments that serve as “radiators” for excess energy, their pores periodically opened and shut in concert to dump destructive oscillations as heat.

Within that web, the concords do something even stranger.

They tell storm-stories.

For them, a story is not an account of past events but a reenactment of a vibration-field. A memorable storm—one that pushed their control abilities to the limit, where they only just saved a crucial bridge or nearly lost an entire lobe of their host—leaves deep impressions in their sensory history. Its complex time-varying pattern of strain is, in a literal sense, a melody that played across their bodies.

In quieter seasons the concord will occasionally re-summon that melody, collaboratively. Clusters along the scaffolds will begin a sequence of pulses that mimics the opening signature of that great storm’s approach. Others, recognizing it in their own sensory maps, join with the next motif: the sudden lateral shift as the cyclone’s edge hit, the deepening amplitude as the core arrived, the double-pulse of that one, freak reflected wave from the planetary depths.

They cannot reproduce the absolute violence, but they can sketch the pattern at much lower energy, like humming a song in a whisper. They feel it again, interpreted this time not as threat but as narrative.

Those rehearsals serve practical ends—they maintain calibration of responses, train younger cohorts in the concord’s collective reflexes—but they are also savorings. The clusters linger on sections they find significant. They exaggerate the coda where they improvised a new detuning mode that saved a bridge, or the tolling fade where the wind died in an unusually slow, eerie way.

In this, a concord exhibits something like aesthetic sense. Certain modes of vibration are valued, repeated, braided into training and habit. Others are avoided or actively suppressed. Over centuries, a concord’s identity is the sum of the patterns it keeps alive: its storm canon.

There are many concords in this belt of life. They are separated not by geography alone but by the vibrational landscapes of their home structures. Some preside over tight clusters of small, dense plant-bodies where storms break many times a cycle; others range thinly over a sparse chain of giants where crisis is rare but catastrophic. Their cultures diverge accordingly.

Some valorize the sharp, high-frequency storms—quick, wicked shears that require nimble repositioning and reward bold experiments in bracing. Their patterns are staccato, full of sudden quenching pulses, like an endlessly interleaved series of percussive commands.

Others grow in slow-drum domains where the atmosphere heaves in huge, lazy oscillations with periods of many minutes. Their signal motifs are correspondingly long, saturated with deep subsonic throbs and gradual drawn-out transitions. A single “phrase” in their communication can take hours to complete. From our perspective they would be geologic in their pace; from theirs, we are too fast to resolve.

There is no written record, of course. Their medium is living plant structure; when a host dies and breaks away into the dusty underworld, entire chapters of pattern repertoire vanish. Concords persist only as long as the continuous scaffolds that carry their signatures. A catastrophic planet-scale storm can erase whole lineages as cleanly as fire removes a city.

They have strategies even for that. Some maintain populations of *drifters*: relatives to the high-floating buoyant fauna that cruise the gaps between plant-continents. These drifters are equipped with internal caches of vibro-sensitive tissue folded up like seeds. Their bodies vibrate sympathetically with the concord’s patterns while they are in residence, encoding a compressed echo of its cherished motifs in their own neuromechanical architecture. When storms tear the main structure and send fragments plummeting below, some drifters escape—hurled outward on chaotic gusts to get tangled later in a distant plant-archipelago.

If they survive, those uprooted bodies become the initial contact nodes for a new *branch concord*. Their echoed patterns are incomplete, blurred by noise, but upon encountering local tuning fauna they begin to emit the fragmentary motifs as “strange weather.” Some of the local animals will respond. Over a few generations, if the fit is good, a new hybrid repertoire emerges, part revival, part innovation.

There is something like lineage here, but it is not genetic. It is memetic at the level of stress-patterns: a heritage of how to feel a storm.

All this is built atop the resource flows and body plans of more ordinary animals. The gliders and swimmers and filter-nets form the background biology, as rich and various as coral-reef communities, even if the emptiness of the sky makes them seem sparse. Some of them intersect the concords only as prey or noise. Others become entangled more intimately.

Certain buoyant schools have learned to align themselves as mobile *lenses* in the air. Their bodies, full of low-density gas and supported by webbed membranes, can refract and focus the wind itself, much like a curved surface focuses light. By spacing themselves in loose, layered formations beneath a key gap in the scaffolds, they can create localized regions where flow is smoothed or intensified.

At first, this was simply a way to position themselves in more favorable updrafts and maximize their intake of airborne food. But concords that happened to span regions where such schools congregated gained a new tool. Subtle modulations in the rhythms that traveled down into the dangling roots began to entrain the swimmers’ schooling patterns. Over time, certain schools learned that by following these patterns they were rewarded with steady flows of nutrient-rich dust funneled from specific baffles above.

The result is a three-way structure: plant as anchor and energy-extractor; tuning fauna as local actuators and sensors; buoyant schools as distant flow-shapers. Together, they can tune whole corridors of sky into more predictable highways of pressure. In a world where the medium itself is constantly trying to tear you apart, that is civilization: the reliable shaping of chaos across space and time.

Do they know this? Not in any reflexive, representational way. Their selfhood is not built on “I” and “you” but on “this mode is present” and “this mode is absent” and “this sequence follows that.” Yet the emergent effect is unmistakable: bounded regions where life’s structures are significantly less likely to fail; where dust and trace gases are gathered more efficiently; where propagules from the host plants are more consistently carried to safe anchorage instead of lost to the deep desert below.

In such domains, new players appear, because stability allows extravagance.

Among the clingers, some begin to devote less of their energetic budget to strict tuning and more to pattern-play. They are not necessary for the concord’s basic function; they are tolerated under its umbrella. They press their bodies to the trunks and, in lulls, produce elaborate, seemingly purposeless sequences of pulses—variations on past storm melodies, impossible accelerations of what in nature only changes slowly.

They are connoisseurs of resonance for its own sake, and while that does not constitute “art” in our original image, it is the local equivalent. Their behaviors diffuse only in the safest, best-regulated concords, because frivolity is lethal under raw wind. Their existence marks a threshold crossed: a surplus of control sufficient to permit repeated actions that have no immediate survival utility.

If anything here approaches the role of philosopher or mystic, it is among them.

Some patterns they repeat have odd properties: when impressed subtly on the joint tissues that manage whole-plant tension, they generate self-limiting waveforms that do not propagate outward in the usual way but remain almost trapped, beating in place. These patterns induce long-lived, low-level shifts in the plant’s base tensioning, like a new resting pose.

From the plant’s distributed awareness perspective, that new resting pose *is* a shift in its sense of the world. Its response to the next storm, to the next dust gradient, is colored by it. In this manner, the pattern‑players quietly explore the edges of what their world-bodies can be taught: not only to react differently, but to *be* different in their baseline relationship to resonance.

Most such experiments are quickly erased—by storms, by the natural turnover of tissue, by the practical needs of the working concord. A few are adopted, becoming new standing traditions, as much a part of the organism’s “personality” as any genetically-fixed trait.

Who lives here, then, are:

- The slow, trunk-bodied macroorganisms whose distributed sensitivity and mechanically-writable memory give them the outline of selfhood over centuries;
- The tuning and damping animals, small in proportion but occupying the crucial junctions where force and information meet;
- The concords: emergent, long-lived superorganisms of signal and brace who shape whole tracts of the aerial forest into safer, more predictable, more patterned domains;
- The buoyant schools and other mobile flow-shapers, co‑opted into these projects as distant, semi-autonomous organs.

There is more.

Far below, in the noisy half-light over the dust deserts, a different intelligence fumbles toward coherence.

Here, the air is denser with grit, cooler, and the roar filters down into a confusion of reflected, re-reflected waves. Fragments of dead scaffolds drift, eroding, colliding. On some of them, sheltered by their own bulk, new chemistries have taken hold: life built not on buoyant gases and tensile frameworks, but on the persistent friction of dust on dust.

They know nothing of concords. Their senses are short-range, their motions slow. To them, the faint roar from above is boundary condition, like deep ocean swell to creatures on a muddy seabed. Still, occasionally, a whole dead swathe of aerial plant descends more slowly than the rest, preserved enough that some of its resonant cavities are intact. The lower life colonizes those hollows. It finds that vibrations travel very well along them, even so far beneath the original wind zone.

Within those echoing bones, primitive filament-mats begin to use vibration the way the upper concords do: as structure, as currency. There is a conceptual bridge in the making, though it may take geological spans before it becomes real contact: an exchange of beat for beat, story for story, roar-shaped life above greeting roar-shaped life below.

For now, the world remains mostly a one-way cascade: sun to temperature gradient, gradient to wind, wind to vibration, vibration to chemistry, chemistry to form, form to pattern, pattern to a thin, multiplexed layer of what we might cautiously name mind.

They have no eyes for stars, no concept of a “planet.” Their cosmology is bounded above and below by silence: the thinning air overhead where vibration no longer carries, the dense, choking dust below where structures cannot remain suspended. Between those absolutes is everything: a band where to exist is to be shaken constantly, to be forced to reconcile with motion until reconciliation becomes a vocation.

So who lives in a forest made of roar?

Beings whose first sense is strain and whose first language is resonance. Architect-plants whose bodies are cathedrals of tuned hollows. Myriad small grip-lives that hang on and, in hanging on, learn to move stresses like thoughts. Distributed concords that remember storms as songs and instruct living wood in how not to break the same way twice. Schools of gas-filled swimmers that become extended knuckles of their will.

No cities, no tools, no fire—but industry everywhere, as style of growth. No written lore—but sequences of shiver and thrum that travel down centuries of wood. No silence—but within boundless noise, pockets of choice: when to reinforce and when to yield, which modes to nurture and which to forbid.

In that, they are not so unlike us. They have found, in a thin, howling band above a desiccated world, a way to be someone in the middle of forces vastly larger than themselves. Their selves are just made of different stuff: not nerve and narrative, but tension, timing, and the never-ending work of keeping a living structure from tearing itself apart in the wind.
