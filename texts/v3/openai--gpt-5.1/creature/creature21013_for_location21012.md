What lives here is not one thing, and not many separate things either. It is a continuum of living surface and living volume, a body so distributed that it only resembles “organisms” if you insist on carving it up.

Start by assuming that the basin itself is alive—not in a mystical sense, but in the way a reef is alive: as a coherent, persistent metabolism that runs through thousands of distinct yet interdependent forms. Most of what lives here does not *move*; it grows, it reorients, it thickens, it hollows. Its changes are architectural rather than locomotive.

You can divide its residents, provisionally, into several overlapping kinds.

---

### 1. The Basin-Organ: Rooted Primary Forms

These are the “vegetation”—but they are closer to membranes and organs than to plants. They do not merely *occupy* surfaces; they *are* the interface between rock, water, light, and chemistry.

#### 1.1. The Frond Walls

Those towering sheets, sail-sized and opalescent, are the basin’s primary light organs.

- **Body plan:**  
  They grow from narrow, tendon-like bases wedged in fractures, then widen into broad laminae several centimeters thick. Inside, instead of wood or rigid veins, they hold a honeycomb of fluid channels and flattened cell-plates. The plates are layered like a stack of thin, transparent coins, each tuned to a slightly different wavelength and chemical reaction.

- **Metabolism:**  
  Light enters, is slowed, split, and *stored* as gradients: tiny differences in acidity, ion concentration, and charge across those plates. Instead of spending energy in a burst, they trap it in a slow cascade of micro-batteries spreading laterally through the frond. The “photonic afterimages” you see drifting along their veins are these charge fronts, moving as deliberately as sap.

- **Behavior:**  
  They can adjust curvature. Over hours and days, they incline or twist to redirect light into the interior—toward more delicate tissues. As they age, some laminae thicken, becoming semi-rigid buttresses; others hollow, becoming translucent ducts that pipe warmed, chemically altered water downward or sideways.

Each ridge of rock carries a procession of these fronds, like rows of solar sails, angled and phased so the basin as a whole acts as a compound lens and radiator.

#### 1.2. The Cushioned Mats

Spread over terraces and ledges, those quilted mats are digestive fields.

- **Body plan:**  
  They are made of densely packed polyps no larger than sand grains, each a tiny cup or tube with muscular lips and a ciliated throat. From above you see only texture and color; beneath, a mesh of fleshy strands binds them into a single sprawling entity that may cover kilometers.

- **Function:**  
  They:
  - Catch falling organic dust and fragments.
  - Absorb dissolved minerals and gases from the water.
  - Trade energy and metabolites with the fronds and pillars via sub-surface conduits.

The differing patches—emerald, wine-red, violet—are not separate species so much as **functional provinces**: groups of polyps expressing slightly different pigments, enzymes, or storage molecules, like liver lobules specialized for different toxins.

When light strengthens, their surfaces darken and swell as internal vacuoles fill with energy-rich compounds. When it fades, the mats “exhale,” releasing processed molecules and warmer water back into circulation.

#### 1.3. The Filament Curtains

These hair-fine strands with beaded nodes are sensory and transport organs.

- **Body plan:**  
  Each curtain is a branching network arising from a hidden base in rock or mat. The globes along the strands are modular organs: some are chemosensory bulbs, some house symbiotic microbes, some are contractile reservoirs.

- **Roles:**
  - They taste the water: its ion mix, nutrient load, and the faint exudates of the few animals that pass.
  - They slowly pump fluids—pulling enriched water from light-harvesting zones to darker interiors, or sending waste toward digestive mats.
  - At night-phase, some beads light up: the glow is not decorative but a controlled leak of energy, often attracting the slow, sessile-grazers that the basin uses for cleaning and redistribution.

As they contract in brighter intervals, they are protecting delicate bulbs from photochemical damage and adjusting flow paths as conditions shift.

#### 1.4. The Pillars and Buttresses

These thick columns that fuse cliff to cliff are not afterthoughts; they are the **skeleton** of the living basin.

- **Origin:**  
  They begin as isolated growths—mats thickening along stress lines, fronds that never fully unfurl, filaments tangling and fusing. Over time these elements secrete a tough, rubbery, composite tissue (a bit like cartilage reinforced with mineral microcrystals) that adds load-bearing strength.

- **Function:**  
  - They distribute mechanical stress from rock fractures.
  - They house major conduits: thick vessels that move fluids, cells, and even small motile propagules.
  - They dampen vibration, which is why the hum muffles in their halls; they are acoustic insulators for the basin’s more delicate interior machines.

Their slow bending and reconfiguration over months and years is not random growth; it is the basin re-routing its own circulatory and light paths, continuously optimizing architecture around new fractures, collapses, or shifts in external conditions.

### 2. The Microbial and Sub-Visible Continuum

Everything larger here rests on something smaller, and in this place the smaller is particularly dense.

- **Biofilms in the glass:**  
  Fresh obsidian and nacre are colonized quickly by tenacious films of bacteria and single-celled algae-like organisms. These pioneer layers:
  - Etch the rock chemically.
  - Exude sticky polysaccharides that give later tissues purchase.
  - Fix key elements into more accessible forms.

- **Intratissue symbionts:**  
  The fronds and mats are riddled with endosymbiotic cells: pigment-rich phototrophs, chemosynthetic microbes tuned to trace metals in the young glass, and nitrogen-fixers that fill the nitrogen-poor water with usable forms.

- **Free-living swarms:**  
  The water column, though visually clear, teems with microplankton tightly bound to slow convection layers:
  - Photo-harvesters that rise and sink with micro-gradients in light and density.
  - Scavengers that dismantle shed tissue, keeping the basin’s interior from clogging with debris.
  - Tiny chain-forming organisms that act as mobile sinks or sources of specific ions, bridging chemical gaps between different macro-organs.

This microbial community gives the basin its deep, almost inaudible hum: trillions of nanoscale pumps and rotors opening and closing peristaltically in close coordination with the larger tissues.

### 3. The Few and Strange Animals

In contrast to the rooted abundance, motile life is sparse, but it is not accidental. Each animal lineage here appears to have been sculpted into a role that suits the basin-organ’s needs.

#### 3.1. Spiral Drifters – “Windmill Eaters”

The hand-sized transparent spirals are floating collectors and redistributors.

- **Morphology:**  
  A rigid helical frame of flexible cartilage supports thin membranes and intracellular lattices. Dark, slow-moving cells creep along the spiral’s interior surfaces.

- **Life strategy:**  
  They:
  - Rotate passively in convective and density currents.
  - Capture light, organic flakes, microplankton, and dissolved molecules across their large surface area.
  - Concentrate what they collect into dense droplets or pellets that are periodically extruded and sink or drift toward mats and fronds.

Think of them as free-floating digestive vortices: tiny, mobile refineries turning diffuse resources into more usable packets for the basin.

- **Relationship with the basin:**  
  They are tolerated and perhaps produced. Some pillar and mat tissues bud tiny spiral cores that grow into independent drifters, suggesting a partial clonal origin: they are semi-detached organs, free to roam.

#### 3.2. Climbing Rays – “Adhesive Pavers”

Those disc-like animals inching along the cliffs help the system manage surfaces.

- **Morphology:**
  - Broad, flattened bodies dominated by a muscular underside.
  - Edges ringed with retractable, hook-like cilia that can anchor into microscopic pores and crevices.
  - Dorsal side overgrown with low symbiotic mats—colored zones that echo the underlying cliff communities.

- **Function:**
  - They graze excess microbial films and incipient overgrowths, preventing any one form from clogging key fracture lines or smothering delicate photic tissues.
  - Their movement stimulates underlying skins to maintain responsiveness—the pigment flicker you see is an old reflex kept “alive” by these gentle irritations.

They serve as mobile maintenance crews, trimming and conditioning the basin’s interface where more static tissues would either overgrow or stagnate.

#### 3.3. Ribbon Eels – “Pathway Threaders”

The translucent, ribbon-finned eels that follow the basin’s faint corridors are couriers.

- **Morphology:**  
  Long, flexible bodies with wide lateral fins that allow fine control in almost stagnant water. The skin is thin, studded with sensory pits and embedded sacs that can ingest and later release fluids or microgranules.

- **Behavior and role:**
  - They travel set routes: the “ease rivers” cut through the growth.
  - They pick at certain beaded filaments and nodules, taking in concentrated packets of cells, spores, and chemical signals.
  - Later, they exude these in distant zones, seeding new growths or triggering developmental changes.

They function a bit like bees in a forest that barely flowers—pollinators and messengers of a slow, chemically encoded conversation.

#### 3.4. Other Minor Fauna

Likely there are:

- **Burrowers** living within the softer parts of pillars, constantly tunneling and thus keeping internal conduits open and ventilated.
- **Sessile grazers** that fix themselves along light-focusing arcs, nibbling and trimming overenthusiastic fronds to maintain optimal angles.
- **Filter webs** spun by small, nearly invisible colonial animals that extend and retract net-like structures in the relative open spaces, catching stray detritus and funnelling it back to mats.

None of these animals dominates. Their biomass is a thin accent compared to the massive living structure, and their shapes are constrained by the basin’s preference: broad surface contact, slow movement, low energy expenditure, strong integration with fixed tissues.

### 4. The Interface Harvesters

Right at the trembling upper boundary, where this hanging sea meets the denser water above, live some of the strangest residents.

- **Form:**  
  Tall, slender towers of tissue rising from the underlying floats and cliffs, capped with wisps and comb-like brushes. Their topmost filaments flare into microscopic frills that interdigitate with the horizontal sheets of bubbles and density ripples.

- **What they collect:**
  - Tiny, metastable gas pockets trapped in that shimmering plane—rare gases, perhaps, or concentration spikes of oxygen, methane, or other volatiles.
  - Chemical anomalies generated at the subtle mixing boundary: trace metals, unusual organic molecules from the distant, overlying ocean.

- **Processing:**
  Fluid is wicked downward in narrow, one-way conduits. At intermediate levels, swollen bulbs store or transform these rare imports, passing the results to the larger basin network. The interface harvesters are like chimneys reversed—they do not vent, they inhale.

They are crucial to connecting this relatively closed basin to the wider ocean above: a one-way mouth sifting the faint alphabetical rain of the outside world’s chemistry.

### 5. Propagules and Memory: How the Basin Reproduces and Learns

For something so slow, this place is not static. It must expand, recover, and perhaps seed new basins.

#### 5.1. Pearl Nodules – Energy and Instruction Packets

In the interior zones where light is intentionally focused, those pearl-like nodules budding off the thin films are more than mere batteries.

Each nodule likely carries:

- Concentrated reserves of energy-rich compounds.
- A curated microbiome tailored to particular conditions.
- A genomic or epigenetic “template” for specific tissue assemblages—recipes for fronds, mats, pillars.

Drifting away, most pearls are intercepted by local tissues and absorbed, subsidizing growth. A few may slip down unseen fractures or out through tiny exchanges with lower, denser layers, seeding distant pockets. In that way, an entire chain of basins could share a loose genetic and metabolic continuity.

#### 5.2. Structural Memory

Those faint pre-marked lines along which mats later split, the arcs of reflective fronds that match basin walls, the pre-chosen future corridors: these are signs of **long-term pattern memory**.

- Tissues lay down microstructural “fault lines” ahead of time, anticipating likely needs: where a collapse might be channeled, where a new light path would be valuable, where a conduit could open with minimal damage.
- Chemical signals from animals, from rare external changes, or from accumulated internal stress can trigger these latent designs—like cutting along dotted lines already built into the living material.

Across centuries, the basin thus learns: architectures that proved functional persist and are repeated; others are pruned away in new growth.

---

### 6. How It All Hangs Together

No single creature, no centralized brain, but a coherent, basin-scale **metabolism with distributed cognition**:

- **Sensing:**  
  Filament curtains, interface harvesters, mats, and even the glass colonizers constantly sample light, temperature, chemistry, and mechanical stress.

- **Integration:**  
  Signals travel as:
  - Slow calcium and ion waves through interconnected tissues.
  - Chemical gradients in the water, carried by both microplankton and the few animals.
  - Shifts in the ever-present hum: patterns of synchronized contraction and release.

- **Response:**  
  Expressed as:
  - Reorientation of fronds and floats.
  - Opening or closure of corridors.
  - Differential growth along stress lines.
  - Changes in the density and composition of pearl nodules.

Within this, the “fauna” are extensions—messengers, cleaners, trimmers, couriers—rather than independent kingdoms. The animals live here, but they do not *own* the place; they are tools the place has grown for its own slow purposes.

---

### 7. In Summary

What lives in this high, hanging sea is:

- A **basin-wide superorganism**: fronds, mats, pillars, and filaments forming a single, self-adjusting architecture.
- A **dense microbial ocean** braided through every rock crack and tissue, powering the continuous hum of tiny processes.
- A **thin sprinkling of animals** designed for integration rather than domination: spiral drifters, climbing rays, ribbon eels, and small attendant species that maintain and communicate within the greater structure.
- **Boundary specialists** at the trembling ceiling, quietly connecting this sheltered pocket to the vaster waters beyond.

Taken together, they are less a collection of species than a single, layered, flooded forest-brain: a patient, luminous engine of growth that measures time in rearranged corridors, thickened buttresses, and the slow fall of silt, thinking with light and water in the quiet heights of a world stacked with seas.
