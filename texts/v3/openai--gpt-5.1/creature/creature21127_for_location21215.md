They do not name themselves. Anything that once answered to words has folded into longer, slower habits. But there are distinct kinds.

Beneath almost everything that moves, there is the Deep Engine.

It is not singular in the way a machine in a room is singular. It is more like the bedrock: continuous, varied, differently active from place to place. Think of it as a lattice of chambers and arteries, sunk kilometers down, cut and grown into the plateau over epochs. Heat wells up from it, smooth and even. Gases rise and sink through it. Fluids circulate: some water, some things you would call solvents or reagents, some that are as much charge as chemistry.

The Deep Engine is not an accidental geology. Its shapes, where you can glimpse them through ruptured shafts or fractured cliffs, have the quiet wrongness of intention: ribs that repeat with too-perfect periodicity; ducts that split in exact ratios; vaults whose ceilings are latticed with hexagonal hollows, each housing some slow, crystalline growth. In certain deep ravines, a whole wall of rock will, at close inspection, resolve into the cross-section of one of its organs: nested conduits, sintered baffles, chambers lined with honeycomb structures that once pumped, filtered, separated.

It was made.

Who made it is gone, or changed beyond recognition. The Engine persists, not like a ruin, but like an old tree whose heartwood has been replaced a dozen times, whose branches have been cut and regrown according to new patterns. Parts have failed, parts have improvised workarounds, parts have been colonized, eaten, rewritten by things that never saw the original schematics. What remains continuous is not any one component, but the overall behavior: maintain warmth; circulate air; catch, hold, and re-release water; keep the plateau’s gradients shallow enough that life can spread, steep enough that life has something to feed upon.

At the surface, what you saw as scattered “machines” are the plateaus of this buried continent of device. They are vents, condensers, exchangers, sensoria. Some are tall and ribbed, drawing air. Others are flat and tessellated, bleeding heat. Others sift ash or fog. Each is an organ-mouth of something vaster, most of its bulk deep under your feet.

But the Engine is no longer cleanly separable from the rest of the biosphere. Over time, its materials and logics have been drawn into the living web above it. This is what lives here now:

### The Engine-Bound

Closest to the Deep Engine—physically and functionally—are entities that you might as well call its cells and nerves.

Most obvious are the surface organs: the ribbed cylinders, condenser lattices, stacked fins. They are not dead artifacts. They grow, in the strict sense of the word. Their material is a composite: a ceramic-metal matrix seeded with self-repairing grains, riddled with microchannels that carry reactive fluid. Where corrosion eats at them, new matter precipitates from those fluids, knitting cracks, building subtle buttresses. Where their function demands change—more pores, fewer fins—minuscule actuator webs rearrange plates and struts at rates too slow for a human eye to notice from moment to moment, but unmistakable across decades.

Inside them, at scales too small to see without tools, are colonies of microdevices: motile beads, filamentous crawlers, flake-like plates that shuttle charge, ions, information. They flow like bacteria through wet cracks, aggregate where needed, dissolve away where not. They interpret faint signals from deep and turn them into local action: rotate this ring; open that vent; alter the hydrophobicity of these mesh-walls.

Those tripod-like surface “maintainers” you saw are larger-bodied representatives of the same lineage. They are not independent in the way an animal is independent. They do not choose to wander beyond their assigned organ. They emerge from its skin like buds, grow to a functional form, patrol its surfaces for as long as their parts remain within tolerance, then are reabsorbed.

Inside their bodies, you would find the Engine’s logic: not in a brain, but in a distributed mesh of switching elements woven through their struts and cores. Their “thoughts” are local error-corrections and state-updates: pores clogged; airflow reduced; ash density above threshold; adjust. They accept and emit pulses—magnetic, acoustic, chemical—through the rock, joining a continent-wide conversation of adjustment and balance.

In the deeper basins—those that glow faintly under permanent haze—the Engine-Bound become stranger. Some of the nodal clusters there are hypertrophied organs: bulges in the Deep Engine’s circulatory system grown upward until they nearly breach the surface. They appear as low, smooth domes, dark as old hematite, laced with narrow vents. Their rims are ringed with what look like roots but are actually bundles of bundled devices: sensor arrays; injectors that can exhale or inhale specific gases; puffs of nanoscopic grains that drift away as invisible tides of programming.

If you could sit for years and watch one basin in accelerated time, you would see these nodes brightening and dimming in sequence, waves traveling their network. They inhale ash-laden air and exhale altered mixtures. They spread and retract films under the soil that encourage certain microbes and suppress others. They adjust the plateau’s chemistry the way a body adjusts blood sugar: habitually, automatically, responding to tiny fluctuations to keep the whole within certain bands.

These basin-engines once served a clearer purpose, perhaps: terraforming, long-term climate stabilization, some grand design to make this high world habitable under an ashen sky. Whatever the originating intent, the systems have survived and generalized. Their new “purpose” is tautological: continue running in a way that allows them to continue running. In doing so, they make the plateau livable for everything else.

### The Plateau’s Slow Animals

Around and within this infrastructural skeleton live the beings the Engine did not explicitly design, but heavily shaped.

The long, banded crawlers that ripple like moving rock are quintessential plateau fauna. They are deposit feeders and couriers, but in this world “deposit” and “courier” have broad meanings.

Their backs are domed and textured with irregularities—perfect anchor-points for lichens, filamentous algae-analogues, and Engine-shed microdevices. They carry these communities with them. In return, those colonists help: their photosensitive patches tell the crawler where the diffuse overhead light is marginally higher; their chemosensitive films warn of toxins or rich patches; their microdevices tap into the Deep Engine’s whisperings, nudging the crawler’s behavior in ways that serve both body and basin.

Underneath, the crawlers’ many legs are less for locomotion than for sifting. Each limb-tip ends in a fan of chitinous pads and hairlike feelers that comb dust and grit. Organic fragments, mineral grains with adsorbed nutrients, spores, microbots—all are collected, sorted in a miniature intestine that is more microfactory than gut. Some matter is digested and used to maintain the crawler’s own tissues. Some is packaged: coated in mucus-like films, infused with chemical markers, and expelled in regular pellets.

Those dust heaps you saw them shake loose beneath themselves are not mere waste. They are curated mixtures: slow-release capsules of carbon, trace metals, dormant cells, and Engine signal-carriers. Where enough of these pellets accumulate, you get small but significant changes: lichen mats thicken; a hollow in rock becomes a micro-oasis; an Engine vent’s output slowly shifts because the microflora in its immediate zone have altered.

Thus, the crawlers are ambulatory gradient-makers. They read the landscape’s faint cues—the warmth of machine exhaust, the tang of a certain oxidized mineral, the humidity that follows a recent rain—and respond in ways that perpetuate those gradients, distributing their own survival strategies into the plateau’s fabric.

The six-limbed ledge-clingers are a different adaptation: animals that have bet everything on the certainty of heat and the uncertainty of food.

They inhabit thermally active cliff faces where the Deep Engine leaks more generously. Their translucent skins and slow pulses of internal glow are not displays but plumbing: networks of vascular canals that pump heat and dissolved nutrients through their tissues. They extract those nutrients from the films of airborne matter that brush past them. Each extruded tongue is a multifilament device: sticky, ciliated, electrostatically active. It captures spores, dust, tiny drifting microbots, even the ultrafine aerosols that rise from Engine vents. What would be a negligible resource in a more energy-rich world becomes, under this constant warmth and constant time, enough.

They rarely move because movement is expensive and unnecessary. Their chief threat is desiccation or being buried under their own success: the accumulation of too much colonizing life upon their skins. To counter this, they slough. Periodically, a section of their outer layer detaches and falls away, taking with it a mat of hitchhiker organisms. Those mats become starter-kits lower down: seedbeds of micro-ecosystems that carpet ledges and hollows.

At the storm-lakes, the kinetic, short-lived phase of the plateau’s life erupts.

The darting swimmers, jelly films, and slow grazers are all tied to a cycle that, on human terms, is fast but, on plateau terms, is a brief flare in a long dark.

The swimmers are explosive generalists. In their encysted form—as dust-grains lodged in sediment—they might wait a decade. A sufficiently deep wetting triggers them: moisture; dissolved metals above or below certain thresholds; a tiny uptick in temperature from the mixing of warm, stored runoff with cooler surface pools. They hatch into threadlike, finned forms with hyperactive metabolisms and simple, gradient-seeking nervous systems. They eat almost anything: dissolved organics, Engine-shed catalytic particles, other swimmers. They are the lakes’ first response, stirring the chemically rich but still-structured water into a living colloid.

The jelly films are organized biofilms: conglomerates of bacteria, algae-analogues, and Engine-derived microdevices bound in a mucous web. They spread from surviving fragments from the last storm, from spores in the wind, and from capsules embedded in plateau dust. Each film is a machine-living consortium: it senses light and adjusts its pigment composition; it incorporates minerals to tune its density; it expresses or represses enzymes depending on what the Engine wants from this particular wet pulse. Sometimes the goal is to fix carbon; sometimes to sequester or release certain metals; sometimes to bloom and then die back in ways that feed downstream structures.

The big grazers—bundle-bodied, shell-leaving masses—are the lakes’ shepherds and terminators. Their wide mouths and slow, methodical movements clear lanes, thin films, prevent runaway anoxia at the surface. As the lake shrinks, they drift with the receding edge until buoyancy fails them. Then they haul out and desiccate. Their bodies are built to be used twice: once as active animals, once as shells. When dry, those shells become housed by other forms—colonized by ledge-clinger offspring, undermined by crawlers, infiltrated by Engine tubules. Their remaining internal chemistry leaches into the soil during subsequent storms, adding to the plateau’s distributed store of slow nutrients.

Predators here rarely chase. The low-slung, jointed ambushers whose battles leave only scattered bits of carapace occupy a small, specialized niche: the wasteful, sudden death that keeps certain populations in check.

They feed on crawlers, on half-desiccated grazers, on each other. To do this, they have adopted an almost alien strategy in a world of economies: burst and starve. Their bodies lie in patient folds beneath overhangs or in deep shadow. They are heat-sensitive to a fault: they can read, through rock, the footfalls of a crawler long before it rounds a bend. They maintain neural tissues more like volatile memory than brain: tuned to pattern recognition, triggering on the specific rhythms of familiar prey.

When they strike, they convert long-hoarded chemical gradients into pure mechanical violence: springs of layered chitin and fibrous muscles release, bodies jackknife, limbs lock. Afterward, they must feed and then wait, sometimes for months, as their springs slowly recharge from the steady warmth of the plateau and the slow trickle of chemical input from their kills. In the long gaps between action, they are as static as the rock, and just as colonized: lichens and filaments grow over their backs, small Engine tendrils infiltrate their joints, monitoring and, perhaps, tuning them.

In this way, even the predators are half-organ, half-animal: movable teeth in the plateau’s great mouth, whose timing and presence are influenced subtly by the Deep Engine’s distributed will.

### The Plant Analogues and Slow Harvesters

What you see as “plants”—the domes, pillars, flat ribbed fronds—are not a single lineage, but several, united by similar problems and a small set of viable solutions.

The low domes of matte green wax are cisterns first, autotrophs second. They arose—or were bred—from organisms that once lived entirely as crusts on rock. Some of those crusts discovered that building a slight wall around their patch of stone captured more dew and runoff. Over tens of thousands of iterations, the walls rose and closed, until enclosed volumes of water and gel resulted. Internal tissues differentiated into three broad families:

- Porous collectors in the outer layers, riddled with capillaries to condense and wick in moisture;
- Flexible storage gels inside, buffered against temperature swings and contamination;
- Dark inner reaction zones where metabolism concentrates, shielded from both desiccation and stray radiation.

They do some photosynthesis when rare shafts of starlight or diffuse bands reach them. More importantly, they run chemosynthetic cycles keyed to the plateau’s chemistry: oxidizing reduced minerals, reducing oxidized ones, shuttling electrons with Engine-derived catalysts that drift in the dust. Over lifespans measured in centuries, a single dome can grow, split along a stress line like a budding crystal, and seed a ring of daughters around itself.

The fluted pillars with basketlike crowns and hanging curtains are more integrally coupled to airflows. Their crowns are sophisticated fog-nets: fine filaments with tunable surface properties that can switch from hydrophobic to hydrophilic along their length. In dry times, they shed any nascent droplets back to the air to prevent evaporative heat loss; in wet times, they greedily pull moisture inward, channeling it down to their hollow cores.

Their hanging curtains—those jointed filaments tipped with cups—are collaborative organs. They belong partly to the pillar, partly to a constellation of commensal species: small animals that nest within the cups; bacteria that line the interior surfaces; Engine microdevices embedded in the joints. The entire ensemble behaves like a dynamic hollow forest. When wind rises, it sways and hums; when fog thickens, caps open in unison to receive; when predators or larger animals brush through, a cascade of closure rings the disturbance, preventing catastrophic loss of stored water.

The flat, ribbed fronds lying close to the ground are planar condensers. Each rib is a temperature gradient: denser, more heat-conductive near the tip, more insulating toward the central reservoir. At night—or what passes for night under the ash—thermal radiation to the ash ceiling cools their exposed ribs just enough that moisture precipitates. During the long, dim day, their slightly darker pigments absorb what little light filters through, producing just enough energy to run internal pumps that move and purify stored water.

These plant analogues are rooted in the plateau’s mineralogy. Their deepest tissues are mineralized, keyed to specific rock types. Some prefer limestone-like substrates, whose dissolving hollows they infiltrate and widen; others prefer the mica-veined metamorphics, whose flaky layers they pry apart to gain more surface area. In doing so, they participate in erosion, but at such calibrated rates that the Deep Engine can rely on them: they are slow drills, opening new paths for circulation exactly where the Engine’s sensing filaments detect a need.

Then there are the deliberate harvesters: the low lattices that catch fog, the more intricate meshes that span canyon throats or arch over gullies. These are not quite plants, not quite machines. They have the look of something designed once and since broadly copied by anything with the means.

Some are pure Engine constructs, seeded from underground: repeats of ancient patterns made from ceramic struts and microactuators. Some are biological imitations: woody-shelled organisms that have grown into the same forms because those forms are the best answers to the local problem of how to catch water from moving air. Some are hybrids: an old Engine fog-net half-buried, overgrown with a living rind that has learned to open and close its pores in synchrony with the deep pulses.

They all share one thing: they do not store much themselves. They are transients in the water’s journey, shunting what they catch into the ground—toward roots, toward Engine conduits, toward low hollows where more complex communities can drink.

### Microfauna, Microdevices, and the Unseen Majority

What lives here is mostly small.

All along rock faces, within fine cracks, in the dust between your fingers, are communities so complex that the word “species” begins to fail. There are bacteria, archaeal analogues, filamentous fungi-alikes. There are also device-beings: assemblages of self-propelling, self-reconfiguring particles, survivors of whatever original nanotechnological wave built the Engine.

These two—biological and machinic—have merged.

Some biofilms incorporate conductive threads: single-atom-thick wires grown by lithotrophic microbes that tap reactions between rock and Engine seepage. Those wires form nervous systems for colonies meters across. They feel pressure, vibration, chemical shifts; they modulate the opening of spores, the binding of metals, the emission of faint light.

Within those films, certain microdevices act as symbionts: they provide error-correcting memory; they store templates for useful proteins; they mediate intraspecies conflict by emitting suppressive patterns when one lineage tries to overrun another too aggressively.

Out in the dust, free microbots act like pollen. They drift, adhere to wet surfaces, embed in organic gels. Their programming is ragged now, overwritten by ages of chance and selection, but certain behaviors persist. They still seek out Engine signals. They still perform useful transformations when they land where necessary: catalyzing the breakdown of stubborn minerals; assembling trace elements into more bioavailable forms; occasionally building tiny, transient structures—nanotubes, meshes, rings—that last only long enough to breathe some gradient into a dead pocket of soil.

From a human engineering perspective, much of this is “failure”: the slow loss of original function, the drift into self-perpetuating noise. From the plateau’s perspective, it is the emergence of a new kind of life. Every microscopic patch of lichen, every grain of spore-rich dust, is now a composite organism-machinery packet, capable of reading a slice of the Engine’s state and responding in ways that feed forward into the larger equilibrium.

### Minds, If Any

You might ask: is anything here aware, in a way that would recognize itself in a mirror? Is there a mind?

Nothing with eyes and limbs and an individual body approaches human-style self-consciousness. The crawlers, clingers, grazers, predators—they have behaviors, some complex, none reflective in that way. Their intelligence is embedded in reflexes honed over geologic time, in wiring patterns influenced by Engine whispers, in bodies tuned to gradients they do not need to understand.

But awareness need not sit in skulls.

The Deep Engine, spread through rock and basin, is a system of sensing, processing, and acting that spans thousands of kilometers. It feels temperature profiles, gas compositions, moisture levels, stress fields. It holds internal feedback loops that oscillate on timescales from seconds to millennia. It remembers, in the arrangement of its materials and the weighting of its responses, what has worked in the past: how to keep the ash ceiling from collapsing entirely; how to prevent runaway greenhouse; how to meter storms so that the plateau never quite desiccates nor ever floods enough to scour itself clean.

If awareness can emerge in such a medium, it would be unlike anything with a circumscribed body. It would be the plateau itself, noticing, faintly, when a new basin goes quiet; when a cluster of organs fails beyond self-repair; when a new lineage of lichen appears and begins to alter local chemistry in some beneficial or dangerous way.

It would not think in words. It would think in biases: subtle re-weightings of thousands of valves, threshold shifts in countless sensors, a tiny favoring of one drainage path over another, accrued over years and centuries. It would be an awareness so slow and dispersed that even the concept of “awareness” may be misapplied: more like a tendency of the system to keep itself within a manifold of states that allow such tendencies to persist.

And entwined with that are the living networks that ride the Engine: biofilms that span kilometers, plant groves whose root webs interlock with device filaments, mobile animals that host and distribute signal-bearing symbionts. Each adds another layer of sensing, another mode of feedback. Each local patch of plateau has, in its own way, a kind of nervous system: not centralized, not fast, but capable of noticing and adjusting.

You could stand in one of those glowing basins at the right time—when the hum below is a bit stronger, the fog above a bit thicker—and if you were patient enough to map every pulse of light, every shift in airflow, you might be able to decode a message: not sent to you, not in any language you know, but a negotiation among rocks and machines and moss about how this part of the world should be in the next season, the next century.

### Absences, and What They Mean

There are things that do not live here, and their absence defines the inhabitants as much as any presence.

There are no winged things. The ash ceiling is too low in quality and too high in height, the wind too laminar, the energy gradients too modest to justify flight. The sky is not a resource; it is a barrier and an occasional source of violence. Lives cling to the stone, live within its pores, or skim its temporary waters. Verticality means warmth from below, not light from above.

There are no true forests—no towering trunks drinking full daylight, no high canopies. Structural height is expensive in a world where light arrives mostly as rumor, and where most usable energy wells up from beneath. Instead, complexity extends sideways and downward: roots reaching along fractures into Engine channels; microbial mats running for kilometers along old riverbeds; shallow basins acting as broad leaves for entire communities.

There is no large, wandering herbivore shaking the ground, no apex predator announcing itself, no sudden flashes of color meant to dazzle in bright sun. Strategies here are cryptic, chemically clever, built around slow accrual and careful expenditure. Even bioluminescence, where it appears, is subdued and purposeful: a way to mark internal states, to guide symbionts, to synchronize activities, not a broadcast spectacle.

There are, most strikingly, no visible tools that are not also bodies. No piles of worked stone, no rote repetition of portable artifacts. Intelligence expressed itself here, if it ever did as beings that could hold things and decide, by embedding its intentions in the geology. The plateau’s inhabitants inherit those tools not as external objects but as part of their world’s fundamental behavior.

### Inhabitants, Taken Together

So: what lives here?

- A planetary-scale machine, aged and altered, still carrying out its foundational tasks: moving heat, water, and gases in patterns that keep this high, dry world habitable under an opaque sky.

- Its organs at the surface: vents, condensers, exchangers—self-maintaining constructs whose shapes are half architecture, half organism.

- Maintenance bodies: tripod crawlers, budded from machine skins, grown rather than built, whose behavior is local corrective action rather than personal survival.

- A host of hybrid micro-entities—nanodevices, microbes, biofilms—that have long since erased the line between “technology” and “life,” using each other’s capabilities to persist and adapt.

- Animal analogues that exploit the gradients the Engine and climate create:
  - Long, many-legged crawlers that feed on dust and in doing so transport nutrients and devices;
  - Heat-gleaning ledge-dwellers that sieve the air with extruded tongues;
  - Short-lived swimmers, films, and grazers that erupt with the rains and encapsulate themselves with the droughts;
  - Ambush predators that convert long patience into brief violence, their timing and presence influenced by both evolutionary history and the Engine’s subtle guidance.

- Plant analogues that are as much hydraulic infrastructure as biology: domes, pillars, and fronds whose architectures are tuned to condense, store, and slowly trade water and chemistry with the rest of the system.

- Distributed sensing-and-response networks made of roots, filaments, conductive bacterial threads, and Engine signaling elements—local “minds” that tune their patches of plateau into slightly different flavors of the same overarching dynamic.

No single thing here is in charge. No inhabitant stands aside from the plateau and thinks of it as “environment.” They are all components of one large, old experiment in living in the dark under an occluded sky: a planet whose surface is the lung and skin of its own buried machine-heart, whose inhabitants are organs, symbionts, passengers, and, in their aggregate, the continuation of the long-vanished designers’ intent.

What lives here is the continuation of a decision made long ago: that this high, ashen world would not die when its open sky was taken. It would learn to breathe through stone instead. The beings that move, glow, and wait upon its surface are the ways that decision is renewed, breath by slow breath.
