They are not the first thing you notice.

The first thing is the height, the hollowness, the sense of having stepped inside something that is already occupied by a will larger than any people. The inhabitants come later, as a recognition: small, deliberate perturbations in a vast living system. You realize, slowly, that what lives here is stratified not just in elevation, but in intimacy with the greater organism that makes the world.

What lives here is:

- the world‑organism itself,
- the people who have made a life within it,
- and a whole suite of in‑between beings, half‑plant, half‑animal, that blur any clean boundary between “builder,” “built,” and “inhabitant.”

I’ll start at the largest scale and work inward.

---

## The first inhabitant: the world that grew

The primary inhabitant is the thing you are inside.

Calling it a “tree” is like calling an ocean a puddle. It is better to think of it as a *vascular world‑reef*: a single, coherent growth whose tissues span the entire interior volume. It is not merely substrate; it is an active participant in everything that happens here.

It has:

- **A body:** the trunks, beams, membranes, cisterns, and leaf‑vaults. Every support you see is its skeleton; every pond‑basin, its organ; every lung‑chamber, its breath.
- **A metabolism:** sap pulsing through kilometer‑long veins, oxidative heat that keeps the air warm, digestive pits that grind fallen beasts and old wood back into feedstock.
- **A nervous system of sorts:** not nerves in the animal sense, but layered feedback. Growth hormones, electrical potentials along moist cambium, and the movement of specialized motile cells that relay “events”—weight, wound, use—across huge spans.

It remembers, but not in the way animals do. Memory here is:

- **Morphological:** where something has been trodden for centuries, the beam thickens, hardens, becoming a permanent record of traffic.
- **Scar‑coded:** deliberate cuts made by the people to redirect growth become lasting punctuation marks in its body, instructions baked into new tissue.
- **Dream‑imprinted:** in some regions, its young cambium leaks impressions into those who sleep against it—flashes of movement, heat, impact, like echoes trapped in the rings.

It responds to inhabitants on multiple time scales:

- **Instantly,** with pore closures, leaf shivers, a flex of a local tendon‑root to support a new weight.
- **Cyclically,** with long pulses of transpiration and humidity that form daily or seasonal rhythms, even if “day” and “season” aren’t driven by any sun outside.
- **Geologically,** by retiring old spans, growing new hollows, rebalancing its own interior architecture over centuries.

It is not benevolent in any human sense. It is not cruel either. It is *biased toward persistence*. Anything that meshes into its feedback loops—helping distribute loads, cycling nutrients, maintaining breathable air—tends to be supported. Anything that strains it too far tends to be starved, redirected, dissolved.

The people will tell you: “We live in the body of a thing that does not know we are separate.” Yet that is not entirely true. At certain interfaces, the organism’s responses clearly reflect a learned familiarity with their presence.

Those interfaces are where its other main inhabitants dwell: the gardeners.

## The people of the beams

They are not high‑technological, but they are not primitive minds. Their genius is temporal and biological, not mechanical.

### Bodies

Their bodies are built for height and for speaking to wood.

They are:

- **Light‑boned,** tall but not heavy, because weight is danger in a world of long spans.
- **Four‑limbed or more,** but the extra grasping is subtle: elongated toes that can hook into cambium; hands with a second thumb‑like digit along the wrist to help braid and knot.
- **Skinned in matte,** moisture‑holding tissue: not fur, not scales, but something between bark and hide, ridged in patterns that wick condensation toward the mouth and fingertips.
- **Scent‑marked:** each carries its own complex mix of oils secreted through patchy glands. These mark individuals, but also serve as a language toward the plant, which has evolved—or been induced—to respond to certain blends.

Their sensory organs are tuned not to light and sound alone, but to the substrate:

- **Eyes** wide and light‑sensitive, with a reflective layer for twilight. Color vision skewed toward greens, deep reds, and the near‑infrared glows that mark sap flow.
- **Ears** broad, flat, and connected by cartilage arcs to the bones of their shoulders and spine, letting them feel subsonic groans through their skeleton.
- **Feet and palms** loaded with mechanoreceptors; they “listen” with their skin to changes in vibration, pulse of sap, slight thermal gradients.

For them, a quiet beam is not silent; it is full of information. They place a hand on a support and “hear” how far away the next mass lies, how stressed the span is, whether a predator moved there an hour ago.

### Social arrangements

They live thinly, like everything else here: small, spread‑out bands rather than teeming cities. The volume is enormous; a “village” might occupy a handful of pods along one beam, with neighbors a kilometer of empty air away.

Their social structures are built around three axes:

1. **Lineage of craft:** expertise in growth‑guiding is passed down through trained lineages. Children are apprenticed early, taught to read plant responses, to cut and bind in ways that echo across generations.
2. **Anchor rights:** because every stable, load‑bearing junction is precious, families hold traditional claims to certain nodes and pods: rights to sleep there, to farm epiphytes from them, to coax the plant to grow personal niches.
3. **Weather‑rings:** looser circles of alliance centered on shared interior “climates”—a set of beams that sway together, breathe together, and thus define a common rhythm of humidity, warmth, and migratory float‑herds.

They are neither strictly nomadic nor fully sedentary. They oscillate.

- **Settlement cores**—clusters of carved living chambers in thickened beams—are long‑term; some have been maintained continuously for centuries, grown and re‑grown as the underlying wood shifts.
- **Satellite camps** move with resources: when a great float‑herd begins to graze through their volume of air, families will establish hanging nets and temporary pods in its path; when a rot‑chamber swells ripe with edible fungi, they string bridges to it for the season.

Their population is small. The world could not support billions of them without changing fundamentally. Instead, there are perhaps tens of thousands scattered across volumes that would dwarf continents.

### Housing: living inside the living

They do not build separate houses. They *convince* the structure to envelope them.

A dwelling begins as:

- a *scar,* cut with stone and tooth and chitin in a chosen locus of cambium.
- *pressure,* applied by woven bands that constrain growth in certain directions.
- *feeding,* adding their own wastes and carefully chosen rot into small perforations to draw the plant’s vascular attention.

Over years, the plant yields a swelling: a hollow, thick‑walled pod extruded around the seed of their interference. Inside, they eat away soft tissue, lick and scrape and polish. The plant seals the inner surfaces with a flexible, waxy bark. Valve‑membranes form at entrances where they repeatedly pass and push aside new growth.

Within, furnishings are more grown than built:

- **Sleeping berths** are shallow bulges of softened wood, overlaid with mats of dried, spongy epiphyte.
- **Storage niches** are small tumors coaxed to form along the interior; they can be pinched shut to seal in dryness or coolness.
- **Light** filters down translucent sap‑pipes hung like chandeliers. Refill organs—bioluminescent bulbs in distant trunks—are “tapped” by elders who know which growth nodes can spare illumination.

Each dwelling has a specific relationship to the organism’s larger flows:

- Some sit on slow, warm exhalation paths, always comfortable, always in a faint current. Those are prized by elder families.
- Others are perched near nutrient arteries; their walls thrum faintly when the rot‑chambers empty.

To live there is to be inside an organ of a larger being, and the people accept that. They do not think of themselves as separate from the architecture, only as a clever, mobile part of its interior life.

### Craft and tools

Their tools look crude to an industrial eye, but the *system* they operate is sophisticated.

They work with:

- **Cutting implements** of obsidian‑like glass grown from the plant’s own silicates, chipped and set into resin handles.
- **Bone and chitin blades** whose edges can be renewed by nibbling away worn sections and encouraging regrowth.
- **Fiber ropes** twisted from peeled cambium strips, sometimes reinforced by braided, living vines that remain rooted and continue to thicken around what they bind.

The true “machines” are sets of growth instructions:

- A pattern of shallow, evenly spaced cuts along a young support that, over fifty years, turns it into an elegant spiral ramp.
- A ring of constriction bands on a budding pod that forces it to elongate into a corridor instead of bulb.
- The careful seeding of motile symbionts—those internal, rearranging colonies—at chosen positions, to cause an arch to shift a few meters over three generations.

They do time‑delayed engineering:

- A child might be taught to make a cut whose *useful* result will only appear when they are elderly.
- An elder dies knowing that a beam they guided will become safe for settlement only in their grandchildren’s lives.

Their technology is full of such slow fuses. It means their “infrastructure” is alive, and in constant quiet negotiation with the larger organism that owns the tissues they manipulate.

## The in‑between allies: symbionts and semi‑people

Between the great plant and the fully mobile people are a set of beings that participate in both nervous and vascular systems. The people treat them not as animals to be domesticated, but as *colleagues living on another timescale.*

### The rootwalkers

These are liminal creatures whose lower halves are a mass of root‑like tendrils fused to the plant’s flesh. Their upper bodies are more obviously animal: soft, flexible, with jointed limbs capable of delicate work.

Without anchoring, they weaken quickly; their metabolism relies on constant exchange through their roots, tapping sap, excreting hormones. When they detach to move, they drag their trailing tendrils like wet, fibrous skirts, leaving scented, nutrient‑rich streaks behind.

The plant responds strongly to them:

- Wherever they rest for days, the local cambium swells, opens pores, and thickens; they are like traveling stimulus nodes that can kickstart architectural change.
- Their root‑secretions contain complex blends that can imitate or override the plant’s own signaling molecules.

The people cultivate long relationships with particular rootwalkers. An elder might “adopt” one early in life, feeding it certain epiphytes and fungi so its secretions skew toward desirable effects: faster thickening, increased resin production, suppression of particular rot.

When major structural changes are needed—a weakening beam that must be reinforced, a pond‑basin that must expand—they will entice a circle of rootwalkers to linger there with offerings and grooming. Over months, the plant reshapes in response: thickening, bulging, or subtly rerouting sap flows where the rootwalkers “vote” with their presence.

Rootwalkers communicate very little by air sound; they communicate primarily through what their roots say to wood. The people read their intentions by touch and by changes in scent.

### The motile colonies

Deep in the outer wood of some beams live swarms of soft, bead‑sized entities: neither plant cells nor animals in any simple division. The people call them *shifters*.

They dine on the plant’s structural carbohydrates, but in exchange, they:

- re‑lay fibers,
- alter micro‑porosity,
- and change the directionality of stiffness in the wood.

The net result is that, over time, a section of beam can be:

- made more flexible or more rigid,
- subtly twisted,
- or even “migrated” a fraction of its thickness one way or another without obvious cracking.

The people have learned that by introducing certain spores or pheromones into drill‑holes, they can attract or repel shifter colonies.

- To **stiffen** a treacherous, springy span, they seed it with a particular fungus that the shifters dislike; they flee, leaving that region to ossify.
- To **bend** a section toward a desired connection point, they smear it with fermented rot that the shifters love; the swarm moves in, and decades later the beam’s curvature has shifted.

Some lineages of people host shifters in small, painless cysts under their own skin, believing that dreams and structural intuitions pass through that contact. Whether that is true or not, those individuals often become the most skilled architects.

### The lung‑flutes

These are specialized plant‑organs that border on animal status: pods hung along certain beams whose interiors rhythmically expand and contract, pulling air through pleated membranes.

Tiny, hair‑thin cilia along their inner walls can adjust airflow, producing low, flute‑like tones when driven hard. The people have learned to “play” them by altering local humidity and by gently constricting their exteriors with woven bands.

To outsiders, this would look like a kind of hollow‑organ music. To the plant, it is:

- a way for the people to send acoustic patterns into the wood, vibrating long trunks at particular frequencies.
- another channel of influence; certain rhythms correspond to acceleration of growth, others to the closing of pores, others to inducing rest in over‑active floaters that roost nearby.

Rituals involving lung‑flutes are half ceremony, half large‑scale message to the habitat itself. Births, deaths, major growth decisions—the rhythms accompanying these events encode information and, over centuries, have trained the plant to associate particular vibrational patterns with categories of change.

## The wild neighbors

Not all inhabitants are collaborators. Many simply *exist* in the niches the big organism offers, interacting with the people only glancingly.

### Surface grazers and predators

Along every accessible beam run:

- **Multi‑limbed browsers** with padded, leaf‑gripping feet and wide, soft mouths for scraping epiphytes. Their droppings fertilize localized moss meadows that the people sometimes harvest.
- **Crested nibblers** whose bony headplates can crack open hardened seed‑pods and resin nodules; the people track them to free‑feed on what they scatter.

Stalking these are predators tuned to the place’s quiet:

- **Beam‑cats**—not feline, but occupying that niche—move in complete near‑silence. Their forelimbs have retractile hooks for sudden anchor in a fall. They communicate in low subsonics that travel through wood; the people sometimes eavesdrop by pressing cheeks to beams.
- **Ambush veils:** animals that look like hanging sheets of fungus until you get too close, at which point they snap shut, enfolding and digesting anything of manageable size. The people’s children learn to test suspicious curtains with long, sacrificial sticks.

These creatures mostly ignore the people unless starving. The people, in turn, hunt them sparingly, preferring predictable epiphyte harvests and float‑meats.

### The floaters and the sky‑herds

The great gas‑sacs drifting in the hollow air are *entire ecosystems in themselves.* Their upper bladders provide buoyancy; their lower fronds host:

- symbiotic algae and photosynthetic tissues that drink the dim, scattered light,
- small, shrimp‑like grazers that clean their surfaces and become food in turn,
- and parasite seeds from the main plant, which occasionally root into them to extend its sensing range into the void.

The people interact with floaters in several ways:

- **Hunting and harvesting:** When a floater weakens, they read the sag of its tendrils from afar. Nets are strung in its likely fall path, lattices of living vine that can flex without breaking. The carcass, once caught, becomes food for months: gas‑sacs turned into buoyant storage bladders, tough skin into long‑lasting slings and hammocks, protein‑rich interior tissues boiled in plant‑bowls.
- **Travel:** Some lineages have learned to partially domesticate midsize floaters, lashing simple platforms to their tops and using graded ballast—baskets of water and stone—to control rough altitude. These cloud‑rafts are not precise vehicles but allow long migrations along established updraft lanes.
- **Omen‑reading:** Because float‑herds are sensitive to the subtle gravity and time quirks in the habitat, their movements forecast the approach of “fast seasons” or low‑gravity nodes. People watch them the way sailors watch clouds.

### Micro‑climate specialists

Within every rot‑chamber, cistern, and leaf‑vault live dense communities of small life:

- **Fungi** that can bioluminesce softly when brushed, providing living starfields along dark corridors.
- **Spray‑worms** that position themselves under drip points, snapping up water as it falls, their bodies transparent enough that you can watch droplets fizz down their interior canals.
- **Pore‑cleaners:** near‑eyeless arthropods that roam the plant’s respiratory openings, eating blockages and being periodically culled by the plant via bursts of toxic sap.

The people know these small beings intimately, even when they do not “use” them. A shift in the color of glow‑moss inside a rot‑chamber might warn them of a new, dangerous fungus; a decline in pore‑cleaners could foretell an upcoming period of botched respiration and staler air.

## The people’s inner life

The habitat shapes not only their bodies and tools, but their thinking, their religions, and their sense of self.

### Time and story

They grow up with three timescales:

- **Quick time:** breaths, footsteps, the arc of a jump between beams.
- **Living time:** seasons of growth, child‑rearing, the maturation of a guided support.
- **Deep time:** the plant’s own adjustments—the slow dimming of an old leaf‑vault, the vanishing of an ancestral beam into a rot‑chamber.

Stories are told across all three.

Children’s tales often feature:

- anthropomorphized beams that “learn” to carry more weight,
- floaters that remember their former sky and long to rise higher,
- rot‑chambers that swallow wicked ancestors over the course of generations.

Genealogies are recited not only through *people* but through *structures*: “We are the Line of the Third Spiral, whose grandfather‑arch bent toward the east when the high leaf cracked.” To forget the changes in the plant’s body is to forget your own history.

They also accept uneven time: certain places where things grow faster, thoughts dream more quickly. These “fast pockets” are both feared and used.

- Seeds for critical bridge‑vines are sometimes placed in jars and hung within such regions to accelerate their readiness.
- The sick might be briefly taken there to try to hasten a fever’s resolution, though many elders forbid this as dangerous and disrespectful to the plant’s uncertain physics.

### Religion and worldview

They do not worship the plant as a god in the sense of an external chooser of fates. It is more intimate: it is *their weather, their earth, their sky, their past.*

Their spiritual practice centers on:

- **Attunement:** learning to feel what the plant “feels.” Novices undergo periods of stillness pressed against young cambium, fasting until they start to perceive sap pulses as distinct from their own heartbeat.
- **Conversation:** rituals involving lung‑flute playing, rootwalker hosting, and scar‑making. These are said to be how they “answer” the plant’s endless, slow questions.
- **Confession to wood:** before major cuts, important births, or deaths, they will press their foreheads and chests against a chosen span and vocalize softly—not because they think the plant understands language, but because they believe motions of sound and emotion leave patterns in its tissue.

They have no myth of “creation” in the sense of a beginning. For them, the world is a state: it **is** growing. The question is not “who made this?” but “how deeply are we allowed to participate in its growth?”

What terrifies them most are two possibilities:

1. That the world‑organism might one day **decide**—in the only way it can, by altering growth—that mobile inhabitants are no longer a favorable pattern.
2. That some misstep in their manipulation could tip its strange physics, collapsing hollows, tangling float currents, choking the slow breath that keeps everything alive.

So their ethics lean heavily toward *restraint.* Stories of hubris are always about over‑building, over‑filling void, overusing fast pockets.

## How they move through height

In a space that is almost all air, getting from one beam to another is not trivial.

They have developed:

- **Path‑tuned bodies:** a sense of balance and trajectory so strong they can, from a single glance, judge a leap that will take several heartbeats through open space.
- **Living bridges:** vines and epiphyte chains that respond to use. When trodden regularly, they thicken and braid themselves tighter; left alone, they thin, wither, and drop away.
- **Anchor tools:** bundles of weighted, spore‑tipped seeds. Thrown across a gap, these embed in bark and sprout within days into hand‑thick cords—temporary, living ropes designed to last a season, then be reabsorbed.

Their children begin with harnesses and multiple tethers, crawling along broad, forgiving spans. As they age, they learn the different “textures” of gravity in the habitat:

- **Light zones** where gravity slackens just enough that a long run and leap can carry them absurdly far.
- **Heavy eddies** where even a short drop hits harder, and where they avoid unnecessary jumps.

They calibrate these regions the way sailors chart currents: by watching how long a falling droplet takes to cross a given span, by throwing weighted tassels and clocking their descent along carved marks. Maps exist, but they are not paper—they are memories etched in song and in the scars along certain beams.

## Contact with the strange rules

You asked what lives here; part of the answer is: **they do, in partnership with soft edges of physics.**

They do not “understand” gravity’s drift or time’s lurch in equations. They understand them by *practice*.

- An elder might stand at a known low‑gravity node, toss a seed, and say, “See how the arc lengthens? Remember this place. When the floaters come low and tired, they will rise again if you push them off here.”
- They will mark fast pockets as taboo with braided, irritant vines. Stories say, “Step in, and you may age in an hour.” They do not dissect it further; their caution is enough.

Some among them—seers, dreamers—spend long nights half‑embedded in young wood, limbs slowly sinking into that almost‑liquid interstice through which nutrients and some stranger substances flow.

They emerge with:

- half‑remembered visions that might be the plant’s own perspective,
- or that of animals long digested,
- or of future growth plans encoded as spatial intuitions.

These people become advisors when major structural choices are debated. They are not always believed, but their *hunches* have over generations saved bands from settling on spans that later thinned, or from investing effort in beams the plant had quietly slated for rot.

---

## So: who lives here?

Summing without simplifying:

- A **world‑sized organism** that grew into a lattice of hollows, whose metabolism and slow cognition define the stage.
- **People** finely tuned to its body, whose culture is a language of cuts, knots, and patient guidance; who live in pods coaxed from the plant’s own flesh and think of themselves as one of its interior symbionts.
- **Half‑rooted beings** like rootwalkers and shifter colonies, whose lives are inseparable from both plant tissue and the people’s long‑range designs.
- **Wild populations** of grazers, predators, floaters, and decomposers, each adapted to the odd mix of emptiness, softness of rules, and omnipresent living substrate.
- And, in a distributed way, **the memories of all of them**—impressed into the plant’s scars and growth bands, accessible in dreams and in the way beams thicken where footsteps have long passed.

What lives here is not just a list of species. It is a *relationship*: between mobility and rootedness, between structure and inhabitant, between quick time and the slow thought of a living architecture that long ago ceased to distinguish sharply between “itself” and the beings that move within it.
