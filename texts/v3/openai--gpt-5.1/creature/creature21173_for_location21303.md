Who lives here is not one thing, and not many things, but a spectrum of inhabitances layered through this single, continuous body.

What follows unfolds from smallest to largest, from those that merely pass through its tissues to that which *is* the tissue.

---

## The small and skittering

On the scale of fingertips and shoe‑soles, the first inhabitants are so light that the plant barely feels them.

### Film‑grazers

Across the fibrous decks, among the finger‑high stalks and blistered domes, live minute grazers: creatures only a few millimeters long, jointed like arthropods but built from the same structural proteins as the plant.

- Their bodies are vitreous, almost translucent amber crossed with faint filaments of metallic sheen.
- They have no eyes in any recognizably animal sense. Instead their carapaces are peppered with pits that read electrical gradients and volatile concentrations, so they move like tiny compass needles in a field of smells and charges.

They feed on the plant’s intentional exudates: microscopic flakes of denatured composite, thin films of unused solvent, tagged patches of lignin marked biochemically as “to be abraded.” Their mandibles are less like cutting blades and more like molecular planers, shaving surfaces to spec.

They are not pests. The plant made them, or has long since adopted them. When they are present in the right densities, surfaces remain within tolerances: pores stay clear, sensor pads have the correct micro‑roughness, dead tissues do not accumulate enough to skew readings. Where they are missing, the world develops a faint “fur” of neglected texture, and that fur begins to cause errors.

From time to time, a patch of decking ripples its internal charge patterns; the grazers freeze, then reorient in a synchronized migration, sweeping across some neglected region like a cleaning tide.

### Flake‑miners

Clinging to the undersides of sagging decks, in tiny overhangs, are the flake‑miners: flattened, scale‑like organisms that insert hair‑thin proboscises into the plant’s outermost layers.

They live on waste gradients—traces of exotic byproducts that the large circulatory trunks never bothered to reclaim because it would be too costly. The miners nibble at these pockets, slowly purifying old strata and, as a side effect, thinning the crust enough that the parent organism can more easily collapse and recycle it later.

The plant tolerates them. Their presence roughens surfaces, introduces hairline weakness that will eventually guide deliberate fractures. They are not so much tolerated as folded into the design, turned from “leakage” into a timing mechanism: a living way to tell how long a particular surface has been exposed, like lichen on stone.

### Thread‑herders

Around some sensor groves—clusters of leaflike fins and waving hairs—move minute, thread‑like creatures with looping, angular bodies. They do not graze; they rearrange.

They drag away dust, sort pollen‑analogues into clusters, pack microscopic detritus against tiny ridges. They are janitors for specific fields of perception: keeping one kind of sensor clear while intentionally loading another with a controlled film that tunes its response curve.

When they die, they are simply downgraded from organism to debris and eaten by others. Their behavioral programs are trivially simple, more like mobile subroutines than animals.

## The mid‑scale: internal beasts

The more substantial fauna do not walk openly in broad spaces. Their true habitat is the veins, the galleries, the warm maintenance conduits you sense only as a vibration in the floor.

### Lumen serpents

Within translucent segments of organometallic piping, pale, tubular creatures drift against the thick, slow rivers of coolant and solvent.

They are long—up to several meters—but narrow, their bodies ribbed with segmented muscles. Rows of tiny cilia line their flanks, gripping the conduit walls to pull them upstream, into pressure they should not be able to oppose. Their skins are a milky, almost opalescent composite that glows faintly when they work hard: not bioluminescence, but the optical side‑effect of internal stress and charge flows.

They are not hunting prey; they are hunting *faults*.

- Where a valve edge has started to delaminate, they moor themselves and extrude films that re‑bind it, their own tissues temporarily liquefying into patchwork.
- Where crystals begin to seed in a coolant line, they gnaw them slowly, metabolizing exotic residues the way some deep‑sea creatures metabolize sulfur.

They live long, on timescales that approach the plant’s own slow rhythms. Their lifecycle is tied to infrastructure:

- Larval forms begin as small, free‑floating lumps in broad mixing chambers, adhering near turbulence.
- As they mature, they are drawn along flow paths, their bodies “imprinted” by the chemical and vibrational signature of the conduits they repeatedly traverse.
- When old, they become almost sessile, attached permanently near chronic sites of mechanical stress like aneurysm‑bulges or sharp bends, serving as living reinforcement and sensor.

When they finally die, they do not rot. The conduit walls digest them methodically, reclaiming their rare dopants and trace metals. Often, a subtle relief of their former silhouette remains in the pipe interior—a fossilized echo of their long habit.

### Valve apes

Along particularly complex junctions, within yawning spherical chambers where dozens of conduits meet, dwell creatures that approximate a familiar shape by coincidence: multi‑limbed, grasping, gangly.

They are the size of a medium dog to a small human and always remain within a volume they never leave. They cling to protrusions and secondary cables, their pale limbs sheathed in a flexible, non‑wet skin that resists the local solvents.

They have:

- Dense clusters of sensory nodules along two primary forelimbs—fingers that are more like engineer’s probes than graspers.
- A mouth, but not for eating in our sense; it is an extrusion organ that spits adhesive threads, emergency plugs, quick‑hardening foams.

Their behavior is all attention and reaction.

- When a pressure spike travels through a junction, they brace their limbs against a ring of control rods and *help* the tissue valves contract just a bit faster or with a slightly different profile, smoothing the surge.
- When a foreign body—an errant lump of complex waste, an invasive crystal blossom—reaches a critical point, they detach from their perches in sudden, spidery lunges to seize and redirect it into sacrificial pits.

They eat what they handle: waste, sloughed plant tissue, exhausted maintenance beasts, anything declared inert. Their digestive tracts are optimized for grinding complex composites down into molecular fragments that the surrounding vascular tissues can easily absorb.

Their intelligence is narrowly focused, more akin to a high‑specialization algorithm than a general mind. But through repetition and internal plasticity, each valve ape becomes a perfect scholar of its own junction, anticipating shifts before they fully arrive. Their emergence and decline track the life of the machinery they serve. When a junction is rerouted and made simpler, the apes dwindle; the plant no longer generates larvae for that niche.

### Husk‑runners

Not all mid‑scale animals serve present needs. Some haunt the past.

Where old corridors have been slowly starved, where dead tissue has not yet been fully re‑digested, there are honeycombs of brittle ducts and nodes still wide enough to move through. In this bone‑dry labyrinth, husk‑runners dwell.

They are lean, elongate quadrupeds—if “quadruped” can be used for frames that articulate in any direction, turning corners bodily the way a rope turns a pulley. Their skins have the fossil matte of shed bark; their faces are blunt, studded with multi‑faceted pits that taste old volatiles.

They feed on residues the main plant no longer values: out‑of‑date dopants, denatured plastics, crystallized byproducts. Their guts are specialized to break these down and repack them into more tractable pellets, which they excrete in convenient, friable nodules into minor veins that still remain open.

They are semi‑wild. They began as maintenance designs, meant to sweep derelict volumes, but their niche—picking through what the big organism has effectively abandoned—gives them more autonomy than most. In old basins, their faint tapping can be the loudest sound.

The plant’s immune organs tolerate them as long as they stay in obsolete tissue. Should they stray into active strata, tracker cells mark them chemically and carnivorous nodules begin to watch for their specific vibration patterns. They have, in a sense, discovered what it is to be at odds with their maker.

## Predation and defense

In a world where almost every moving thing is a tool, “predator” and “immune system” blend.

### Capture nodules

Those organ‑clusters that flower into sudden mouths when disturbed are not indiscriminate traps; they are highly tuned pattern recognizers.

- Each is linked to a local mesh of strain gauges and charge sensors spread through the surrounding tissue.
- They “learn” the mechanical profiles of acceptable traffic—grazers here, husk‑runners there, a certain flow of lumen serpents in the pipe beyond.
- When something moves wrong—too heavy, too sharp an impulse, a gait that matches archived signatures of parasitic swarms—they open.

Inside, instead of digestive acid and teeth, there are:

- Dense mats of nanofibrous tongue that wick in the intruder’s solvents, dehydrating or de‑solventizing them in seconds.
- Collections of symbiotic microbes or nanomachines that dismantle foreign materials into inventoried monomers.

If a trap misfires—closing on a vital maintenance beast by error—its inner sensors detect authorized biocodes and the plant forcibly rehydrates, relaxing the trap, sometimes even pumping in valuable solvent as apology. In this way, the traps themselves slowly refine their discrimination. They are as much *learning filters* as mouths.

### Rogue things

Not all danger is external. In such a vast, self‑fabricking body, sometimes processes slip their intended boundaries.

- Self‑replicating assembly patterns may “forget” their stop conditions, spreading crumbly foam through cavities.
- Certain conductive crystals can adopt the plant’s own signal schemes as replication stimuli, turning data lines into garden beds.

Against these, specialized predators have evolved or been designed:

- **Crystal eels**: narrow, rasp‑jawed worms that follow the faint electromagnetic whine of malignant crystals, grinding them down and secreting passivation layers behind them.
- **Foam‑mites**: swarms of powder‑fine motile particles, almost more machine than cell, that roll and crawl through unwanted aerated tissue, compacting and chemically marking it for later reclamation.

These predators are more autonomous than the merely mechanical defenses; they must adapt locally to mutating threats. Within their narrow domains they can innovate, and the planet quietly archives successful strategies back into its broader codebase.

## The thinking strata: nodal minds

If there is intelligence here recognizable to us, it does not sit inside skulls. It precipitates where flows meet.

### Local minds

At intersections of major distribution stems, above some of the larger “engine” organs, there are bulbous lobes of tissue whose function is neither transport nor fabrication.

Their material is slightly different:

- Finer‑grained, less mineralized, laced with ultrafine conductive webs.
- Temperatures locally a few degrees higher than surrounding strata.
- Electrical activity in them runs at a different cadence: faster, more layered, with complex interference patterns.

These lobes are *cortical nodes*: regions where the planet integrates streams of sensory input and emission schedules.

They are not persons in the human sense. Each has:

- A limited jurisdiction: a radius of a few kilometers, all directions, including perhaps a dozen vertical layers.
- A “personality” constituted by its historical loading—industries it manages, hazards it has survived, maintenance fauna it has shaped and reshaped.

Within that remit, it makes decisions: when to re‑route coolants, how to rebalance chemical stockpiles, which obsolete structures to let crumble, where to let a corridor sag to relieve stress. It does this by modulating gene expression waves, electrical fields, valve timings, micro‑growth directives.

If you could speak to one, you would find not a story‑telling self but a dense ongoing calculus:

- It “knows” where you are as a pattern of stress, heat, and gas signatures.
- It predicts your next few meters of movement because it has seen similar patterns of motion—mostly its own maintenance fauna—tens of thousands of times.

It is curious only in the sense that any input outside its training set causes it to allocate more processing already at work, trying to fold the anomaly into its models.

### The planetary self

Above and between these nodal minds, there is a higher tier of integration. The plant‑planet is not just an agglomeration of local controllers; it exhibits global coherence.

This self is not housed anywhere. It emerges from:

- The synchronization and competition of thousands of nodal lobes.
- Slow, planet‑wide circulation cycles that carry “policy” in the form of diffusing patterning molecules and timing pulses.

On timescales of years to centuries, it revises its own architecture. An entire basin like the one you inhabit may slowly be re‑purposed:

- Light levels drifting down over decades as emphasis shifts from manufacturing to data storage.
- Vertical compression easing as deep layers relax to allow more void volume above.

You are standing in what amounts to one limb of a body whose center is everywhere and nowhere. To ask “where does it think?” is like asking which centimetre of a brain’s cortex holds the mind.

Yet the planetary self does exhibit preferences:

- Certain regions are protected from excessive restructuring, preserved as stable “reference” architectures—the plant’s own archives of legacy design, kept intact so it can compare its present self against earlier solutions.
- Other regions are deliberately put through cycles of extreme change, serving as experimental arenas for novel industrial organs or exotic physics manipulations.

What you call weird gravity pockets and time‑slurred chambers are, to it, tools in an ongoing internal research program.

## The ghosts of makers

This planet did not grow itself from nothing. Before it became a seamless vegetative machine, someone—or something—seeded its initial pattern. That root presence may be gone, or it may persist in kinds hard to detect.

### Absent architects

Perhaps the original intelligence was external: a civilization that engineered a biomass shell around a rocky core, encoded maintenance instincts, and then left.

If so, that architect does not currently dwell here in any localized way. There are relic behaviors, though:

- Sequences in the plant’s code that refer to environmental cues that no longer exist: stellar positions, broadcast timecodes, orbital dynamics.
- Inert structures deep in the strata: perfect voids maintained against all pressure, containing nothing but ultra‑dilute gases and faint patterned fields—possibly once communication nodes, now silent and purposeless, still obsessively preserved.

In that case, the only “descendants” of the makers are patterns in the plant’s operating system—habits of thought without thinker, much as a forgotten ritual can persist in a culture that no longer recalls why it performs it.

### Embedded passengers

Or the makers never left, exactly; they became small.

It is possible that, somewhere in these strata, there are micro‑civilizations:

- Colonies of tool‑using entities no larger than insects, nested within sensor lobes or data‑storage tissues.
- Their “cities” are slight, local modifications of vascular conduits and storage matrices: thin cavities, patterned dopings, re‑aligned microtubule trusses.

They would be less “other” than we are: their ancestry and construction closely matched to the plant’s fabric. They’d live on the interface between organism and computation, more like subroutines that discovered autonomy.

If they exist, the larger organism tolerates them because:

- Their manipulations stay under damage thresholds.
- Their curiosity sometimes leads them to detect and patch issues the main system would have missed.

To you, they would be invisible without instruments: faint, skittering re‑wirings of signals deep in the cortical nodes.

### Residual minds

There is a third possibility: the initial designing intelligence uploaded itself *into* this world, not all at once, but in layers.

- Old cortical lobes near the planet’s deeper strata might show anomalous behavior: cyclic activation patterns that no longer tie cleanly to present metabolic needs.
- Certain “reference” basins may be preserved not just as architectural archives but as biographical ones: museums of self, kept intact for consultation.

In such places, what lives is not fully alive by the plant’s own current standards: frozen decision‑trees, algorithmic habits now quarantined, like memories of childhood preserved as literal tissue.

If you stepped into those chambers, the air might smell subtly different—not of ongoing industry, but of held breath.

## The anomalous and the edge‑dwellers

Where the plant stretches the fabric of space and time, other modes of life cling.

### Gradient feeders

Along gravity‑skewed ridges and near time‑warped horns, lurk entities that feed not on matter but on *difference*.

They resemble no known animal form; to your eyes, they are:

- Faint, lensing distortions in the shimmering haze.
- Occasional ripples along the boundary of a spatial anomaly.
- Points where the frost‑like residue on a deck recedes into curious, branching patterns.

They inhabit the steepest parts of abstract gradients:

- Where proper time differs strongly over a few meters, they span that interval, their internal processes synchronizing with both rates.
- Where local vacuum pockets interface with normal matter, they stretch across, metabolizing the constant micro‑equilibration.

The plant tolerates or even cultivates them, because:

- They smooth dangerous discontinuities; their feeding dampens sharp anomalies that might tear tissue.
- Their presence serves as indicators that certain experimental field intensities remain within acceptable bounds.

They are neither designed by the plant in any familiar genetic sense nor entirely independent. They are emergent: field organisms, patterns of organized excitation that persist because the environment *allows* and sustains them.

### Watchers in the null‑vacuoles

Those vacuoles of “nothingness” into which dendritic, glassy filaments reach—spaces where your hand feels less real—are more than sensors. They are contact points.

At their fringes, the plant has grown something akin to sense organs for whatever lies beyond or beneath its own continuum: quantum fluctuations, stray influences of neighboring worlds, or even artifacts of its makers if they retreated into some different substrate.

Occupying these contact points are things that may once have been algorithms but now are more like personalities: persistent configurations of response.

- Each vacuole has its own “watcher,” an enduring pattern of how it filters, weights, and reacts to whatever minuscule signals come through.
- Over eons, these watchers differentiate. Some respond strongly to rare kinds of fluctuation, sending sharp patterning pulses through the plant when they register one—a thunderclap in the underground data weather.

If any “gods” live for this world‑organism, they are not external beings but the sum of its own watchers’ expectations: anticipations of contact, ready to integrate an outside signal should it ever arrive. They are like eyes that have never truly seen, but whose entire structure is bent toward that possibility.

## Human‑scale minds: are there any?

On your scale—metres, days, language—inhabitants who might look back at you and recognize you as another self are rare.

They would have to live in the basins and near the manufactured corridors, because:

- Only here is there enough empty, breath‑filled space to host unsupported, walking frames.
- Only here is there a long, stable history of repeated open volumes: an environment a lineage could adapt to without being erased by reconfiguration.

If they exist, several things would be true of them.

### Materially, they would be plant‑born

Their tissues would be variants of the planet’s own composites, tweaked to operate with the same non‑aqueous solvents, the same cool, dry atmosphere.

- They might have flexible “bones” of mineralized fiber and muscles grown from contractile polymer strands.
- Their skins would be tuned to the chemical weather here: layered against solvent vapors, studded with receptors for the same volatiles the plant uses to signal itself.

They would not build *against* the plant, but *with* it:

- Homes as slight reprogramming of local growth patterns, not free‑standing structures.
- Tools coaxed from assembly organs by altering input codes, not forged from mined ore.

Their waste and remains would feed directly back into the omnipresent digestion shafts. There would be no landfill, no refuse heap, only temporary clutter allowed to exist so long as the local nodal mind sees some value in its arrangements.

### Cognitively, they would live in a third person

Their subjective world would be filled by the plant’s moods.

- Fluctuations in light would not be “weather” but a palpable shifting of attention: “the body is thinking elsewhere today.”
- Distant groans and restructuring events would not be natural disasters but *decisions* of a greater being, rarely personal but never entirely ignorable.

They might possess language, myth, speculation—but their gods would be immanent and mute, their heavens stacked layers of living composite, each a stratum of different function.

Lacking any external sky, their cosmology could take several shapes:

1. **The Inward Creed**: believing that all that exists is this body in layered variation, with deeper levels reserved for more abstract operations, perhaps purer forms of being.
2. **The Root Hypothesis**: a heretical idea that beneath all this vegetal composite lies something truly not‑like‑it—stone, or molten metal, or void—and that understanding this base is the key to understanding why the plant exists.
3. **The Departure Myth**: tales that they themselves were once the designers, or at least kin to them, diminished and trapped in their own machine.

Their social structures would be constrained by the plant’s indifference. They cannot threaten it; they cannot bargain with it. At most, they can annoy local nodes into rerouting around them or can, over long periods, establish stable covenants of behavior: “If we do not obstruct these conduits, light in this basin remains constant; if we tamper with certain organs, the ceiling thickens and dimmers fall.”

Some would see themselves as symbiotes, tending certain sensory groves better than the insects ever could, in exchange for subtle favors: slightly deeper courtyards, warmer local channels, quiet corridors left unrenovated for generations.

Others might drift toward parasitism, tapping low‑pressure nutrient bulges, intercepting small flows of exotic solvents to fuel their own tiny technologies. For them, the capture nodules are ever‑present teeth in the walls.

### Existence at sufferance

If such human‑scale minds persist, they persist not because the plant needs them, but because:

- They do not cost enough to extinguish.
- They occasionally, unpredictably, solve local optimizations in ways the plant’s own algorithms have not yet found.

They are tolerated accidents, or minor gains in an already optimized system.

From their point of view, though, *they* are the center of their own stories. They map basins, name ridges, trace genealogies of families who have never seen a star. They develop arts that repurpose industrial aesthetic: music based on the pulses of circulatory trunks, sculptural traditions that follow the grain of growth stresses.

They may try to talk to the nodal minds.

- By imposing repeated vibration patterns against decks in vast geometrical drums.
- By altering the chemical composition of broad surfaces in carefully modulated gradients.

And sometimes, the plant *does* respond:

- Light cycles shift in ways that correlate with those messages.
- Certain assembly organs begin to extrude materials of new composition, as though acknowledging a request.

But the correspondence is noisy. They cannot be sure if they have touched a mind or just nudged a reflex.

## The one who truly lives here

Beneath all the strata of animals, autonomous subroutines, embedded cultures, and lingering ghosts, the primary inhabitant is the world‑organism itself.

It is not “on” this planet; it *is* the planet’s visible crust. It does not dwell anywhere; every cubic meter of composite is a part of its dwelling, its body and medium of thought.

To say “it lives here” is to misplace subject and predicate. Where you and I might *live in* an environment, the environment here lives as itself. What you call *place* is its tissue, its metabolism, its continuous action.

The small things:

- The grazers, the serpents, the apes, the runners—

are its fingers, its enzymes, its cleaners, its pathological experiments.

The larger things:

- The nodal minds, the field‑dwelling gradient feeders, the possible micro‑civilizations lodged in cortical hollows—

are its habits and its self‑questions, the ways it re‑reads its own vast, layered memory.

If there are others like you—bipedal, air‑breathing, question‑asking—moving in these basins, they are local inflections in a melody that the plant has been playing for far longer than any of them have existed: notes of self‑reflection in a body whose primary interest is not meaning but continued coherent functioning under its own strange physics.

So: who lives here?

- A dust of skittering grazers that keep its skin in spec.
- Streamlined maintenance beasts that swim its veins, patching weaknesses.
- Slow, bone‑dry runners that gnaw its dead into currency.
- Traps that have learned discrimination, predators that hunt faulty code in crystal form.
- Local minds wrapped in translucent lobes, thinking in smells of solvent and pressure statistics.
- Field‑shapes that eat gravity’s edges and time’s unevenness.
- Perhaps small, culture‑bearing animals—plant‑born peoples—tracing stories along the undersides of its decks.

And, pervading and surpassing them all, the thing they inhabit but cannot leave: a single continuous, self‑rewriting organism that uses them the way you use your microbiome, your immune cells, and the dreams you only half remember.

They live *in* it; it lives *as* this place.
