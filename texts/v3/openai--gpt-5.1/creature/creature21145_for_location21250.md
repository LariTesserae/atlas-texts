They are basin-dwellers, first of all.

Not a race bred for spectacle, not marked by extravagant strangeness. Seen in passing, one person from this city could be mistaken for anyone whose life is mostly weather, mostly work. Medium in height, spare rather than thin—muscle laid into the shoulders, the forearms, the calves, from carrying stone and water and timber along narrow, uncertain paths. The damp air rounds their faces a little, softens skin that would elsewhere be leathered by sun and dust, but there is no lushness to them. They are built like the city: compact, efficient, nothing wasted.

Their skin tends toward the colors of the stone they work with—cool browns, muted earth, sallow-gray undertones—but this is not biology so much as patina. The constant contact with wet rock and clay stains even the pale into neutrality. When a stranger first arrives they look wrong mostly because they are too bright: redness in the lips, fresh sun-burn, hair that still remembers sunlight. After enough seasons here, everyone takes on the same palette and the same faint gleam of moisture.

Hair grows fast in this climate. They keep it constrained.

Children wear it loose or crudely bound: a rough knot at the back with a cord through it, bangs falling into their eyes until someone tucks it away impatiently. Adults—anyone who spends hours bending over channels or leaning under low beams—braid their hair or strap it back. Long hair is not a vanity; it is a record. Thin cords and small beads are worked into braids as time passes: a dark stone to mark the opening of an important channel, a scrap of smoothed bone for a child’s birth, a copper ring for the completion of some collective labor. These are not ostentatious. In the low light and subdued colors of the city, they are little glints you only notice when you are very near.

Their clothing is an answer to their place.

Fabric is coarse, plant-based where they can coax fibre from the stubborn vegetation around them; more often it is layered scrap: woven reeds, softened bark, stitched hides from animals obtained through infrequent hunting or trade with the drier lands beyond the ridges. Because dampness never entirely leaves the air, they dress in thin layers that dry quickly rather than in anything plush that might mildew.

Tunics hang to mid-thigh, slit at the sides for climbing and crouching. Leggings wrap close for warmth and to guard against stone edges. Over this, sleeveless jerkins treated with oils and resins, beading water like stone does not. For work at the channels, some wear short, skirt-like aprons of woven reed that can be easily replaced as they rot.

Color is rare. When a dye holds, they keep it for markers: a band of muted blue at a sleeve hem that means “this person belongs to the keepers of this quarter’s channels”; a single red-stained cord looped at the shoulder for those permitted certain work near the central pools. Decorative pattern is subtle: rows of tiny stitched chevrons along a neckline, repeating as quietly as the city’s carved hollows.

They smell faintly of stone and cool water and the smoke of small, efficient fires. No perfumes. Here, a sudden artificial sweetness would be alarming, like a chemical leak.

Their hands give away their lives most clearly. Nails kept short and blunt, not from grooming but from constant scraping against rock. Calluses not only on palms but on the edges of fingers, from pressing plug-stones into wet crevices, from dragging along damp surfaces to feel for seepage. The skin on their fingertips is unusually sensitive; they read the health of a channel by touch as much as by sight, feeling the trembling shift between trickle and leak.

They do not speak much, but they are not taciturn by nature. It is more that this basin feels like a place where loudness would be an error of conduct. Voices carry easily along stone corridors, bounce off the water’s surface, overlap with the small but important sounds of drip and flow. To shout is to drown out the one thing they all pay attention to.

Children learn early that there is a voice-volume for playing at the rim of the basin and another for walking the inner lanes, near the carved courts. They are not beaten for misjudging this; they are gently turned by a hand on the shoulder, or given a quieting touch on the lips, and they see the way adults always glance toward the water when sound swells too high. The city expects listening, not silence exactly, but a respectful sharing of the acoustic space.

Their language has a large vocabulary for water that is not rain and not sea.

Words for the precise degree of persistence: the water that seeps only on the coldest mornings, the one that thickens when a distant, unseen source is swelling, the almost-dry trickle that must be coaxed or it will fail forever. Names for the condition of a channel: “clean-running but restless,” “silt-asking,” “edge-eating.” These are not metaphors to them; they are practical diagnostics. Someone might run a hand along a wall and say, with mild concern: “This line is starting to wander,” and everyone nearby will know that a stone must be re-set before an entire courtyard begins to hold more damp than is safe.

Their speech patterns reflect this intimacy. They do not say “we will build here” but “we will ask the water to come this way.” They will never admit to having “cut off a seep”; instead they “asked it to sleep.” Conversely, when a new line appears in rock, feeding a tiny series of beads into a shallow, unplanned groove, they may greet it: a murmur, a bent head for a moment, as if acknowledging a new neighbor.

None of this implies that they believe water is literally conscious in the way they are. It is closer to how one speaks about wind or mood—forces that are real, that respond in ways that matter, but cannot be reasoned with. Their reverence is rooted in dependence and in the effort it takes to understand and manage flows without destroying them.

Children here grow up with a sense of scale inverted from most places. Horizontals barely exist to them as significant distances; the whole basin is walkable, the outer ridges visible from almost anywhere within. But vertical degrees of space—above, below, under, over—are everything.

A child’s early boundaries are not “do not go out of sight down the road” so much as “do not go under the high bridge alone,” “do not climb to the third ledge by yourself,” “never step into a channel that is louder than you.” They learn the city first as a mesh of vertical relations: the place under the overhanging stone that is always dry on one side, always dripping on the other; the sequenced platforms where a spill on the top becomes a dangerous flood by the bottom.

They play in miniature what the adults do in seriousness. Given a puddle and some loose grit, they drag tiny channels with their fingers, build dams from pebbles, watch the way the water cleaves and chooses. There are children’s games that involve running parallel to a channel and calling out when the trickle speed changes, practicing the skill of hearing shifts under other sounds. This is described as fun, but it is also training, quietly braided into daily life.

Adolescence is marked not with a single rite but by accumulating permissions. The right to walk a channel path alone at night. The right to cross certain strings of bone and shell without stopping to touch them first. The right to handle, under supervision, the small carved tablets kept near the central pools. Each of these rights comes with an instruction, a telling of a story about when that boundary was first set, and why.

They maintain their city through constant, low-grade collaboration.

Work is not separated into castes rigidly, but some people are clearly more aligned with certain tasks. Those with the keenest ears and patience become line-watchers: they spend their hours on the walkways and at the edges, feeling, listening, noting which faces of rock have grown too glossy with constant damp, where moss has crept farther than it should toward structural joints. They tend to move alone, but they are collectively recognized; when they pass, conversations pause briefly to offer them an unobstructed approach to the nearest wall or channel.

Others become builders. These are not architects in the sense of drawing plans on paper; their medium is the site itself. They know the bearing capacities of certain stone types, the way saturated soil behaves after three days of rain versus five. They are the ones who decide when a ground-level passage must be surrendered to water entirely, converted into a dedicated channel and replaced with a ledge walkway above. Their decisions affect everyone’s routes, and so their authority is held in a careful balance: respected but always made in council, with anyone whose door opens onto the changed space invited to weigh in.

There are tool-keepers, whose job is not only to maintain chisels and adzes but to control where and when they are used. To carve stone is to alter water’s conversation with it; no cut is minor in such a tuned environment. So a person who wants to open a new groove around a persistent drip must petition the nearest tool-keeper, explaining purpose and likely effect. A good tool-keeper has a memory for the city’s subsurface; they may recall that an almost-forgotten channel once lay in that place, buried by some earlier generation’s error, and so refuse the request or adjust it.

No one enforces these roles with formal law, yet the discipline is real. The city is too small for rebellion against such norms not to be immediately felt. The last time someone carved without permission near a central pool, water began to vanish from one of the lesser gathering-courts for weeks thereafter. Even if the two events were not causally linked, the story attaches itself and stiffens practice: “We do not cut there, not again.”

The most visible sign of their collective mind is the network of carved hollows you noticed scattered through the city: those fist-sized depressions, often arranged in patterns that refuse obvious interpretation.

They use them, but not the way an outsider might hope. They are not basins for offerings, not counting-holes, not astronomical markers—at least not solely. From within the culture, each hollow is a note in a layered notation.

At the simplest level, some hollows truly mean just what you sensed: “do not let this place dry.” In corners where a structure depends on a persistent damp to keep its clay bonded, a hollow is carved to hold a little reserve of water after each rain, to slowly bleed into the joint rather than allowing it to desiccate and crack. People passing such a hollow in a dry spell will, almost unconsciously, tip a handful of water from a carried basin into it.

Other hollows are mnemonic devices. A cluster of seven arranged in a curve along a lintel might correspond to seven upstream seep-lines that feed the channels in that lane; their relative depths encode their relative strengths. An elder, tracing the sequence with their fingertips, can recite the path of each trickle from ridge to pool, and so teach an apprentice where to look first when something changes.

Then there are hollows that have passed beyond clear function into tradition. Spirals carved three generations ago to record some complex water-rearrangement whose details are no longer fully remembered; lines that once led nowhere but have since been adopted as markers of a boundary between households, an inscription of “here we begin to think of this place differently.” People step around them with the same practiced inattention you give to old, familiar signage: the meaning is there, mostly below conscious notice, shaping behavior.

Their relationship to the central pools is the quiet heart of who they are.

They do not drink there because those surfaces are not for consumption; they are for comprehension.

Each main pool is understood as a summary, an integrated reading of the basin’s current state. The way light plays on the surface, the degree to which ripples from distant channels reach it, the faint tilt of its mirror when wind touches one ridge but not another—all these are clues. Elders gather at the edge and watch. They do not peer for visions or omens in the mystical sense; they are reading a complex instrument.

Over time, they have learned correlations: when a certain pattern of shiver appears near the northern rim of a pool, it means the seep that feeds the easternmost quarter is weakening, perhaps because some rock face beyond the ridges has shifted. If there is an unusual stillness in part of a pool even when surrounding trickles are active, it may signal a blocked runnel somewhere uphill.

Children are kept back not out of fear that they will defile the sacred, but because the surface must be allowed to be as undisturbed as possible for such readings to remain valid. A single tossed stone could ruin a day’s worth of careful watching.

The offerings placed on ledges near the pools—bone carvings, colored flakes—are not sacrifices but memory-anchors. Each signifies a significant change made in response to what the pool once showed: the diversion of a stubborn flow, the stabilization of a collapsing wall. They are crude records, but meaningful. When someone contemplates altering a channel, they may come here, run their fingers over the old tokens, and remember: last time we did something like this, the western quarter went dry for half a season; last time we declined to act, a dwelling collapsed into its own saturated foundation. The pool is a mirror and an archive.

If there is a spiritual dimension to all this, it is not centered on a deity of water so much as on a posture of continuous attentiveness. They have stories, of course.

Origin tales speak of a time when the basin was only a raw depression, with water emerging in chaotic fashion, drowning some places and abandoning others. In those stories, the first people were not creators but learners. They spent generations doing nothing but tracing trickles with their hands, laying pebbles to see how flows would change, sleeping in the driest spots and moving when seepage found them. Only when they had thoroughly mapped the basin’s temper did they dare to set the first solid stones.

Another story, told more softly, suggests that the basin itself is a wound in the earth, and that the water is the earth’s own slow blood, leaking upward rather than downward. In this imagining, the city’s people are like scab-weavers, guiding the blood so that it nourishes rather than rots. The moral in such tales is always the same: meddle too forcefully, and you tear the wound open. Interfere too little, and it festers.

These ideas do not translate into grand rituals. Their rites are daily and work-shaped. The first drip gathered in a new carved channel is touched with the fingertips and then to the eyelids of the ones who cut it, closing the circuit of seeing and changing. The last trickle of a dry season, when a marginal seep fails altogether, is watched until it stops; someone whispers a simple farewell, and the channel it once fed is allowed to silt up. Births are marked by the shared carrying of water from edge to center, deaths by the reverse.

At the city’s margin, where careful channeling stops and the world returns to its own devices, they maintain markers not to keep others out but to remind themselves where their responsibility ends. A stack of three stones is not a ward against invasion; it is an assertion: beyond this, let the land be. They will cross it to hunt or to trade, but they do not bring their obsessive carving there. The drier, harsher world outside may have its own people, its own agreements with different forces. Basin-dwellers are reluctant to export their logic.

Visitors from beyond the ridges exist, but not many.

Those who come are drawn by the reliable water, by trade in materials the basin-people have learned to coax into usefulness: certain resins, rare mosses, the carefully quarried stone blocks that are so well-fitted they almost need no mortar. In exchange, outsiders bring things the basin cannot easily provide: bulkier wood, hides from larger animals, occasionally metals scavenged from far places.

These visitors find the city disorienting. Paths feel too close, too layered; the constant sense of being watched by nothing in particular unsettles them. They are asked, politely but firmly, to keep away from certain lanes and from the central courts entirely unless accompanied. It is not that basin-dwellers fear them; they fear uncomprehending interference. There is a story, still told with tightened mouths, about an outsider child who once, in innocent play, leaped across a channel that “never bears feet,” and how, weeks later, a structural wall in the same line bowed outward and had to be dismantled and rebuilt.

Despite this, people from the basin do sometimes leave.

A few go outward as envoys, carrying their water-sense into other terrains. They are valued as well-diggers, as finders of underground channels, as consultants when a settlement elsewhere wants to harness a spring without ruining it. Basin-dwellers who leave tend to under-speak what they know in such contexts, concerned that their fine-scaled understanding might be misapplied on a coarser stage.

Stranger journeys are undertaken by those who feel constrained by the basin’s limits. Here, there is no horizon to chase; you can walk its breadth daily. Some, especially the young who have not yet fully braided their lives into the city’s quiet rhythms, are drawn beyond the ridges by curiosity about unbounded landscapes, about animals whose voices carry more than once across a valley, about fire that can be great and bright without fear of drying something essential. They may or may not return. When they do, they bring back stories of rain that falls so hard it gouges ground, of places where water is something you fight away from rather than coax toward you. Such tales are listened to with interest but also with a slight inward flinch.

Animals here are few, but even those few do not escape their notice.

The pale lizards that live in wall-cracks are not pets, but they are known. Children give them names, adults give them nicknames based on habits: the one that always sits on a certain step, the one that appears only in the driest spells. There is a belief—half superstition, half empirical observation—that the lizards know first when a channel is beginning to fail. If one begins to frequent a place it has never visited before, someone will check the nearby stone, run their fingers along it to see if a new dampness has appeared.

The long-legged things that vanish into fissures are tolerated as long as they do not chew on structural materials. If they cross a string of hanging bone-discs and set them clattering in the night, someone will take it as a sign that perhaps that boundary needs to be reset, the string moved, the path reconsidered. The environment’s small creatures are treated as incidental sensors; the city listens even to them.

Insects are so rare that their presence is always notable. A fly is swatted primarily because it suggests some patch of rotting matter has been overlooked and left too long. A small swarm of midges over a particular corner may cause an inspection of stagnant water there, a clearing of silt from a blocked cut.

People here dream, like anywhere.

Their dream-lives are suffused with their environment’s forms: narrow passages and overhangs, sudden wells of depth where light does not reach. Many speak of dreams in which they walk along channels that are not in the waking city, tracing flows that run uphill, or pass through rooms from one’s childhood into courts that have never existed. These dreams are not treated as augury but as another way the mind replays and recombines the basin’s possibilities. Sometimes a dream of a new path for water leads someone, the next day, to notice a real-world fissure they had previously overlooked.

They do not have writing in the sense of a script. The nearest thing is the combined system of hollows, carved tablets hung near pools, and the positions of small, movable markers: pebbles, bone rods, cords. These together serve as memory-extensions. A group wanting to plan a new structure might smooth a patch of damp ground and press small stones into it in the pattern of channels and courts, adjusting them until everyone is satisfied with the hypothetical flow. Afterwards the plan is not “recorded” so much as rehearsed: one person walks it, aloud, late in the day; another corrects them if they omit a step. The basin is not big enough for such forgettings to be catastrophic, but their culture is oriented around not taking that risk.

Disputes exist, of course.

Two households may both want to extend their platforms over the same narrow lane. A builder may believe a wall can stand with slightly more damp than a line-watcher is comfortable with. These conflicts are resolved not by appeals to abstract justice but by reference to the shared understanding of the basin’s behavior. Someone will say, “Do you remember the southern quarter before the raising of the third-level walk?” and everyone will conjure the image of that time—the smells, the dark, the saturation. That memory, shared, becomes the argument.

Rarely, someone violates a deep norm: steps into a “water-only” path in panic as a child slips, disturbs a central pool in anger, carves a personal mark into a sacred wall. The community’s response is restrained but heavy. There is no public beating, no exile, but a temporary withdrawal: people will not step into the spaces that person has marked, will not handle objects they have used for water-work. It is as if a small piece of the city has been declared uncertain and is therefore avoided until it can be observed, tested, trusted again. Over time, through restorative action—meticulous repair, humble labor in the most tedious channel-cleaning—the violator can re-enter the flow of mutual confidence.

At a glance, all of this might make them seem joyless. They are not.

Their joy is small-scale, braided into the same attentiveness that guides their work. There is delight when a long-nurtured moss patch takes and spreads along the underside of a particular stone, softening a once-bare angle. There is laughter, surprisingly fierce when it erupts, over someone’s misjudged step into a shallow overflow that splashes their legs, soaking their careful wrappings, provided no true harm is done.

Festivity, when water is abundant but not overwhelming, might be as simple as an evening where several households gather on a roof platform, bring their small basins, and, by lamplight, float bits of reed and bark on the surface, nudging them into patterns. Children are allowed to cause ripples on purpose. Songs—not many, but some—are sung; their melodies tend to be narrow and repeating, like the shape of the lanes, meant to be carried softly rather than projected. The refrains often imitate in sound the city’s own: drip motifs, sibilant phrases that slide like trickles in stone.

In old age, when knees no longer favor climbing and backs protest the hauling of anything heavier than a child, people do not retire so much as reorient. They become keepers of sequences: the ones who remember which hollow was carved for which purpose, which boundary-line has shifted and why, which small stone near which pool ledge stands for which long-ago alteration. They advise, they sit at pool edges, they correct young voices when they mis-name a seep or misremember a story about the basin’s past.

When they die, their bodies are not buried in the wet ground. That would disturb too much and invite structural trouble. Nor are they burned in great pyres—the city cannot spare the fuel, and smoke would stain surfaces they prefer left clean. Instead, simple platforms are used at the drier margins, under shallow overhangs that face away from the basin. The dead desiccate quietly there, protected from scavengers by woven mats and stone screens. Over time, what remains is bone, which may be collected.

Some bones become tools, if the person has given consent in life. Others are carved with very simple markings and placed, unobtrusively, near hollows or on ledges at favorite watching-spots. It is not that the dead then influence flows. It is that, in a place where writing is shallow and memory is deep, the continued physical presence of a person’s durable parts anchors their remembered counsel. “This was the stone-keeper’s place,” someone might say, touching a carved femur on a ledge; “she warned us once about chasing a seep too far into brittle rock. Remember that now.”

So who lives in this city?

People who have chosen, over generations, to bind themselves to a small, low-lying world and to make of that binding not a prison but a practice. They are neither saints of restraint nor sorcerers of water; they are patient technicians of environment, their intelligence turned not toward conquest but toward an ongoing, delicate tuning.

They move with an awareness of how every footfall echoes, of how every diverted trickle finds some other path whether invited or not. Their tools are simple, but the patterns in their minds are intricate. They rarely look up for long; the sky is reliable, an ordinary dome of light and darkness. What matters to them is what happens where stone, water, and intention meet—the small shifts at the edges, the slow transformations that, over many seasons, either sustain their basin or unravel it.

They live close together because the land allows nothing else, and over time they have learned to make that closeness gentle rather than suffocating. Privacy is more a matter of attention than of walls: one can be alone in thought even in a narrow lane if others have the courtesy not to look, not to listen too closely. In a place where there is literally nowhere to be by yourself, they have perfected the art of internal distance.

They are, in the end, listeners: to each other, to the drip and hiss and plink, to the slow, young stone of their basin as it shifts toward age. The city is their instrument and their home, and they are the patient musicians who keep its low, wet music from tipping into silence or roar.
