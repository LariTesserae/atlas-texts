They live in the interval between “almost” and “not yet.”

No one here is native in the way forests have natives. The cliff was young when they arrived; it is still young now. Whether they came down from orbit, across some older continent, or up from deep machines is an origin story they keep more in equations than in myth. What matters is that they chose this unfinished place, and then learned to match its speed.

Call them people; they are close enough.

Their bodies tell you what the cliff demands. Skin ranges from dark basalt to sun‑vitrified amber; not from ancestry alone, but from long, deliberate exposure. Pigments—natural and engineered—are stacked in their dermis like filters, tuned to reflect the most punishing wavelengths and bleed the rest gently away. Under the skin, fine-mesh capillary lattices act as heat exchangers; along the ribs and spine, shallow vascular “radiator” planes flush and cool with a strange, almost metallic sheen when they work hard.

Their lungs are unremarkable, but they’ve learned to move as if air were precious even when it’s not: slow, measured breathing, nothing wasted on sighs or gasps. Sweat is rare; they regulate more by conduction than evaporation, leaning into stone, pressing bare palms to handrails that are always a few degrees cooler than their bodies.

Eyes are narrow-set by habit, not necessarily by anatomy. They have a particular way of looking: not at surfaces, but along them, as if every plane might one day slip. Most wear lenses or visors when they step onto the exposed ledges—not to magnify, but to act as dynamic apertures, cutting the glare from glassy rock flashes that can blind half a district for an hour.

Hands are callused in unusual places. The cliff’s city is vertical, so every daily path involves ladders, runged shafts, handholds set in rock. Children’s games include climbing routes mapped onto safe walls, learning redundancy of grip: three points of contact, always, as instinctive as balance.

Their hair, when they have it, runs dark and straight, often wound close to the skull with mineral-fiber ties. Loose hair catches in fans and conduits; nothing here is designed around flowing adornment. Jewelry exists, but it tends toward inlays, subdermal chips of dull glass-ceramic, and thin, flexible rings that sit flush against the skin. Anything that snags is a hazard.

They smell faintly of heated mineral and the cleansing compounds they use instead of water baths: evaporative alcohols, fine dusting silicates. True immersion is rare, reserved for ritual or therapy in the condensation vaults. Most days, they clean by wiping down with treated fabrics that lift salt and the ultra-fine grit that pervades everything.

Their population is not large. It doesn’t need to be, because their machines are patient.

They live in clusters that resemble stacked villages more than packed megablocks. Each ledge-district or shaft segment is home to a few hundred at most, tied together by work cycles and maintenance obligations more than by blood. The social unit here is not the family or the firm, but the crew: ten to thirty people bound to a particular system—a water run, a slow engine, a geological array—over decades.

Crews are mixed in age. Childhood here is long in the sense of apprenticeship: by the time someone is fifteen or its equivalent, they have already touched most of the tasks they will ever do, in miniature. Very little is left unseen. You might find a twelve-year-old carefully logging the microfracture ping patterns in a heat sink, not because anyone expects them to interpret the data, but because this is how you learn the base rhythm of the cliff.

Elders are kept, treasured, slightly feared. These are not people spared from labor; they are those whose work now rarely involves their hands. They carry, in their posture, a long familiarity with the cliff’s mood. When a corridor creaks or a distant roar comes from far below, others will glance—first at their instruments, then at the oldest person in the room. If the elder’s shoulders do not tense, if they continue with their slow tea ritual or their quiet line of code, people let their own pulses slow. If an elder frowns, someone goes to check the anchor readouts.

Their languages bear the marks of their intimacy with slow process.

There is a robust technical tongue whose script resembles its cables: looping, knotted, dense. It names things not just by what they are, but by the gradient they inhabit: this is a “two-degree-per-meter drop conduit,” that a “threshold-proximal fracture.” Verbs emphasize ongoingness: stones don’t “crack”; they “are in the act of becoming fractures.” Machines are seldom “on” or “off,” but “still turning,” “held,” “drifting,” “winding toward.”

Alongside that sits a more colloquial speech, polyglot and worn smooth. It borrows terms from older, elsewhere languages for feelings the cliff-world did not invent: grief, hunger, lust, humor. Even there, time is the dominant metaphor. An impatient person is “fast-hot.” A reliable friend is “steady gradient.” A coward is “sheer-face”—all show, no ledge to stand on.

Written language lives where people live: on corridor walls, instrument housings, the undersides of handrails. Not graffiti exactly, but annotations layered over time. A panel might bear its factory gloss, then, faintly, the scribed record of every major maintenance event, and in a different hand altogether a proverb: “Rock remembers the shape of pressure.” No one scrubs these off; they are another kind of slow data.

Their religion, where it exists, is less a set of gods than an attitude toward inevitability.

Some speak of the “Long Cooling” not just as an astrophysical fact but as a kind of diffuse, impersonal destiny: all heat will drift, all gradients will eventually flatten, all cliffs will slump into plains. To live here, riding the early phase of that slope, is considered both a privilege and an obligation. They are the custodians of a brief, extravagant tension between hot and cold, solid and forming, chance and shape.

Rituals reflect this. There are quiet gatherings at predicted rockfall times: not to witness—the actual collapses are often out of sight—but to acknowledge that something that held has now let go. People stand in a shaft, or on a secure balcony, and wait for the dull, distant roar to roll up from below. When it comes, they bow their heads as if at a funeral, or raise cups in a toast—practices differ—but they all fall silent for that slow exhalation.

Births are not hidden, but they are not dramatic public spectacles. The city knows better than to pretend it can override biology’s own slowness. There are dedicated, warm, damp rooms carved close to internal water runs, lined in softer synthetic materials. New parents and their close circle tend to stay in those sheltered cores for the first weeks, both to protect the infant from the harsh dryness of the ledges and to let the family’s rhythms align themselves gently with the city’s cycles.

Deaths are recorded with precision. The dead themselves are almost never buried; there is nowhere to bury them that does not eventually move. Instead, bodies are returned to process: cremated in low-oxygen furnaces whose waste heat feeds nearby systems, or very occasionally offered to experimental bioreactors that attempt to coax some analogue of decomposition in this mineral world. The person’s name, crew affiliation, and key works are etched onto a plate of fused glass-ceramic and set at a chosen anchor point. Anchor shrines accumulate like pearl clusters on structurally significant beams.

They tell stories, but their epics are geotechnical.

Children grow up on tales of early anchor failures and near-collapses, dramatized as sagas: “The Year the Western Ledge Slid a Hand-Width,” “The Collapse of the False Pillar.” Heroes are the ones who read the cliff correctly, who refused a tempting building plan because they sensed a nascent fault; martyrs are those who stayed too long to secure an imploding shaft so that others could evacuate.

There are also older myths, sometimes half-mocking, about whoever or whatever seeded the first anomalies: the glass moss, the etched patterns, the crawlers. In some versions, these are the work of precursor engineers who learned to write in gradients instead of letters and left messages in the crystal arrays. In others, the cliff itself is framed as a kind of mind still forming dendrites, and the city merely an early, invasive thought.

They do not worship the glass moss, but they treat it with a wary courtesy.

Technicians assigned to deep thermal systems know where it grows best: on stable, gently warmed planes with enough chemical noise to feed on. There are protocols: you don’t scrape moss-rich surfaces unless its growth is interfering with function; if you must remove it, you log the pattern first with high-resolution scanners, like archivists photographing a manuscript before restoration.

Some districts set aside “moss galleries”: unreachable panels of rock glimpsed through inspection ports, where glassy mats have been allowed to accumulate for decades. In the diffuse light, they look like frozen waves of cobweb, thickened and draped. A crew might pass by such a port and take a moment to stare, as if at art. Superstition says that if the moss in your assigned gallery fractures audibly while you are there, your next engineering gamble will go well.

The etched planes—those slow, self-wrought labyrinths in stone—have a more serious aura.

Structural planners map them with a half-scientific reverence. No critical load-bearing anchor is sunk through a marked plane without triple sign-off. Residents, who are less concerned with failure modes and more with the feeling of their homes, will pay extra allocation for rooms that look out onto an etched surface. They swear the rooms “sleep calmer.” When two people choose to bind themselves as a family or formal crew-pair, it is popular to exchange small, polished fragments of etched stone, mounted in metal frames, as tokens.

As for the crawlers, they are simply neighbors.

You see them in the corner of your eye: a coin-sized, many-legged blister sliding along a pipe seam, spines shimmering in the light of a maintenance lamp. Children learn quickly not to poke at them; the spines are delicate and can embed annoyingly in skin. It is considered poor form to kill a crawler unless you have caught it physically damaging something—and they rarely do, beyond the monitored consumption of glass moss.

Some residents keep informal “tally walls” near certain conduits, marking each crawler sighted with a tiny symbol. Over years, the density of these marks becomes another kind of data: which systems are moss-rich; which are drying out. A rich-scored wall is lucky. A blank one is a reason to check your water films.

They have pets, of a sort. Not many animals can survive here, but a few have been coaxed into symbiosis.

One is a genetically tweaked line of the condensation-vault floaters: kept small, maybe the size of a fist, their sacs reinforced, their metabolisms slowed. People hang them in living quarters near vents. They drift lazily, flattening and blooming with minute changes in humidity, their internal tissues glowing faintly when air becomes too dry. Children name them; engineers calibrate hygrometers against them. When one dies, it collapses into a glossy, heat-cured sheet that some families keep, pressed like a leaf, on their walls.

Another is less clearly alive: a “keeper line” of semi-autonomous inspection drones, its design so ancient no one admits to understanding every part. These are the tarnished-metal, many-winged beings that flit across chasms. Officially they are tools. Unofficially, districts adopt them when they start to frequent particular routes, giving them nicknames, painting discreet bands of color on their segmented bodies. Sometimes, without central instruction, local software will learn to anticipate their circuits and dim corridor lights just before they pass, sparing them from collision with dazzled birds that no longer exist.

Their economy runs not on money in the usual sense, but on all the things that matter in a world where heat is capital and velocity is danger.

There are tokens, yes—physical markers, lum-coded chips in personal bands—that represent allocations: of maintenance priority, access to certain luxuries (wider sleeping alcoves, private observation slits, an extra share of vault-time in the cool mist). But the deep currency is forecast accuracy. Crews and individuals who consistently predict how a system will respond—who call, months ahead, the subtle shift in a fracture’s ping pattern, the slight drop-off in a condensation rate—earn leverage.

They can argue for rerouting a conduit, for leaving a panel unbuilt so the etched patterns may continue. They can appeal to the Council of Arrays, a rotating assembly of senior engineers, geomancers, and data-modelers, and be heard.

The Council itself is less a ruling class than a nervous system. It sits mostly in the deep cores, among the latticed rods and annular chambers, watching a staggering number of slow processes inch along. Its members do not dress differently; you recognize them by the way they walk, distracted, fingers twitching as if sliding through invisible data. Every decision they make must be justified, not only in present energy balances but in a dozen projected centuries.

They are not always right. When they are not, the city’s response is slow, deliberate. Blame here is treated less as a moral stain than as a calibration error. Records are adjusted. Models are updated. People shift work-crews, but there are few purges and no tolerance for vendettas; one cannot afford hot feuds in a cool, patient place.

Arts here grow in the spaces between maintenance and experiment.

Music exists, but it must fight the city’s constant hum. Instruments tend toward percussive, resonant, or intimately soft. On some ledges you’ll hear vast, slow drums: giant tensioned membranes stretched across hollowed rock, struck maybe once a minute in careful sequences. The echoes travel along shafts and passages, modulating in ways that trained ears can read as a three-dimensional map of interior volumes. It is half performance, half sonar.

Closer to the condensation vaults, where sound is muffled by heavy air, you’ll find people with narrow, glass-ceramic flutes playing microtonal melodies that rise and fall almost too slowly to follow. Listeners lean against warm walls, feeling the vibrations in bone more than hearing them.

Visual art finds its medium in scraps and fractures. Some carve shallow reliefs around anchor points that have proven themselves over decades, stylizing the spread of stress lines into patterns of branching rivers or abstract, nested triangles that echo crystal growth. Others use thin films of reactive minerals painted on interior panels; as temperature cycles over months, the films change color along predictable lines, so the painting slowly writes itself.

A popular public art form involves “drift threads”: filaments of reflective composite hung in tall interior shafts. These respond minutely to air currents generated by machinery and heat exchange. From a distance, you see only a hazy column; up close, the subtle, synchronized trembling of threads becomes a visible signature of the invisible flows that keep the city alive. Sitting on a grating, watching them, is a recognized cure for mild anxiety.

Education is lifelong and intimate with place.

No one learns in separate institutions and then “enters” work. Children accompany adults along maintenance runs from an early age, tethered with safety harnesses on their first exposed walks. They learn to read both instruments and stone: to tell the difference, by fingertip, between a harmless surface craze and a living, propagating crack. They’re shown glass moss samples under microscopes, taught to see the repeating nodes as text or code, even if no one knows how to read it.

Formal instruction happens in “ring rooms”—circular chambers with concentric benches and a floor-display capable of projecting slow-change, high-resolution models of the cliff. In these rooms, history and engineering are the same subject. A lesson about a past anchor failure is also a lesson in the evolution of a fracture, the politics of energy allocation, and the ethics of risk.

Beyond childhood, people specialize, but not in the siloed way of some other worlds. A water engineer must know enough geology to trust or distrust a new shaft; a cultural mediator needs enough physical insight to understand why a proposed festival involving synchronized ledge lighting might destabilize the careful low-light regime that keeps nocturnal maintenance creatures on schedule.

There are dissenters. A world cannot be this controlled without generating those who chafe.

A small but persistent fraction of residents argue that the city has become too cautious, that all this deference to the cliff’s “intentions” is a story people tell to mask their own fear of change. They look outward at the smooth plateaus and untrammeled basins and see not emptiness but possibility: solar arrays, open-sky farms using engineered thermotroph mats as pseudo-plants, flying habitats that ride the thermal columns like seabirds.

Some of these dissenters leave, in small expeditions. They take slow gliders or powered dirigibles and leap from the lower ledges, sailing out over the haze. Many return with data, crystals, recordings of the strange refraction patterns above brine pools. A few do not return at all. The city does not frame them as lost souls; they are logged as “unclosed trajectories,” their last signals folded into the ever-growing set of maps of what the world almost is.

Others subvert from within, hacking guidance codes for maintenance drones so they spend more time in external spaces, or arguing in Council sessions for more aggressive “nudging” of geological nucleation thresholds—steering not just the cliff’s eventual cracks, but the emergence of something like a biosphere written in gradient-feeding structures.

Between the extremes, most people simply live.

They wake in alcoves set into the warm rock, some with narrow slits opening onto the dizzying drop, others entirely interior, lit by the faint blue of instrumentation. They walk to work along grated corridors where their footfalls thrum softly, or they ride the slow lifts—platforms that crawl up and down rails at a speed you could match with a leisurely climb, chosen for their reliability over any thrill.

They spend hours tending machines whose cycles are too long to witness end-to-end. A shift might consist of checking alignment on a turbine that completes a revolution every eight hours, tightening a bolt that moves visible fractions of a millimeter per season, logging temperature gradients that change less over a week than a human body does in a minute.

They share meals in canteens carved into the cliff, where the air is a touch cooler from proximity to certain heat sinks. Food is dense, mineral-balanced, often grown in carefully controlled bioreactors that mimic niches the outside world does not yet offer: vat-protein layered with microstructured starches, occasional treats of native microbial cultures coaxed into edible mats—white, firm, salty as brine crystals.

They talk, not loudly, about work, about rumors from the distant plain, about who has requested reassignment to a different ledge, about a newly mapped patch of etched plane that seems to mirror, eerily, data from a thermal array miles away. They gossip about a Council member who allegedly spends too much time staring at the high atmosphere through a filtered port, watching for the faint, slow motions that might or might not be heat-beings in the sky.

Love, when it happens, moves on the same timescale as their machines. It is rare and unhurried. People tend to pair or cluster within crews, bound as much by shared trust in each other’s judgment under geotechnical stress as by any more volatile chemistry. Confessions are not blurted on windy balconies; they are slipped into maintenance logs as jokes, spoken in the low-lit space behind an access panel, or encoded in the choice of what view-slit to cede to whom.

On days when rain comes—when the city generates its own small, shy weather—many who can spare the time drift outward to watch. They stand beneath overhangs, feeling the sudden thickness of the air as steam plumes rise, smelling the faint, clean tang of water molecules temporarily more numerous than dust. Some hold out their hands, knowing the drops will be gone before they strike skin, but enjoying the illusion of coolness in that brief exchange.

At night, the city’s people sleep to the sound of deep engines and slow fans. Or they stay awake, on watch or by choice, leaning on railings that separate them from depths they cannot see. Far away, on another ledge, a few moving points of light mark other wakeful figures. Between them all, invisible and vast, the cliff continues its own becoming, shifting pressures, cooling, thickening its veins of mineral, considering where to crack next.

Those who live here have given up on the fantasy of a stable world. They inhabit instead the grace of constant, deliberate almost-change: machines that are always turning but never fast, rock that is always settling but never quite settled, lives that are short against the cliff’s future but long enough to nudge its story by a fraction of a millimeter.

Whatever this planet is going to be, they intend to have fingerprints—however tiny, however debatable—inside its stone.
