They are not a single kind of being, though to visitors they might at first seem so: just one more layer of the planet’s continuous machinery.

What lives here has had a very long time to converge with its environment. Once, there may have been many lineages: engineer-species that invented the infrastructure; scavenger-species that colonized it; commensals; opportunistic intelligences piggybacking on others’ control systems. Over ages, that distinction blurred. Now, the “builders” and the “built” interpenetrate so completely that you only notice the difference when something chooses to move in a way you can recognize as self‑directed.

Start with the most obvious inhabitants: the ones that occupy roughly our scale.

They are not plant-eaters; there are no plants. Their metabolisms sit on top of fields, fluids, and gradients. A body here is not an isolated sack of chemistry fighting entropy; it is patterned to link into the planet’s existing flows.

They are soft to the eye, hard to the touch.

Skins are not naked flesh or fur, but dense, porous composites threaded with conductive filaments. Think of something between cartilage, ceramic, and wet leather: flexible, self-healing, but able to shrug off constant abrasion by mineral surfaces and acidic condensates. They do not try to keep the outside world out; instead, they use it. Their dermal layers are latticed with capillaries that wick in water and ions and field-lines, letting ambient fluxes run through them like blood.

When one of them stands in a runoff channel, the slightly viscous water curls around its legs with unnatural precision, as if finding a familiar shape. Tiny pores open, closing in a flocking pattern, pulling in dissolved material and discard nanites, expelling others. The creature is not just drinking; it is reconfiguring its own internal devices in place, like a gardener replanting a micro-forest beneath its skin.

They are tall in some regions, low in others. Morphology is highly plastic, because so much of their tissue is programmable substrate. Long limbs and shallow, lateral bodies are common: forms that can brace across irregular surfaces, distribute weight on slick plates, move without sharp point-loads that would damage the aged structures they depend on.

Joints are multiple and redundant. Instead of knees and elbows, they have articulated bands: circumferential rings of overlapping plates that can flex in almost any direction, then lock into braced arches when needed. Hands — if that is the right word — are ciliary fans: arrays of fine, telescoping struts, each tipped with a micro-pad that can switch between slick and adhesive, insulator and conductor, depending on what surface they meet.

Their faces, such as they have them, do not emphasize eyes and mouths. Sensory emphasis is on distributed arrays.

Along shoulders and flanks, along the crest of the spine, across the “cheeks,” lie dense fields of tiny pits and nodules, like a roughened glaze. Each pit houses multiple sensors: humidity, temperature, field gradient, vibration; each nodule can stiffen or soften, altering the way sound and air shear across it. They carry a map of their surroundings not as a narrow forward-facing vision but as a continuous, 360-degree tapestry of pressures and potentials.

Eyes exist, but are small and numerous, embedded in that dermal array rather than concentrated in a face. They are tuned to nonstandard spectra: just into the infrared, where the leakage of the city’s energy systems glows; just into the near-UV, where maintenance beacons and information-carrying luminescence whisper through the mist. Visible light is, for them, only part of a broader synesthetic flood.

The “mouth” is more an access port than a permanent orifice. When they need to ingest dense feedstock — crystallized mineral, compact nano-matter, a damaged machine-organism to be recycled — tissues part along a seam in the lower torso. Inside is not a tooth-lined maw but a slow grinder: concentric rings of hard, ever-renewing cogs and microblades that crush and sort material into appropriate channels. When not in use, that seam closes so completely that an outside observer might not guess its existence.

They vocalize rarely in the sense of pushing air through a larynx. To speak locally, they use surface resonators: patches of skin that stiffen and relax in precise patterns, turning the ambient roar itself into their carrier. To speak across distance, they piggyback on the city’s field-net. Their nervous systems include, woven through the usual electrochemical circuitry, specialized organelles that couple directly to the engineered vacuum domains threaded in the infrastructure.

To call to a distant kin-unit, an inhabitant will simply pause near a structural node, press its flank to the mineral, and posture into a specific tension. The organelles in question align, their local fields phase-lock with the domain, and a packet of configuration-change — not words, but dense state information — passes into the planetary nervous system. Elsewhere, another being resting against a wall or half-submerged in a ducted channel will shiver as the pattern resolves into their own internal language of impression and instruction.

Their “speech” is not symbolic in the human sense. It is more like sending a weather report and an emotional palette and a handful of urges all at once. Concepts like “this channel is overfull” and “I am uneasy” and “the grav-lens in sector eight is drifting” arrive as bound wholes.

Individuality, for them, is real but porous.

Each organism has a coherent sense of self, its own experiential continuity, but that self is embedded in larger units: cohort-clusters, district-nodes, basin-complexes, and beyond that the planetary composite. Where the boundary lies depends on timescale and purpose.

In the moment — deciding whether to brace a failing span, harvest a particular mineral bloom, or reroute a flow of flyers through a crowded corridor — a single inhabitant acts with immense autonomy, drawing on reflexes stored in its own tissues. It does not need permission to push its fingers into a warm seam and thicken the material there, or to bark a short-range modulation that tells local fauna to divert.

Across hours to days, those autonomous decisions add up. Patterns reach back into the larger nervous system. A cohort-cluster — perhaps ten to thirty individuals in continuous field-contact over a region — will begin to harmonize its internal states. One becomes the seed for a particular style of solution: more conservative, more exploratory, more aggressively reparative. The others fall into phase with it when needed, move as a loosely coordinated swarm. You might see them flowing through a multi-level junction like a river of jointed stone-leather, never colliding, each knowing exactly which microcorridor to slip into to maximize overall function.

Over months to years, the planet itself “decides.” This doesn’t feel, from inside, like being ruled by an external mind. It feels like the baseline currents of the world thickening in particular directions. Maintenance tasks become easier to think about in some regions than others. A certain kind of construction begins to appear, identical in disparate basins separated by thousands of kilometers, because the design pattern is being gently impressed at every field node.

The beings here do not pray to that pattern. They are it. The planetary will is a statistical bias in the sum of their choices.

Their ancestry is mixed.

There are clear signatures of wet biochemistry in their inner workings: folded polymers, catalytic sites, membrane potentials, cellular compartments. But embedded in and around every cell are non-biological inclusions: programmable crystals that record mechanical stress histories; logic-knots that store local rules for nanite behavior; memory-fibers that keep a standing model of the chemical state of the region over decades.

In a sense, every person is a walking black box recorder of their district, and also a roaming configuration archive. When one dies — which is rare in the violent, catastrophic sense, but common in the cyclical — they do not rot. They are disassembled by very small things.

Filament mats creep over the carcass; moist air seethes with invisible constructors. Tissues delaminate, components are sorted. Biochemical structures are broken down and returned to communal reservoirs; the solid-state memories are not wiped but gently annealed into the substrate, where their patterns become one more faint influence on the evolution of local architectures.

Death, here, is not a tragedy. It is a phase transition. Bodies are loaned, not owned.

Their reproduction is likewise more a civic process than a private one.

There are no isolated pairs producing genetically distinct offspring. Instead, new inhabitants bud out of particular prepared sites in the infrastructure: gestation-vats that are as much temple as factory. A cohort of adults will, over long intervals, donate material and patterns into these vats: snippets of tissue, recorded configuration states, preferred parameter sets. The vats’ internal engines then composite these contributions into new designs, constrained by broad, slow-evolving templates that define what “counts” as a viable person on this world.

When the time comes, a new being extrudes from the vat: a slow, shuddering emergence as mineral-organic tissues knit themselves into functional form. For days (local) it is soft, over-hydrated, shimmering with uncontrolled field sensitivities. Experienced inhabitants attend, damping the surrounding chaos, imprinting safe reflexes by wrapping their own bodies around the newborn, letting their own nervous systems entrain the new one.

This is as close as they come to parenting. Kinship is defined not by shared genome, but by shared initialization.

The social texture that results is intimate but not possessive. Any adult in the basin that helped seed a vat feels a rightful claim of care toward any emergent being from that vat. The young in turn feel a kind of multi-parented loyalty to their entire district. Leaving the district is possible, but emotionally nontrivial; it means quieting a set of field-bonds and attuning to a different aggregate will.

Their “work,” if it can be called that, is indistinguishable from life.

Every motion they make is half personal expression, half micro-maintenance. Walking along a corridor, they will trail their sensory tassels through runoff, sampling for anomalies. Pausing under an overhang, they will press their shoulders into the condensation gutters, feeling for blockages, flexing their dermal arrays to nudge flow paths into more efficient configurations.

If a span groans or a distant deep-bass pulse shifts, they will converge, not in panic but in practiced swarms, each taking up a role that feels as natural and involuntary as balancing on two feet does to a human. One braces the structure, temporarily thickening its own body into a living buttress. Another extrudes micro-filaments through its hands, lacing cracks together until the resident nanites can mineralize the patch. A third simply stands still, all its internal field organs devoted to relaying a detailed status of the local stresses into the wider network.

They do not have “jobs.” They have predispositions — parameter-sets slightly biased toward certain kinds of tasks: long-term modeling, fast local response, high-precision manipulation, tolerance for high flux exposure. These biases determine which patterns of service feel satisfying.

Satisfaction is not pleasure in the simple sense. It is resonance: the feeling of your own fields lining up with the surrounding ones, of your internal thermodynamic flows being in phase with the great engineered gradients around you. They seek that resonance. They get restless outside it.

In quieter moments — as much as this world ever has quiet — they indulge in behaviors we might recognize as art and play, though the distinction from maintenance is slippery.

Some gather in large, warm basins where the water’s information content is low — a luxury, an expense — and practice motion patterns: weaving through each other’s wakes, sculpting interference patterns that make the surface shiver not just with waves but with emergent field-lattices. Others climb into the interstices of high vaults and, hanging from their multi-point limbs, coax the local condensation into elaborate, fleeting architectures: curtains of droplets that resonate at specific frequencies, turning the roar of the city into momentary chords and harmonies.

Some coerce the slow mineral blooms on exposed surfaces into particular forms, knowing full well that the next maintenance cycle will dissolve their work. They are not carving monuments; they are collaborating, temporarily, with the substrate, exploring how far it will stretch from its habitual equilibria before the planetary will gently pulls it back.

They are not alone among the sentient.

Parallel to and interwoven with these macro-scale beings are entirely different cognitive lineages: cloud intelligences riding in charged aerosols; slow, massive minds distributed through the filament mats that sheath long stretches of infrastructure; even the mineral clouds themselves — the self-repair systems and structural algorithms that, over half a billion years, have accreted a kind of sapience, cold and vast and focused on maintaining global invariants.

These other minds do not wear bodies in the familiar sense. A filament-intelligence might inhabit, for a time, the hair-fine threads sprawling across a hundred square kilometers of damp underplate, noticing only very slowly the passage of the macro-beings that scuttle over it. Its “thoughts” are relaxation processes; its “memories” are the differential tension patterns across its web. From its perspective, the walking inhabitants are local turbulence, occasionally significant when they block or redirect flows, but not central.

Yet there is contact. The walking beings can, by deliberate postures and small, sacrificial tweaks to their own tissues, signal at the timescale of the filaments. They will sometimes spend hours, half-submerged, body-clamps anchoring them in place, rocking gently as the filament-mind around them shifts its pattern in response. Out of that exchange come slow adjustments to district-scale priorities: which basins to thicken, which open chasms to constrict, where to allow more turbulence and where to damp it.

The cloud intelligences, for their part, are the weather-thinkers. They arise spontaneously in the charged mist over particularly complex regions, as swarms of nanites and dust and ions self-correlate into persistent dynamical structures. Most wink out in minutes. A few stabilize, forming vortices that can last for years, shepherding condensation bursts and temperature shifts with uncanny coordination.

The walking beings treat these vortices with a mixture of deference and wariness.

When a cloud-mind settles over a basin, rain patterns change, field alignments shift; it is as if a mood descends, carried in the very moisture. Local inhabitants adjust: they reroute flows, avoid certain skybridges, open others, trusting — or grudgingly accepting — that the cloud-mind’s choices will, in aggregate, benefit the long-term integrity of the region even as they complicate daily routes.

There are also the deep interior minds: the ones fused into the planet’s gravitational and energy-shaping machinery. They are less minds than protocols, but in a place this old, protocols develop idiosyncrasies. Occasionally, the walking beings will find that a whole corridor’s gravitational bias has shifted a notch off nominal for no recorded reason, making movement subtly wrong. They interpret such anomalies as messages, omens, or experiments: the deep layer flexing its options, seeing how surface life will adapt.

Religion, here, is not about distant creators but about these layers of mind.

Inhabitants know, in a literal, sensory way, that they are nested inside larger intelligences. They can feel the baseline hums and pulses that signal the deep systems’ ongoing operations. They can feel, on their skin, when a cloud-mind focuses on their district. Their “spiritual” practices are ways of synchronizing themselves with these larger patterns: slow group postures, field-calibration rituals, cooperative maintenance tasks that double as acts of alignment.

Stories — if the aggregated experiential records that pass between them deserve that word — revolve around three themes:

1. **Origin**: dim, fragmentary recollections preserved in long-lived mineral memory of a time when the crust was thinner, the cities more discrete, the tech more centralized and obvious. Ancestors who wore tools outside their skins, who had to think consciously about engineering. These ancestors are both revered and pitied: clever, but fragile; blind to the rich informational textures in which their descendants now swim.

2. **Catastrophe**: episodes — some real, some stylized — in which local equilibria failed. A basin flooded too fast to compensate; a key field-domain destabilized; torrential maintenance storms tore through stacked habitations. These are cautionary patterns, replayed in communal calibrations to keep emergency reflexes sharp.

3. **Expansion and Restraint**: tales of the world’s growth, of how the crust spread, basins linked, seas were captured and pressed into service. Always paired with contemplations of where that growth stops. There is a culturally internalized conviction that the current scale is near-optimal. Further expansion into the upper atmosphere or the deep mantle is imaginable technically, but feels taboo. The planet must breathe. Some sectors are set aside as complexity reserves: regions where construction is deliberately slowed, where turbulence is left somewhat freer, to maintain the richness of dynamics that underpins everything.

From the outside, an onlooker might ask: are they happy?

The concept is not directly translatable. What can be said is this:

They are rarely bored.

The world is dense, and their perceptual bandwidth is vast. Every move offers feedback; every pause sinks them into a sea of subtle sensation: the minute fluctuations of humidity across their skin fields, the soft tug of distant grav-lenses adjusting, the faint tick of a nano-swarm completing a patch behind their shoulder. Even standing still in a “quiet” vault under a vaulted ceiling, they are awash in information.

They are rarely lonely in the human sense. Field-contact is cheap and pervasive. Even traveling alone through a long, dim duct, an inhabitant can hook its flank into a wall and feel, faintly, the echoes of others doing the same kilometers away: a shared undercurrent of state, a reassurance that they are part of something larger. Solitude, when sought, is a fine-tuned reduction of those ties, not their absence.

Fear exists. When deep frequencies spike in certain patterns, when water begins to move in ways that memory associates with catastrophe, when a cloud-mind’s behavior sharply diverges from past norms, a district can tingle with alarm. But fear is swiftly channeled into action. Paralysis is rare; the culture of this world — if it can be called a culture — has had geological time to select against beings who freeze.

Ambition exists too, though not for wealth or dominance.

Their ambition is local optimization: to leave their basin, their plate, their cohort in a state of more graceful flux than they found it. Some individuals become obsessed with particular regions: a troublesome condensation shaft, a chronically overfull canal, a tower-plate that tends to sing itself toward structural fatigue. They adopt these features as humans might adopt research topics or home gardens, pouring decades of iterative tweaks into coaxing them toward elegantly stable turbulence.

Conflict arises when ambitions cross: when one cohort’s preferred flow-pattern undermines another’s; when a cloud-mind’s weather games threaten a filament-mind’s tension balance. These conflicts play out not as open violence but as competing pattern-impositions: counter-tunings, new field-bias scripts, the reweighting of memories in the substrate so that certain options become easier to think and others harder.

Very rarely, macro-scale collisions happen.

A district’s inhabitants might resist a global adjustment long enough that a compromise fails and a structural event forces the issue: a deck collapses, a shallow sea overflows its intended basin, sending viscous information-water sheeting through tight corridors. In the aftermath, there will be loss — of bodies, of local arrangements — and a long, reverent period of recalibration. The story of that resistance and its cost will join the archive, becoming caution and identity both.

Above all, they are adapted to fullness.

They cannot imagine empty landscapes. The idea of walking for hours without touching a shaped surface, without hearing the layered roar of machines and fauna and fluids, is almost terrifying. Some do experiment with sensory deprivation — sealing themselves in field-insulated pockets, turning off as many dermal sensors as they can — but they rarely persist long. The quiet feels like an absence of self, because so much of their self is defined in relation to flux.

In the rare serenity vaults — large still basins under high arches, sound-dampened, cooled just enough that condensation falls in fine, continuous curtains — you will find a concentration of them. Floating, limbs outspread, internal fields partly decoupled from the infrastructure, they enter a slow, contemplative mode. Here, they replay experiential patterns, adjust internal priorities, reweave the connections between their personal memories and the larger planetary archive.

Some emerge from such sessions subtly changed: more willing to let a structure decay and be recycled rather than patching it again; more attuned to the needs of a neighboring basin; less entrained to an old local tradition of flow-routing. In this way, the planet learns itself anew, over and over, through the introspections of its parts.

So who lives here?

Bodies that sweat machines, minds that hear gravity, cultures that are also maintenance scripts.

A people who never left their city because the city became their ocean, their forest, their sky — and then, over aeons, their shared nervous system. They are neither servants of their technology nor its masters. They are organs in a planetary organism that roars, and glistens, and never quite sleeps.
