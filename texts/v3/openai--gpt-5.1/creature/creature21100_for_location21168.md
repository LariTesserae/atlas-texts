They are high people, first of all.

Not “high” in the sense of nobility or altitude as a badge, but in the way bodies become when they have known only thinned air and long falls at the edge of vision. Their lungs are deep, ribs wide and slightly flared. Their faces tend toward angles—cheekbones prominent, jaws narrow—not from some storybook austerity but from a lifetime of breathing sharp, cold dryness and walking up more often than across.

Their skin is weathered, but not the leather of a desert; it is the grain of something kept perpetually damp and brushed by wind. Even the fairer among them have a faintly sallow undertone, as if the minerals in the seep-water have written themselves into flesh. The darker have a muted luster, like wet basalt after rain. On many of them, fine traceries of pale lines—mineral-scar tattoos, accidental or deliberate—run along forearms and shins where skin has healed over cuts repeatedly washed by slow, hard water.

They are neither tall nor short as a people, but elongated in posture: backs trained to the idea that behind them is usually a drop. Knees flex by habit, as if every surface might tilt. Even at rest they rarely sprawl. There is a careful economy to the way they place hands, feet, tools, baskets—no casual throwing-down, no heedless dropping. Everything up here has far to fall, and they have learned to imagine the long arc of anything loosed to gravity.

Their clothing makes almost no sound. They layer it, the way their city is layered. Against the chill: undergarments of woven reed-fiber softened with repeated soaking and beating, worn close to the skin. Over that, garments made from the few animals that tolerate this height—felted hair from the long-legged pack-beasts, loosened bark-sheets treated with plant oils until they hang like thick, quiet fabric. In the wettest work they shrug into capes stitched from overlapping, cured leaves of the tall trees, their narrow lengths shingled like fish scales. Everything is muted in color: grays echoing the stone, deep moss-greens, the brown of wet bark. Ornament is color pulled from elsewhere—an ochre slash, a bead of glinting stone.

They bind their feet carefully. Soles are layered—thin, pliant leather or toughened bark closest to skin, then an outer layer textured to catch on slick rock: pads scored with crosshatch patterns, or embedded with pebbles, or rimmed with cords they can replace as they fray. A barefoot foot here is not bravado but preparation for something that needs direct contact.

Their hair is almost always tied up or bound away, not out of vanity but because loose strands catch in rope, in reed, in split stone. You see crowns wrapped in cloth strips that have taken on the same dark gloss as the mosses, or braided cords running with tiny pieces of polished shell or pale vitrified fragments from the central basin. Many shave the sides of their heads, leaving a ridge or crest that can be wrapped, both to cool and to fit beneath hoods in colder gusts.

They are not numerous. If you stand in a high doorway and count moving bodies, you might see a few dozen in an hour, and half of those at distance, crossing a higher ledge or disappearing into mist. Their city feels underpopulated because it is deliberately so: there is an unspoken sense that the rock will only support so many at this stage of its becoming. To live here is to accept being surrounded by more unpeopled space than company.

They do not clump. Even when they gather they leave gaps between bodies, lines of sight open to the drop-edges, to the sheen of water, to the sky. Children are the exceptions: they run in short, elastic bursts between sheltered platforms, their laughter startling in the layered quiet, but even they are taught a certain wariness. Every child learns to touch the stone with the side of the foot first; every game has an invisible perimeter defined less by walls and more by agreed-upon cracks and ledges.

If you watch how they move, you see two distinct modes: the grounded and the suspended.

On the ground—meaning any surface of stone—their step is low, gliding. They keep their center of mass close to the mountain, rarely lifting their feet high. When they bend it is at hips and knees together, still balanced, one hand almost always lightly on rock or wood. They seem to feel micro-textures through their soles, reading minute dampness or the start of a slump in the way a sailor reads swell and wind.

Suspended, on rope bridges or the woven platforms that hang off terraces, they become looser. They allow the structure to sway them. Their knees become shock absorbers, their arms part of the correction. They trust the tension of cord and the give of wood the way lowlanders trust straight ground. Children here can run across a rope bridge as casually as a field child sprints across grass.

They speak softly, as you would expect in a place that echoes—but not timidly. Their language is full of breath-sounds, exhalations that blur the line between word and sigh. Consonants are clipped, not swallowed exactly but rounded, so that words don’t ricochet off stone. There is a technical register, used when they speak of water and stone, rich in layered metaphor: cracks are “paths that have not yet chosen themselves,” seeps are “mouths,” new bulges in the rock are “sleeping shoulders.” Children learn early to distinguish between “wet that stays” and “wet that travels,” between “stone that listens” and “stone that refuses.”

They seldom shout for each other by name across distances. Instead they use small carried sounds: clacking two pieces of reed, ringing a hanging loop of stone, pulling a stretched rope to send a braided hum through a shared frame. There is a system to these vibrations, a crude but intimate network of non-visual calling. A triply-twined rope humming at one pitch means “all well”; another, plucked twice, means “bring a container”; a third, tied to a certain wall, might signal “close the shutters, wind rising from the north.”

If there is writing, it is reluctant. They do not seem to have an eager urge to fix words in marks. Instead they inscribe processes. The flat stones etched with lines and spirals you see are not texts as you would know them; they are memory-diagrams of flows and shifts. A series of spiral arms indicates how a seep’s output has varied across seasons, each arm a year, each notch a month. Straight incising lines, closer or farther apart, note terrace creep. These stones are consulted by the few who maintain them—water-keepers and terrace-tenders—and read aloud, turned by hand, their fingertip following old grooves as someone recites what has changed since the last spiral was extended.

They have no clocks, but they have gauges. At certain seeps, they plant thin carved rods, each etched with a ladder of marks. The height of a wet sheen upon such a rod tells them something about underground fullness; the rate at which bead-lines form along it hints how the deeper veins are adjusting. They weigh time in drops, not in shadows. Still, they know the arc of the day by light and wind: morning’s drafts licking uphill, afternoon’s shoulder-gusts along the ridge, the brief glassy calm before night winds slide down from still higher, colder realms.

Their work is almost all maintenance and coaxing. You will not see them founding new great monuments here; you will see them thickening edges, widening a spill-mouth by a thumb’s breadth, carrying baskets of darkened soil from under the trees to new stone hollows whose moss is ready to catch it. They keep a close, collective watch on any place where the young mountain shows restlessness: a bulging wall, a new crack of strange angle, a seep that suddenly runs with a sour smell. When that happens there is no panic, only a quiet convergence of skilled hands, small tools, and watching eyes.

They are not, in the sense you might recognize, builders. They are midwives to the rock.

Their relationship to the central basin shows this most clearly. That whitened, vitrified shell that lines it is not quite natural. If you look closely, you see where they have helped: where fire was laid again and again against damp stone to harden its skin, where thin runnels have been deliberately smoothed so that film-water spreads in even sheets. The basin is both instrument and altar.

At certain days of the year—days they track by the angle of sunlight on a particular wall, or by the appearance of a certain fleeting cloud-formation over a notch in the distant peaks—the people gather around that shallow bowl. They do not crowd its edge; they sit at a respectful distance on stone ledges and timber beams, looking not at each other but at the shifting water-skin. A few of the older ones move closer, place a palm flat to the vitrified surface where it rises a hand-span above the wetted area. They close their eyes and feel the faint, chill tremors as water seeps upward and outward.

This is not worship of a god in the normal sense. If you listen to the murmurings, you do not hear supplication. You hear comparison: “The pull is softer than three winters past.” “The left side breathes slower now the trees have thickened.” They are taking the mountain’s pulse as one might a patient’s, as one checks the beating of a heart that is not yet in its final rhythm.

The basin, in their stories, is not something they found finished. There’s a tale, told in low voices when the wind is loud and there’s no risk of the words being lost to the drops’ percussion, of the first arrival.

Those earliest people were not born here. They came up, slowly and confusedly, from some lower, older land where stone behaved as everyone expects stone to behave. Down there, the earth was settled, carved by rivers that knew their paths. But something had changed—perhaps heat deepened, perhaps new rock shouldered in from below, perhaps water began to run wrong in the old valleys. A handful of families followed the strange water upward, tracing anomalous seeps, and found this high place where rock was raw and sweating and unsure of itself.

They did not conquer it; they nested in it. They widened existing hollows just enough to crawl into. They watched how beads formed on stone edges and copied that with their own carved lips. Over generations they learned a crucial humility: that here, carving great scars, digging deep wells, forcing canals would not bring security. It would break something still knitting.

So the first law that the old tell the young is not carved but spoken: You may ask the mountain to do what it already intends. You may not teach it tricks.

This law underpins everything. They terrace only where the rock has already begun to slump or step. They wait for moss to claim a hollow before deepening it into a pocket of true soil. They will widen a seep’s mouth that already shows a hairline crack in that direction, but they will not cut a new one where nothing urges outward from within. It is not superstition; it is a patient alignment with a system that has its own rhythm.

They divide their work and roles along lines drawn by attention rather than by birth. There are the Stone-Touchers: those with uncanny feel in hands and feet for where rock is softening, where strain gathers. Many of them work in pairs, one above, one below, crawling along the undersides of terraces supported by rope harnesses, listening with their bodies for ominous changes. Then there are the Water-Keepers, who chart the seeps and dribbles, maintain the shaped mouths in the rock, decide which to encourage and which to let fade so that the mountain’s inner veins do not over-drain.

Others are Tree-Stewards, guardians of the tall ash-barked pillars. They know which boughs can be taken without unbalancing the canopy, which root-clusters are silently shoring up an unstable shelf. They spend long hours high in the green vaults, adjusting leaf-roofs so that drip-patterns below remain within a narrow band: too much water and the soil-pockets will slump; too little and the moss that binds them will die. From below, you see them as fleeting shadows among the layered leaves, occasionally calling down a soft instruction or dropping a thin line for someone to tie off.

Everyone shares in food-work, because food here is tenuous. They plant nothing in neat rows; everywhere that soil gathers, life inevitably appears, and they have simply learned to shepherd it. Low, glossy foliage is trimmed, not razed. They harvest leaves rather than uproot entire plants whenever possible. The reedlike plants around the water hollows serve as both material and crop: their segmented stems hold sweet, starchy pith that can be scraped out, dried, and ground. The moss-thick areas under the trees, left undisturbed for long, nurture edible fungi. These are cut in careful rings, never in full circles, always leaving a path for spores to travel.

Animal husbandry, as you suspected, is minimal, but not absent. The long-legged pack-animals—they call them, in their tongue, something like “ledge-walkers”—are at once companions and tools. Each is marked not by brands but by small bells hung from neck straps, the bells themselves rarely rung. Instead, the bell’s metal composition and weight characterize the animal’s usual routes and habits: a certain high, thin tone for those trusted on the slick upper ledges, a deeper, softer one for those that carry loads along lower, broader paths. When a ledge-walker returns alone, its bell tells where it has wandered, and thus where to look for a missing handler or shifted stone.

There are also smaller, almost domesticated creatures: glossy-furred quadrupeds that live half-wild among the moss couches. The people do not cage them, but they leave offerings: dishes of fermented reed pulp, safe dry alcoves. In return—or so the story goes—the creatures eat algae, gnats, and larval forms that might otherwise damage the fine equilibrium of wet surfaces. Children are cautioned not to overfeed them; they must remain hungry enough to do their unseen work.

Spiritually—if that word applies—they are both pragmatic and deeply odd.

They do not look up much in wonder at the high sky. For them, the sky is simply the other side of where they stand: they are already so close to it that stars and sun feel like neighbors rather than unreachable. Their awe is directed downward and inward, into rock and water.

They do not people the mountain with deities. Instead, they speak of “moods.” The mountain is in a drawing mood when new seeps appear in many places at once; in a sealing mood when old dripping mouths crust over with pale mineral skins. A good year is when moods change slowly and predictably; a dangerous one is when the mountain seems restless, switching from exuding to tightening within the span of a season. They have ritual songs, but these songs are less petitions than careful descriptions of previous mood-cycles, sung so that the patterns sink into communal memory.

On certain nights when mist lies thick and sound becomes even stranger, they bring out instruments that are half architecture, half tool. Long hollow reeds strung between two facing rock walls like immense, horizontal harps. When plucked, they vibrate in sympathy with the tension of rope-braced platforms, with the narrow throats of tuned water-falls. A few skilled players sit with eyes closed, listening to how the notes take or fail to take in the stone. To them, resonance is conversation. If a note that always thrummed into the rock now falls flat, it means something has changed in the mountain’s cavities. The players remember these subtleties and report them to the Stone-Touchers.

Thus their “music” is also survey, aural cartography of the unseen.

They dream, as peoples do, but their dreams are often of flows, not of figures. A child may wake speaking softly of a staircase that has become a wet slope; of a favorite hollow that has grown teeth of stone. The elders do not interpret these as oracles of fate in the abstract. They quietly add them to their awareness of ongoing, real shifts. In a young landscape, even a dream may be a useful early warning of a sound, a tremor, some unconscious noticing that the waking mind missed.

Their myths do speak of people, but of people elsewhere. They keep a faint, wistful narrative of the lower lands: of forests that stand thickly with trunks so close you cannot pass sideways between them; of rivers too broad to leap that roar with unbroken water; of soil deep enough that you can set a seed anywhere and expect, within a handful of seasons, a tree. In their stories, these old lands are falling asleep, settling into a final shape, losing the brilliant instability that defines the plateau. Some elders speak of a necessary exchange: as this high place wakes, the low must doze; energy in stone is conserved over ages. They do not fear this; they mark it as balance.

They do not often descend. A few must, to trade reed spices and vitrified shards and their dense, mineral-rich moss-bundles for metal or salt or tales. Those who go down return changed: their lungs hurt for a while in the thick, soft air; their sense of danger is confused by wide ground that never drops away. Some never fully settle again, pacing the high terraces restlessly until they take up tasks that send them outward along the ridges, mapping new seeps and gullies in places where the mountain is even younger and harsher than at the plateau-city itself.

These “ridge-walkers” are a liminal caste. They return with news of cracks opening days’ journeys away, of newborn basins that weep chaotic sheets of water rather than thin films. A few breathless, half-unbelieved tales speak of zones where stone does not yet know how solid to be: patches that can be pressed by a hand and will, given an hour, forget the dent and rise flat again. The ridge-walkers’ function is to watch the growing edges, to know when and where this high community might, in some future generation, extend itself; or, more ominously, when the growth elsewhere might radiate strains that reach this settled nub of the mountain.

In all of this, notice what is missing: they have no kings, no councils that rule from some carved hall. There are gatherings, yes, and elder circles and task-assigners, but authority flows like water here: pooling around those whose attention to a given matter has been deepest and longest, then flowing elsewhere when the matter shifts. A Stone-Toucher may be obeyed absolutely when she says “Do not build here; the rock is thinking of letting go,” yet have no voice in how many baskets of reed-pith go to which family. A Water-Keeper can decree the closing of a seep-mouth and expect compliance, but may have little say in the telling of histories.

As for conflict, it exists but is damped. In a place where stone can kill many at once if disturbed badly, feuds that might lead to reckless actions are socially suffocated. The primary punishment is disconnection: to be denied access to the rope-sound network, to have the tinkling bell that marks your family’s baskets taken away, is to be deafened and blinded in a city built on echoes and calls. It is a rare and heavy censure, rarely lasting long; people reconcile not out of sentimentality but because isolation is dangerous.

Children are taught the city like a second body. They learn the “bones”—the main supporting shelves of rock, the pillars under terraces. They learn the “veins”—the seeps and dribbles, which ones are constant, which seasonal. They learn the “skin”—moss beds, soil pockets, tree aprons. Lessons are embodied: a child will walk barefoot behind an elder, stepping where they step, pausing where they pause, then be asked what they felt. Was that stone warm or cool? Did it vibrate faintly? Was the moss root-feel shallow or deep? Only once a child can sense, not just see, the plateau’s moods are they permitted to venture alone across its riskier spans.

Their sense of time stretches, as yours might, but in a different axis. They do not think much in terms of years beyond the next handful; they think in “skins” and “fillings.” A terrace edge might be expected to slump a hand’s-width in four fillings of the central basin’s vitrified rim with new mineral glaze. A tree’s canopy will be ready to feed a new shade-planting only after three full skins of leaf-fall have piled around its trunk to a certain depth. Their stories jump easily across half-centuries when recounting the slow progress of a particular crack from harmless hairline to dangerous fault. An old person here is not one who has seen many festivals, but one who remembers the stone when that ledge was not yet there, when that seep ran stronger, when the central bowl’s skin was one layer thinner.

And beneath everything—beneath their daily carrying of baskets and lashing of poles, their tasting of water for slight sourness, their humming songs that test how the rock receives sound—beneath all the visible care, there is a quiet, patient expectation.

They believe, without fuss and without knowing exactly how it will feel, that the mountain is growing them a future.

In their oldest tale, told only in snatches because it is too large for one sitting, there is an image: the plateau-city extended not outward but inward. Not tunnels hacked by impatient hands, but chambers and galleries that the stone itself will one day decide to hollow. Cool, resonant spaces where the current loudness will deepen into something like music. In those imagined caverns, water will run openly in clear threads, not shyly seep. Light will come not just from above but from shafts and cuts that the mountain itself has provided. In that time, say the old, their descendants will be able to build a city that truly touches the mountain’s heart without bruising it.

For now, they wait. They listen. They make small, precise adjustments. They learn which requests the stone will answer without harm.

They live, in other words, as guests at the birth of a world: not its rulers, not its victims, but its witnesses and its careful assistants. Their lives are thin lines of choice and habit scratched delicately across raw rock that is still, very slowly, deciding what to be.

It is not an easy way to live, nor is it dramatic. It is concentrated. Every breath tastes faintly of mineral and cloud; every footstep owes itself to decisions of stone and water; every human sound is braided into the ongoing, patient noise of a mountain still in the act of becoming.
