They live everywhere the world here can hold a shape: in films of water, in tuned cavities in the rock, in the edited gradients of heat and weight and sound. There is no single owner of this place, only a layered accumulation of lives that have spent a very long time learning how to inhabit a slightly modified reality.

Start small, because the small things are the oldest tenants.

Beneath the moss, in the sweating stone, there are microbiomes that never entirely decided whether they were wild or designed. Some are conventional enough—bacteria, archaea, filamentous fungi—except that their metabolisms have been nudged by those slow, spiraling pools and the nanoscopic machines within them. They do odd things with minerals. They can draw trace elements out of seemingly inert rock and assemble them into complex organo-metallic clusters, then hand those off—chemically, osmotically—to other organisms.

You find whole lineages of microbes that specialize in translating between realms: converting the patterns in those engineered fields (subtle fluctuations in local gravity, charge distributions, acoustic standing waves) into chemical signatures. They are, in their way, the nervous system of the plateau—diffuse, unconscious, but responsive. When a slope starts to saturate with water, microbial consortia sense the way sound changes in the pores of the rock and alter their biofilms, momentarily increasing friction. Landslides are not prevented, but coaxed into extremely slow-motion, generations-long slumps.

From these gradients rise the fungi. The visible ones—those that throw fruiting bodies into the perpetually damp air—look like crossbreeds between bracket fungi and miniature antennas. Many grow thin, translucent fins that always align to the same set of invisible vectors, like iron filings to a magnetic field that is not quite magnetic. Their spores carry photoreactive coatings and tiny packets of metal-inflected enzymes, gifts from the machines in the pools. When clouds of them billow at sunrise, they are not just seeding new logs and leaf litter; they are also sampling the structure of whatever fields the old technology is presently sustaining, feeding that back into the distributed chemistry of the soil.

In the soil and along the rock skin live countless tiny grazers tuned to water itself as terrain. The translucent arthropods you see at your feet have limbs that end in delicate, ciliated pads. They do not so much walk on stone as wade through the microscopic meniscus of moisture that covers it. Their bodies are prismatic, riddled with internal canals that both circulate fluids and guide light—sunlight from above, faint, colored glows from below. When they feed on lichen or fungal threads, they are as much harvesting information as nutrition; their gut flora pass some of those organo-metallic clusters downstream again, depositing them as gleaming, resistant grains in their droppings, subtly altering how that patch of soil will conduct heat and sound.

Slightly larger are the leaf- and stem-biters that produce the constant ticking in the undergrowth. Many are beetle-like, but with flexible carapaces broken into overlapping plates to accommodate swelling in the humid heat. Under the plates lie panels of specialized cuticle that resonate at specific frequencies favored by the plateau’s acoustic honeycombs. Their chewing is not random; it is modulated. Over evolutionary time, individuals whose feeding produced discordant, destabilizing noise found themselves less successful—predators heard them too easily, or plant defenses reacted more violently. The result now is an army of small mouths that, simply by doing what they do, keep their own sound tucked into the great, self-smoothing chorus.

Amphibious things thrive in the slow seeping world under the roots and along the plateau’s shallow channels. Some are recognizably frog-like, but often elongated and soft-bodied, with skin that can seal or open microscopic pores in response to shifts in local gravity-gradient. They launch themselves with a practiced sense of how long they will float before descent becomes inescapable. Over generations, they have mapped, through instinct, invisible “lighter” corridors where a leap carries farther for the same effort. Their breeding pools cluster in these paths, so that the fat, gelatinous eggs and later the tadpoles can drift just a little longer in suspension when storms send sudden sheeting flows across the rock.

Others have made their homes inside the rock itself. Think of pale, blind, wormlike creatures with toothy, keratinous raspers, chewing steady spirals into cliff faces. They are the visible half of a partnership with mineral-sensitive bacteria that coat their chambers. The worms eat lichen and the occasional drowned insect; the bacteria eat the infinitesimal powder of softened stone loosened by the rasping. In return, the microbes extrude films that stiffen and tune the cavities, adding each new chamber to the great acoustic organ beneath the plateau. These burrowers are archivists without intent, inscribing their slow life histories into the resonance of the landscape.

On the surface, the vegetation shelters its own invertebrate guilds. In the moss forests at ankle height live minute, multi-legged grazers with semi-gelatinous bodies that can stiffen along certain lines, allowing them to “lock” into the moss mat against sudden torrents of rain. They reproduce in sync with the plateau’s thermal cycles: their larvae go quiescent in tiny, water-filled cells in the moss when the heat peaks, then emerge en masse when cooler, denser fog swaddles the highlands. Their synchronized hatching contributes to pulses of biomass and sound that the acoustic substrata seem designed to absorb—a seasonal bass note of soft rustling.

Move upward, and the creatures change with the edges they occupy.

Along the cliff rims, the long-limbed climbers with wet-black skin are one of the plateau’s more obvious macrofauna. They are omnivores, patient and deliberate. Their bodies are rangy, with torsos built more like trunks than like the narrow waists of primates. Their limbs can flex in improbable directions; each joint carries a small pad of glandular tissue that exudes the mineral-reactive fluid they use to grip the rock. The chemistry of that secretion is an evolved answer to the plateau’s tech-altered geology: it forms transient, reversible bonds with certain organo-mineral complexes that the nanomachines have encouraged in cliff faces. On unmodified stone, these climbers are merely competent; on their home rock, they are uncanny.

They have thick, translucent lids under their main eyelids, smooth as contact lenses, sliding across the cornea to seal out spray and spores while still transmitting dim images. Their faces are expressive, dominated by nostrils that can open into wide slits to sample the layered scents the air carries. They almost never vocalize at high volume; instead, they use low, throat-deep notes that carry far in the tuned air without startling prey. Much of their social communication occurs by touch and by leaving transient chemical signatures on rock. Those iridescent handprints you see on certain ledges are not just residue—they are brief messages, changing color and reflectivity as they react with thin films of bioactive minerals, readable by others as maps of fruiting shrubs, safe paths, and the recent passage of predators.

They are tool-users in a minimal, context-bound way: a stick chosen to pry open a particularly tough bromeliad, a woven sling of vine to hold collected fruit while climbing. But the plateau’s old technologies are so seamlessly environmental that there is no sharp distinction between “using nature” and “using machines” for them. They know, without understanding, that certain overhangs lighten their jumps by a subtle amount, that calling in specific timbres elicits a faint humming from the filament clusters that seems to calm their young. Around some of the perfectly flat anomalous planes, these climbers habitually pause, flattening themselves to the surface while their young experiment with gravity—dropping leaves, leaping, feeling the slightly extended arc of their fall as a kind of play.

Where water and stone meet most intimately—in rivulets, seeps, and those delicate curtains of droplets that veil the cliffs—live ribbon-bodied fish analogues. Their bodies are thin and muscular, with flexible fin-frills down their sides that let them “swim” in water that is not always continuous: beads, films, splashes. They can cling to the underside of a rock ledge, half-embedded in flowing sheets, then release and dart into the air in a glistening arc, only to vanish into another sheet below. Their lateral lines are exquisitely tuned not just to pressure differences but to the way the old engineering causes droplets to hang a fraction too long in certain places. Juveniles born near anomalous planes learn to ride those slowed-fall zones downward, dispersing to lower terraces with far less risk of being dashed against bare rock.

In the groves atop the plateau, heavier-bodied herbivores and omnivores move like quiet weather systems. Some resemble massive, thick-furred tapirs, but with broad, splayed feet that distribute their weight over the soft, wet soil and dense mats of root. Their hides are host to complex micro-ecosystems: algae that tint them greenish, symbiotic fungi that confer resistance to skin rot, tiny arthropods that clean wounds. These beasts are slow, deliberate feeders, stripping wet bark, crunching fallen, ferment-soft fruits, occasionally rooting with prehensile upper lips to expose protein-rich invertebrates in the soil.

They are drawn—perhaps unconsciously, perhaps by ancestral memory—to certain ridges where the glass-like arches and terraces emerge from the rock. There, the air feels easier to breathe, the heat less oppressive. Under the arches, sound dampens slightly, and the great animals often stand in contemplative herds, chewing, swaying gently, their deep calls softening into near-silence. In their seasonal rounds, they help maintain the grove structure, breaking seedlings in some areas, fertilizing others, redistributing spores and seeds stuck to their fur. Through them, the engineered gradients and the biological cycles weave tightly together: where the old machines have made motion easier, the big grazers go, and where the big grazers go, the forest follows.

Above the canopy, the sky is crowded with mid-sized fliers that are not quite birds, not quite bats. Many have membranous wings stretched between elongated digits, but the membranes are thickened and pleated, capable of subtle deformations that engage the plateau’s acoustic fields. As they glide, they can “lean” on certain frequencies, turning calls into pressure differences that give them minute advantages in lift or steering. Their bones are laced with hollow tubes partially filled with a ferromagnetic gel, a quasi-biological feature that makes them sensitive to the invisible lines laid out by the old engineering—a kind of multi-sense where gravity, electric potential, and sound interplay.

Some are nectar- and pollen-feeders, with delicate, furred muzzles and tongues that unfurl to coil around tubular flowers. Their presence has guided the evolution of plants that bloom primarily during the cloud-lit periods of sunrise and sunset; flowers that open then glow slightly from within, their pigments excited not just by light but by the faint, field-induced luminescence in the air. The fliers, in turn, have developed plumage or membrane patterns that briefly fluoresce when passing through certain nodes in the plateau’s invisible infrastructure—a conversation of glows and scents between flower and pollinator, orchestrated across decades by technologies neither consciously know.

Among the more striking aerial inhabitants are the floating colony-creatures—the veils and sacs adrift in the thermals. Each is a composite: a central, gelatinous bag filled with light gases chemically separated from the air, wrapped in a mesh of tougher, fibrous tissue, studded with countless small organisms embedded like beads in a net. These symbionts include photosynthetic algae analogues in translucent patches, chemoautotrophic bacteria living in micro-chambers where the field gradients are strongest, and tiny stinging or grasping polyps that filter-feed on airborne plankton and spores.

The colony’s nervous system is distributed: waves of electrochemical activity ripple across the membrane in patterns modulated by local field conditions, by light, by the faint electrical and acoustic signatures of the plateau’s tech. What you see as internal flashes of light are both communication and orientation—a way of feeling larger-scale structures in the air. When one part of the colony encounters a rich seam of spores, it pulses a particular rhythm of glow; neighboring sectors adjust their cilia, steering the entire drifting entity toward or away.

These colonies are neither wholly animal nor plant, neither wholly engineered nor fully wild. There is evidence, written in their biochemistry, that their ancestors were once deliberately created to monitor and gently adjust atmospheric conditions above the plateaus: to seed clouds, to mediate charge buildup, to recycle certain aerosols. That purpose has blurred, but a ghost of it remains. During build-ups to storms, they congregate along particular bands of sky where invisible conductors in the rock below favor lightning paths. The colonies absorb a minute fraction of the charge, enough to drive rare internal processes—repair of deep structural damage, recombination of genetic material between colliding drifters—before releasing the rest along safer courses, down the glass arches and into the plateau’s quiet, glowing filaments.

Predators track all this choreography. Some are ambush hunters cloaked in dangling moss and epiphytes, their bodies flattened and mottled, waiting along established herbivore paths. Their senses have tuned to the acoustic and gravitational rhythms of those routes; they can tell, by a subtle shift in the drop-intervals of drip curtains or the way a distant call is refracted, that something large approaches around a bend. Their strikes are explosively quick, taking advantage of localized anomalies in inertia: muscles coiled and timed so that a leap spends its force precisely as a micro-gradient in gravity momentarily eases, extending reach by a crucial half-meter.

Others are pursuit predators of the edges—sleek, quadrupedal or hexapedal forms that race along plateau rims, dipping claws into emptiness, trusting the slightly forgiving physics. Their claws are laminated with mineral-rich keratin that interacts with the stone, finding extra purchase in those tech-influenced surfaces. Youngsters learn, often painfully, the fine difference between an ordinary edge and an anomalous one; stories of miscalculations are written in the scars of the survivors and in the stains far below.

In the cloud canyons between plateaus lurk the truly abyssal fliers: enormous, winged shapes that rarely, if ever, land. They are black or deep violet, absorbing most of the light that falls on them, warmed by it in the cold, high fog. Their size is made possible not by muscle alone but by the way their hollow bones and vast sails of skin anchor into the plateau’s long-wavelength fields. They feel the whole region as a textured pressure in their chests and skulls, climbing and diving not just through air but through the contours of slightly bent space. They feed on floating colonies, on swarms of sky plankton herded by thermal and field, on unfortunate surface-dwellers swept from the edges. The plateau does not see them often; their presence is marked instead by shadows moving above the fog, by sudden silences in the chorus as smaller creatures pause, listening for a rush of displaced air that may or may not come.

Around the most obvious remnants of construction—the rings of glassy slabs on knolls, the cool, dry chambers—the wildlife behaves differently. Some creatures avoid them: certain ungulates balk at crossing the threshold into the perfect circle, their hooves skidding on invisible gradients in friction. Others, particularly the long-limbed climbers and some of the more cognitively flexible fliers, seek them out. They have learned that inside those domes, heat is bearable on the hottest days, sound is gentler on storm-nights. They bring their young there, huddling in the dim, diagram-inscribed spaces, their bodies pressed to surfaces that hum faintly.

Over long spans, an emergent intelligence has grown at the boundaries between biology and tech, particularly in those low hums and distributed control systems. The nanoscopic machines in the pools are not many, and they are not fast, but they are persistent and networked through chemical and physical channels. They “listen” through changes in vibration, charge, flow, light. They do not think in any human sense, yet patterns of activity recur: stabilizing a slope when too many roots lose purchase at once; dampening lightning when a grove’s canopy is already shredded; encouraging, via mineral bias, the regrowth of certain root-webbing trees on eroding saddles.

The organisms have come to rely on this background shepherding. Seeds that land in machine-favored microbasins find just enough trace nutrients to outcompete neighbors; microbes that align their metabolism to the machines’ preferred reactions proliferate. Over time, this feedback loop has created something like a shared, unconscious intent: the plateau, as a whole, tends toward states that maintain its thick, wet, loud equilibrium.

If there are any descendants of the beings who originally laid down this subtle infrastructure, they are no longer “people” in the old, separate sense. Perhaps they have dissolved themselves into these systems, become long-lived strains of guiding microbes, or rare, nearly immobile organisms burrowed deep into the honeycomb, tasting the plateau as an inward sea of vibrations and fields. Or perhaps they are gone entirely, and only their edits to physics persist, interpreted by evolution as just another set of environmental constraints.

In any case, what lives here now is a community that includes the technology as much as the trees and animals. A moss tuft on a drip-sheen cliff is as much a user of the modified world as the long-limbed climber testing gravity with a playful hop, as the floating colony pulsing light in the charged air before a storm, as the blind worm etching a new resonance-chamber inch by inch.

They have no word for the difference between natural and artificial, between field and forest. For them, the plateau is one continuous body: hot, high, wet, humming—a place where stone, water, life, and old intention have long since grown together into a single, breathing system, and every inhabitant, from bacterium to drifting sky-veil, is a cell in that larger, slow-thinking organism.
