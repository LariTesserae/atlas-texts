They are people who have learned to live as if they were one more surface of the plateau—another place where water slows, settles, and changes shape without ever really leaving.

They are not many. From a high ledge, looking down at the town, you would count more cisterns than bodies in the open. When they move in numbers, it is in tens, not hundreds. Their settlement reads as the work of a modest population that has been here a long time without ever feeling pressured to multiply or to spread.

They are lightly built, as if the same economy that governs plants has shaped them. Limbs tend long rather than thick, with the wiry strength that comes from lifting stone and carrying water every day of a life. Their skin lives in that middle tone that makes less and less sense to name: sun has browned the exposed parts, but their underlying color sits somewhere the eye reads as “rock warmed by light,” more practical than striking. The even weather has spared them the deeply etched lines that harsher climates cut into faces; aging shows as a softening at the jaw, a slackening around the eyes, a slight stoop in the spine, but not as that carved, survival-hardened look you would see elsewhere.

Clothing is of their own making and looks it—no unnecessary ornament, but careful attention to function and to the simple pleasure of doing a thing well. Most wear knee-length tunics of woven reed-fiber, the cloth coarse enough to see the individual strands. The weave is tight, but you can feel the air through it. Over this, on cooler mornings and evenings, they hang mantle-like wraps, stiff at first but shaped by constant handling into something that lies easily over shoulder and back. The colors are the colors of the land: off-white where the fiber has been washed and bleached in sun; soft greys and yellow-browns from plant dyes coaxed out of leaves and lichen. Brightness exists, but in reserved, almost private ways: a border of deep moss-green along a hem; a single rust-red stripe across the back of a worker’s mantle where they have boiled some rare bark or lichen to exhaustion to pull that color out.

They go mostly sandaled or barefoot. Sandals are simple: soles of layered fiber or, more rarely, a tough bark, straps that loop over instep and around ankle. The undersides are polished by rock to a shine you could mistake for something engineered.

Hair is grown out to a length that can be tied, then controlled. Women and men alike gather it at the nape or crown, fixing it with woven rings or simple cords. In a place without insects, hair needs no defensive braiding; the styles are more about containment for work and the quiet satisfaction of order. You see loops bound flat against the skull, short plaits keeping strands from falling into eyes as they lean over channels or stone faces. Children’s hair goes looser, sun-bleached at the tips where it has been neglected or where elders have not yet bothered to demand neatness.

Eyes, when you come close enough to see them, have the habit of looking slightly past you as often as at you—not from disinterest, but from a lifetime of gauging distances across bare rock, of tracking the fall of water and the spread of moss. They are used to measuring space without much in it.

They are quiet in two ways: by training and by necessity. In the absence of the small, masking noises of animals and insects, every sharp human sound carries. A dropped stone tool ricochets in the valley like a crack. A shout feels instantly wrong, as if it might bruise the air. Generations have learned to modulate themselves accordingly.

Children are taught early to walk with the kind of unconscious care you’d expect from someone stalking game—except there is no game to stalk. Feet go down flat, testing surface before weight commits. Doors are made to close with a padded bump instead of a slam: the wood, where it is used, fitted with little cuffs of braided fiber or small cushions of dried moss. Laughter happens, but it rolls low and close to the chest, often half-stifled in a hand. By custom, the shrill register of voices belongs mostly to the very young and to ceremony.

Speech between adults tends toward the measured. Sentences are left to taper instead of ending on hard, emphatic consonants. Conversations in open spaces are usually brief and close. When longer talk is needed, people either stand near—a pair with foreheads nearly touching over a question of a channel’s path—or they withdraw under a roof, into the muffling enclosure of stone.

All this quiet has shaped how they think of one another. In other places, you might show attention with enthusiasm, with extra volume and movement. Here, attention shows as deliberate proximity. To sit down within arm’s reach is to say: I am fully with you now. To move with someone along a path, step-matched and sharing the same moderate pace, is as expressive as a hug elsewhere. Their gestures are small but exact: a hand resting for a few breaths on another’s forearm; a palm held briefly up in front of the chest, fingers spread, before being set to work again.

They are good with their hands, and they know it. Every person above the age of childhood has the competence of a craftsperson in at least one domain, and a working familiarity with several. Specialization exists, but it is shallow, more a matter of preference than of guild or caste. You might find one woman whose stone shaping draws others to consult her on difficult corners; a man whose fiber-thread is known for an evenness that makes good roofing mats; an elder whose eye for water’s behavior is treated with quiet deference when a new seep is found. But any adult can lift, place, and dress block; any can split a plant, twist a cord, repair a leak in a cistern’s wall.

Their tools are extensions of their hands rather than separate, complicated contrivances. A particular hammerstone, its edges battered just so, is known almost as a collaborator. People keep them close, tucked into simple belts or set in the same place every evening. The hafts of chisels glow with a dull sheen where fingers have polished them over years of use. Edges are maintained not to razor keenness—stone will not hold that—but to a reliable bite suited to this plateau’s rock.

Their relationship to water is intimate, almost tender. It is not that they worship it—there is little overt ritual in how they act—but they attend to it the way one attends to an elder relative there from the beginning: with patience, respect, and a firm understanding that you do not waste their strength.

Children grow up with strict rules about water, but without the harshness you’d expect from a culture of scarcity. Water is everywhere; what they fear is not running out but letting it run uselessly. From the time they can toddle, little ones are shown, again and again, that every drip has a path. Elders guide small hands under a seep, let them feel the cool beads form, then trace with a finger: here to a groove, here to a shallow, here to the town’s channels. “Follow it,” they say, in whatever language they call their own. They learn to see stray wetness—a leak, a mis-angled stone—not as trivial but as a small wrongness to be corrected.

This attentiveness has become an aesthetic. They take pride in “clean lines of flow.” A well-laid channel, its bed smoothed so that water runs evenly and does not catch and chatter, is praised in the same way a good carving might be elsewhere. People will stand and watch how the film of water slides over the stone lip of a spillway, judging how well the mason read the rock that day.

In the absence of animals, their sense of what life is has narrowed and deepened at once.

They do not have words for “wildlife” in the sense we would. They gesture instead to degrees of motion and awareness. There is “that-which-holds” (stone, worked or raw), “that-which-lives-by-water” (plants, moss, the thin ochre and grey films of microorganisms they notice only as changes of color or texture), and “those-who-choose” (themselves, human. Possibly, for some, water itself.) Within plants they distinguish finely: there are those that “drink standing” (shrubs with roots deep in cracks), those that “drink lying down” (mosses, lichens, low mats), and those that “carry drink” (fibrous reeds that can be cut and used for straws or pipes).

They have no stories of beasts. No myths of a great horned thing on the horizon, no cautionary tales of prowling jaws. Their nightmares, when they talk of them at all, are of absences: a seep that suddenly dries; a day when the sky remains blank and cloudless far beyond its usual rhythm; a stone that splits unexpectedly where it should have held. Children sometimes dream of falling from the clean edges of the plateau, not into teeth and shadows, but simply into a fathomless distance of air.

Yet they are not sterile, as a culture, nor are they joyless. In a world with so few variables, small variations acquire an outsized meaning. A bloom—a plant that, once in some span of years, throws up a stalk and opens a delicate, pale flower—is a community event. People will walk out to it and sit around it in a loose ring, saying little. Children are allowed to get closer; small fingers are permitted, for once, to touch the petals, to smell. Some will sketch the shape into wet soil or on stone with a bit of char, knowing it will be gone soon. The elders might frame the moment with a spoken piece that is part observation, part recitation: “This plant has taken in seven years of drizzle and thin sun, slowly, to make this one color. Remember.”

That is much of how their teaching works: through notice, through repetition, through drawing attention to the world’s few departures from its norm.

Their days cycle with little drama. Work begins soon after the mild dawn. There is no rush to beat a scorching midday or fend off freezing dark; the plateau’s kindness with temperature gives them a broad, forgiving band of time. They break their labor into long, unhurried sessions punctuated not by strict clocks but by bodily cues: a certain heaviness in the limbs meaning it is time to sit and drink; the angle of a shadow along a wall meaning the morning’s channel cleaning is enough for one day.

Tasks divide along lines of skill, preference, and strength more than along stark gender roles, though patterns exist. Those with the steadiest balance and surest feet—often younger adults—take on work at height: trimming overhangs, tending rooftop basins, cutting new paths along cliff faces when a seep is discovered. Those with the patience for repetitive, fine-handed tasks—often but not always women—do much of the fiber splitting, spinning, weaving. Heavy lifting and foundational stone work are done by groups organized more by friendship and kin than by any formal command. You might see three siblings setting blocks together, or a cluster of unrelated neighbors shaping a cistern wall because the person who needs it is aging and respected.

Children’s work is to learn—and to fetch. They carry messages, simple tools, small bundles of reed. They shadow adults, imitating with their own tiny set of wooden implements. They are given little plots at the edges of planted basins where they can experiment: arrange pebbles, dig shallow channels in mud, watch how water patterns itself around their efforts. If a child’s miniature watercourse works unusually well, an adult will nod, maybe copy some small trick into a real, permanent channel in the town. Credit is not recorded, but the child will remember, and so will the neighbors: “She has the water-sense,” they might say, years later, when it comes time to choose who will advise on a more serious redirection.

Social structure is as dispersed and interlinked as their architecture. There is no single central building that rules the others, no palace or hall of chiefs. Authority lies in networks of respect and demonstrated competence. When something must be decided that affects everyone—whether to tap a new seep, for instance, or to alter a main spillway—those known for their grasp of that element convene under the broadest roof in town: a low, wide rectangle whose walls are lined with benchlike shelves. People gather, sit, listen. Few speak. When someone does, it is because others have turned their faces toward them in a silent invitation.

Disagreement does happen, but it rarely flares. In a place where nothing comes from elsewhere—no new materials, no animals to trade, no strangers arriving with novelties—conflict has little food. Everyone depends on the same few flows of water, the same open rock. To behave in a way that risks those things is felt, immediately, as an absurdity. Arguments tend to be technical: where to cut a drainage notch, how deep to set a cistern. They are worked through with demonstration, with small mock-ups scratched into dust or pebbles moved around to show imagined courses of water. When consensus is reached, it is not sealed with oaths or signatures. It is sealed by bodies rising from the benches and going together to the place in question, taking up tools.

They have rituals, but they are so tightly braided into daily life that an outsider might not recognize them as such.

Morning might begin, in many houses, with a small act at a household basin: a person cupping water onto their face, then, with the same wet palms, smoothing their hands down the front edge of the threshold stone. It wets the place they step over. It is an acknowledgment of the line between in and out, between the private basin and the town’s paths. No one speaks while doing this. When you ask, much later, why, they might simply shrug and say, “It is what we do to begin.”

At the first tapping of a new seep, there is a gathering. Not elaborate—no costumes beyond everyday clothes cleaned and dried the day before, no drums, no procession. Those who come stand below the wet face, watching as the chosen stone-cutter levers out the last thin sliver of rock to let the water fall into a waiting depression. When that first new drip lands, people exhale, softly. Some will reach out and let it fall on upturned palms. An elder will say a few words that are half gratitude, half bookkeeping: “From there to here, now. It will go to your houses, and there,” naming directions, making a map in the air. Later, when the channel is properly walled and sealed, someone will return and set a small carved stone beside it, marked in simple lines: the year’s sign, their people’s equivalent of a date.

Death they handle simply, and very practically. Without scavengers or burrowing creatures, bodies do not vanish quickly on their own. Earth is thin, and they will not foul water with organic matter. So the dead are borne on simple litters to places just beyond the town’s usual paths, where the plateau drops away in sharp steps. There, shelves have been cut into the cliff faces: long, shallow recesses under overhangs where water drips most slowly. Bodies, wrapped tightly in woven cloth that has been used, patched, and used again over a life, are laid in those shelves. Stones are slid into place over them—not sealed tight, but enough to hide form and discourage casual gaze.

Over time, moisture seeps through, fibers decay, flesh returns to that thin, dark soil that grows behind the stones. Sometimes, in years of more zealous clearing, younger people might pull these recesses open, clean out the mixed remains, and pack the new soil into basins where nothing has yet wanted to grow. It is not considered a desecration. The dead have gone where all must: back into “that-which-lives-by-water.” Bones, if they are recognized, are set aside in neat stacks in other small hollows, white against the lichen. No one visits to speak their names there. The memory of a person is kept in the ongoing corrections of channels they once shaped, in the routine gestures they taught.

The absence of animals has left gaps in what they know—and they know those gaps exist, even if they cannot name their contents.

They are aware that plants do not move from their own will, and that they themselves do. They see water “choosing” certain paths, hesitating at lips of stone, gathering in hollows, and they use the same grammar—at least in casual speech—for water and for themselves. A child might say, “The water likes that way better,” and an adult will not correct them. They have never had to define their aliveness against that of another, active species. If pressed, they would probably say that what makes a person a person is their grasp of pattern: their ability to see that this seep feeds that pool, that this cut in stone will change that flow. Plants, in their view, have patterns but do not understand them. Water has an odd halfway status: it does not think, but it behaves with such consistency and subtlety that they treat it as one thinks of an old habit—sluggish to alter, but predictable once you know it.

They do not domesticate anything, because there is nothing to domesticate. Even plants are not “tamed” in the way we might imagine. They transplant cuttings from place to place, they frame basins so certain reeds thrive where they want them, but they do not pull living things entirely out of their original context. No orchard, no field. Instead there are coaxed patches. The word they use for such places might translate to “helped-growing” rather than “owned land.”

Food, in this world, is mostly plant and mostly uncomplicated. Edible parts are taken from shrubs that bear oily seeds or small, nutrient-dense fruits; from bulbs hidden deep in cracks; from thickened stems that can be peeled. They eat much of it raw or lightly softened in water warmed in sun or over a small, smokeless fire of dried plant matter. Cooking is gentle, more about making things chewable than transforming their flavors.

Where rock has cracked deeply and soil has very slowly accumulated, they have encouraged a few higher plants—small, hardy things that in wetter, fuller worlds we might call proto-trees. These do not tower or spread wide; they are perhaps twice the height of a person, with sparse leaves clustered toward the tips of firm, pale trunks. The townspeople prize them not for shade (the sun is kind enough) but for their ability to hold water in their tissues, their fibrous inner bark, and their rare gift of a slightly different texture of wood. That wood is not common. It appears as dowels holding stone plates on a roof, as the crosspiece of a frame where cloth dries. Many households have nothing made of it at all, relying instead on twisted and hardened plant fiber. When a proto-tree must be cut—because it is failing, or because its water is needed—it is done deliberately, with the same community presence as the first tapping of a seep.

Fire, in this place, is a tool sharply circumscribed. Without abundant fuel, blazes are small and focused. There is no constant hearth smoking up a house; warmth is not needed, and the smell of burning would linger too strongly in such a clear air. Fires are kindled in pits near the central wash, fed with dried reeds, gathered fallen branches from the rare higher plants, and the crumbled, woody remains of long-dead growth. They smoke only for as long as it takes to harden resin in baskets, to roast a batch of seeds, to char a tool handle so that it grips better in the hand. Ashes are not thrown away; they are mixed back into the thinnest soils to change how they hold moisture, to see if some patch of ground will yield a slightly different growth the following cycle.

There is no metallurgy. The stone of the plateau is not generous with easily spotted ores, and no one has yet had reason to look harder. In a world where all needs can be met with stone, water, fiber, and the occasional gifted shard of wood, the ambition that leads to smelting might simply never have arisen. Sparks from flint-like stones are known, used to start the rare fires. The idea of melting rock is as remote to them as the idea of a bird.

Their stories, told in low voice in the long level of evening, lean inward rather than outward. Without animal characters, without the drama of chase or hunt, they explore other axes: patience versus impatience; heedfulness versus carelessness; the difference a single stone’s thickness can make over the course of a year’s worth of dripping.

There is a frequently retold tale of a person—usually unnamed, but sometimes given a simple placeholder like “the One Who Rushed”—who, tired of the slow filling of cisterns, cut a channel too wide, too straight, to bring more water more quickly to their own basin. The surge scoured away fragile soil elsewhere, left patches of moss to dry and crisp. In some versions, a child has been carefully tending a small outcrop garden that is ruined by the diverted flow. The moral is never shouted; it does not need to be. By the end of the telling, the listeners know the story’s shape: the One Who Rushed is made to undo their work, stone by stone, in full view of everyone affected, and to watch, over seasons, as the harmed places recover—or not.

Myths of origin concern not beasts or gods, but time and level. One common story, murmured more than declaimed, speaks of a time when all ground was unsettled, tilting and sinking, and people had to cling to what stone they could. When, finally, a piece of land lifted and held—this plateau—those ancestors who stood upon it vowed to keep it in balance: not to weigh it too heavily with towers, nor to hollow it out beneath, nor to strip it bare. They imagine, in that story, that if they ever forget and tip the balance too far, the whole shoulder of the world might shrug, and they with it. That possibility is both absurd and serious to them, the way any cherished cosmology is.

They know that there is a beyond, because the plateau edges give a view to distances: hazed shapes of other highlands, faint suggestions of lower lands. But the drop is sheer, and there are no beasts to ride, no need to seek new grazing, no seasonal compulsion from migrating herds. The idea of going down is so alien that when it surfaces in tales, it takes on an otherworldly, almost mystical cast. Once in a long time, someone restless may walk the full length of the plateau, tracing its perimeter, peering over wherever the edges allow a cautious look. They return with little to report beyond different arrangements of rock and basin, different pale bands of vegetation clinging to distant cliffs. There is no practical pressure to throw ropes over and descend. Their world is big enough as an expanse of level stone.

Emotionally, this makes them self-contained. They have never had to define themselves against an Other that arrives on four legs or on two, with feathers or claws. Their boundaries are set by stone and water alone. Hospitality exists; when someone from a more distant cluster of dwellings comes, they are offered space on a terrace, food, a share of water. There is no concept of hostile stranger, but there is a subtle gradient of belonging: those whose footfalls have worn these particular paths, whose bodies know these specific echoes, are “of here.” Others are “near-here” or “like-us-elsewhere.”

Because nothing predates upon them, they lack certain reflexive fears. A child can wander, within reasonable distance of the town, without triggering adult panic. Losing sight of a toddler is cause for concern not because something may seize them, but because they may slip on a wet ledge or lose their way among lookalike outcrops. The fear here is of the impersonal, not the hungry. People teach the young to always stay where they can see at least one building or at least one marked stone. Tales of those who went too far and vanished are told, but even they are quiet: no screaming, no blood, just a person who walked and walked until the plateau’s sameness swallowed them.

In that calm, their inner lives have room to notice subtleties. They are good at small differences. A person who has watched one patch of moss for years will know when its shade deepens by a barely perceptible degree and will wonder: is there more water now? Less dust? Some change in the seep? They will test with fingertips, check nearby lichens for correlated shifts. They build mental maps of how thin films of moisture appear on different faces of rock at different times of day and year, and they share those maps in offhand comments the way others might talk about weather: “The north wall at the third bend darkened sooner this morning than last cycle.”

Music, in such a place, is used almost like dye: sparingly, to mark and color specific times, rather than as a constant background. The most common instrument is that single flute you imagined—a simple thing, holes drilled in a reed or a length of hollow bone from some once-living plant or root grown to stiffness and then dried. Its tone is clear but not piercing, spreading without distortion in the clean air.

The person who plays it—perhaps there are several in the town—does not perform long, intricate songs. They send out short sequences of notes, repeated, varied slightly each time, like the pattern of drops from an overhang. In the evening, as people sit on their thresholds or on the low walls of terraces, those notes thread through the near-silence and give it structure: this is the end of work, this is the hovering between day and night. Children learn to whistle those fragments before they learn any formal chant.

They do have chant, though, for certain collective tasks. When many hands lift together—a particularly massive stone slab, a nearly full cistern cover—someone might begin a simple call, just a rising and falling tone, and the others will take it up. They breathe with it, time their exertion to its swell. There are no drums; the weight of their own bodies thumping softly as they step or lower counts as enough percussion.

What they lack in diversity of sensory input from the world, they make up for in the layering of meaning onto what they do have. A particular pattern of lichen—white specks clustering in an arc—might, for them, echo the curve of a familiar cistern and so be jokingly named after it. A slightly darker band across the sky at dusk might remind them of the wrap an elder wears, and so they will say, one to another, “The sky has borrowed so-and-so’s mantle tonight.” Humor is quiet but present, built on recognition rather than surprise.

If you stay with them long enough, what initially feels like an eerie lack—no birdcall, no buzz, no scramble in the underbrush—recedes. You begin to hear the thickness of their chosen soundscape: the individual character of each drip, each patch of wind-brushed reed; the way a voice carries along one channel and not another; the microscopic rasp of fiber against stone as someone adjusts a rope. Their lives are tuned to these registers.

In that tuning, they have become, themselves, part of the plateau’s equilibrium. They are not an afterthought, not an intrusion into a pristine balance of plant and rock and water, but an additional layer of pattern. Their walls hold water as the stone’s own fractures do. Their terraces catch soil and allow new, thin skins of life to form. Their cisterns, once established, maintain small, stable microclimates that moss and reed have come to rely on. Were they to vanish suddenly, the plateau would not collapse, but it would change: water would resume the simpler paths it had before channels and basins, some plant patches would dwindle without the consistent, careful redirection, some would overgrow without harvest.

They live there as those who understand this mutual shaping. They are not guardians, not masters, not subjects, but inhabitants in the strictest sense: beings who dwell in a place, who let it mark them as they, in their slow, deliberate way, mark it.
