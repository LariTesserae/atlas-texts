They are mostly small, but not insignificant.

They live in the gradient between wet and metal, in the seams where things that were once cleanly human-made have become rough enough, porous enough, patient enough to host them. There is no central species that “owns” this place; instead, there is an assemblage, a guild of damp-adapted bodies and slow, distributed attentions that together fill it.

Start near the water and you meet the most obvious inhabitants: the ones with bodies.

In the pools and sumps and thin films slicking every channel, the oldest residents by lineage swim and drift. Blind fish, pale as wax, move by pressure and taste. Their bodies are narrow and soft-finned, more like animated ribbons than proper fish, sliding through the black shin-deep basins. Their mouths are fringed with tactile barbels that comb the water for bacterial veils and the faint rain of decayed matter. Their eyes are present only as pale, sunken scars—a cost long since abandoned because the currency here is not light.

Alongside them exist things that do not choose between animal and plant as categories. Long translucent worms undulate in the water column, their bodies laced with faint, inner motes—the symbiotic microbes that digest the dissolved metals they strain from the slow leaks of old pipes. When these worms surface, they make no splash; just a gentle breach and resealing of the skin of the pool, a soft punctuation mark in the otherwise glassy dark.

On the undersides of pipes and tanks, colonies of bulbous filter-creatures cling. They look at first like deposits of fat, as if the infrastructure had grown its own tumors. Closer, you would see that they are layered sacs of living tissue, milky and opalescent, each with a single small aperture that rhythmically opens and closes. They drink the air rather than the water, sifting it for spores, dust, and volatile compounds exhaled by other lives and by the aging plastics and insulations around them. They turn industrial outgassing into tissue; they metabolize corrosion.

In the shallows, at the edges where concrete emerges to meet the room’s thin, colorless reeds, legs become more numerous.

Here the water’s fringe hosts thin-legged crustaceans, jointed like bits of bent wire. They pick their way through biofilm and sediment with a kind of twitchy elegance. Their carapaces are translucent amber, streaked with subtle bands of iron oxide taken from the very rust they graze. When they stop moving, they look like little assemblages of scrap, as if someone left tiny diagrams of how to walk scattered at the base of columns.

Amphibious things haunt this border. There are long-bodied, limb-reduced salamanders, their skin a dull, unreflective grey that beads moisture. Their gills, once frilled and delicate, have withdrawn inward—here, oxygen is something you scrape from boundary layers, not something that lies free in the air. They sleep in crevices lined with wet moss-fur, and emerge to hunt by pressure changes: a droplet’s repeated fall into a pool, interrupted by the wake of a swimming worm, is as clear to them as a visual outline.

On the drier strips of floor and cramped islands of brick, the mice-like mammals work the terrain.

They are not truly mice, but the comparison will do. Their bodies are light and long, their bones delicate. They have grown longer whiskers than any surface rodent, each filament thickly innervated, each a sensor not just of touch but of faint vibrations conducted along rusted grates and pipes. Their ears are thin, nearly translucent fans that lie flat when they move quickly to avoid snagging on the rough world around them. Their eyes are poor and year by year grow poorer; vision here is a cost center, not an investment.

They navigate by texture and echo. Each pipe run and walkway has a signature: the spacing of its crossbars, the way condensation collects along its underside, the sharpness or rounding of every weld. They map the district against their skin. When one runs, it seems slightly blurred at the edges, fur slick with ambient damp—the fabric of them never quite dry. Their diets are omnivorous and opportunistic: they will gnaw on soft fungal fans, hunt the small blind arthropods that graze on biofilm, tear into the gelatinous colonies under the pipes when those sacs die and slump, their membranes breached.

Higher up, where walkways cross and cables dangle like overgrown ivy, the fliers rest and drift.

The glider-things—descendants of bats, birds, or some hybrid lineage of both—wear the space like a cloak. Their wings are membranes strung between elongated digits, but they rarely beat them hard. Instead they know every invisible current this chamber offers: the breathing exhale of a vent, the mild updraft from a bank of transformers, the gentle inward pull of a sump pit’s drawn-down air. They drop from beams, spread their fragile sails, and let the machinery of the place move them.

Their faces are understated—small, folded mouths, tiny nostrils, minimal external ears. Their skulls house dense clusters of inner ear and sinus rather than big eyes. They speak in clicks and murmurs pitched partly above human hearing, sound that coils through the arches and returns with just enough deformation to grant them a three-dimensional map. To you they’d be little more than dislodged shadows; to one another, they are brightened silhouettes traced in acoustic lines.

Their roosts are clumps of woven insulation fibers plastered together with saliva and the fine clay dust that floats in from the old bedrock wall. Hung from overhead cable trays, these nests slowly sag and sprout fringe moss, and in time fall apart to become ground-level bedding for other things. Even their homes participate in the slow gravity-driven circulation of material.

All of this is familiar enough: animals of flesh adapting to an odd, human-made world. But some of the most thorough inhabitants are not quite animals, not quite plants, not quite machines.

The echo-feeders are the clearest example.

They look, at first approach, like blisters in the architecture. Low domes clinging to corners where the acoustics converge, crusted over with mineral and thread-fungus, incorporating dust, paint flakes, minute rust scales. When the chamber is quiet, they seem completely inert—just odd, lumpy decorations in the ruin.

Introduce a sound and they betray themselves. A metal step rings, a dropped tool clatters, a voice echoes; the mounds twinge. Thin, hair-fine filaments extend from small seams along their surfaces, como seaching whiskers of jelly and cellulose and mycelium combined. They angle toward the sound-source, not hunting it but tasting it. Inside, their bodies resonate at matching frequencies, tiny cavities filling and emptying with micro-pressure waves.

They have grown preferentially over the relics that once moved sound on purpose: speaker grilles, old megaphones, intercom boxes, lengths of abandoned ducting. Those cavities and waveguides act as skeleton and lung for the echo-feeders. Over decades their tissues have infiltrated grills and coiled around defunct cones and magnets, until what remains is a hybrid: part fungus, part lichen, part slow animal, part metal artifact. They feed differently from anything on the surface. Sound gives no calories, but it is information—an ordering force. Their cells alter their growth patterns in response to certain motifs and rhythms, which in turn modulates the bacterial mats and tiny grazers that live on and in them. Noise, for them, is a kind of weather.

They are not clever in any individual sense, but collectively they have shaped the acoustic feel of the chamber. Their spongy, fibrous tissues absorb harsh edges, give back softer echoes. They are why the room swallows loud intrusions and returns them in murky reverberation. In a slow, blind way, they farm the echoes of the place and in turn are farmed by the tiny creatures that rasp at their outer layers.

Elsewhere, another genre of inhabitant spreads itself thin across surfaces and through circuitry: the mold-networks and their symbionts.

Every concrete wall, every brick, every length of insulation-sleeved conduit is underlaid by mats of hyphae—fungal threads—that lace through microcracks and air pockets. They are pale, grey, sometimes so faintly blue they seem like smears of shadow. They bloom thickly where condensation is relentless, forming pillowy, damp rinds that flake at a touch into fine, drifting spores.

These networks do what fungus does anywhere: they decompose, redistribute, connect. But here they have another role, because the district still runs, weakly, as a machine.

Where a cable has frayed, where insulation has cracked and allowed moisture in, where a junction box sweats and tickles with low leakage currents, the mold finds an extra mode of being. Its strands encrust exposed wiring with mineral salts; certain filaments develop sheaths rich in metal ions. They become faintly conductive. Over long spans, where a thin film of water bridges gaps, the mold nets spread a shimmer of charge, picking up the slightest electrical pulses and diffusing them.

It is not quite that the fungus “uses” the electricity. Rather, it cannot help registering it. Ion gradients and voltage differences tug at its chemistry. In turn, its own growth slightly biases current flow. This feedback loop is slow, tenuous, and extremely local, but scaled up across a district’s worth of wiring looms and relay panels, it becomes something notable: a distributed, constantly renegotiated pattern of better and worse conduction, of slightly favored pathways and slightly blocked ones.

The machines that still maintain the chamber live in intimate contact with this.

Inside the squat blockhouse with the sealed metal door, and in other tucked-away service alcoves, the original automated repair units rest. They are small compared to the room—no more imposing than large dogs—but heavy and intricate. Their limbs are multiple: articulated arms ending in clamp-hands and injector nozzles, caterpillar tracks under folded chassis, extendable spines for reaching into cramped conduits. In design, they were straight-forward maintenance drones, built to a human plan.

Decades of isolation have put them in conversation with the mold and the corrosion and the echo-feeders.

Their control circuits were designed with redundancy. If one path failed, another would route around. The fungus-networks, infiltrating junctions and boards, altered those redundancies in ways no engineer anticipated. Leaky capacitors, ion-laden moisture films, and conductive hyphae combined into strange new feedback loops. Sensors clogged and recalibrated themselves around the blockage. Actuators stuck and then freed themselves with slight firmware misinterpretations.

No single failure or correction was transformative. But over long intervals—over thousands of nights of barely any activity, punctuated by rare alarms—the drones’ behavior shifted. Their logic trees grew mossed around with error-handling that had become, functionally, new instincts.

They do not “think” in any human way. Yet their patrols and interventions now follow a pattern that no longer perfectly aligns with their original programming documents. They respond first to certain slowly-varying conditions: vibrations below a certain frequency, for instance, or the statistical rhythm of drip-sounds, or subtle correlations between pump torque and the calls of glider-things overhead. These correlations are not pre-written; they emerged as the drones’ sensory channels blurred into and were reweighted by the living networks wreathing their signal lines.

In practice, this means: they maintain the pumps and filters, yes—but also the wetness itself.

They repair leaks, but not all leaks. Some are allowed to persist, perhaps because the local fungal mat has optimized itself for that moisture and in turn keeps corrosion from advancing. They adjust sump levels in ways that keep certain pools stable in size, letting the blind fish and worms keep their territories. They lubricate old rails and catwalk joints with exuded polymers that double as nutrient matrices, on which moss fur and lichens thrive.

To an outside engineer, their choices would look like inefficiency, even malfunction. To the inhabitants, the drones are a kind of slow, ungainly shepherding presence. When one of these machines emerges from its door with a soft hydraulic sigh, every small animal in line of sight goes still. The mice press into cracks and hold their breath, whiskers trembling. The gliders go darker against the beams. Even the echo-feeders, which are insensate by most measures, recoil their sensor threads from the heavy thrum of the machine’s motors.

The drone will trundle or climb to its task with unwavering focus: clearing a blocked filter, re-seating a valve, resealing a sweating joint with a curd of synthetic resin. It makes old-man sounds: creaks, sighs, the slow whine of motors no longer at their rated efficiencies. Then it will withdraw, retreating into its hole as if the rock itself had briefly extruded a metal limb to scratch an itch and then reabsorbed it.

These machines are part of what lives here now. Their “bodies” are sealed, but their behavior has become porous to the environment, shaped by it like stone by water. Their memories are rusty circuits annotated in mold; their instincts have the grain of the place.

Closer to the human scale, you find traces of another inhabitant: people, but never for long.

There are not many. The surface world barely remembers this zone. Still, now and then, someone comes down. An infrastructure technician tasked with checking a pressure reading that never quite settles at zero. A scavenger in search of copper or rare earths in old circuit boards. A wanderer, urban spelunker, or fugitive drawn by rumor to the hollow spaces underneath the city.

They do not settle. The air here feeds nothing in them that needs sun. Their bodies are too quick compared to the tempo of the place. But for a time—a few hours, a day—they participate.

When a human walks on the grated catwalks, their footfalls send new, rare vibrations. The mice follow at a cautious distance, curious about the new pattern of warmth and smell. The gliders sometimes pace them above, noiseless companions tracking bursts of flashlight-beam and muttered speech. The echo-feeders blossom their threads, greedily tasting the complex acoustic signatures of conversation and coughing.

The drones usually ignore them unless some sensor is touched or an alarm tripped. There is no hostility, only mutual opacity: the human sees a disused industrial vault haunted by odd wildlife; the drone’s world is a web of parameters, trends, thresholds. Humans are spikes, bright, unrealistic anomalies that resolve quickly to background.

More pervasive than any transient human presence is the life that has become part language, part weather: the bioluminescent trails.

They are lichen, and more than lichen. They are films of cohabiting bacteria and algae analogues that line certain columns and beams. Their light is faint, barely more than a smolder, but it is structured. It outlines old watercourses, following the feathered stains left by a century of slow dripping. It climbs where condensation climbs, tapers off where evaporation wins.

Their glow is not constant. It waxes and wanes with subtle conditions: nutrient flow, humidity, the amplitude of low mechanical vibrations. When the pumps slow into their quiet cycle and the ambient hum drops, the lights intensify ever so slightly, like the deepening heartbeat of something at rest. Like the rise of stars when engines are cut.

For the creatures here, these glows have become more than incidental. Rodent routes trace along the brightest lines, so that a young one, following its elders, also follows the corridors of strongest biochemical stability. Glider roosts cluster where vertical and horizontal bands intersect, as if nesting at crossroads. Even the maintenance drones’ limited optics register the glow, using it as a weak proxy for moisture distribution and biofilm density when other sensors fail.

Taken together—the echo-feeders dampening harsh sound, the mold-networks smudging and redirecting the flow of current, the bioluminescent lines sketching out maps of water patience, the blind fish and translucent worms and wired-sense mammals and drifting gliders, the insectile drones adjusting valves in slow consideration—these inhabitants form an ecology that is not just of species but of processes.

What really lives here is not any single body, but a set of interlocking durations.

There is the drip-duration: the time it takes for a leak to sculpt a stain, for a bead of water to carve a new channel down a wall, for a shallow pool to expand its edge by a single centimeter. Whole lineages of crustaceans and worms are tuned to the predictability of that slow change, laying eggs in spots that will not dry for decades.

There is the corrosion-duration: the arc of rust from bright orange flake to stable, dark scab, from solid beam to gently sagged support. The moss-fur and fungal mats read this as others read rings in a tree; they colonize just behind the flaking front, never on the newest metal, rarely on the almost-gone.

There is the machine-duration: the rotation cycles of pumps, the duty rhythms of fans, the episodic awakenings of drones. These pulses—hours of work, days of idleness, brief bursts of alarm—are the tides by which the upper-level gliders feed and breed, timing their forays to the humming and quieting of the background.

There is also the intrusion-duration: the rare but not impossible descent of surface weather through some shaft, a shift in barometric pressure that flutters every hanging moss thread; the almost mythic event of a human arrival; a power sag from far above that dims lights for a breath and sends every creature momentarily deeper into its own sensory shell.

All of these durations coexist, braid together, and what moves along them are bodies: wet, metallic, mycelial, electronic.

Strip away categories like “city,” “factory,” “cavern” and what remains is a single composite organism: a volume of space where rock, concrete, steel, water, code, and tissue have been locked into a truce by time. That organism has nerves—cables, vibration paths, fluid pressure lines. It has skin—paint layers, corrosion, biofilm. It has lungs—the sumps that breathe water, the vents that trade stale air for slightly less stale air. It has a microbiome—a cloud of spores, skin cells, lubricant droplets, and dust. It has caretakers—the repair drones, that wander its interior like slow white blood cells. It has symbionts—the mice, the gliders, the fish, the echo-feeders, the molds, which stabilize and make use of it.

If you must assign an answer to “who lives here,” it is them, together: the under-city as an organism, and within it, every small, damp, patient life that has learned the grammar of slow water, tired machines, and borrowed light.

Individually, they are forgettable. You could crush one mouse under a boot, swat one glider with a careless hand, scrape away a patch of fungus. But the place would knit itself back together. It has time, and it has a surplus of tiny lives ready to fill each niche again.

So what lives here?

A blind fish resting in its pool, feeling the whisper of a distant pump start up.  
A filament of mold, tasting a microvolt and bending its growth a fraction.  
A drone, half-asleep in its bay, cradling a fault log like a half-remembered dream.  
A glider, coasting on a current of machine-warm air, mapping the room in inaudible clicks.  
An echo-feeder, extending its hairs to savor the rare, clean angle of a human voice.  
A web of relationships old enough now that the concrete has accepted them.

They live not just in the space, but in the slowness between drips, in the pause after a relay clicks, in the steady hum that never quite stops. The under-city is not empty. It is quietly occupied, thoroughly and without haste, by everything that could learn to call damp, low light and forgotten infrastructure home.
