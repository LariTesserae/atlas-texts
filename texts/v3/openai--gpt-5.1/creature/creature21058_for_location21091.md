They are small, first of all.

Not insect-small, not quite. They are as long as your forearm if you stretch your hand, maybe as long as your whole arm for the largest of them, but they think at the scale of hands and breaths, not at the scale of vistas. A height of three human spans from floor to ceiling is more than they will ever need; they live in the snugness of interstice and hollow.

They are not yet committed to being anything you know.

---

### Bodies: made for closeness

Their bodies are built for contact with surfaces, not with open air.

Spine long and supple, limbs short and numerous: not a centipede’s scandal of legs, but more than the human four. Six points of contact, usually. Two primary forelimbs, dextrous and slow; two midlimbs that can be hand or foot; two rear that are mostly anchors. Each limb ends in fans of soft, gripping pads, ridged like the underside of certain geckos, but wetter—made to cling to damp stone, to flex on root and shelf without slipping.

They can stand upright when they must, braced against a wall, but that is not their preferred posture. To see them at ease is to see a creature half-leaning, half-hanging: belly close to the surface, one side pressed to stone, the other open to the tiny volume of chamber air. Horizontal is safer; vertical is for urgency.

Their skin is not skin as you know it. It is a tight, thin integument, hairless, faintly translucent, with underlying structures just barely visible in the half-light: tendon-cables, pores, lattices of supporting cartilage. Its outermost layer is always damp—never dripping, but beaded with a constant microfilm of moisture. If you touched them, they would feel like a warm, breathing mushroom cap.

Color is an afterthought. Their bodies are the color of the place: pale clay beneath, mottled with patches of mineral-white, streaked with faint greens and browns from symbiotic films that live on their skin. In some, the pale glow of internal bioluminescent organs shows through as a soft wash of color under throat, along flank, or at the tips of certain fingers.

Eyes are small relative to head, set far apart, protected by hooded ridges and membrane-thin lids that can close sideways as well as down. In the dimmest pockets, the eyes are almost useless; they rely instead on:

- A dense array of sensory cilia fringing the pads of their limbs, detecting vibration in the substrate.
- Sensitive pits along the jawline and shoulders, tuned to temperature gradients and humidity shifts.
- A long, soft tongue that can taste mineral and chemical traces from walls and water films.

Their ears are not visible in the way you expect. The whole lateral line of their bodies is an organ of hearing: thin cavities beneath the skin that register the tiniest fluctuations in air pressure and resonance along the stone. A single drop falling three chambers away is a clear event in them.

The largest difference from any surface creature lies inside. They are made to hold and manage water.

Their blood—or whatever serves as blood—is thick, sluggish, loaded with dissolved minerals. Under their ribs (they have many, curving around in a basket) lie three major sacs:

- One is essentially a lung adapted to wet air; it never fully dries, and its inner surfaces ripple with ciliated folds that drink moisture as much as they drink breath.
- One is a storage bladder for excess water, drawn from pools and condensation, which they share among themselves in leaner flows.
- One is a slow organ of stone: filled with a gel threaded by silica and carbonate crystals, constantly exchanging material with the outside world through the skin and via deliberate excretions. This is how they can guide mineral growth.

They are not quite exoskeleton-builders, not quite stone-shapers. But when they press their palms to a damp wall and stay very still, the chemistry of that wall changes.

### Gait and presence

They move as if they are never fully separate from their surroundings.

A person of theirs (let us call them that) does not simply walk down a corridor. They lean into it, maintaining three or four points of contact at all times. Forelimbs test each new patch of substrate before committing weight. Midlimbs drag lightly along the wall, cilia tasting for instability. Rear limbs push them forward in small, sure shifts.

Their speed is low but consistent. They rarely run. Suddenness is dangerous in a world where surfaces can slump, crack, give way. Their reflexes are not built for dashing; they are built for braking.

Silence is natural to their bodies. Pads land without slap; joints articulate with wet, muffled sounds. Their breathing is shallow and continuous, without the big in-and-out gusts of surface mammals. When they vocalize, it is usually with the back of the throat closed, the sound resonating more in their sinuses than their chests: little clicks, soft pulses, a narrow range of tones that won’t carry far or jar the walls.

You might first know one is near not by seeing them, but by a change in how the space feels: an alteration in the rhythm of drips as they redirect a trickle absentmindedly, a faint new exhalation in the overall breath of the chamber.

### Skin and symbiosis

They do not live alone in their own bodies.

Across their shoulders and upper backs, on the ridges of their forearms and along the sides of their necks, they cultivate thin gardens of microorganisms: algae, fungi, and stranger intermediates, intimately tuned to their chemistry. These films are not visible at a distance, but up close you see iridescence, the faint roughness of colonies.

At rest, when they huddle in their nests or cling in loose clusters to a favored wall, these microbial mats photosynthesize faintly if any borrowed light reaches them, or else they eat dissolved nutrients from condensation and the slow exhalation of the stone. In return, they provide:

- Subtle glows, allowing individuals to recognize one another by pattern in the dark.
- Local modification of skin pH and secretions, deterring some parasites and encouraging others that they find useful.
- A way to carry certain metabolisms with them, concentrated in patches they can bring to new walls.

From time to time, you see one meticulously scraping a spot on their own arm or a companion’s shoulder with the edge of a mineral flake, collecting excess growth into a small organic bowl. That scrapings will later be smeared onto a new surface they wish to seed.

Their hair—if it can be called that—grows only from narrow bands along the spine and wrists. It is not made of keratin, but of fused microbial cords, forming a soft, felt-like layer that soaks up and slowly releases moisture. They do not groom these bands for appearance, but for function: combing them with woven tools to ensure even saturation before a long crossing between wetter pockets.

In a sense, their clothing is their ecosystem. They rarely add external layers beyond occasional harnesses or belts grown from hardened root-fibers. Warmth is not an issue; water management and interface with surfaces are everything.

### Senses and perception

They are exquisitely attuned to the city’s slow movements.

To you, the chamber is still. To them, the world is always leaning, swelling, settling by micrometers. Their cilia feel the difference as someone crosses a bridge three turns away; their lateral ears hear the fatigue in a root-lattice as it begins to complain under weight; their tongues taste shifts in mineral balance telling them whether a seep is stable or about to reroute.

They think of space not as a map of open volume, but as a mesh of flows and resistances:

- Where humidity is fractionally higher along a wall, they sense the presence of a hidden fissure, a possible future path.
- Where the taste of the substrate grows bitter, more acidic, they know that something below is digesting, rotting, becoming dangerous.
- Where the patterned silence of a favorite pool changes—crustacean clicks slower, fish flicks sharper—they know that some larger presence now watches from the silt.

They inhabit durations that you rarely notice. They can sit pressed against a wall for an hour, doing nothing obvious, feeling tiny pulses of water push through microscopic pores of rock. That hour is not emptiness to them; it is data.

Their memories are folded around such perceptions. An elder might recall the “year of the fast drip” in a particular elbow of a corridor, when a new trickle briefly opened there and fed a flush of wall-flora. Directions are given not in lefts and rights, but in references to remembered flows: “Follow the cool air until you feel the double drop; then climb toward the sweeter stone.”

### Origins and ancestry

Their ancestors were not born whole into this city; they grew out of the same first mats that now coat the walls.

Somewhere in the deep past of this land—though “deep” here is measured in tens of thousands of years, not the geologic stretches that built mountain ranges—a line of soft-bodied, amphibious root-things followed moisture down. They were extensions of plants above, explorations into darkness.

Those roots brushed up against simple animals already living in the damp gaps: worm-things, blind crustaceans, early amphibians. They traded genes, chemicals, behaviors. The plant-roots learned to recruit muscle; the animals learned to carry symbiotic microbes as if they were still part of the soil.

The result, over many experiments, was a creature that always half-belonged to the land that bore it. It did not merely survive in hollows; it could modulate them, accelerate their thickening or delay their crumbling. It could “speak” faintly to sediment and mineral with exudates, encouraging some crystals, discouraging others.

As the substrate itself rose and dried above, as surfaces cooled and hardened, those early intermediates turned inward, deeper, into the warm layer where conditions stayed fluid. There they diversified, acquiring more limbs, more complex sensory systems, and, eventually, the neural density to knit their perceptions into something you might consider culture.

They have no myth of having come from the open sky. Their oldest stories begin with being pressed on every side, in contact with stone and water, guided by drips. To them, the surface—if they conceive it at all—is a dangerous mirage: too open, too dry, too loud.

### Their relationship to the city

You wonder whether they built this city or whether the city grew them.

To them, there is no distinction.

The ledges and bridges, the gently channeled flows, the mineral bowls that catch luminous water—these are not “additions” to a neutral background; they are grafts on a living host. And they understand themselves in the same terms: they are a specialized tissue of the land, a responsive framework inserted into its pores.

When a young one is first taught to press their hands to the damp wall and listen, the instructions sound religious to your ears:

- “Feel the slow lift of the wall’s breath. If it rises into your palms, it will thicken here. If it sinks away, this place is already ending.”
- “Taste the edge of the seep. If it stings the front of your tongue, it is too sharp; leave it. If it settles heavy at the back, encourage it.”

They do not say “build a shelf here.” They say, “Ask the wall whether it wants to catch its own water.”

That phrasing is not metaphorical courtesy. They have learned that ignoring the substrate’s quiet preferences leads quickly to collapses, floods, drownings. In a world this young and plastic, willfulness is punished. Cooperation is survival.

So their interventions are minimal, nudges more than impositions:

- A single hair-fiber bundle glued into a forming drip-line to split one fast trickle into two slower ones.
- A ring of pressed clay around a condensation hollow, making its lip slightly higher so it will deepen instead of spill.
- Slender root cuttings introduced into promising cracks, so that future bridges will already have a toe-hold.

Over decades, these tiny acts accumulate into what, to an outsider, looks like intent, like urban planning. To them, it is more akin to weeding and pruning a garden they are part of.

They do not think of themselves as “civilized” or “technological” in the way surface dwellers might. They think of themselves as good listeners.

### Dwelling and sociality

They live in clusters—a word which, for them, is more accurate than “household.”

A cluster is a knot of relationships centered on a favored patch of substrate. This patch may be:

- The underside of a dependable seep-line that has produced good mineral shelves for generations.
- A fork of root-bridges in constant, comfortable tension over a slow ravine.
- A niche in the wall where the breath of the chamber is particularly slow and even.

In such a patch you will find:

- Sleeping nests: shallow hollows padded with fibrous material harvested from certain plants that thrive in moist air but dry quickly when removed. These nests are not fixed forever; they are moved if the wall’s taste changes.
- Shared water organs: large, organic bladders grown from hollow gourds or anatomic grafts, fed by drips and attended as carefully as infants. From these, members of the cluster drink when flows elsewhere thin.
- Working surfaces: low ridges where mineral tools, fibrous cords, and seed-colonies of microbes are arranged in careful patterns, both for organization and because the patterns themselves help remember sequences of tasks.

A cluster may comprise a dozen individuals, or twenty, or three, depending on how generous the patch is and how stable. They are not strictly bound by blood. Kinship is more easily measured in shared drips than in shared parentage. To “leave” a cluster is to gradually invest care and attention in another patch of wall, until one day you realize you have slept elsewhere for many cycles and your old nest has quietly been given to someone new.

Children—thin, gangly versions of the adults, with proportionally larger eyes and more restless limbs—are carried often in early cycles. Not on hips or backs, but chest-to-chest or belly-to-belly, their small pads adhering easily to the parent’s smooth skin. This way they feel the parent’s way of listening to the stone, its tiny shifts, its changing breath.

The young are taught less by spoken instruction and more by shared posture:

- “Press your pads like this when you test a ledge.”
- “Hold still long enough for this drip to grow familiar.”
- “Taste here; remember how this seep feels; later, when it tastes sharper, you will know something is wrong.”

Groups do not gather around great fires or in central plazas. They come together along shared bridges, where the structural tension itself can be felt through their limbs, as if the meeting has a body. There, they exchange story-patterns, prophecies of flow, knowledge of where the substrate has thickened or hollowed since their last passage.

Their language is a threefold thing:

- Vocal pulses: small, efficient, seldom loud.
- Tactile grammar: sequences of touch, pressure, and extremely slight movement across another’s forearm or shoulder.
- Shared attention to the background soundscape: some statements are simply pauses that invite both speakers to listen to a certain drip or creak, and to register its change since last time.

To an outsider, a council of theirs might look like a cluster of quiet bodies hanging from different parts of a wall, eyes half-closed, touching forearms occasionally. Yet in that apparent stillness, more information passes than in an hour of surface arguments.

### Making and tools

They are “tool users” only if you insist on applying that term broadly.

They work with three classes of materials:

1. **Mineral flakes and spines.**  
   Where the substrate fractures cleanly or where veins of denser material run, they free small shards: thin, flattened pieces that can scrape, incise, or wedge. These are their knives, chisels, and levers. They do not haft them into complex devices; instead, a shard is chosen for a task, used, then left embedded in the wall where its presence will guide future fractures.

2. **Fibrous cords.**  
   Harvested from rootlets, tough plant stems, and the long microbial hairs they grow themselves, these cords are spun by hand in patient spirals. Knots, loops, and braids become both structural elements and records. A certain pattern in a bridge’s binding can tell an initiated hand who made it and when, much as tree rings do for you.

3. **Soft containers and organs.**  
   They grow bags from plant-derived tissues, hollowed gourds, even the repurposed bladders of large animals. Some are left purely organic; others are lightly mineralized by bathing in successive seeping films, acquiring a flexible but durable crust.

The most “advanced” object you might find among them is not a machine but a negotiated composite: a living root trained through a braid of cords reinforced at key points with mineral flakes and smeared in symbiotic biofilms. A single such beam can last generations, flexing under new loads, thickening where stressed, shrinking where unused.

They do have fire, but it is modest and occasional. In a world of high humidity and careful quiet, flame is a rare, almost ceremonial tool.

Fires are kindled in deep, vented hollows where smoke can creep upward through known fissures without choking the chambers. They burn the driest plant material, certain resins that give steady, low flames. Fire softens some minerals, cooks certain foods, sterilizes seed-colonies when a culture needs to be reset. It is not a source of light in the way you are used to; its glare is too disruptive, its crackle too loud.

Light, when deliberately produced, is almost always biological:

- They coax brighter luminescent patches in certain algae by feeding them select nutrients, concentrating them into shallow basins. These become “lamps” that glow for hours after being stirred.
- Some individuals have internalized symbionts so completely that they can brighten or dim their skin-glow slightly under conscious control, signaling in narrow bands during emergencies.

There are no wheels, no engines, no separate stores of power. Instead, there is knowledge of cycles: knowing which flow will be strongest in which season, which root will bear weight this year, where the ceiling is due to sag.

### Diet and exchange with other life

They are omnivores tuned to scarcity.

Their primary sustenance comes from:

- Films of microbial life scraped from walls and from the undersides of shelves.
- Small crustaceans and insect larvae harvested from pools.
- Fleshy parts of certain wall-flora, cultivated in shaded niches for tenderness and nutrient density.

They rarely take the larger amphibians or fish outright. Such creatures are more valuable as ongoing participants in the system: eating pests, stirring silt, fertilizing microbial mats.

When they do hunt, it is swift and quiet: a sudden press of a broad palm to pin a slick body against stone, a quick bite from specialized front teeth that can pierce carapace or cartilage with minimal struggling. The event is over in moments. Afterwards, they wash the site carefully, not for ritual purity but to erase the scent-shifts that might mislead others into thinking the substrate itself is failing there.

They also eat stone, in a way.

Not lumps of raw rock, but fine scrapings dissolved in water, sipped slowly. Over a lifetime, an individual will consu­me as much mineral as their own mass many times over, cycling it through their organ of stone before excreting it as tailored films: hard here, porous there, crystalline or amorphous as needed.

This excretion is not a waste product; it is a tool. They “paint” certain surfaces with it, seeding new growth or sealing unwanted seeps. Their bodies are the vector by which the city’s architecture is tuned.

They tend some animals semi-deliberately:

- Small grazer-creatures kept in gently flowing side-pools, their diet controlled by selective shading of algae. Periodically, a few are taken for food; the rest maintain the pool ecology.
- Winged insects attracted by sweet exudates smeared in particular patterns on the rock. These insects in turn become prey for wall-dwelling salamanders, whose density around a cluster doubles as an alarm system: if the salamanders vanish, something is wrong.

The line between hunting, herding, and cohabiting is fuzzy. They do not see themselves as masters of a domesticated world, but as one guild among many, each adjusting the others.

### Reproduction and inheritance

They do not pair-bond in the sense familiar to many surface species. The substrate is their constant partner; other individuals are co-parents and collaborators.

Reproduction is rare and slow. A single adult may bring forth only a handful of offspring across a long life.

Gestation takes place entirely within the parent’s own body, in a chamber adjacent to the water-sac and the organ of stone. During this time, the parent grows stiller, more wall-bound, spending long stretches absorbing and transforming minerals, tuning their internal chemistry to provide the right slowly changing signals to the developing young.

Birth is less a dramatic event than a gradual separation. Over days, the young’s outline becomes visible just beneath the parent’s skin along the flank, like a bubble of different density. Then, in a period of higher humidity when flows are full and stable, the parent finds a favored, well-known patch of wall and presses that flank against it. Moisture softens their skin; a seam opens; the young oozes outward, still slick with internal fluids, held briefly between parent and stone.

The first thing the newborn feels is the wall. The first thing it drinks is not free water but the thin, nutrient-rich film on that chosen patch. The parent’s hands encircle but do not lift; they keep the body close enough to the rock to share its slow pulse.

Others from the cluster attend, bringing carefully chosen smears of microbial cultures to lay across the infant’s skin. In this way, the young is inoculated not only with its parent’s symbionts but with those of the whole community. Its microbiome is a negotiated inheritance.

There is no word of theirs that translates simply to “child” in the sense of an individual to be guided into independence. The closest concept is more like “new extension”: of the cluster, of the land, of the ongoing conversation between seep and shelf.

### Time, story, and what they remember

They count time in drops and in thickness.

A short moment is a drip-interval. A longer one is the time it takes for a particular bowl to fill. A much longer one is measured in the growth of a ledge: “Since that shelf first showed its edge.”

Their oldest communal memories are not of heroic ancestors but of structural transformations:

- “When this ravine was slick wall and not yet cut.”
- “When the ceiling above here lay lower, and we crawled instead of hanging.”
- “When the upstream chamber decided to end in a slow fall, and we moved our nests.”

They tell such stories in three dimensions. On certain walls, in out-of-the-way niches, they maintain “memory meshes” of cord, knot, mineral drip, and microbial stain, each thread representing a flow that has begun, ended, or altered during remembered generations. An elder, guiding young fingers through such a mesh, narrates: “Here, the water changed direction; here, the plant-roots thickened; here, the quiet cracked once.”

There is no single “city name.” Instead, there are many names, each applying to a pattern of flows and shapes. When they speak of their home as a whole, they sometimes gesture outwards and downwards with all limbs, fingers spread, and make a soft, breathy consonant that is less a word than a sigh of residence.

They have no myth of apocalypse. What they fear is not a sudden ending, but an accelerated softening—a time when the substrate around them might, under some unknown impetus, start to slump and re-liquefy faster than their senses can register and their nudges can correct.

To ward against that imagined calamity, they cultivate caution like a virtue:

- Never overburden a new shelf.
- Never silence a warning creak with too much padding.
- Never trust a flow that changed too quickly.

Yet they are not rigid. The city is young; innovation is constant, if quiet. A new pattern of root-weave, a new mix of mineral wash that produces a stronger lip for water bowls, a new partnership with a recently evolved insect—these things spread not as decrees but as admired tricks. Someone tries something; if the substrate answers well and the drips sweeten, others copy it.

### Their view of “above”

Some few have gone higher than the usual band of chambers, into zones where the rock grows cooler, the air thinner, and the hints of weather more direct.

Those who return are changed—not physically, but in the way they speak of space. They bring tales down of:

- Vast, unsupported spans of emptiness where their bodies feel suddenly small and too light.
- Light that is not filtered by water and tissue, but comes from a point so bright it hurts to consider.
- A sky that is not a ceiling at all, but something they cannot name: an unbounded, stone-less above that refuses to press on them.

Most of their kin find these accounts unsettling. Without walls and flows, how does anything know where it belongs? How can a drop fall reliably, if there is so much room for it to be distracted by wind?

A few are nonetheless curious. They imagine that up there, the processes that shape their city might be glimpsed in an earlier, rawer state: rain on newborn ground, rivers sketching their first hesitant curves.

If any of them ever decide to become intermediaries between below and above, they will be a new kind of being again—ones who must carry with them pockets of damp, remembered stone in order to survive in wider air.

For now, the majority remain content within the soft bone of the world, in chambers like the ravine room, where they can feel the land still deciding.

### In the example chamber

In that particular space—the ravine, the glowing bowls, the living bridge—several of them are awake.

One hangs from the underside of the upper root-platform, all six limbs engaged: four gripping the root directly, two splayed to either side, pads pressed lightly to the stone of the dome. They are listening upward, cilia searching for the faint rasp of particles shifting in some unseen cavity above—a precursor to a small fall of dust. Their eyes are half-closed; their tongue flicks now and then against the roof of their mouth, tasting the air’s mineral hints.

Below, on the mineral shelf with its row of luminescent water-bowls, two younger ones sit with their backs to the wall, forelimbs extended, fingertips resting in the glowing water. They are concentrating on the faint tingling that passes from liquid to skin: the signature of the microbes that live there. One bowl’s tingle is subtly wrong compared to the others; a new organism has arrived. They will watch it for cycles before deciding whether to encourage or discourage it.

Across the ravine, on the ledge where you stood in imagination, an elder is slowly drawing their palm along the wall, leaving behind a thin, nacreous film. In some places, they press harder, thickening it; in others, they barely touch, leaving it porous. They hum very softly as they work, a low vibration their own stone-organ feels as much as their ears do. The hum matches the slow drip from the ceiling; their excretion aligns with it, inviting future precipitates to build on this guide.

No one speaks. There is no need.

The bridge between them flexes once as a heavier body crosses—a pregnant individual, water-sac and organ of stone full and heavy. The structure responds with a slow, deep creak that runs along its length, into the roots, into the walls. Six heads tilt minutely in unison, registering the sound. A new memory forms: the bridge can hold that weight; the tension is within safe margins; its next reinforcement can wait.

In this moment, you can see how they and the city are the same act carried out in different materials. The rock thickens, the ledges grow, the roots harden, and between them move these damp, many-handed beings, listening, nudging, accepting, refusing.

They are what lives here: a people still in negotiation with their own definition, as their world is with its. Nothing is finished. Everything is listening for what it might become next.
