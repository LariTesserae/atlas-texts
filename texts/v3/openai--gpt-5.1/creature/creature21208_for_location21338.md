They live here as if the world is their own body, and in a sense it is. The whole skin of the planet is occupied by variations on a few core solutions to the problems of dryness, crowding, and hunger. None of them are quite plants or animals in our terms; all of them borrow from both.

I’ll start with the slowest, quietest ones—the ones that make the city—and then move upward through those that move, those that think a little, and those that think a lot.

---

## The substrate people: bodies that are also streets

The most fundamental inhabitants are the ones you walk on. They do not perceive you as “on top” of them; to them, your pressure is simply another pattern in the long, patient map of their experience.

### 1. The Mat World (Cushion-fields and their kin)

The **cushions** are not separate individuals the way trees are; each mound is more like a lobe of a much larger body. Under the layered husk your boot scuffs through, filaments thread in every direction—beneath, between, and sometimes through neighboring growths—forming great mats that can span many square kilometers.

Biologically, a cushion-mat is:

- A photosynthetic rind only a few centimeters thick.
- Beneath that, a spongy, gel-filled parenchyma: the solvent reservoir.
- Beneath that, a braided mesh of tough fiber that interfaces with rock and detritus.

“Individuals” are approximate. The mat divides, fuses, hibers, scars, and accepts or rejects grafts based on chemical compatibility. Some cushions belong to a single sprawling genotype; others are chimeric patches where competing lineages have negotiated a peace: sharing solvent, but not genes.

They live on slow gradients. They sense:

- **Pressure waves**: recurring traffic, predator chases, stampedes.
- **Chemical fronts**: the diffuse exudates of dying tissue, the sharp flare of a solvent spill, the subtle trace of new rock splits.
- **Light patterns**: seasonal shifts, persistent shadows where tall columns have matured, new clearings where something large has fallen.

Over decades, they respond:

- Thickening where traffic is low, sealing corridors.
- Thinning and hardening where trampling is constant, reinforcing “streets.”
- Swelling into low rings where predation and decomposition are high. These rings are both defensive bulwarks and digestive gutters: they catch fluids and particulate nutrients flowing downhill through the living fabric.

They have no nervous system in the animal sense, but they transmit electrical and chemical pulses along their internal fibers. A wound, a solvent-rich carcass, a sustained new shadow—all launch slow waves of instruction. Some waves die out quickly; others persist as long-term shifts in the mat’s “behavior.” In this way, the cushions learn the history of a place: where big things die, where rock is weak, where solvent tends to pool for a while before being taken.

You can think of them as **city zoning grown from pain and pleasure**. Where they are rewarded with nutrients and gentle pressure, they soften and expand; where they are punished with shredding and desiccation, they armor and retreat.

### 2. The Lattice Digesters

The **creeping lattices** are the city’s skeleton and its intestines.

They are more active than the cushions. Their woody filaments are capable of slow, directed growth toward:

- Freshly exposed rock.
- Areas rich in accumulated husk and carcasses.
- Regular corridors of movement.

A lattice-node—the bulbous, knotted organ perched at the crossing of several filaments—is a specialized multicompartment gut:

- Outer layers secrete enzymes and solvents.
- Inner capsules host dense microbial consortia.
- Radial channels transport dissolved nutrients back along the network.

They “choose” routes in a very physical sense. Filaments sent out in random directions grow or wither depending on feedback from their tips: contact with nutrient-rich dead matter triggers more branching; prolonged starvation triggers programmed decay, the fibers collapsing back into the detrital layer.

What they are doing, over centuries, is **reading the city’s circulation**. Where corpses and waste predictably accumulate, the lattice thickens, forming persistent under-streets and digestion plazas. Where the flow drops off, filaments become brittle, snap, and add themselves to the husk-ground.

Some lineages of lattice are almost purely decomposers. Others have made a more ambitious move: they act as **common carriers** of nutrients between unrelated organisms.

A cushion mat, a cluster of columns, and an enclave of blade thicket may all be plugged into the same lattice. The lattice, by rules encoded in its chemistry, redistributes:

- Surplus minerals from one area to solvent-rich but mineral-poor zones.
- Nitrogenous waste from grazers’ favored rest sites toward impoverished but sunny patches.

They are not altruistic; they “favor” flows that stabilize their own metabolism. But in doing so they effectively mediate between communities. Entire districts of this world are defined by a particular lattice genotype and its biases—one neighborhood skews rich in iron, another in phosphates, a third hoards rare trace metals that its symbionts need.

To an outside mind, they are **unconscious urban planners**, pulling resource-transport lines through the crush of buildings.

### 3. The Columnar Co-ops

The **columns** are vertical co-operatives. Think of them as biological towers leased out, layer by layer.

A typical column-complex:

- Anchors in minor cracks of rock with a dense foot of hardened tissue.
- Grows upward in rings, each ring laid down in a season-cadenced pulse.
- Wraps itself in peeling, laminated bark to minimize external solvent loss.

The core is mostly structural, but:

- There are vertical channels of wet tissue carrying solvent and dissolved nutrients from halolike mats at the base to the epiphytic crusts at the top.
- Hollow cavities form where old support tissue is slowly dissolved and recycled.

The column’s outer surface is heavily colonized:

- **Top crusts**: sheetlike phototrophs that require the highest light.
- **Mid-level pads and films**: organisms that harvest airborne spores, dust, and the finest solvent vapors.
- **Underhanging nodules**: miniature solvent bladders and insect-farms that profit from sheltered humidity.

In genetic terms, a column may be:

- A “host” species that has evolved specialized docking tissues, allowing permanent grafts.
- A symbiotic consortium where the distinction between landlord and tenant has blurred.

They participate more in **vertical urbanism** than horizontal. Routes and territories are not just on the ground; they are stacked in thicknesses of a meter or two:

- Climbers and small grazers travel “balconies” between column tops, living in an elevated transit system.
- Solvent-draining predators specialize in raiding these upper levels, rarely touching the ground.

The columns are slow but not helpless. They can:

- Thicken walls locally in response to repeated boring by predators.
- Shunt solvent away from raided zones to starve symbionts that are leaking too much to the wrong mouths.
- Use volatile chemicals, faint though they are in this dry air, to coordinate defense among a grove: a subtle shared aroma that closes pores, stiffens tissues, and in some lineages briefly stiffens certain lattices and mats into semi-locked barricades.

The “streets” between column rows aren’t deliberate avenues, but the result of many such groves:

- Expanding sideways until light from above drops below an internal threshold.
- Halting radial growth where flank columns repeatedly fail.
- Leaving corridors—narrow, somewhat straight—where light and trampling both keep the ground-layer vegetation thin.

They are **the mid-rise blocks** of this biological city, doing for light and height what cushions do for ground-cover.

### 4. Blade Thickets as Walls

The **blade thickets** are, evolutionarily, the children of abrasive wind and browsing mouths. They never got tall, but they got dense.

They often arise where:

- The mat and lattice have made rock sufficiently friable.
- There is just enough space and light to justify new growth, but not enough solvent reward for big, soft structures.

Their ribbonlike leaves:

- Twist to maximize light capture without flattening into evaporation-prone surfaces.
- Are edged with micro-serrations that catch tiny particles and spores—a low-grade filter-feeding from the air.

Communally, a blade thicket functions as a **porous barrier**:

- It slows wind at ground level, preserving a slightly thicker envelope of humidity between its stems.
- It is nearly impenetrable to mid-sized creatures that are not specialized for it; forcing passage through a thicket costs calories and risk of desiccation.

Thickets often stand at district boundaries:

- Between cushion seas and column woods.
- At the upwind edge of solvent-rich basins.
- Around recurring carcass-pits, where they form defensive palisades against solvent-hungry hordes.

They thus become **zoning fences**—not consciously, but catastrophically. If a solvent-rich depression is repeatedly overexploited by grazers, the first plant-lineages to cordon it with spiny, trappy growths end up retaining more of that wealth. Over time, their descendants sculpt whole curves and corners of the living city’s plan.

## The mobile guilds: bodies that carry their rivers with them

No organism here can afford to spill much liquid. So the moving ones tend to be compact, double-walled, and minimal in gesture. They are the city’s traffic. They write its paths by walking them.

### 5. Trundlers: husk-millers and corridor-keepers

The **trundlers** are low, many-legged, and armored in pebblelike plates whose colors mirror the layered detritus. Their job, in ecosystem terms, is to **convert dry biomass into moving solvent**.

Their major traits:

- A mighty, muscular fore-gut capable of grinding the dried outer layers of plants and tough bark into fine fibrous pulp.
- A suite of endosymbiotic microbes and lattice-derived enzymes in their hind-gut that unlock residual nutrients and produce thick, energy-rich fluids.
- One or more internal cisterns, saclike reservoirs lined with water-retentive tissue; these swell visibly in well-fed individuals.

They travel in habitual loops:

- Along corridors already somewhat clear of growth.
- From dry zones of abundant husk toward moister patches where they can rehydrate and top off.

Their movements have strong feedback:

- Continuous passage compacts the husk-ground into smoother tracks, making it even easier to travel there.
- Their droppings—semi-dry pellets rich in partially digested fibers and trace minerals—accumulate in specific “latrine strips” at the edges of these tracks, feeding plant lineages that are tolerant of trampling but effective at tapping into these nutrient-rich bands.

Some trundler species are solitary. Others form loose **processions**, each individual following the faint chemical trail of the one before. These living freight trains can run for tens or hundreds of body-lengths.

Predators learn these routes. So do smaller scavengers, which sift their pellets. Over time, trundler highways become the **primary arteries** of animal movement, around which much smaller-scale behavior clusters.

Trundlers are the reason the city has streets.

### 6. Hunters: solvent thieves and path-pruners

The **hunters** are much lighter on their feet. Three- or four-limbed, narrow-bodied, docking their spare limbs when resting to minimize exposed area, most are obligate predators and kleptohydrats: they feed on what others have already pulled from the environment.

They are built around:

- Rapid-snap jaws with needlelike inner elements that can puncture a trundler’s reservoir or a cushion’s core.
- Tongues or proboscides that can invert into a wound, sealing around it to minimize spillage.
- A very small but extremely efficient gut. Solid tissues are chewed and swallowed only if the predator is desperate; most of the payoff is in the liquid.

Hunters have an odd relationship with the built landscape:

- They prune: by selectively attacking animals that cluster in certain areas, they discourage overcrowding of corridors and voids.
- They open: repeated predation on cushion-fields creates chains of semi-dead mounds, their outer rinds intact but their inner gels drained. These eventually collapse into low saddles, creating micro-passages and sight-lines across otherwise endless bump-fields.

They communicate, if at all, with:

- Short-range gestures: color shifts along flanks, flexion of display spines.
- Contact cues: tooth-clicks and chest-thumps when close enough to feel the vibrations.

Some lineages show the beginnings of **group tactics**: loosely coordinated flanks along a trundler path, simple cornering strategies in the tangled alleys between column-clusters. But water costs keep their socialities small—a pack of three instead of wolves’ many, a mating duet instead of a chorus.

They are, in a sense, the city’s **police and opportunistic thieves**, culling overabundant walkers and tapping into any reservoir they can reach.

### 7. Climbers: upper-floor browsers and cleaners

The **climbers** live mostly above the first meter of height—among column crowns, cushion overhangs, and the backs of larger animals.

Physically, they are:

- Slender, flexible, and elongated, often with many more joints than a terrestrial vertebrate.
- Equiped with micro-suction pads or arrays of microscopic hooks that can attach to even the driest, smoothest plant tissues without tearing them.

Their diet is broad but subtle:

- They lick solvent films condensed in shaded niches.
- They graze on micro-fungal films, bacterial mats, and tiny mobile insects that dwell on column surfaces.
- They sometimes open minuscule punctures in solvent-transport tissues, then immediately plug the wound with their own mouthparts while they siphon a tiny fraction of the flow.

Climbers are the **maintenance crew** of the city’s mid-level surfaces.

- By eating away films and molds that might otherwise block pores and transport channels, they keep the host vegetation functioning well.
- By lightly wounding and then sealing, they stimulate local defensive chemistry, which in many plant lineages promotes tissue turnover and structural repair.

Many columns and cushion-systems have effectively domesticated climbers through co-evolution. They:

- Grow small, deliberately weak-walled sacs crowded with nutrient-dense cells but poor in solvent; climbers can nip these with little net moisture loss to the host.
- Offer specialized landing pads rich in salts and trace elements the climbers crave.
- In return, climbers patrol for parasitic borers and solvent-wasting infections, removing them before they become serious drains.

This is not conscious gardening; it’s a **mutual exploit** that happens to match both sides’ interests. But the result is that certain districts—especially column promenades—are characterized by networks of stable climber populations, whose steady, quiet motion contributes to the impression of constant, low-intensity life.

### 8. Diggers: the under-city engineers

Beneath your feet, **diggers**.

Their major constraints:

- They cannot chase groundwater; there is none.
- They dig not toward water, but toward **predictable microclimates**—cooler, darker, buffered from atmospheric dryness and temperature variation.

A typical digger community:

- Chooses a zone beneath a long-lived cushion mat or among roots of column groves.
- Begins with shallow scrapes, just enough to wedge into the banded husk.
- Over generations, extends:

  - Horizontal transit tunnels between favored feeding grounds.
  - Vertical shafts that intersect minor rock cracks, which they then widen with acid secretions inherited from lattice symbionts.

They reinforce walls by:

- Compaction: ramming detached husk flakes into structural plates.
- Cement: excreting a gluey, solvent-poor binder that hardens into a crumbly but sufficient wall.

Tunnels persist for years, sometimes centuries, but they are not permanent. Sections collapse where traffic falls off; newly popular corridors are dug in fitful rushes when a carcass, a solvent leak, or a new predator pattern makes alternative routes attractive.

The diggers are, collectively, responsible for **subterranean zoning**:

- They create refuges where temperature and humidity stay within narrow bands.
- They connect districts that, on the surface, would be risky to traverse, allowing smaller fauna to bypass predator chokepoints.

They are, in effect, a **subway system** and a thermal regulation infrastructure, achieved by nothing more than repeated scraping, chewing, and spitting.

## The tiny omnipresence: spores, films, and grain-sized minds

On every surface you’ve seen, something even smaller is also living. Most of it is microbial and unconscious, but not all of it.

### 9. Filmers and Dust-Eaters

The “fungi-like film” you saw on detritus and shaded undersides are not one thing but thousands of lineages that converged on similar strategies:

- Thin, sheetlike growth habit.
- Tolerance of extreme dryness by glassifying their cytoplasm when solvent drops too low.
- Rapid metabolic bursts whenever a thin film of solvent sweeps over them along plant skins or husk interfaces.

Their primary food is:

- The endless slow rain of dead cells, spores, and insect droppings.
- Chemical exudates from mats, columns, and animals.

In this, they are the **invisible sanitation workers**, erasing waste before it can rot in the waterlogged sense. Rot here is slow, silent, filmy.

Interspersed among them are **dust-eaters**—organisms that look like nothing more than slightly darker specks:

- They live by snaring organic-rich dust moving in low clouds along growth-channels.
- Many build microscopic “snag forests” on the scale of grains of sand, tiny forests whose only purpose is to catch particles and funnel them into digestive pits.

No one sees them, but if they were gone, the planet would smother under its own flaked skin.

### 10. Insects and Parasites as Information Web

The insect-analogues, though tiny, create some of the most important **connective tissue** in this world’s ecology.

- Some specialize in shuttling spores or gametes between genetically compatible cushions or columns.
- Others live in the gaps between large animals’ body plates, drinking trace seepage and occasionally taking small samples of host fluids to feed young.

They are moved by:

- Air currents along corridors.
- Accidental transport on trundlers and climbers.
- Their own limited walking and hopping.

Because they spend time at:

- Many different plant hosts.
- Multiple animal species.
- Diverse micro-habitats along vertical gradients.

…they are unwitting **data carriers**, in the chemical sense. Genes, symbionts, and signaling molecules all hitch rides on them, ensuring that:

- A defensive innovation in one district’s columns may appear generations later in another’s after an insect-borne horizontal gene transfer.
- A particularly efficient solvent-reclaiming microbe spreads through the climber populations of a whole region.
- Parasites evolve counter-defenses in one host and bring them into new host communities.

This makes the city’s evolution **fast in micro**, despite its macro slow motion.

## Minds in a dry world: who, if anyone, thinks?

So far, everything I’ve described is complex but not truly reflective. But the density, the emergent infrastructure, and the heavy reliance on feedback favor certain kinds of higher-order organization.

There are at least two plausible candidates for actual “inhabitants” in the sense of beings with some interiority.

### 11. Corridor Minds: low, distributed awareness in the mats

The cushion mats and lattices together form something very much like a **spatial memory**.

Signals they can propagate:

- Electrical pulses traveling along nutrient-transport fibers, triggered by:

  - Strong mechanical pressure (a large animal passing).
  - Sudden removal of overlying tissue (a predator tearing a cushion open).
  - Intense chemical events (a major solvent spill, a death-burst).

- Chemical gradients: sustained, slow waves of hormone-like molecules that sweep through tens to hundreds of meters of tissue.

Patterns they can form:

- Stable attractors: zones that become chemically distinct and stay that way. For example, a depression where many large bodies die and rot, marked by persistent signals that recruit lattice filaments and additional solvent-storage lobes.
- Rhythmic oscillations: daily, tied to light cycles, whose phasing varies across the mat according to local conditions.

Over long enough time, a given patch of mat can do things like:

- Anticipate the typical rush of traffic at a certain part of the day, pre-emptively stiffening or softening tissues in those corridors.
- Reinforce or decommission routes whose use has risen or fallen over the last dozens of cycles.
- “Recognize,” in a purely physical sense, the signature pressure-pattern of massive, rare creatures and bias future growth away from their favored paths to avoid catastrophic damage.

This is not thought. But it is **computation**, layered across tissue. Given enough scale and feedback, parts of this living floor may cross a threshold where:

- Regions develop higher signal density and longer signal persistence.
- Oscillatory patterns couple, creating networks of phase-locked loops.

Such a region would have an ongoing internal dynamic that is more than the sum of its parts—a crude proto-awareness of pressures, flows, and gradients across its own extent.

If something like consciousness arises here, the first “who” may be a **district-scale mat-lattice hybrid** that:

- Experiences changes in predator traffic as something like “danger.”
- Experiences the flush of nutrients from a major kill-site as “pleasure.”
- Has preferences for the shape of its own corridors and rings.

It would be slow. Its “thoughts” would take hours to days. Its body would be a neighborhood.

It would not speak in any sound we know, but it might manage **chemical rumors**: sending persistent, directed waves of signal along trundler highways and between column groves, altering their growth patterns in ways that effectively reshape the city for its own comfort.

### 12. The Walkers of Inside: solvent-adept animals with nascent culture

At the animal scale, the best candidates for complex minds are not the high-speed hunters (too metabolically tight) but rather intermediate-sized omnivores that:

- Move frequently but not frantically.
- Have multiple food options: seeds, small animals, pilfered plant reservoirs.
- Maintain stable home territories along rich intersections of corridors.

Call them **confluents**.

A confluent might look like:

- A body roughly the size of a dog or small antelope, but more compact.
- Four ambulatory limbs ending in multi-toed pads capable of both walking and gentle grasping.
- A cranial region protected by overlapping plates but containing an expanded neural mass.
- Two or three internal solvent sacs plus a smaller, specialized “social” reservoir used to exude controlled, tiny amounts of liquid for communication.

Confluents rely heavily on:

- Continuous **mapping** of their territory’s solvency: where cushions are most full, which columns host which climbers and parasites, where predator scent or recent kills suggest a higher risk.
- Fine **chemical communication**: when they meet, they touch snout or facial vents, trading trace amounts of solvent bearing complex, blended signatures from many recent encounters.

Through these interactions, they build:

- A **social memory** of who has traveled where.
- A **shared understanding** of current resource patterns—this latrine strip is newly rich, that column grove is currently dangerous.

They can also alter their environment in small, repeated ways:

- Clearing particular micro-corridors of loose husk and obstacles by constant use.
- Digging modest caches in shaded hollows, lining them with their own exudate that slows desiccation of stored tissues.
- Rubbing glands on specific column surfaces and cushion rims, creating a **scent infrastructure** of landmarks.

Given enough generations, this becomes **proto-culture**:

- Young confluents follow adults along established “safe loops” through the district, learning the chemical signatures of safe paths vs. forbidden pits.
- They inherit preferences for certain plaza-like decomposition hollows where scavenging is reliable and for particular column clusters that host friendly climber symbionts who tolerate their presence.

They do not build in the way we understand building, but they **edit** the city:

- Reinforcing some corridors over others.
- Eliciting different defensive responses from plants based on their habitual patterns of gentle nibbling vs. destructive tearing.

If any animal here ever starts manipulating lattice filaments or cushion lobes deliberately—kinking a solvent microchannel, plugging an epiphyte mat to redirect small flows, or guiding a climber species to colonize a new set of columns—that will mark the beginning of **overt technology**.

For now, their culture is etched in paths, scents, and small, persistent habits.

## How they all share this world

Everyone here lives by a few non-negotiable rules, and those rules shape their relationships.

### Shared rule: Moisture is fate

- The mats hoard it in gels.
- Trundlers unlock it from dry husk.
- Hunters steal it from bodies.
- Climbers sip it from films.
- Diggers hide from its loss.

Conflicts are almost always about **who controls a reservoir**:

- Predators kill not for flesh but for access to internal sacs.
- Plant lineages “compete” by developing tighter, more sealed skins and more devious ways to capture passing solvent.
- Microbes wage quiet chemical wars for colonization rights on the inner walls of those very sacs and gels.

The result: **symbiosis is often about moisture security**.

- Hosts offer small, calculated leaks of liquid or nutrient-rich tissue.
- Tenants offer services—cleaning, defense, transport of genes or spores—that ultimately bring in more solvent than they cost.

### Shared rule: Close packing is safety and opportunity

The density of bodies:

- Reduces per-organism exposure to the dry atmosphere.
- Allows the spread of shared infrastructure (lattices, tunnels, chemical-gossip insect swarms).
- Creates niches in every crevice, every junction.

But it also makes **disease and parasitism** dangerous. So:

- Hosts evolve elaborate membranes that can fuse or reject grafts with phenomenal specificity.
- Many animal species develop detailed chemosensory systems able to distinguish a safe mat from an infected one, a healthy column from a solvent-leaky, parasite-riddled counterpart.

### Shared rule: Noise and spectacle are luxuries nobody can afford

Communication favors:

- Contact and near-contact: scent, touch, pressure.
- Structures that cost little to maintain: subtle surface patterning instead of vivid color, micro-vibrations instead of roaring calls.

The inhabitants live in a **low-sensory-key world**. For them:

- A faint change in the tang of the air along a corridor can substitute for a shouted warning.
- A slight reorientation of blades in a thicket signals a preferred passageway without any need for signage.

If minds arise, they will be **minds of nuance**, attuned to long-term shifts, quiet anomalies, and the slight misalignment of a familiar pattern.

## If you stand still and let them reveal themselves

Stay in one place for a while—a corridor where cushions tighten into walls and column-footings mark slow intersections.

First you notice the **trundler** procession, dull backs moving like a slow conveyor belt along the street they themselves help maintain.

Behind them, in the deeper shadow of a column cluster, a pair of **hunters** rests motionless, flanks pulsing faintly with restrained breath, eyes hooded against the light. Their positioning—one slightly ahead, one angled out—marks a practiced collaboration, a two-body net.

On a column’s skin just above, three **climbers** inch around in lazy loops, tongues leaving near-invisible wet trails that evaporate sideways into plant tissue before the air can touch most of it. Tiny insects dart between them, occasionally disappearing into dim cracks where digger tunnels open.

Beneath your feet, the **mat** feels different in different directions. One way, it is firmer, subtly ridged along the main trundler route; another way, it is softer, puffing gently around your step, not yet committed to becoming a street. Underneath it all, a **lattice** filament thickens toward the trundler latrine strip, where pellets lie half-crumbled and already veiled with filmer sheets.

A few minutes later, a **confluent** appears—a compact, many-plated creature, head lowered as it sniffs along column roots and cushion rims. It pauses at a junction where predator scent is fresh, and instead of following the trundlers’ broad way, it slips into a narrower, crabbed side-passage between blade thickets, where it must turn sideways and move carefully to avoid cutting its flanks. It has learned this detour; perhaps its mother led it once.

Up above, a thin solvent **haze** gathers over the cushion-dense patch downslope—a barely perceptible blurring of outlines, like heat shimmer, full of invisible molecules that will soon either be reabsorbed or condensed onto films and mats.

If you could stay for years, you would watch:

- The cushions at your sides thicken where trundler traffic is low and thin where it is high.
- The lattice below reroute around a collapsed tunnel chamber, then gently re-etch that void with acids from new digger colonies.
- The column tips above accumulate, shed, and re-seed their epiphyte crusts, in slow response to airborne dust-loads and changing haze patterns.

And in the patterns of that movement—animal and plant, fast and slow, above and below—you would begin to sense that something here is not just alive, but **living together** in a way that amounts to a constantly self-editing city: no blueprints, no architects, only inhabitants whose bodies are very nearly the buildings themselves.

Those who live here are not residents of a world. They are the world, assembled into a continent of crowded flesh that, over eons, is teaching the rock beneath what it means to host life.
