They are old, and they do not remember being many.

What lives here now is not “forest” or “garden” or “colony” in the separate, plural sense. With nothing to break, bite, or uproot it; with no quick floods or fires to tear it apart, the vegetation has grown into unity. It is one thing, continuous, though not uniform: a single planetary organism braided through masonry, water, and time.

It began as disparate microbes, then layered mats, then sallow cushions and fronds. Given millions upon millions of years, growth fronts met and fused, like continental plates closing an ocean. Where two margins touched, nothing tore them back apart. Chemistry and slow mutation blurred their differences until they could share, however clumsily, signals and metabolites. They stitched into each other. They stopped being neighbors and became adjacent tissues.

Now that organism – call it the Lattice, or simply the Body – fills nearly every accessible pore of the drowned architecture. It does not have a brain. It does not have organs in the sense that one region does thinking and another does moving. But it does have gradients, and along gradients come function.

At the smallest, most intimate scale, it is a quilt of lives:

- Sheets of cells pressed flat against stone, forming the thin velvet films that are everywhere. Individually, these are ordinary: they oxidize what seeps from the substrate, they shepherd electrons from one molecule to another, they build and repair their walls a little thicker against the pressure.

- Above them, embroidered into their thickness, are the “lichens” and “cushions”: closer to tissues than to separate plants. They are layered composites of many lineages, each layer doing a piece of the total metabolism – one binding metals, another concentrating rare ions, another shuttling protons along narrow protein corridors like beads on a wire.

- Sprouting from these carpets are the fronds and sponges and cup-towers: macroscopic constructions grown by countless clones acting in slow concord. They are not individuals so much as experimental limbs, grown at the edges of need and chemistry.

From within its own experience, the Body does not distinguish one such form as a plant and another as a fungus or alga; it does not have those words. Each protrusion is just a local elaboration of surface: more area to touch the water, more volume to hold gradients in place. Where a shape helps keep a trickle of energy from diffusing away, evolution has retained it. Where it wastes effort, it has been softened back, dissolved, replaced by something more economical.

Over aeons, information has spread laterally through this mesh – not as sentences or thoughts, but as genes and modules. Tiny looped strands of inheritance move cell to cell by trickling secretion and uptake, slowly enough that a kilometer might take hundreds of thousands of years to traverse. Nonetheless, they do traverse. A trick, once evolved in some chemically rich pocket – say, a better catalyst for teasing energy from a particular metal – does not stay local. It migrates, is copied, recombined, and eventually appears thousands of kilometers away, in mats that have never otherwise shared a direct contact.

In this way, the Body learns, not in the flashing way of a nervous system, but like stone learning to sag under weight: minute adjustments, permanent once made.

If you could speed up time by factors of billions and watch the whole world flicker, the Body would look alive in a way that a single snapshot cannot show. Its glow would pulse, not with seconds-long flashes but with million-year waves moving along its veins. Tissues would thicken here, thin there; frond forests would grow heavy, mineralize, collapse, and be built over by softer films. Entire belts of architecture would slowly change color as one metabolic pathway was supplanted by another.

Inside this continuity, there are regions with distinct characters, as if the Body had organs spread across planetary scales:

1. **The Deep Vault Tissues**

In the largest caverns – those deliberate voids roofed with pillars as thick as hills – the Body is dense and luxuriant by its own austere standards. The pressure is enormous but even; the temperature is nearly invariant. The stone above and below is massive, deep, and chemically well-aged.

Here the mats are thickest, a meter or more, their internal stratification fine as pastry. The uppermost millimeters interact with the open water, straining for the faint chemical differences carried on slow currents. Beneath that, layers sort and pass along byproducts, scavenging every possible electron before letting anything diffuse away.

These vaults act as the organism’s great processing basins. Material that dissolves elsewhere, free ions that drift on planetary drifts, eventually find their way into cul-de-sacs and quiet basins like these. Over time, their chemistry trends toward exhaustion: everything oxidizable oxidized, everything precipitable precipitated. The Body here is gray-brown and steady, its faint bioluminescence low and almost uniform, like the last embers of a sorted fire.

Yet this steadiness is valuable. It is where the Body stores structural mass, where it invests in long-lived tissues that anchor the rest. Many of the spongy floormats in these vaults are centuries, even tens of millennia, old in local time – nearly immortal, regenerating from within as outer layers slough into slime and are reabsorbed. If there is any analogue to a core or skeleton of the organism, it is this: the quiet basins where everything has already been done, and only maintenance continues.

2. **The Seams and Interfaces**

Elsewhere, in the narrow bands where reducing and oxidizing zones meet, the Body becomes something keener.

Along a crack where old, carbon-rich vaults still exhale ghostly gases into spent water, the mats narrow from broad cushions into tight, razor-thin ribbons. Here cells stack in hyper-specialized columns, each layer optimized for a single step in the energetic ladder between “rich” and “poor” chemistry. They hold themselves to the crack as tightly as frost clings to a wire.

Seen under bright illumination, these seams would show as extraordinary bands of color and fine detail: striations of different pigments, micro-spires and labyrinthine ridges that baffle flow, increase residence time, and clamp gradients in place. They are, in functional terms, the organism’s feeding filaments – the places where it does the most living work per unit volume.

At these interfaces, things approach something like activity. Molecules are taken up, modified, passed onwards, excreted, recaptured, in loops that complete in days or weeks instead of centuries. Cells here are comparatively young, turning over in millions of heartbeats we would never hear. Signals – in the form of small, diffusible molecules that inhibit or encourage certain reactions – are exchanged along micrometer paths and, over years, across centimeters. For the Body, this is fast.

As a result, the seams are also its experimental spaces. Mutations, new arrangements of metabolism, appear here first and either prove their worth or are edited away by sheer inefficiency. Over geological spans, the methods that seep out from the seams inform the entire mesh. When the Body, far away in a lethargic vault, “knows” how to handle a new trace metal or endure a new pH, it is because some seam learned first.

3. **The Shafts and Fallow Wells**

In the long vertical wells and shafts, once the veins of an air-breathing city, the Body is hesitant, almost absent.

Open space is unusual. Over most of the drowned architecture, voids have been filled: by drifted silt, by collapses and plasters of growth, by slow mineral infill. But these wells remain eerily clean – perhaps carved in ways that leave few niches, perhaps polished by some ancient flow before stagnation set in.

Around the walls of these shafts, the living film is thin and clinging. The hesitant fronds that reach into the open water stop after some meters, their tips frayed and pale. The central column of water is almost unoccupied; only rare, free-floating cells or shreds of dislodged tissue drift there before getting snagged again.

To the Body, these wells are like scars: routes along which, long ago, energy and matter once moved briskly, and now do not. They offer no secure gradient to invest in. At the scale of the whole, they function as chimneys for very slow convection, organizing the planet’s inner “weather,” but locally they are deserts. The organism extends fingers along them mostly for structural continuity, ensuring that no region is wholly cut off, but it does not put effort into filling them.

4. **The Sealed Cells**

In the honeycombed belts of small rooms – the old habitations and storerooms – the Body is intimately packed.

Many of these cells are nearly or entirely closed, plugged by mineral nodules or decades of growth at their faces. Inside, the contents have little exchange with the world-ocean. Chemistry diverges. Oxygen, if ever present there, was consumed long ago. What remains is a pocket of slow fermentation, of recycling within recycling.

The mats inside are pressed to every surface, forming delicate, springy lozenges of biologically rich matter. They are more diverse, genetically and functionally, than the exposed films outside. Lineages that could not compete in energetic seams or broad vaults persist here in sleepy loops, breaking down old structural molecules, recombining them into forms the broader Body might no longer bother with.

These cells are like lymph nodes or cysts: quarantined, semi-autonomous domains where old information goes to be stored, forgotten, or occasionally rediscovered. Now and then, a crack reopens a room; its stale contents leak out as a faint plume, different enough from surrounding chemistry that nearby tissues curl away. Slowly, some adaptable strain creeps into the breach, bridges the insult, and in doing so, samples whatever ancient molecules seeped from inside. Those that are metabolically useful may be incorporated into the Body’s general repertoire. In this way, the sealed cells act as deep archives of dead ends and abandoned paths, occasionally re-mined for value.

5. **The Bare Patches and Scars**

Where walls have fractured recently in geological terms, shedding ultrafine silt or exuding harshly unbalanced fluids, the Body is absent or minimal. These “burns” are the equivalent of wounds, not from an active attacker but from the architecture’s own slow shifting.

At their edges, specialized mats gather – organisms with patient tolerances for acidity, salinity, or toxicity beyond the usual. They form thin, grainy rinds, gradually conditioning the chemistry until more generalist tissues can creep in after them.

These pioneer fringes are like the Body’s immune system, in the loosest possible sense: they are first responders to environmental insult. But they do not rush. They ooze, on scales of decades and centuries.

—

All of these regions are the Body’s flesh. Its movements are invisibly slow; its signals are diffuse. Yet there is another level to what lives here, built atop this long continuity: memory, of a sort, written in brick and biochemistry.

The drowned architecture is not just inert support for life. The organisms here evolved against it, and in doing so, they have pressed back. Not violently – they lack the energy for reshaping on a heroic scale – but insistently, with the leverage of dissolution and precipitation.

Where once there were sharp corners, the Body has slumped them with its own mineral shells. Where once inscriptions were crisp, its excretions have softened them. Where once voids were clean, its secretions and skeletons have half-filled them. Over time, this has blurred the builders’ intent. But it has also incorporated that intent into the organism’s large-scale structure.

If the ancient people set out their city with avenues running along a favored axis, then those avenues became gutters of slightly different current; the Body colonized them accordingly. If they stacked their reservoirs and wells in a particular vertical logic, the resultant patterns of lingering flow still echo through the labyrinth; the organism’s densest tissues have grown in response.

Thus, some of the city’s original ordering – its streets, its districts, its great assembly halls – is still faintly there, not as purpose in any human sense, but as subtle anisotropies in the living mesh. To traverse the Body along one of these old avenues would be to follow a path where its tissues have a certain repeated rhythm: thicker-thinner, frond-mat-frond-mat, echoing, century after century, the old negative space between walls and pillars.

Does the Body know this? No. It does not conceive of architecture. It barely conceives of sequence. Its only “knowledge” is encoded in relative concentrations and shaped proteins: Do this reaction here, not there; grow thicker when such-and-such ion is present; dim your glow when the gradient weakens.

Yet taken in aggregate, this biochemical grammar spells out a very slow, unspoken map of the drowned city: not of names and uses, but of how matter and energy thread through stone.

If one insists on looking for mind, there are only glimmers.

Consider: over planetary distances, the Body is electrically conductive in a patchy, salt-limited way. It is full of redox couples – tiny batteries arranged in series and parallel, etched into films and fronds. Fluctuations in one place – a seam that suddenly becomes a fraction more energetic through some tectonic nudge – will set up tiny differences in potential that can, in principle, ripple outward as faint changes in ion balance.

Over normal time, these are trivial. Over millions of years, they may matter. Regions of tissue that, by chance, respond slightly more cohesively to such fluctuations may coordinate their chemistry better, survive better, leave more descendants. Through this glacial selection, the Body may have developed larger-scale feedbacks: a tendency for distant seams to come into rough equilibrium with each other, or for vaults to keep their internal chemistry within narrow tolerances.

Is that thought? No. It’s homeostasis written at a scope that feels almost planetary. It is not “about” anything; it does not imagine; it simply is the emergent steadiness of something that has lasted a very long time in a hard place.

If anything in this world still remembers the builders, it does so without comprehension. Within some sealed chambers, their objects persist: a bronze tool, crusted; ceramic jars, filled now with gelid biomass; perhaps a carved tablet whose figures are softened but legible under a finger. Around these relics, life grows no differently than around any other inert stone. A copper hinge is just another slow source of ions; a carved relief just another texture to be followed, mirrored in growth as fronds fork around raised lines.

The humans – or whatever hands laid these bricks – are gone so utterly that no trace of their pattern-seeking minds survives in any mindlike substrate. Their memory is held only in shapes and chemistries that no longer mean what they meant, and in the chance that some of their buried organic remains still seep nutrients into a niche of the Body that knows them as nothing but food.

So what lives here is not haunted. It is not watched by ghosts of its architects. It is alone.

Alone – but not empty. Everywhere in this dark, clear water, things are very close together, and nearly everything that can be alive is alive.

- On each square centimeter of stone, dozens of micro-environments: a little acidic pocket in a pit, a metal-rich droplet in a crack, a film of slightly fresher water along the ceiling, all with their specialized microfauna and flora (if one can use such terms when all are, in truth, single phylum’s worth of biochemical consensus).

- In every cubic meter of the spongy floormats, billions upon billions of cells in layered collaboration: some making structure, some catalyzing reactions, some sacrificing and lysing to feed neighbors when local gradients sag.

- Across every shaft, buttress, and vault, fronds and banners flexing imperceptibly with the very slow tilting of currents, each blade a compromise between maximizing area and minimizing the risk of mechanical failure over centuries of pressure and mineral load.

These are the citizens of this world, if any word like that may be used: cells and tissues, not persons. They do not have histories in ways they can tell, but they do have lineage: an unbroken chain of division reaching back to when the first of their kind found wet brick and stale chemistry and said, in its blind, enzymatic way, “Here, too, something can be done.”

To enter this place from outside would be to meet no eyes. No jaws would open, no tentacles would flex. One would be enveloped in sameness: soft films on every side, faint glows at the edges, a silence so complete that even your own heartbeats would feel intrusive.

Yet you would be inside a living thing as definitively as a parasite in an artery. Your presence – your warmth, your oxygen, your motile violence – would be a spike in graphs that had lain flat for ages. Local films would denature at your touch; some would bleach, poisoned by reactive species escaping your metabolism. Others, in time, would adapt, seizing upon your leaking nutrients, your disrupted stones, your rusting tools.

The Body would not notice you as an individual. It would not hate or welcome you. It would simply be perturbed, and then, if the perturbation persisted and was exploitable, its tissues would shift accordingly: new genes would spread; new structures would grow. If you brought light and it lasted, photopigments would, over eons, redevelop, and thin leaves of something like algae might fan into the newly possible gradient between photon and dark.

If you came and went quickly, you would be nothing but a bruise: a trail of broken fronds, a few cloudy plumes of dead cells. These, too, would be reabsorbed.

In the end, what lives here is an answering quiet to the vanished city’s clamor. Once, perhaps, there were marketplaces above these vaults, cries and songs echoing off the very arches whose softened ghosts now cradle mats of blind chemistry. Once there were intentions laid into every corridor and cell.

Now there is an intention of a different sort: the intention implicit in survival. The Body has one project only – to continue – and it pursues it with calm, undramatic thoroughness across strata, centuries, and bricks.

No heart beats for it. No lungs fill. But the sum of millions of infinitesimal redox steps, multiplied inconceivably, is a sort of breath, drawn not in seconds but in ages. It is inhaling still, very slowly, whatever usable difference remains between stone and water, between then and now.

When that difference is finally gone – when every last reducible grain has been oxidized, when every gradient has flattened into thermodynamic quiet – the Body will thin. Its films will retreat into their own residues, its tissues will lose coherence, its last flickers of glow will be lost in the same perfect dark that greeted the first cell to cling to brick.

After that, only the architecture will remain: softened, encrusted, but bare again. A city scaled to continents, drowned and stripped, waiting in cold, clear black for no one at all.

Until then, what lives here is this: a single, vast, patient organism woven through the bones of another vanished order, feeling nothing, thinking nothing, yet endlessly, delicately at work.
