They are not many, and they are not in a hurry.

What lives here has learned to be the same way the water is: persistent, exact, uninterested in spectacle. The inhabitants fit the plateau so closely that, at first, you will not know you are seeing them.

## The People of the Wet High Place

They are not tall.

An adult stands only a little higher than the shoulder of the tallest ferns. Seen from above—through the mist, from a shelf of rock—you might miss them entirely: rounded backs, damp-dark hair, clothing the color of moss and stone. The plateau has no use for straight lines or big gestures, and neither do they.

### Bodies

Their bodies hold water as well as live in it.

Skin: matte rather than shiny, with a soft, almost velvety grain that breaks surface tension. Droplets do not roll quickly down them; they gather, cling, fatten, then slip away in slow beads. Where your skin would prune and chill, theirs simply darkens. The outermost layer is a little thicker, a little more resilient, like the soft bark of a young tree.

Color: variations of deep brown and muted olive, with cooler undertones. On some, the cheeks and shoulders carry faint mineral tints—grey-green, bluish, rust—where the skin often meets weeping stone. Sunlight rarely strikes them directly enough to mark them; it is water that writes on them instead.

Hair: dense, heavy when dry, but rarely fully dry. It grows in layered waves or tight coils that tend to clump when wet, forming natural channels for runoff. Many braid thin minerals or cut reeds into it, not as ornament so much as scaffolding: structures that guide dripping water away from neck and ears so sound remains clear.

Eyes: wide-set, with large irises and a soft reflective sheen, like something tuned for a world of diffuse light. Their color sits in the same range as wet stone—grey, pale amber, deep mossy green. In mist they see well; in sharp, direct brilliance (which is rare here) they tend to look down, half-close their lids, and wait for the clouds to re-form.

Hands and feet: long, spreading fingers and toes with slight webbing at the bases, not enough for true swimming, more for stability on soft ground and slick stone. Skin at the tips is thick but deeply ridged, gripping like a leaf’s underside. Their gait is low to the ground—knees bent, weight tested before fully placed—so each step becomes an agreement with the substrate: I will not sink if you will not slide.

Breath: steady and unhurried. The plateau is warm and full, and they are used to its heaviness. Their chests rise shallowly; the sense is of a creature not needing to drag breath in, but simply exchanging it with a surrounding that is already close to their own temperature.

They do not smell of sweat the way a drier species might. The air takes care of cooling; their own chemistry is muted. Instead they carry faint traces of things they touch often: the iron tang of a preferred seep, the green of crushed moss, the resin of certain shrubs.

## Clothing and Adornment

They dress the way plants grow: in layers that manage water.

Base layers are of softened inner bark and long fibers stripped from certain plateau shrubs. These are beaten, soaked, then worked by hand against stone until they become flexible, somewhere between felt and cloth. Worn next to the skin, they do not repel water but hold it evenly, preventing the sudden chill of evaporative patches.

Outer layers are more deliberate. They weave or knot broader leaves—thick, waxy ones that naturally bead water—into cloaks and wraps. The pattern is shingle-like, each leaf overlapping the next so droplets are guided downward and away, much as the plants themselves guide water to their roots. Over time, the leaves become tougher, darker, infused with oils they rub in from seeds and insect wax.

Colors: mostly the natural spectrum of what they use—browns, dark greens, slate greys. Occasionally a strip of brighter pigment appears, stained with a mineral slurry or a rare flower’s juice: a line of dull copper along a sleeve, a muted purple mark at the hem. These are not fashion so much as notation—reminders, family signs, or quiet acknowledgments of certain places.

Footwear is minimal. Slippers of layered moss and bark are bound to the sole with woven cords if a journey crosses unusually sharp stone, but often they go barefoot. The ground speaks richly through bare skin, and they value that contact.

Jewelry, when present, is small and tactile: a polished pebble with a natural groove, hung at the throat; a ring of carefully bored mineral lip from an old drip-ledge, worn on a cord at the wrist. Many objects are chosen less for look and more for the sound they make when handled—a tiny clink, a soft tap, a whisper of roughness—adding private notes to the plateau’s constant water-music.

## Shelters and Dwellings

They do not build houses; they select places.

The plateau is full of ready-made rooms: undercut shelves, shallow caverns behind weeping walls, dry pockets under the roots of the rare larger shrubs. The people find these and refine them in ways subtle enough that from a distance they still look like accidents of stone and moss.

A dwelling might be a dry bench just above a perpetually damp floor. They lay down woven mats of bark-fiber there, thickened with compressed moss that has been sun-warmed and dried carefully on the few bright days. Above, they sometimes suspend a thin canopy of interlaced reeds and leaves. This does not keep water out; it slows it, turning large, heat-stealing drips into many tiny threads that are almost sound rather than wetness.

Entrances are rarely clear doorways. More often you notice a slightly cleaner line through a veil of hanging ferns, or a narrow gap where the moss is flattened by repeated passing. Inside, space is not large; a family might sleep in an area no larger than a modest bedchamber, tucked into its curve of stone the way a pool tucks into its basin.

They do not gather all in one place. Instead of a village, there is a net of scattered, half-hidden shelters, each close to a particular feature of water—a favored seep, a pool that glows on certain nights, a resonant drip-formation whose sound they find companionable. Kin-groups cluster around these water-places, their dwellings within quiet walking distance of one another but separated by folds of land and curtains of mist.

Nothing is permanent. When a seep shifts or a ledge begins to flake, they slowly ease their presence away—mats carried elsewhere, cords unwound, stored implements moved to another naturally dry recess. The old spot is left to return to itself: a faint, quickly softening memory in the moss.

## Tools and Making

Their tools are few, precise, and mostly soft.

### Materials

- **Stone** is used sparingly and never broken in large ways. They do not quarry. Instead, they collect what the plateau already loosens: pebbles that roll out of banks, flakes from natural fractures, drip-lip fragments fallen from mineral terraces. These they grind against harder surfaces to shape edges or rounds, rarely sharper than a careful thumbnail.

- **Bone** appears, but seldom and small. Animals are not numerous, and nothing is wasted. From a long limb bone they may make several narrow tools: awls for piercing, smoothers for working fibers, little curved spatulas for scraping minerals from weeping walls.

- **Plant fiber** is their main medium. They twist, braid, and weave it wet so it dries into shape. Baskets, cords, slings, mats, straps—these can be unmade, repaired, repurposed. Nothing rigid if it can be flexible.

- **Wax and resin** from certain shrubs and plateau-dwelling insects are valued highly. They soak fibers in these to create water-slowing layers, line the inside of containers, and sometimes seal small caches in rock crevices.

### Kinds of Tools

- **Carriers for water**: not to move it far away, but to take it from one kind of water-place to another. Shallow, wide-mouthed baskets lined with leaf and wax, made less to hold volume and more to preserve the particular “taste” of a seep long enough to compare it with another.

- **Filters**: neat bundles of cleaned moss, stratified with sand and powdered mineral. These are used to clarify certain seep waters, particularly those rich in slow, glowing life or rare dissolved elements. They are also used to make small infusions—water passed through crushed leaves or spores for medicine.

- **Scrapers and polishers**: hand-sized stones smoothed except for one working edge. These polish the lip of a frequently used rock basin or the handle of a favored tool. Over years, the scraped stones themselves become objects of significance, marked by use more than by design.

- **Sound-objects**: small frames of bent twig strung with fibers of varying tension, hung where drops fall in particular patterns. The impacts make quiet plucked sounds; the device wears away and is replaced or moved. These are more listening instruments than music-makers: ways to make the water’s pattern legible.

They rarely leave clear-cut marks. When they must cut a branch, they prefer to twist and peel. When they must harvest moss, they take in scattered patches to avoid leaving bald scars. Their technological thinking leans toward cooperation with existing forms: encourage this seep, slow that rivulet, catch what already falls, polish what is already almost a bowl.

## Food and Sustenance

They eat what grows between water and stone, and what drifts in the thin, hidden currents.

### Plants

They cultivate, but not in rows.

Certain mosses, richer in starch, are gently encouraged around favored stones. The people will clear away encroaching species, crumble a little mineral-rich sediment where they want the good moss to spread, shade it or open the canopy above depending on what it likes. Harvesting is done by pinching off upper layers, leaving the base intact.

Ferns: some are edible when tightly coiled. They know which by taste, by smell, by the look of the sheen on the cut stem. These they take sparingly, leaving the majority to unfurl and replenish.

Shrubs: a few bear small, dense fruits that grow along stems rather than at tips, protected from constant wet by tight-pressed leaves. These fruits are tart, almost salty; they eat them fresh, and also mash them into pastes that are spread thin on flat stones and left in brief sun-breaks to thicken and concentrate.

Roots: the soil is shallow, but some plants send thickened rhizomes sideways rather than deep. The people read the lay of stems and leaves to know where to dig minimal, surgical holes, prising out a segment with a horn- or bone-hoe, then folding the soil back.

Fungi: shy, flat things clinging under overhangs, or splayed like pale fans on the underside of damp logs. A sharp nose can distinguish the edible ones: they smell faintly like wet grain or clean rock. Some they slice thin and dry on warm stone ledges; others are eaten fresh for their medicinal qualities.

### Animals

They take animal life rarely and with deliberation.

Small, translucent amphibian-things in the pools are an occasional food: lifted with cupped hands or woven scoops, never in greedy numbers. They have very little flesh; most of the value is in their concentrated minerals and oils.

The pale, long-limbed quadrupeds that walk the mossways are not usually hunted. There is a quiet truce between them and the people: each follows established paths, each avoids the other’s young. On the rare occasions these creatures die naturally near habitation, every part is used: hide treated for tougher garments, bones for tools, meat shared widely.

Insects and similar small things are gathered more usually for their by-products than as food—waxes, resins, silk-like fibers—but some larvae, fat with stored moisture and nutrients, are eaten during certain seasons as concentrated sustenance.

### Water as Food

Water is not just hydration; it is a spectrum of flavors and effects.

They have names for different classes of water: the bright, quick-tasting seep that sharpens thought; the heavy, metallic trickle that strengthens the bones but must be drunk in moderation; the soft, almost sweet condensation that collects under particular stone lips just before dawn, good for soothing the stomach or calming a fever.

A person here drinks according to need, not merely thirst. They learn young which weeping wall cures a certain ache, which bowl’s overflow steadies the heart after grief, which pool’s water should not be drunk at all but is perfect for soaking aching limbs.

## Social Life and Organization

Their society is spread thin but woven tight.

### Groups

They live in small kin clusters of three to seven, much like the plateau’s scattered features: one here by a resonant bowl, another there by a glowing pool, a third a long, careful walk away near a dry-tongued overhang. These clusters form loose constellations—what, elsewhere, you might call clans—that share a set of water-places and paths.

There is no central authority. Influence accumulates in those who know the plateau’s moods best: elders who can predict when the mist will thicken long before it does, midwives who sense when a certain seep’s mineral load has shifted and should be avoided for a time, quiet walkers who know alternate routes when a familiar moss-bridge becomes too saturated and soft.

Decisions happen when paths converge. A narrow ledge where many must pass, a favored open rock shelf where the sound is less dense and conversation easier—these become informal councils. Words are not rushed. A thought is often spoken, then left for a span of days, revisited when someone next passes and has had time to listen for the plateau’s answer.

### Childhood and Age

Children are not kept away from wet or danger; they are taught how to move in it almost at once. A baby strapped against a caretaker’s chest feels each shift of footing, each hesitation on a slick stone. Toddlers are allowed to fall frequently—but always in selected places, over moss, into shallow pools—so they learn how it feels to slip and how to catch themselves.

Games often involve balance and stillness: who can stand longest on a small, tilting rock; who can trace with bare feet the exact course of a root hidden in soil; who can move from one side of a seep-wall to the other touching only dry patches.

Age is visible in posture, not in frailty. The elders bend forward more, lowering their center of gravity, but their steps are still sure. Their role shifts from foraging effort to pattern memory: they become walking archives of “when this pool shone last,” “when those mosses grew thickest,” “what the sound of that hollow means when it is two tones higher.”

## Language and Thought

Their language is shaped by water and by the nearness of sound to everything.

They speak softly and close to one another. Conversation at a distance is nearly impossible over the plateau’s constant murmur, so they do not expect it. Words are for near ears. For farther communication they use gestures—broad, deliberate motions of hand and arm that contrast with the otherwise economical stillness of their bodies.

Their spoken words are rich in onomatopoeia. There are verbs whose sounds mirror specific drip-patterns: *tchik-tchik* for fast, light tapping on leaf, *golum* for a heavier plunk into soil, *sir-r-r* for sheets over stone. They use these not only for water, but for analogies: a person can *golum* into a conversation, or an old memory can *sir-r-r* through the mind.

They have many words for states between. Between wet and dry, between soft and hard, between moving and still. Their grammar leans on aspect rather than tense: not so much when something happened, but whether it is continuing, flickering, thickening, thinning.

They think in terms of patterns repeating, not events one after another. A story might begin, “In a mist like this, but thinner,” rather than “Many years ago.” Time is anchored to environmental texture: “When the standing wave in the northern bowl hummed lower than my voice,” or “In the season when the copper flowers stayed closed three mornings in a row.”

Written language, if you can call it that, is indirect. They sometimes arrange moss growth by careful scuffing and guiding, making subtle crescents or lines that only those who know to look for them can read: a sign that someone has passed, that a seep has changed, that a certain shelter is being used.

There is also sound-sign: hanging leaves cut to flutter at a very particular frequency, or small pebbles placed under drips so they make a characteristic rhythm. A person passing will hear, under the general noise, a pattern: *tik … tik-tik … tik*, and know that a kin-cluster is nearby or that a certain plant has been harvested recently.

## Belief, Story, and the Plateau Itself

They do not separate themselves strongly from the place. To speak about “the plateau” as an external thing would feel to them like speaking of “the rest of the body” as separate from the hand.

### The Waters

They distinguish between *hungry water* and *patient water*.

- **Hungry water** pulls: it undercuts stone, sucks at soil, carves tiny gullies. They watch it warily, do not sleep where its sound is sharp, do not place shelters over hollows where they can feel an insistent draw. Hungry water is not evil; it is a necessary force, part of the long shaping. But it is not to be leaned upon.

- **Patient water** beads, clings, makes no sudden moves. It fattens moss, slowly thickens mineral lips, condenses along stable lines. The best shelters are near patient water. The best stories are told over it.

They have stories of a deep, unseen reservoir below the plateau—*the Held Pool*—which breathes in and out over lives and generations. When mist is fat and constant, the Held Pool is said to be full, pressing its wanting into every pore of rock. When things dry slightly, it has drawn back, sighing. No one has seen it, but they speak of it as a slow animal that has chosen to lie beneath them, keeping the land up with its broad back.

### Stone and Change

Stone, to them, is not dead. It has speeds of change so slow that only the plateau and its long memory can track them. They listen to the faint cracklings and pops of mineral layers; elders can sometimes feel with bare palms where a shelf will flake next season, or where a new seep will emerge in a few years.

There is a quiet reverence for places where water and stone reach equilibrium—old drip-lips that neither thicken nor crumble, polished basins whose rims stay exactly the same through remembered time. These are seen as proofs of possible stillness within motion, and many important conversations or rituals happen nearby.

### Weirdness as Living Signs

The patterned moss that looks almost like script? To them, this is not language, but listening. They believe that where moss takes those shapes, the plateau is aligning many tiny tendencies at once: light, mineral, inclination, condensation. Such places are visited for guidance. One might sit, eyes half-closed, letting the visual patterns seep in the way a sound seeps in elsewhere.

Standing wave hollows—where one precise tone emerges if you stand in the right place—are known intimately. Each has a name: sometimes an onomatopoetic echo of its hum, sometimes a metaphor—*Bone-Softener*, *Child-Quiet*, *Memory-Shifter*. People go there for personal recalibration: to sing with the tone, to listen, to feel their sense of self briefly dissolve into the plateau’s organized vibration.

The faintly glowing pools are not worshiped, but regarded the way you might regard a relative with an unusual talent. They are visited at the right times, offerings of nothing more than presence and silent observation. Children are brought to see their own reflections outlined in that low light, told in soft words: “This is how the plateau remembers itself in the dark.”

## Relationship to Other Life

They do not tame animals; they share routes with them.

The pale quadrupeds, the gliders, the pool-dwellers: each has its own understanding of where to place foot or fin. The people watch these patterns carefully. A new animal path appearing along a ridge might signal a subtle shift in ground firmness; a change in glider landing spots may herald a new warm updraft from an emerging seep.

They sometimes mark shared places with double-care: humans tread here, animals tread here. Moss-cushions are left particularly thick, stones particularly clean of loose grit. In their thinking, a well-kept path is one that carries the weight of more than one kind of life without yielding.

Predation by other creatures is accepted as part of the overall circulation. When a glider drops low to take one of the small water-skim insects, they watch but do not intervene. Loss and gain are background currents. Only when something threatens the plateau’s long balance—a disease in the moss, an invasive dry-weed creeping up from lower slopes—do they act in concentrated ways, uprooting, burning in low, smoldering heat, spreading counter-growths.

## Change, Memory, and Death

They measure time in water-patterns more than in seasons.

A life might be recalled as: “Born when the northern bowl first found its double-tone,” “Reached full strength in the mist-heavy decade when no midday sun was seen,” “Died after the last shining of the western pool.” Their calendars are rings of remembered hydrological oddities.

Death is neither hidden nor dramatized. The body is washed—not with any special water, but with the normal, constant trickle of a familiar seep—then placed where dissolution will be thorough but gentle. Often this is in cavities behind weeping walls, where flow is enough to erase form without scattering too widely. Clothing and tools are given back to kin, not buried.

They mark the death-place not with stones or carvings but with a slightly altered flow: perhaps a new arrangement of guiding leaves that causes water to sheet more broadly for a time, or a small dam of pebbles that makes a new, higher note in the drip-sound. Over years, these adjustments wear away, merging with the plateau’s own preferences; the remembrance becomes diffuse, like ash in soil, like dissolved minerals in the general run.

Elders sometimes choose their own resting-place well in advance: a ledge from which they have listened all their life, a moss-curve they helped guide into being. It is as much a return to a familiar contour as a departure.

## Visitors and Boundaries

They do not often leave the plateau. The outer slopes—steeper, drier, louder with wind and with the kinds of water that fall hard and run fast—feel to them not alien, but unsettlingly quick. The idea of rivers, of water that goes away and does not immediately return as mist or seep, carries a flavor of wastefulness.

When something from outside arrives—seeds carried by birds, a stranger from a lower world climbing up—they regard it first as they would any new pattern of dripping or mist: watch, listen, see what path it chooses for itself.

Strangers who move with care, who learn quickly where to step and how to read the sound, are gradually woven into the net of overlooking: a shelter offered, a safe path indicated via a re-hung leaf or a tapped stone. Those who flounder and flail, who break moss mats and thrash through fragile driplines, are gently but firmly guided back down-slope, not as punishment but as a correction of misfit.

They know, in a quiet way, that their plateau is not infinite. They can stand at certain edges and feel the shift in the pattern of air and stone beyond. But they do not long for elsewhere. To them, richness is not in extent but in depth: in how many notes you can hear in one hollow, how many kinds of water you can distinguish between two outcrops, how many quiet agreements you can make with the same piece of ground over a lifetime.

---

What lives here, then, is a people whose bodies, tools, stories, and days are tuned to a world that never quite dries, never quite rushes. They are as numerous as the plateau can hold without haste, as visible or invisible as the mist allows, as enduring as any creature can be in a place where everything is always, slowly, becoming something else.

They are not masters of this high wet land, not tenants either. They are one of the ways it has found to continue being itself.
