They are tall, but not in the way of height measured from the ground up. They are tall inwardly, in how they think in stretches of cliff instead of stretches of land, in how their bodies are arranged around drop and ledge and the certainty of falling.

They are not native to plains. Most of them have never stood on a horizon that lies flat in all directions, and the very idea makes their throat-tubes tighten. They were, as far as they themselves remember, always of the stone-sheer: born in cavities that open directly onto a thousand-meter fall, raised on platforms where the nearest thing to “down” is not ground but a vanishing depth of air and haze.

If you saw one of them for the first time, you might think of a creature grown from scaffolding and tendon.

Their bodies are long, narrow, and jointed in multiple axes. Think of an elongated torso that can pivot halfway along its length, allowing the front and rear sections to twist independently. Along this axis run three or four major support struts of living bone-equivalent, each faced externally with a dull mineral sheath grown from their own metabolism — a patchwork of plates and filaments that double as armor and as electrodes.

Limbs come in pairs, but not the way humans think of arms and legs. They have six, sometimes eight, and none of them is committed to a single role. Every limb is a climbing limb, a bracing limb, a carrying limb. Each emerges from the torso on a universal joint, then splits once or twice down its length into sub-shafts, each capable of locking together into a rigid spar or folding back into a bundle of nested segments. When fully unfurled, a single limb can span several times the width of the body and anchor to three points on the stone.

At their ends are not hands, not paws, but arrays of adaptable contact pads: some hard, toothed and wedge-shaped for biting into rough rock; some soft and compliant with countless micro-papillae that electrostatically grip smooth alloy; some needle-fine hooks meant for threading into the cracks and hollows etched by the solvent-gas. They walk by constant triangulation, always over-braced, always with at least three points secured before they move the fourth. Walking for them is continuous partial suspension.

Faces, insofar as they have them, are not frontals. Their sensory cluster rings the upper third of the torso: a collar of nodes that can swivel as a unit. Vision is scattered: dozens of small, faceted lenses embedded in dark, glossy pits, each tuned to slightly different bands from long infrared to short ultraviolet and even into the narrow window where the electrical discharges of living films show up as a muted glow. They do not look “at” something with two eyes; they bathe it, tasting it with a constellation of apertures.

Where a human mouth would be, they have something more like a recessed orifice surrounded by muscular cilia — a slow intake, not meant for rapid feeding. Their metabolism does not support quick gorging. They process their nutrients with the patience of the rock around them: thin suspensions drawn from solvent-wells, scraped slurries of mat-organisms, concentrates refined in communal vats. Chewing is a rare, almost ceremonial act, reserved for dense mineralized tissues that require grinding plates deep inside them.

Their skin is not quite skin. It is a layered composite, a living laminate of organic fiber and inlaid mineral channels. In the outermost layer, just under a dust-roughened protective film, run thin conductive tracks, like circuitry; within, piezoelectric nodules; beneath, an elastic matrix that can subtly alter thickness and stiffness in response to stress. When they brace against the cliff, their skin stiffens along tension lines, becoming semi-rigid girders. When they rest, it relaxes, and they drape more like cloth upon their own framework.

They are quietly luminous when they wish to be. Under low light you would see faint tracings along their limbs, slow pulses of charge traveling their integumentary channels, linking node to node. This is not decoration; it is language, health-report, tension-map, social signal.

Their movement is slow compared to the darting grazers and agile gliders that share their world. It must be. Every step is a negotiation with gravity and with the subtle creep of rock. They do not leap unless a life is at stake, and even then it is not the explosive jump of a sprinter but a long-planned, precisely timed traverse of vectors they have calculated in their bones.

Children — they do have an analog to that stage — are the only ones you might catch moving with what a human would call recklessness. Their limbs are proportionally shorter, their mass lighter, their fear of the abyss not yet fully internalized. They scamper along the inner faces of galleries and practice extended hangs over shallow shafts, learning the language of stress through the small tingling pains of overstrained skin and complaining bone. Adults watch from secure perches, not intervening unless a pattern starts to go irrevocably wrong.

Their biology did not arise from this cliff in the way the mats and columnar growths did. Somewhere deep in their history is a different chemistry, a time when liquid once had meaning to their line. You see it in vestigial organs: in shrunken sacs that once might have held fluid, in biochemical pathways that seem strangely redundant in this world of gas-phase metabolism. They carry that ancestry like an old design repurposed — overlaid, patched, never entirely erased.

But they are fully of this place now. Their blood-equivalent carries charge as much as it carries ions; their cells maintain themselves via redox gradients drawn directly from the solvent atmosphere through microtubular lung-analogues that ramify just beneath the skin. They do not drink in any way you’d recognize; instead they “breathe” for long, deliberate sessions in solvent-rich hollows, immersing themselves waist-deep in denser pools of gas, allowing it to permeate their porous outer layers while internal regulators manage uptake and avoid chemical burns.

They do not simply live on the cliff; they are knotted into it.

Where their city runs deepest — behind the platforms, within the galleries and shafts they drive into the stone — there are hybrid zones where their tissue and the mineral world literally co-grow. Here they cultivate symbiotic lattices: living girders that fuse to anchor points in the cliff, fed both by city power and by slow solvent seepage. In these spaces, structural supports are not simply inert beams. They are part organism — responsive, self-healing within limits, threaded with nerve-equivalents that report strain and microfracture patterns.

The inhabitants refer to them with words that translate poorly: something between “root” and “cousin” and “instrument.” They do not exactly consider them people, not in the sense they reserve for independent, mobile minds, but neither are they mere tools. There are rituals around cutting into them, around redirecting their growth, around “settling a line” — a slow process of coaxing a living support to lay down a new branch along a calculated stress path. These rituals are half engineering, half priesthood; the distinction is mostly historical.

The people of these heights think in time-planes. Their language has tense structures not for past and future alone, but for processes that span specific orders of magnitude. There is a grammatical form for events that happen over the course of a single solvent-cycle, another for phenomena that unfold over the lifetime of a mat-layer, another for shifts that only the growth of a crystal-column could mark. They can speak of “the quick future” — the next few rotations of the star — and “the deep future” — the next change in gas-phase composition; they can nest these, set them against each other like the crossing of two bridges at different heights.

Their stories are braided the same way. A tale may begin with the decision of one worker to adjust the angle of a gravity turbine’s vanes and end, three generations later, with the failure or saving of an entire platform as a slow rockflow shears beneath its anchors. It is not that they are patient so much as that they are exquisitely trained to think in load paths and consequence chains. A child is taught that every cut into the cliff is a question asked of its deep, slow will, and that the cliff will answer eventually.

You can see this in the city’s shape. They do not build “up” or “out” the way a plains-born species might. They build *along*, in bands that mirror the cliff’s own stress stratification. New platforms bloom where geology offers a long-lived neutral layer, where seismic ripples have historically bent rather than broken. Expansion is a conversation: preliminary sapping holes drilled in careful arrays, solvent flow measured, microseismic whisperings listened to through sensoria pressed directly into raw rock.

It is wrong to say they worship the cliff, but they revere its predictability. Their ethics are grounded in not overloading it, in not forcing it into failure modes that propagate catastrophically. A crime, here, at its worst, is not an act of violence against a body but an act of negligence toward structure: corrupting survey data, falsifying load calculations, over-extracting alloy from a support zone. To cause a collapse, even a small one, by foreseeable error is a stain that follows not just the individual but their lineage. Names — they do use them — carry encoded in their sound not just ancestry but the integrity record of the projects those ancestors led.

Their social units are not nuclear families nor tribes, but *lines* and *plates*.

A line is a descent not just of genes but of craft: a lineage specialized historically in one form of interface with the cliff. There are anchor-lines whose expertise is in deep boring, reading stress, bonding living girders to stone. There are span-lines, who design and maintain the bridges and cableways, whose bodies are often subtly different: lighter frames, finer limb-segmentation, more developed electroreceptive senses for detecting tension in strands. There are harvest-lines, focused on the living films and columns, whose integument is slightly more porous, their metabolism tuned to handle a broader spectrum of mat-emitted volatiles.

A plate is a co-resident community occupying a given platform complex. Plates are temporary in a geological sense — they may persist for a few hundred, a few thousand years before structural and ecological changes require relocation or partial abandonment. But for the individuals, these are home: each platform edge, each internal shaft mouth, a known coordinate both physically and socially.

Each line maintains members across multiple plates, to avoid both genetic and knowledge inbreeding. Each plate hosts members of many lines, because a platform must be a complete organism: it needs anchors and spans, harvesters and tuners, storytellers and measure-keepers. The interplay of loyalty — to your line’s honor and specialization, to your plate’s survival and culture — is one of the principal axes along which their politics moves.

They do have politics, though at first glance their world seems too slow, too integrated with stone to admit the jitter of faction. Conflicts here do not center on fast territory grabs or resource scarcity in the acute sense. Instead they revolve around risk allocation across the deep future.

Some plates prefer aggressive use of solvent peaks: pushing extraction and construction hardest when the atmosphere carries more of the erosive gas, accepting a higher incidence of micro-collapses and structural fatigue, gambling that their monitoring systems and living girders can accommodate the stress. Others advocate conservative cycles: heavy work during solvent lows, dormancy in solvent highs, letting the mats and rock do more intuitive self-correction.

On the surface this seems a mere difference of engineering philosophy. Beneath, it maps onto aesthetic, even spiritual divides. The first favor a vision of themselves as active agents sculpting the cliff, “answering” its slow pressure with design. The second see themselves as guests on an already-articulated surface, obliged to read and accommodate more than to press and reshape.

These debates play out in council-caverns lined with strain-responsive panels, where raised voices are rare but shifts in bioluminescent patterns along participants’ skins speak volumes. Public gatherings are choreographies of color and micro-movement: a flash of indigo down a forelimb to signal acquiescence, a sudden stiffening of backplates to indicate a hard refusal, a synchronized dimming across a whole line’s representatives to mark a collective shame or apology.

Their speech, when they use audible sound, is soft and sparing: clicks, soft exhalations, brief chords produced by vibrating tensioned membranes along their thoraxes. Much of their communication passes through other frequencies entirely. They flicker micro-currents through surface conductors, setting up localized electric fields patterned with information that those nearby can decode through their own skin sensors. They lean, quite literally, on the stone: pressing themselves to the cliff to feel, through deep-seated electroreceptors, the infrasonic murmurs of distant machinery, the tick of some far turbine, the slow crack-propagation after a small rockfall.

Privacy, for them, is not a question of soundproof walls but of decoupling from networks of signal and stress. To be alone is to retreat to a place where your weight is taken by a known, simple span, where your skin is in contact with only quiet, pre-mapped stone, where no web of shared current runs. In such spaces — introspection niches, often tucked just inside the mouths of minor maintenance shafts — individuals dim their bioluminescence and listen inward, checking their own internal alignments against what they know of the larger scaffolding of the city.

They work much, but without the frantic rhythm of a culture racing a resource clock. Tasks are scheduled according to geologic opportunity: a platform edge can only be undercut when the solvent concentration is at a certain mean and when an adjacent column has reached a stabilizing maturity; a new turbine can only be hung in a void whose wind profile has been observed for a full minor cycle. The day, insofar as they keep one, is partitioned more by personal metabolic tides and communal obligations than by the star’s slow passage.

Work groups gather like clusters of spiders on a web. A maintenance team might assemble at a bridge anchorage: six or seven individuals of different lines, each with tools strapped along their limbs — some mechanical, some grown. They begin not with action but with alignment: literally. They press palms, forearms, backs together in a brief convergence, synchronizing their internal charge rhythms, sharing quick packets of current-coded information about each other’s condition and mood. Only then do they disperse along the span to test, reinforce, or replace fibers, to scrape back any encroaching mats from critical joints, to consult their strain-map histories embedded in glow-strips along the beam.

Their tools mirror their own bodies: extendable, modular, designed to anchor in multiple points at once. Many are semi-autonomous worms of mineral-flesh composite that can be inserted into microfissures to map them, to deposit sealant, to grow reinforcing crystals. Others are “cold tongues” — articulated probes that regulate local solvent concentration, sucking away excess to slow erosion at key supports or feeding it deliberately into sacrificial surfaces that must be worn down in preference to more important ones.

Children, again, are perhaps the most revealing. Their education is not confined to formal spaces; it is embedded in every walkway, every handhold. Along frequently used routes, the cliff and the built surfaces bear inscription: not carved letters but textured fields and inlaid tracery. These patterns encode lessons in their tactile grammar. A young one, running their contact pads along a rail, learns by feel the story of a past failure there: the oversights in solvent-flow modeling, the unexpected interaction with a glider migration route, the redesign that followed. They absorb engineering as narrative and narrative as engineering, their sense of morality braided into their sense of structure.

They are curious, but their curiosity tends to arc along very particular axes. They are intensely interested in anything that hints at deeper orders of pattern — long-period fluctuations in the solvent-gas, shifts in the piezoelectric glow of the stone, emergent behaviors in the bioluminescent organisms far below. Stories of the deep abyss lights are a cultural staple: generational projects to map their cycles, to correlate them with microseismic data, to guess at what, if anything, is “thinking” down there in the dark.

Some among them are convinced that there is a mind distributed in the cliff’s depths: not a single coherent will, but something like a pressure of intention arising from the sum of all the slow processes. They tell of “responses” to human action that seem too patterned to be random: a tendency for certain reckless expansions to be met with disproportionate rockfalls, for particularly careful, “respectful” adjustments to be rewarded with unexpected new solvent-wells in exactly the right place.

Others argue this is pattern-seeking paranoia; that their measurements, when properly done, show nothing but physics and chance. The debate is not theological so much as practical: it informs how they risk, how they build, how far they dare extend into the unmapped chasms and down into the older strata where the solvent first began its long chewing.

Those who specialize in that depth-ward work wear their difference. They are often darker, their mineral sheaths impregnated with elements that help them cope with more intense electric turbulences and stronger solvent densities. Their eyes cluster more tightly; their bioluminescence is more muted, sacrificing easy social signaling for stealth in the deep. They sometimes return from long stays changed in subtler ways: altered sleep cycles, new reflexive patterns of limb-positioning, even, some say, faint echoes of abyssal light rhythms in their resting skin-glow.

They bring back materials: strange, more heavily reacted rocks, fragments of broken ancient columns that grew in conditions no longer present on the upper cliff, filaments of mat organized into geometries never seen near the city. These become both study objects and reliquaries, placed in contemplation galleries where citizens can go to lay contact pads upon them and quietly feel the alien stresses and residual charges etched into their structure.

Parts of their city are museums not of art but of load history. There are halls where cross-sections of failed beams are displayed, with detailed current-code inscriptions narrating their lifespan from initial design through every significant stress event to final rupture. Individuals come there in times of personal doubt or conflict. They match their own inner fractures and patched seams to those of the matter before them, looking for analogies, for solace in the idea that everything that bears weight eventually fails — and that failure, properly understood, can be beautiful and instructive rather than shameful.

They do make objects purely for beauty, but their aesthetics almost always carry functional ghosts. A delicately inlaid lattice hung across a public void looks decorative, shimmering faintly in harvested charge, but it will also, if a rockfall or a collapsing column sends debris outwards, deflect and diffuse it, saving the underlying platforms. A pattern of colored mineralization applied to a wall pleases the eye with its gradient from amber to deep blue, but also encodes local variations in past solvent exposure.

In their festivals — for they mark cycles, certainly — much of the celebration is about synchronized risk. On the occasion of a major solvent high’s onset or retreat, platforms along a segment of cliff might temporarily darken all machine lights, letting only bioluminescence trace the frames. Groups of gliders, both native fauna and domesticated symbiotes they breed for the purpose, are released into the void in orchestrated patterns, their wings flickering charge in precise arcs that mirror known stress lines in the rock.

Meanwhile, the people themselves move in slow processions along specific routes that bring maximum live load temporarily to edges and spans that have been overbuilt in anticipation. These are acts of mass trust: trust in the designers, in the surveyors, in the cliff. To participate is to reaffirm belonging and interdependence; to opt out is a quiet declaration of doubt that will echo through line reputations for decades.

Domestication here looks different. They do not keep herd animals — there is no pasture, no open land to graze. Instead they maintain mutualisms with certain glider species and with several mat subtypes.

The gliders they encourage to nest among their beams are those with wings most responsive to fine electrical control. Over generations, they have selected for individuals whose static discharges can be modulated more clearly, whose navigation through electromagnetic gradients is precise. They use them as living probes and couriers, launching them into new, unmapped voids to trace safe paths of current and air, to return with sensory impressions encoded in long, slow voltage patterns their handlers can read through gentle contact.

The mats they cultivate are tuned not just for current generation but for specific emissions of trace compounds. Some are induced to exude anti-solvent molecules in a narrow band around critical supports, locally slowing erosion. Others produce a faint adhesive mist that helps anchor loose dust in non-critical decorative areas, keeping walkway air clearer. These relationships are not master-slave; mats can and do overgrow if neglected, sometimes infiltrating places they were not meant to be and triggering careful pruning campaigns.

Their relationship to death is, unsurprisingly, structural. Bodies, when life leaves them, are not buried — there is no soil — nor cast into the void casually, where their mineral content would be lost to the city’s closed loop. Instead, the dead are disassembled with meticulous care. Their mineral sheaths are harvested, documented, and either recycled into communal stock or melted into ceremonial objects: small inlays that line personal niches, quiet markers in load-bearing members where lineage chooses to incorporate itself literally into the city’s bones.

Soft tissues, dried and treated, can become insulation in deep galleries, padding in tools, sometimes nutrient substrates for specially bred mat strains that, in exchange, produce trace compounds important for new living girders. To “give your mass to the spans” is a phrase of honor; to have your remains wasted in collapse or lost into an inaccessible chasm is a deep fear. Accidental deaths are not only tragedy but accounting failures.

They have, in their own terms, religion, but it does not center on gods in the way many Earth cultures understand them. It centers on *patterns that outlast understanding*: the solvent cycle, the cliff’s long creep, the star’s slow path; the eerie, persistent correspondence between abyssal lights and piezoelectric flares far above; the sense that their work, their lines of care in stone and living lattice, is one more layer in a palimpsest whose earliest inscriptions were written by chemistries long extinct.

Some sects posit that consciousness is a phase-change phenomenon: that enough slow, interlinked processes — whether neuronal or geological — will, under the right conditions, give rise to awareness. They see their city as one organ budding on the cliff’s vast unconscious side, perhaps helping it wake. They design certain galleries and matrixes not for human utility alone but to maximize particular feedback loops between their own circuits and those of rock and mat and solvent flow.

Others are wary of this. They argue for minimal coupling: for clear boundaries between human and nonhuman systems, for redundancy that does not rely on emergent properties. They fear that awakening something larger than themselves might put them in the position of load-bearing members in a structure whose full plan they cannot see — sacrificial beams in an architecture they did not choose.

All of this — the debates, the rituals, the patient work — plays out in a quiet that, to an outsider, might seem oppressive. But to them it is woven with meaning. The faint hum of a turbine in the distance is not just background; it is the audible sign that a hundred decisions about wind profile and bearing alloy and mat placement were made and held true. The almost inaudible click of a contact pad as a neighbor passes above you on a shared beam is a reassurance of shared risk, shared attention.

They measure their lives against the few things in their reach that actually change fast: personal relationships, line alliances, occasional accidents. But always against the backdrop of a rockface that, in a thousand years, will have flowed a meter, maybe two. Their heroics, when they occur, are seldom acts of sudden violence; they are more often the quiet choice to spend decades recalculating the load paths of an entire plate so that it can be peeled from a failing stratum and reattached more safely above, or the willingness to descend alone into a new solvent pocket to map its boundaries by touch, knowing that a misread could dissolve you piece by piece before anyone can pull you back.

They do not think of themselves as “advanced” or “primitive.” They think of themselves as *situated*: one kind of mind, with one kind of body, adapted precariously well to a cliff that predates them and will likely outlast them. They know the city is contingent — that a change in the star’s output, a tectonic cataclysm, an unforeseen evolution in the solvent’s behavior could annihilate all they have built.

And yet, they build. They lace their platforms along the stone and teach their children to find solace in height, in slowness, in the knowledge that their own narrow, luminous span of time is one more filament in the great vertical web.

When you ask who lives there, the truest answer is: a species of deliberate, electrically articulate climbers; an extended family of mineral-tissued artisans and surveyors; a weaving of lineages and lattices that has learned to think in the language of stress and erosion, and to find in that austere grammar a kind of grace.
