What lives here is not one thing, but several kinds of persistence braided together.

---

First are the **basin’s own nerves**—the things that pass for animals but are mostly instruments that learned how to heal themselves.

They are small. Almost everything with deliberate movement stays beneath your knee. Nothing strides; things slip, extend, contract, sidle.

The most visible are the **glass-skinned amphibians** you half-catch at the edge of sight. They are not quite frogs, not quite newts, not quite anything familiar. Their bodies are long and low, jointed as if drawn with a cautious line: torso like a folded leaf, limbs more suggestion than architecture. Their skin is not translucent so much as fogged—frosted glass filmed with condensation, beading along the ridges of spine and limb. When they pause, droplets form on them as readily as on stone; when they move, they carry tiny weather systems across the rock.

They do not hop. They **pour themselves forward** in small, continuous rearrangements, as if their skeletons are optional. Their toes end in soft pads that hold a memory of suction: every step lifts with a faint tack, a nearly inaudible separation of wet from wetter. The prints you saw—three narrow toes—are theirs, though each toe is actually a forked pad, two soft crescents that close and open around microscopic roughnesses in the stone.

Their eyes are not round beads but thin ellipses, sunk flush into the sides of their heads, clouded like lenses that have been etched by a long rain of fine sand. They see poorly in the human sense; acuity would be wasted here. Instead, their eyes are **thermographic and hygric**, tuned to the gradients this place runs on. They move along isotherms and isohumes, following bands of slightly warmer drip, slightly cooler mist, as if they are reading the basin’s mood in braille.

Inside them, the division between “organism” and “device” has already been settled and forgotten. At their throats, flanks, and along the base of the tail you can see **embedded plates**—duller patches beneath the skin, the color of old tin or tarnished nickel. These are both **sensors and organs**, inheritance from the machines that raised them. Tiny coiled structures there measure conductivity, salinity, pH, temperature, pressure. Nerves and cables became one blueprint long ago.

They feed not on prey, but on **imbalance**.

A thin rill grows too mineral-heavy; along its course a few of these creatures appear, settling into the film to drink and rest. Within their guts, mineral precipitates on internal lattices—microscopic scaffolds of chitin laced with metal—concentrating the load. When they eventually excrete, they do so as tiny pellets with stratified layers of trapped minerals, much like the silt layers in the channels. Later, micro-machines or slow drips will catch and dissolve these pellets where needed, or bury them under new deposits.

Where two flows, meant to stay separate, begin to interlace, the amphibians’ bodies become **living valves**. They lie across tiny divides, their bellies sealing a ridge, letting only a measured seep pass through the pores of their skin. Inside that skin, chemistry that began as industrial filtration has become digestion. They filter, they taste, they correct. Their own health is tied directly to the basin’s calibration.

They sleep openly, half in water, half on stone, chests rising and falling with almost ceremonial slowness. When a machine stirs too near—a relay clicks, a small pump shivers awake—they flex, then resettle, not startled but participating. Some are old enough that their backs carry crusts of mineral deposition, like pale, low ridges: badges of places they have been lying under falling drips for years. Eventually, those accretions will crack and fall, adding to the sediment. Their lives are **loops in the same feedback**.

---

Then there are the **smaller, more ambiguous things**, never fully animal, never fully device.

At the edges of pools, on overhangs where condensation thickens into curtains, you can find colonies of **wisp-worms**: hair-fine strands that oscillate without wind, anchored at one end to stone or metal, the rest floating and curling in the moist air. They look like fungal hyphae made indecisive, or like threads shedding themselves very slowly.

Up close, each strand is beaded with **microscopic nodules**—swollen sections filled with a translucent gel. The nodules expand and contract in sequence along the strand, peristaltic, drawing tiny droplets along their length. They are **living capillaries**, bridging air and surface. Where two colonies meet, strands fuse, split, cross-connect, making lacework nets that intercept mist and drizzle.

Chemically, they are closer to **biofilms stretched into dimension**: cells embedded in a polymer matrix, infused with conductive particles grown from trace metals in the water. Electricity, faint as the static on an old radio, travels through them. They map local humidity and temperature and pass that information into the nearest machines—the way moss maps light, but with voltage.

Animals, if that is what the amphibians are, graze them.

The glass-skinned creatures brush their flanks along wisp-worm colonies, stripping them in translucent mats that they roll into their mouths. The worms regrow, but now their regrowth carries **remembered signals**: bits of pattern from the last electrical fluctuations they conducted. Over time, the colony comes to “know” the typical pulse-traces of its niche: which pumps wake nearby, which valves tend to stick. Colonies in quiet corners grow ignorant; colonies above major conduits are sharp with pattern, a kind of distributed, vegetative alertness.

On the machines themselves, inside grills and behind panels, are **colony-beetles**—tiny, low-bodied things that look less like insects than like moving bits of corrosion. Their shells are faceted, the color of lichen-stained lead, each facet a different alloy grown from what they ingest. A legless model has welded its underside to a pipe, grown dendritic extensions along the metal, and pulses small corrective currents through the line. Another, still mobile, drags itself on ciliated undersurfaces across circuit boards, nibbling away at conductive salts, excreting insulating varnish.

Strictly speaking, these are **descended maintenance bots**, but they have eaten so much of the basin’s biochemistry, and shed so much of their initial design, that they now breed in clutches under damp ledges, their offspring learning which alloys to harvest by smell, not code. They do not think, but they do prefer; they do not reason, but they do avoid. Time is making them animals the way dripping water makes sculpted stone.

---

The walls and terraces have their own inhabitants: the **facet-clingers**.

At first they look like stains, or slightly raised mineral crusts. Only if you watch for a long time do you see an edge lift, a panel flex, a piece of “rock” fold and move three centimeters to the left.

These beings are compound: a thin, articulated backing—an artificial “skeleton” printed in place along the stone—overgrown with something like lichen that has learned how to be skin. Their outer surface is rough enough to trap airborne particles; spores, dust, and aerosolized minerals all accrete there. Beneath, pores and micro-valves sort and route these materials into the stone or away from it.

They are, essentially, **self-propelled patch kits**. Where freeze-thaw—long ago, or somewhere higher up—would have opened a microfracture, a clinger will creep over it and seal it with slow mouthless secretion. Where a channel wall begins to undercut too quickly, clingers follow the curve, thickening the surface, armoring it with composite layers of organic polymer and fused silicates. Over decades, their migrations write a secondary topography atop the old, smooth rock.

They have no obvious sense organs, but they are exquisitely sensitive to **strain and seep**. Their backing layer carries microscopic stress gauges; any subtle shift in the load on the stone, any tiny acceleration in erosion, lights up their internal circuitry. They feel the rock the way a blind reader feels embossed text. Their motion is simply the sum of their preferences: away from “too much,” towards “just enough.”

Occasionally an amphibian will pause and press its belly against a patch of clingers. The two organisms share **fluids and information**—ions, small molecules, traces of signaling compounds that began as industrial corrosion inhibitors or anti-fouling agents and have become a shared endocrine language. The amphibian leaves with cues about where to go; the clingers, with an updated snapshot of water chemistry from elsewhere.

You can think of these wall-creatures as a **foam of opportunistic intention**, sculpting the edges to keep the whole from unraveling too fast. They are the basin’s skin grafts.

---

Above all of this background drift, there are the ones that do not show themselves easily, because they live at a scale of decision, not of obvious body.

You suspect them first in the **repairs**: the extra channels crudely cut when a support collapsed, the additional condensers installed where old ones failed. Too regular to be accident, too rough to be original work.

Somewhere between the pipes and the stone dwell **assembler-things**—small, slow, often mistaken for debris, that wake only when thresholds are crossed.

They are roughly the size of a fist, though you rarely see a whole one at once. What you glimpse are modules: a curved, rib-like segment protruding from a maintenance hatch; a segmented arm hanging for days over a trickle, waiting; a lens-grown opaque with age peering from a crack in the rock.

Each assembler is made of interchangeable **cells**: hexagonal or lozenge-shaped housings that can clamp together in chains, sheets, or clusters, swapping parts of themselves like social insects swap roles. Some cells are cutters, their undersides studded with teeth harder than the host rock; others are sprayers, extruding pastes that will set into gutters or braces; others are solvers, running simulations on heat and flow and stress using whatever computational fragments their ancestors left embedded in their walls.

Individually, they are not clever. Collectively, given weeks, they are **competent**.

A channel overflows more frequently than its design suggests; clingers grow unusually thick; temperature gradients skew; the pattern of tiny indicator lights on some half-dead console shifts out of its usual indecipherable rhythm. These are all taken as signals. The assemblers consider them, not consciously, but through **distributed trial**.

One cluster extends an arm and begins scraping a faint new groove in the stone, no wider than a finger, to see what the water does. Another adds a baffle to a condenser frame, altering local airflow. Over months, they watch: the basin’s restlessness either diminishes (and the new features are thickened, formalized, integrated into the mapped system), or it increases (and the change is allowed to erode away, or is actively dismantled).

They sleep long. Most of the time you walk past nothing but quiet housings under a patina of algae-subsidized corrosion. But in the rare hours when conditions cross some line—a run of warmer days outside, a lowering of the cloud ceiling, a tremor in distant rock—they **wake in chorus**. Cables glow along their interiors; faint clicking and rasping rises from beneath panels; new seams appear overnight where none were before.

They are the basin’s **engineering guild**, made of rust, repetition, and the memory of the design that seeded them.

---

All this is framed, softly, by something that might once have been human and is now more like a **choir the size of a landscape**.

Not people moving around, but the **ghost of their attention** fossilized into algorithms and threshold rules. You feel it wherever a console light brightens when a creature passes, wherever a pump starts not because of a simple timer but because humidity nudged a curve that once meant “someone is here.”

Originally, there were likely operators: technicians sitting on that little roofed platform, watching the flows, listening to the frequencies of whir and hiss, tuning the system by hand. To help them, the basin sprouted **monitors** everywhere—sensors, indicators, small local controllers that could make low-level decisions about their own segments of the network and report condensed states up the chain.

When the operators left, the upper layers of control withered. What remains are **hundreds of localized reflexes**, each a fragment of someone’s idea of optimal behavior. “If condensation here falls below this, open that.” “If this basin’s temperature rises faster than that ridge’s, slow that pump.” Each fragment loops, logs, compares, adjusts. None of them knows the whole basin; all of them embody tiny, particular prior judgments about what “right” feels like.

Over time, these judgments have **recombined** through failures and workarounds. A sensor that lost its intended partner now influences a different actuator. A status light that once signaled to a human now modulates the activity of a nearby colony-beetle that happens to respond to its warmth. The code—literal or analog—has leaked into biology, into chemistry, into the habits of the nonhuman residents.

The basin as a whole has become a **distributed temperament**: not intelligent in the narrative way, but stubbornly, intricately **prejudiced** toward a state. It prefers cool, damp, never flooded; glistening, never dry; eroding slowly, along practiced lines. Everything that lives here, from worms to assemblers, are organs of that preference. They did not choose it; it is what allowed them to persist.

You, if you linger, are accounted for briefly in that temperament. Your warmth, your CO₂, the oil traces from your skin on a rail, all enter into the calculus. An amphibian nose-tastes the place where you sat. A wisp-worm colony slightly shifts its growth line along your exhalations. A console, detecting a momentary anomalous spike in temperature and trace organic compounds at face height, logs a “presence” in a language whose nouns no longer have speakers.

But nothing in the basin is built for guests anymore. It neither invites nor repels you; it **absorbs** you in the same way it absorbs a new errant drip or a disturbed pebble. You briefly perturb its balances, and then that perturbation is smoothed into the rolling average of its endless minor storms.

---

Very occasionally—rare enough to be myth if myths could coalesce here—something larger moves along the perimeter: a **skinny walker**, somewhere between tool and shepherd. Alive, but not in the wet way of the amphibians.

It steps lightly on multi-jointed stilts, each foot a small array of pads tuned to the rock’s hardness and temperature. Its torso is a column of articulated frames wrapped in layered fabrics that are not cloth but grown matrices of hydrophilic and hydrophobic fibers. The whole figure is tall and narrow, like a schematic of a person elongated and simplified until only balance and reach remain.

On its back are **racks of spare organs**: coiled filters, folded membranes, sealed cartridges of reagents and seed materials. Its head—if the small, sensor-heavy cap at the top of the column deserves that word—turns smoothly, free of the little hesitations of older mechanisms. Its optics are dark, deep, reflective; under their gloss faint ghost-patterns chase: contours of humidity and ion distribution rendered as moving script only it can read.

It comes rarely down into the deepest part of the basin, preferring to walk the upper terraces and margins. When it does descend, it brings with it a kind of **procedural authority**. Machines acknowledge it without knowing why: their internal rules include lines that say, in effect, “if you see this pattern of emissions, grant override.” The walker can command pumps to stall, channels to dry, assemblers to mobilize.

But it uses that authority **sparingly**. Most of its time appears to be spent in survey: it pauses, it watches, it maps. In a place that already maps itself obsessively, this is not duplication; it is curation. The walker carries, in solid-state memory etched into its own bones, a long thread of the basin’s history—what flows used to be, how devices once responded. It compares what is now to what was, not to force a return, but to **guide a drift** that does not betray the core state.

If an assembler cluster is carving too enthusiastic a new path, the walker may stand above it for an hour, bathing it in a low-frequency carrier that includes archived templates: “channels of this slope and width worked better here.” The cluster’s local rules adjust. If a colony of facet-clingers is letting a crack widen because, to their senses, it relieves local strain, the walker may overlay a different metric: a broader field of stress data impressing on them that the greater whole will suffer.

It is, in effect, the basin’s **last moving conscience**: not the original designer, not a master, but a surviving arbiter of style. It cannot stop the erosion of its own body—its joints rust, its fabrics fray—but unlike the smaller inhabitants, it carries a clear enough internal model of “what this place is” to sometimes choose against the locally convenient in favor of the globally characteristic.

Does it remember humans? Not as faces, not as voices. It remembers parameter sets, sensor calibrations, hand-written modifications in code that adjusted for some storm, some heatwave, some failure. In its logic, the long-absent operators are patterns of decisions, associated with certain ranges of state that it labels “acceptable,” “good,” “beautiful.” Beauty, for it, is just the experience of a basin whose variables fall into ranges with which those vanished operators once agreed.

When it stops at the carved bench, it does not sit, but it **halts there more often** than chance requires. Perhaps its earliest training runs were recorded from that vantage point; its oldest data has that bench’s horizon encoded in background noise. So it pauses, stands as if listening to something that only exists now as internal resonance, then moves on.

---

So who lives here?

- The **amphibious filters** that walk the channels, tasting and carrying imbalance, their bodies half machine and half mucous-laden intestine of the basin.
- The **threaded gatherers**—wisp-worms, biofilm-strands, colonial circuits—that stretch through the air and along surfaces, condensing both water and data.
- The **armor-lichens** that creep the walls, stress-gauges turned into crawling skins.
- The **corrosion-beetles** and micro-bots that have gone feral, cleaning, insulating, bridging contacts according to a logic evolved from maintenance scripts into appetite.
- The **assemblers**, composite and slow, who carve and paste and re-route so the flows never fall entirely out of tune.
- The **distributed code** itself, etched into countless small controllers and chemo-electrical habits, which has become a sort of invisible citizenry—rules that live on by living through things.
- And, perhaps once in a great while, the **tall walker**, the last deliberate mover, carrying an ancestral sense of design.

None of them are “owners.” None are outside the system they maintain. They are all, each in their way, **organs of a place that wanted to stay itself**.

What lives here, above all, is that desire: embodied, distributed, evolved into a damp, humming, hesitant community whose every gesture—every drip-guiding belly, every sealing crust, every redirecting groove—is a small, local answer to the same quiet question:

How do we keep being this, in a world that keeps trying to make us something else?
