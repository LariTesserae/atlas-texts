They are not pioneers here so much as first drafts, the life that lives where the planet has only just lifted its skin into the cold.

Most of them are still close to the stone, as if reluctant to trust the air.

### The stone-lickers

The most visible things with bones are the stone-lickers: low-bodied, many-toed climbers that move as though they were poured onto the rock rather than walking across it.

From a distance you might mistake one for a low, moving patch of damp fur—dull slate-gray, stippled with darker flecks in a pattern that confuses depth and outline. Up close, its body is compact, almost rectangular: spine straight and stiff, belly held a hand’s breadth above the ground. The main impression is in the legs: four of them, but each ending not in a hoof or paw, but in a broad, fleshy pad that fans into ten or twelve short, jointed toes.

Each toe has a blunt, rubbery tip backed by tiny cartilage struts. Pressed to the rock, the toes spread and grip; lifted, they fold close like the ribs of a deflating umbrella. In motion, the animal never commits to a step the way lowland walkers do. It always has three points anchored, sometimes four, shifting its weight in tiny, incremental transfers. On a vertical face it looks more like an insect than a mammal, a thing that simply adheres, rearranging its contact pattern as if solving a slow puzzle.

Their heads are narrow, almost wedge-shaped, with nostrils set high and back, where spray and freezing films of water can’t easily clog them. The eyes are forward-facing, hooded by stiff, hairless ridges that break the wind and shed constant moisture. The mouth splits farther back than seems necessary, giving them a permanent, faintly amused grimace.

They live on whatever the plateau will give them, which is never a single thing. In a day you can watch one work through a sequence of minor hungers: nibbling the brittle twigs of a thornshrub, grinding slowly, pausing often to let its gut handle the cold, lignified material; then scraping at a patch of moss with horny, under-tooth extensions, sucking in the slurry of plant tissue and trapped insect eggs; then lowering its tongue to a mineral-streaked seep and rasping at the rock itself, taking in both water and the dissolved salts it needs to keep its body chemistry from seizing in the cold.

They browse in lines or arcs rather than tight herds, each animal a few body-lengths apart, their routes tracing the edges of ledges and the lips of basins. Distance is a defense: any predator launching from a crevasse or dropping from the air can reach at most one before the rest have time to glue themselves to the rock and flow away.

Come coldest periods—in whatever passes for the season here—they grow fat along the flanks, but not for hibernation. They don’t sleep through the lean times; they endure them by becoming less themselves. In deep, windless cracks they press flank to flank in clusters, their fur beading with each others’ breath, their metabolisms turning down until their hearts flick in slow, patient beats. In these hollows, the rock at their backs gradually takes on a waxy patina of oils and flattened fur: the plateau’s first step toward animal-made insulation.

Their young are born soft-padded and clumsy, toes fusing and splitting as they grow, patterns of grip consolidating with practice. You can sometimes see a juvenile misjudge a ledge and skid, a brief, terrifying slide arrested only when instinct throws all limbs out and the dozens of little digits find enough purchase. Those that misjudge too often vanish into the clean, unarguable vertical space.

### The edge-pouncers

Where prey has found a way to move, something else has found a way to interrupt that movement.

The plateau’s primary ground predator is all edges and angles, as if something had drawn a mammal as a series of straight lines over a blueprint of the faulted landscape.

Tall, narrow-hipped, and long-limbed, the edge-pouncers move like sketches of motion: brief, fast transits between stillnesses. Their fur is shorter than the stone-lickers’—a clipped, tight layer that lies along their bodies in the same direction as the prevailing winds, minimizing any turbulence that might betray their presence. The color is an abstracted version of the plateau’s palette: long bands of gray and off-white running lengthwise, a pattern that breaks up their outline on ridgelines and, when they lie low behind a lip of rock, makes them seem like another stratum in the stone.

They do not climb the vertical faces the way stone-lickers do, but they own the transitions: the broken edges where one level steps down abruptly to another, the intricate staircases of natural ledges and shelves. Here they move with alarming economy, traversing throws of three or four body-lengths with single-skid leaps, nails biting into thin seams, tails flaring as counterweights.

Their heads are narrow triangles. Ears are small, tight against the skull, able to flatten completely in high wind. Eyes are pale and glossy, adapted less to detail than to motion and contrast; in this world without shadows, anything that moves differently from the wind stands out. The jaw hinge is oversized relative to the skull, giving them a slightly dislocated look when they gape. They must bite fast and hard; a wounded prey animal that manages to fling itself into a chasm is wasted energy.

Hunting, they use the land as much as their own speed. More often than not, a kill is a geometry problem. A stone-licker moves along a narrow causeway between two voids. The pouncer appears on a slightly higher path that will intersect just as the prey’s options narrow. A feint sends it to the more dangerous side; panic and the instinct to avoid open air drive it back toward the waiting jaws. The moment of the pounce is brief and ugly, wind and weight and claws finding the one direction—down and inward—that ends with meat on rock instead of meat in space.

Their dens are not excavated (there’s nothing soft enough to dig) but appropriated: deep, branching tension-cracks with one widened notch some adult discovered fit its shoulders. Over time, their bodies and breathing warm the stone enough for ice to retreat a little, for moisture to bead and drip in regular rills. In these deeper reaches they cache their kills, anchoring them with fragments of bone wedged into natural slots, high enough that scavengers can’t easily reach but low enough that the meat freezes into long-lasting stores.

There is intelligence in them, but not the kind that shapes tools. It is an intelligence of routes and rhythms: knowing which ledges catch morning’s faint non-sun-warmth first, which fault lines have begun to open into hunter-sized traps, which thermal vent has thickened the moss so much that stone-lickers will surely come. They do not mark territory in the mammalian scentscape way. Odors would be diluted too fast, washed too clean. Instead, they own patterns of presence: the same path walked often enough that the stone’s thin frost is continuously polished away, the same watch-stone used generation after generation, their paws engraving a shallow, polished hollow in the raw rock.

### The air’s accountants

Up where the wind is more constant than anything on the ground, the plateau’s fliers rule.

They are not large by lowland standards, but here every gram of weight is a debt, every square centimeter of wing a cost that must be paid in muscle and maintenance. They have found a compromise: long, narrow wings with a slight forward sweep, feathers lying in tight shingle patterns that shed moisture as efficiently as slate roofs. In silhouette, they are sickles and needles, barely-there lines that angle and slide against the lightless clouds.

They rarely flap. Instead they budget altitude and distance as carefully as the rock budgets its cracks. Riding the thin, slow thermals rising from darker stone faces, they quarter the plateau in patient, overlapping grids, head tilted ever so slightly to keep one eye on the ground, one on the shifting air. Their bones are light but not brittle, laced with an unusual density of internal cross-bracing—nature’s trussuork, allowing them to withstand sudden gusts and the sharp turbulence that forms where wind knifes through narrow fissures.

They see heat more than color. In the constant gray, objects’ own warmth is the only truly reliable contrast. To them, the plateau is a black-and-white map overlaid with ghostly signatures: the warm tracks of recent animal passage, long since invisible to human eyes; the subtle bloom of geothermal vents; the cool shadows of pools and fresh ice. A stone-licker huddled in a crack is just another dim ember, but one that moves in patterns distinguishable from rock’s slow lunging.

Their feet are a compromise between perching and killing. Talons are sharp but not tremendously curved; they must grip bare stone, not branches. Many kills are not the dramatic midair snatches of other worlds but targeted drops: a swift, gravity-aided dive, wings tucked just enough; impact a meter behind a browsing grazer’s hindquarters; the shock and stumble that send it skidding; then a quick, finishing strike to the skull as the body teeters on some unforgiving edge.

Nesting is as much a problem of engineering as reproduction. There are no trees, no sodden banks to dig into. They build where the plateau’s flaws offer a cradle: in the re-entrant corners of cliffs, on small stone projections shielded from three sides, occasionally inside the mouths of vertical chimneys where rising warm air can keep ice from building too thick. The nests themselves are broad, flat structures of scavenged plant matter, bones, and their own frozen droppings, glued together by successive layers of thaw and re-freeze until they resemble rough, lumpy platforms of pale concrete. In time, these eyry-disks become part of the geology; even after a lineage dies out or moves on, their discs remain as slight irregularities that catch additional frost, alter local wind, and influence where future cracks and melt lines will form.

They are not the only things in the sky. Twice a turn of whatever passes for a year, tight, arrow-shaped migrations cross overhead: bands of smaller, faster birds that seldom land, hurtling between better lands elsewhere. To the residents, these are less neighbors than weather phenomena, pulses of life that pass, leave a few weak or dead in their wake as scavenger gifts, and vanish into the gray.

### The ciphers in the cracks

Drop to your knees and lean close to the wet seams, the hairline fractures stained faintly darker where water seeps. There, the world changes scale and taxonomy.

A whole colony of creatures exists between the plateau’s wider gestures of life: small, jointed beings the size of grains of rice to thumbnails, pale as old wax where not stained green-black by algal pigments. They have too many legs for easy counting and too many joints in each leg to move elegantly, so they scuttle and compress themselves between crystalline asperities with uncanny efficiency.

They eat the plateau’s film foods: the slick, faintly iridescent layers of algae that line persistent trickles; the ghostly biofilms on condensation-washed walls; bacteria rich in iron and sulfur that bloom near the smallest, coolest vents. Their mouths are simple scraping organs, rings of keratinized hairs that comb microorganisms from rock and ice and pass them inward.

They are born, if that is the word, as little more than crawling stomachs, translucent and soft, barely more structure than their feeding brushes and the tiny hooks at the ends of each limb. As they grow they add segments, each with a new pair of legs, extruding a sort of exoskeletal memory of where they’ve been. When one molts, its old shell is left clinging to the rock like a shriveled, colorless version of itself, quickly colonized by the very biofilms it once consumed.

These crack-creatures are the plateau’s custodians of excess. They make use of everything too small or too fleeting for the larger animals: proteins from drifting corpses that the water dissolves, spores blown from lower, greener altitudes, even the microscopic particulates of new volcanic ash that sometimes dust the plateau, their glassy shards rich in element combinations no other life has yet learned to use efficiently.

Predation exists even here. Slender, translucent worms lurk in the thinnest films of water, heads no more than a darker grain, bodies almost invisible. They strike with speed that seems impossible at such low temperatures, mouths everting to ensnare one of the scuttlers in a ring of hooks, then retracting, dragging their prizes into narrower depths.

The result of all this micro-hunger is that very little organic matter survives long in its initial form. Even in death, animals are quickly disassembled down the fractal levels of scavenging: flesh to scraps, scraps to smears, smears to invisible residue, all drawn into the next generation of algae and bacteria. The plateau’s immature soils—those few, darkened pockets of half-made earth—are the aggregated memory of a thousand such cycles.

### The wet-warm things

Where the plateau’s geology breaks its own rules and lets heat and water share the same cut, something stranger happens.

Near the smallest thermal vents—not the dramatic geysers of storybooks, but little, persistent warm breaths out of nail-thin cracks—the air is almost comfortable by local standards. Ice recoils a few centimeters, water runs a little more freely, moss gains a kind of courage. Here live the wet-warm things: animals that have found ways to tie their existence intimately to these tiny, inconsistent engines.

Imagine an amphibian, but strip away your associations with pools and ponds under trees. These are compact, flattened bodies no larger than a human hand, skins smooth and semi-translucent, showing faint tracings of capillaries beneath. Their coloration is that of the moss they sit upon—murky purples and bruised greens—which is less camouflage from sight than from radiant heat detection. To the fliers above, a vent-ring full of these creatures looks like just another slightly warmer blotch.

They spend much of their time as motionless ovals pressed to stone, drinking both water and temperature. Their skins are studded with microscopic, fingerlike projections that increase surface area; moisture condenses there even when the surrounding rock looks dry, drawn from the high humidity and trapped against the body where it can be slowly absorbed. Internally, they host an ecology of their own: symbiotic microbes that metabolize dissolved minerals and faint chemical traces from the vent gases, providing a slow but steady trickle of nutrients that supplements the animals’ sparse diet of insects and crack-creatures.

On the rare occasions when conditions are just right—slightly more warmth, slightly more moisture—they become suddenly social. Many individuals crawl together into tight carpets on rock directly above or alongside the vent. Their collective bodies form a living sponge, altering how the vent’s heat and gases dissipate, in turn creating a momentary microclimate that allows moss and algae to surge. Then, over days or weeks, the moss grows denser, the animals disperse again, and the cycle resets.

Their reproduction is opportunistic and damp-dependent. Eggs are laid not in free water but in thin, gelatinous films smeared onto underhangs or the underside of small stones, where drip patterns guarantee intermittent wetting. The young hatch as tiny, legged slivers that already resemble the adults, bypassing any aquatic larval stage. On a plateau where liquid water is here and gone in hours, there is no time for tadpoles.

### The water’s ghosts

In the shallow basins and rock-bowls, the near-freezing tarns that gather the plateau’s hesitation between solid and liquid, eel-like shapes inhabit an existence so closely bound to the phase of water that they seem to blur with it.

These are not fish as the lowlands would recognize them. They have no true fins, only ribbons of flexible membrane running their length, and no scales in the usual sense, just overlapping plates of cartilage that flex slightly with each movement. Their color is the color of the water itself under cloud: a gray that only declares itself when contrasted against the darker stone beneath.

They feed primarily at the instant margins—where water seeps in, where ice forms or dissolves. Their mouths are sets of tiny, course-comb teeth that strain out whatever life is swept in: microbial clumps, insect larvae from windblown eggs, bits of organic debris. When a surface film begins to freeze, they press their bodies against the underside of the forming ice, using capillary attraction and the slight difference in density between their flesh and liquid to remain suspended as the world firms above them.

In the deepest cold, when small basins freeze solid and even the under-ice corridor to the fissure drains is closed, they do not die. They contract. Their bodies tolerate being frozen—cells packed with antifreeze proteins and glass-forming sugars that prevent ice crystals from punching them apart. For weeks or months they are more sculpture than animal, encased in the small, transparent coffins of their home pools. Only when water returns and the ice loosens its grip do their metabolisms flicker back on, like lanterns re-lit in already-built housings.

Miraculously, predators cannot fully track them. To the heat-sensing eyes of the fliers, they are only slightly cooler currents within already-cold water. Only when a pool is crowded, when movement disturbs the surface or sends a visible flex along the ice, do opportunists drop in to snatch at them, beating water with wings to lift away the flopping, silver-gray lengths.

### The low machines

Plants here are not scenery. They are slow machines, each built to do precise work in the unstable medium of rock, water, and wind.

#### Moss as engineer

What looks from standing height like dull, inanimate smudge on a ledge is, up close, a carpet of individual structures, each no more than a few millimeters high. Unlike lush, feathery moss of wetter, older lands, these grow as stacked plates and stiff cylinders, crustlike. They drink in brief inundations and fogdrips, then slam valves shut, holding onto every drop as ice crystals form among their leaves.

They are also physical modifiers. The very act of covering stone changes its fate: slowing radiative heat loss at night, slightly insulating against the freeze that would otherwise pry flakes away, retaining enough moisture in their rootless mass that, when they do finally desiccate, they leave behind a thin mat of organics—proto-soil—that collapses into protected cracks and depressions below.

Some host colonies of crack-creatures and micro-arthropods within their interstices, an unintentional farming that benefits both. The animals get shelter and retained humidity; their waste, in turn, feeds moss growth. Over many cycles, these mats thicken, darken, and become literal weight on the rock, encouraging sagging where tension once ruled, subtly influencing how and where new fissures open.

#### Scrap-shrubs

Those sprawling, thornlike shrubs hugging the ground are neither random nor passive. Their roots seek out microfractures, exploiting and widening them as they push. Many of those hairline cracks that seep just enough water to form a persistent trickle exist because a shrub’s rootlets have already wormed their way in, breathing carbonic acid and organic exudates into minerals that would otherwise resist change.

Above ground, their architecture is all about deflection. Branches grow in low, overlapping fans that break the wind into a hundred minor eddies, reducing the scouring of moisture and frost from the protected rock beneath. Snow-granules (when they fall) lodge in their mesh, melting slowly in place instead of blowing on. In effect, each shrub is a small, living snow fence and wind baffle, capturing more water than its own modest leaves could hope to transpire.

Their seeds are armored beads, hard and dense, with roughened exteriors that allow them to lodge in the tiniest irregularities. Most land mere centimeters from the parent; a few are carried in the guts of stone-lickers to new cracks and ledges miles away, deposited in dung that, in this world, is treasure: concentrated organic matter in a sterile, mineral sea.

### The shy engineers

No structures of worked stone or crafted wood rise on the plateau, but there are hints of an intelligence that does more than merely navigate and endure.

You will not see its owners unless you are patient and still.

The first sign is a pattern that the wind would never lay: small stones arranged in tight rings around the mouths of certain warm vents, not fallen or tumbled but placed. Each piece is the size of a fist or smaller, each fitted against its neighbors so that gaps between them are narrow and consistent. The effect is a low, perforated collar around the vent, like a crude crown.

These collars serve a purpose physicists would admire. They moderate the vent’s output, forcing escaping warmth and vapor through multiple tiny channels instead of one open hole. This evens the heat flux, spreads moisture more uniformly around the vent’s circumference, and reduces dangerous surges. Life within the collar—moss, algae, wet-warm things—benefits from less boom-and-bust, more steady trickle.

On some slopes, where the rock tilts just enough to invite a dangerous, sheet-like glaze of water that would freeze to treacherous glass, you find thin lines of gravel laid in contour: miniature terraces that intercept flow, break rivulets into beads, and guide them in safer, slower paths toward basins where stone-lickers drink and basins where the eel-things spawn.

These are not the work of accident or animal herds’ repeated passing. The spacing is too consistent, the respect for gradient too precise. No creature that moves on four limbs would naturally place stones exactly so, then move to do it again meters uphill.

The makers are small by human measure—waist-high at most when they stand to their full but rarely-used height. Their default posture is a forward-leaning crouch, knuckles not quite touching the rock, long arms counterbalancing a relatively heavy torso. Fur covers most of their bodies in a short, dense layer, grayed at the tips no matter their age by the plateau’s abrasive damp. Their faces are blunt and wide, with eyes set deep under prominent brow ridges that serve both as sun and ice shields. Noses are flat, nostril slits horizontal—adaptations to sniff in slicing wind without freezing the tender interior.

Hands are the key. Five fingers, but the thumb can oppose not just the first two but all of them, the whole hand able to wrap stones of various shapes and hold them against the palm. Pads are thick, cracked, and leatherlike, bearing the faint etchings of mineral grains ground in over a lifetime. In a resting group, you see them manipulating pebbles almost reflexively: turning, testing, pressing one against another as if always running a mental simulation of fit and flow.

They do not build shelters in the human sense. There is nowhere to anchor walls that ice and tectonics would not eventually tear. Instead they occupy existing overhangs and cavern mouths, augmenting them subtly: wedging stones into cracks near the lip to redirect drips away from sleeping places; lining the deepest parts with gathered moss and the soft under-fur shed from stone-lickers they hunt or scavenge.

Their tools are few and opportunistic. A shard of exceptionally tough stone used as a wedge or lever, its edge preserved and passed down through generations. Long, straight lengths of shrub-branch stripped of thorns and used to probe uncertain ice or the depth of newly opened fissures. Occasionally, a flattened stone with a naturally smooth surface, used to crush seeds or soften dried meat before eating.

Language, if it exists, is not loud. Voices do not carry well in this vast, thin resonance, and any prolonged sound risks alerting hungry things with better hearing. Their communication leans heavily on posture, the angle of a head, the flare or flattening of fur along the spine. Up close, in the muffled half-light of a shared overhang, they murmur in consonant-heavy strings, the sounds clipped as if air were a resource to conserve along with heat.

Their relationship with the other plateau life is neither purely predatory nor benign. They hunt small stone-lickers and crack-creatures, raid bird nests when they can reach them, and occasionally cooperate in driving a larger grazer toward a waiting chasm. But they also guard certain vent-collars from excessive trampling, shooing away over-enthusiastic moss-eaters with thrown pebbles. They redirect the course of a nascent rill not just for their own water needs but to preserve the delicate habitations of wet-warm things that, in turn, sustain insect populations they depend on.

Most importantly, they remember. In a world where seasons are subtle, a memory of when a vent last surged dangerously hot, or when a particular rock wall last sheared, is survival. They pass that memory down in stories acted out with hands and bodies: the angle of the fall, the hiss of steam, the smell of cooked moss. Their young watch, mimic, and internalize, adding one more cycle to the plateau’s slow accumulation of pattern.

They are few. You could walk the plateau for weeks and never see more than a blur retreating into a crack. Their tracks are lighter than a stone-licker’s, their droppings indistinguishable from those of other omnivores. Only their rearrangements of inert material truly mark them: the gravel lines, the vent-crowns, the cleaned and slightly smoothed ledges that serve as communal sleeping platforms.

If they dream, it is likely not of conquest. This is not a world where expansion rewards effort. Instead, they dream of more reliable warmth, of fewer surprises from the stone under their feet, of flows of water and air that behave themselves just enough to be predictable.

### The unseen majority

Beyond the named and noticed creatures—climbers and pouncers, fliers and cracklings—there is a vast company of invisible lives working below the plateau’s threshold of drama.

Bacteria sluice through the emerging underground streams, using chemical energy stripped from freshly fractured minerals. They coat pipe-smooth the insides of subterranean channels, their biofilms altering how water flows, how quickly it picks up or sets down certain ions. Some, adapted to the low but constant radiation at this altitude, repair DNA faster than it can be broken, turning what would elsewhere be damage into just another environmental rhythm.

Fungi—modest still, no towering mushrooms—thread their hyphae through the first real soils forming under animal congregations. They link moss clumps, shrubs, and algae in thin, white networks, redistributing nutrients from carcass-rich hollows to starved outcrops. Their fruiting bodies are small, dark nubbins hugging rock so closely they’re almost indistinguishable from lichen. Spores ride wind and bird gut alike, seeding potential wherever a bit of organic shadow can support them.

Viruses shuttle genes between all of them, blithely indifferent to species boundaries as they have always been. In this young world, where niches are still fuzzy and adaptability is supreme, they are carriers not just of disease but of solutions—enzymes for cold-resistance, tweaks in membrane chemistry, novel ways to harvest light from dayless skies.

### How they fit into the workshop

None of these beings, on their own, define the plateau. They are not its goal, not the reason the stone lifts or the water threads its first tentative paths. They are byproducts and opportunists riding on the great, indifferent labor of tectonics and climate.

And yet they are also the only witnesses, the only local memory.

The stone-lickers’ mineral-hungry tongues help expose fresh faces that water can more easily erode. The edge-pouncers’ kills leave nutrient-rich patches that accelerate soil formation. The birds’ nests become new textural elements for frost to grip and wind to tear, changing how cliffs spall and echo. The wet-warm things around vents modulate the release of geothermal energy, smoothing its sharpest spikes. The shy engineers’ gravel lines and stone collars nudge entire micro-basins of water and warmth into different, slightly more stable futures.

Taken together, they are the smallest of inputs into the planet’s vast, grinding calculus—but they are inputs. Over the aeons, their wastes, their bones, their quiet preferences for one crack over another will become as much a part of the plateau’s eventual mature form as the unyielding parameters of rock type and uplift rate.

For now, they live as all first things must: close to necessity, with little margin. They cling, shuffle, glide, and curl; they freeze and unfreeze; they test and remember. In a place that is itself only a sketch of what it will become, they are sketches too—provisional answers to the question of how to be alive on a high, damp, barely-born roof of the world.
