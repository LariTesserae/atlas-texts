Begin with the light, and they will tell you that it is not light at all.

They have a word for it that is closer to “revealing” than to “shining”—something like the lifting of a veil. To them, darkness is not the absence of radiance but the presence of concealment, and this place simply refuses to conceal. They are born into that refusal. Their eyes open on brightness that comes from everywhere and nowhere. There is no point for them where light is a thing you turn on; it is simply the state of being awake in this world.

They have eyes, but not as you think of eyes. Most have three or four—two large and forward-facing, milky-pale with overgrown irises, and one or two smaller, set higher or farther to the side, more like slow, sensitive lenses than quick pupils. The larger eyes are for the near-world of movement and gesture; the smaller are for the subtler changes in the brightness itself—the faint deepening when fog thickens in a ravine, the delicate blue-shift when a mineral plane is newly exposed and still “fresh” with stored light.

Their skin is a continuum rather than a single pattern. Children are nearly featureless: soft, pale, wet-sheened, their bodies beaded constantly with condensation like every surface around them. As they age, the environment writes itself on them. Microscopic mineral films accumulate in the shallow textures of their skin, guided by hereditary patterns and by conscious practice.

On some, this becomes a faint, even opal-like shimmer over shoulders and back—useful for shedding water in smooth beads. On others, it is coaxed into ridges and plates along the shins, forearms, and ribs: protective where they often brace themselves against rock. A few elders bear true crusts—thin, chalky growths in deliberate stripes, which they keep, scrub, and “garden” as carefully as others tend hair. These crusts are not dead matter. They are symbiotic: slow-growing organisms that eat trace ions from sweat and seepage, glowing softly when certain chemistries change, prickling with discomfort when the air shifts in dangerous ways.

Their average height is not strictly average. In the central, well-established districts, adults stand perhaps to your chest, their bodies compact and long-torsoed, legs sturdy, arms longer than human, fingers tapered and calloused at their tips. The bones of their forearms are subtly different: each arm split internally into two primary levers, allowing a strengthful twist of the wrist and a span of motion particularly well-suited to bracing, hooking, and levering stones. Their feet are broad, with three main toes and a strong opposable rear digit; the soles are slightly ridged, secreting a thin film of viscous moisture they can consciously increase or reduce to climb wet stone.

Farther out, among those who live closer to the raw edges and the deeper basin-rims, bodies stretch and compress according to need. In a district of tall pillars and wide pools, you might see lineages with longer limbs, lean and almost spidery, adapted to bridge gaps and swing on ropes. In low, sagging vaults where the ceiling presses close, families tend toward flattened, powerful frames, neckless, with shoulders that roll inward so they can squeeze through crawlways carved by generations. The differences between these lineages are not yet speciation, but they are directional—one can read geography in the angles of a stranger’s joints.

Their color is the color of stone that has been washed: pale, more bone than flesh to the eye. Under that pallor runs a subtle internal palette: faint vascular blush where blood flows closer to the surface, greyish tints where mineral crusts have taken deeper hold, rare blue-green hues along veins in individuals with a genetic habit of binding certain metals in their tissues. Hair, where it exists, is sparse, mostly on the head. In some families it is slick and dark as wet basalt; in others it is delicate and white, more like the feathery feelers of cave insects than human hair. Many shave it or abrade it away—hair catches too much water, chills too easily, and to go bare is to feel the air more precisely.

Names, they will not give you easily, because names, to them, are not meant to be thrown loose into the echoing halls. They are built in layers as everything else is: the personal click-and-breath that kin use in close quarters; the more elaborate gestural name, a small choreography of fingers and posture used in negotiation and council; the slow name, carved over years as notches on a flowstone ridge or as a pattern coaxed into a personal crust. To outsiders, they might be summarized as Houses, but even “house” is misleading. They think of themselves more as Currents.

Currents are lineages and alliances organized around particular flows: of water, of heat, of migratory animals, of mineral-rich drips. There is the Current of the Steep Echo, whose claim is a series of near-vertical shafts where the acoustic behavior of the place lets them hear tremors from a long way off. There is the Current of the Half-Warm Troughs, who have rights over a chain of barely-geothermal pools that support abundant filter-creatures. There is the Current of the Tilted Bridges, inheritors of a crumpled arc of old architecture that grants fast passage between otherwise isolated basins.

One does not “belong” to one Current only. Birth gives you one, apprenticeship another; marriage or the formal exchange of fosterlings binds you into yet another. Individual identity is like a braid of these flows: how the Currents meet in your person determines which paths you walk, which disputes you can speak in, which basins you can drop a plumb line into without offense.

They map their world in three overlapping ways: by sound, by drip, by memory of touch.

Sound-maps are not drawn; they are sung in low, chesty tones that bounce off the pillars and arches. To a novice, this is only humming and clicking. To someone raised here, a short pattern of notes and percussive tongue-clicks describes a whole route: an upward-sloping hall with a high soft ceiling; a narrow kink to the left past a curtain of fragile calcite; a broad, low space where the echoes smear and come back diffuse. Old travelers can stand in a new place, give a testing hum, and say, “This is kin to the Long Shoal Vaults to the east,” not by what they see, but by how their own voice returns to them.

Drip-maps are physical: arrays of bowls, basins, hanging gourds, and hollow bones in certain communal spaces where people listen, at rest, to the different rhythms of water. Each basin is tuned—not by altering the drip, which is inviolate, but by altering the receiver. Size, depth, throat-width: those things produce distinctive tones. A council hall in the central districts might have two or three of these arrays active at once: one representing the current state of drips in the city’s heart, another representing the edges, read by delegated listeners who have traveled and memorized remote patterns. Time is tracked as these rhythms shift. A phrase like “three-falls-past the Double-Hollow-Basin” is as specific as a date.

Touch-maps are held in the body. Children on their first long walks are blindfolded, told to trail fingers along a particular wall, to count the rounded scars of old tool-strokes, the subtle change where natural flowstone begins, the sag where a collapsed ceiling was once cleared and the rubble stacked in a distinctive pattern. They learn the city the way climbing plants learn trellises: through continual, repeated contact.

What you might call their “religion” is not a set of faith-propositions but a set of obligations toward stone, water, and change.

They have no creator-gods in the surface sense. The idea that the world was made in an instant would be absurd to them. They can see, touch, and time the making of things: the slow building of curtains, the rounding of what were once sharp carved blocks. For them, there are only three abiding presences, conceptual rather than anthropomorphic: the Weight, the Fall, and the Sway.

The Weight is the overburden—the stone above, the endless pressure of the world bearing down. It is neither benevolent nor malevolent; it is simply inarguable. To live here is to accept Weight. They speak of accepting Weight when they take on an irreversible duty, when they agree to care for a crack-prone ceiling or a difficult flood-basin.

The Fall is water in all its forms: dripping, weeping, surging, thin mist. The Fall is change, because every drop, given enough time, remakes what it touches. Fall has a double character: it gives life and destroys structure. Rituals around Fall are about harnessing without insulting. When a new drip appears, no one simply channels it away; they spend time listening to it, learning its pattern, tracing its path with powdered mineral to see where it wishes to run before they redirect it with carved lips and small dams. To block Fall entirely is taboo. To intercept and guide is skill.

The Sway is what you feel in your bones when distant stone grinds on stone. It is the slow convulsion of the deep earth, the subtle tensing and easing of rock under pressure. The Sway gives no warning in words, but the elders with the thickest crusts claim to feel it in their plates days before the rest notice it underfoot. In stories, the Sway is sometimes given a voice—a low, infinitely patient murmur that never stops; the sum of all tiny fractures.

Their “turbulence” is a matter of alignment with these three. A person who lives well is someone who bears Weight without false defiance, who honors Fall by moving with it, and who listens to the Sway. To ignore any of the three is to invite a personal collapse—a sudden rooffall of fortune, an unexpected flood through a complacent district, an internal sinking.

They do not kneel before idols. Their sacred things are arrangements: the angle of a stone set under a threatening crack to take just enough Weight that it will fall safely and controllably; the precise depth of a relief basin cut below an unpredictable seep so that its overflow will feed a series of growth-walls rather than erode a crucial path. To them, piety and engineering are the same habit: attentiveness to how the inanimate wishes to move.

They have stories of other places, but not from their own experience. These are hearsay, ancestry, sediment in legend. There were Times Above, they say, when those like them—perhaps not quite them—walked on the open crust where the brightness came from a single point instead of everywhere. The air there was thin and dry, and things called plants intercepted Fall’s work, building their own patterns at great speed. The telling of these tales is half envy, half disbelief. Most here cannot truly conceive of darkness that is not choice, of light that might be taken away by something as simple as a turning of the planet.

Yet some Currents keep vertical routes. From certain high vaults, narrow chimneys rise into the haze. Along these, carefully notched, with hidden resting-places and caches of fat and water, small parties sometimes go upward as far as they dare. They return talking of colder brightness, tinged with blue, of fibers of ice hanging in tight mouth-like constrictions, of a noise like endless distant breaking when the ice above shifts. They say the light there is sharp enough to hurt. None now living have seen any sky beyond stone, but the idea persists that somewhere, further still, the brightness resolves into a single burning eye.

In the other direction, down, there is less curiosity and more dread. Below the level of their city, water rules more completely. There are sink-holes where a thrown stone does not return a sound; shafts that funnel all seepage down into invisible currents whose roar can only be heard through bone pressed to floor. There are stories of people who went too far into those depths. Sometimes they come back changed: skin darkened not with radiation of sunlight but with metal, fingers elongated and webbed by callus, eyes filmed over. Sometimes they do not come back at all, and their names are absorbed into the long roll-call of “Those Who Fell When Weight Shifted.”

They are not alone in the sense of having only their own kind. Other sapients share this basin-world, though “share” might be overly generous.

There is a people of the water proper—vertebrate, elongated, with translucent sails of skin between flexible supporting rods. They occupy the deeper basins and slit-shaped channels, their lives a ribbon of whirling motion and still-hanging rest. To your eye, they would look like a crossing of eel, manta, and fern: a central spine with radiating, feathered limbs that can furl tight or splay for maximum surface area. They are pale as all else here, but their innards carry a slow pulsing bioluminescence, not bright enough to rival the ambient light, but visible underwater as faint moving shadows of greenish glow.

They are not tool-users in the dry sense, but they do work: they sculpt silt and soft mineral by the constant fanning of their limbs, creating terraces, burrows, intricate honeycombs in submerged stone. The upright-dwellers call them the Veils. The relationship is old and complex. Veils are not domesticated, but their habits are predictable enough that humanlike Currents build their herds and basins around Veil-constructed spaces. Certain pools are understood to be Veil territories: off-limits for netting or harvesting. Elsewhere, agreements stand: Veils periodically drive shoals of small eyeless crustaceans into channels where the people can easily scoop them, and in return, constructed spillways ensure that flow patterns the Veils favor are maintained after seasonal surges.

Communication with the Veils is half myth, half practice. Adolescents in one Current undergo a rite where they enter a Veil-dense pool alone, unarmed, and let themselves be investigated. If they emerge unscratched, the elders say that Veils have learned their taste and will recognize them in future. Whether anything so sophisticated occurs is arguable. What matters is the courage and the acceptance of powerlessness—sinking into a medium where one is no longer the primary actor.

There are also the Lurids.

The Lurids do not like the constant brightness as it is. They seek its absences. They dwell where ceilings have slumped low enough that shadow is almost complete; where the geology intrudes odd, light-absorbing minerals that give back nothing of the ambient glow. They are related to the upright people in some distant way—you can see it in the hinge of jaw, in the pattern of ribs—but their bodies have gone with narrowness and compression and teeth.

Lurids are longer-limbed, but thinner, jointed like folding knives. Their skin is darker, not by pigment but by structure: a dense layering of fine scales or plates that scatter and internalize any light that hits them, rendering them oddly flat to the eye, as if even in this world of reflections they refuse to participate in reflection. Their eyes are smaller, sunk deep; their hearing is acute. They move in silence because the stone has been violent to them too often. Roof-falls are more deadly in the crumpled margins; floods come with less warning.

They deal with the central Currents through a cautious dance of exchange and predation. Lurids poach from the slime-walls and steal loose animals; in return, they sometimes bring things from even wilder margins—rare mineral nodules, unusual spore-crusts, reports of new cracks opening. There are raids, there is retribution, there are old feuds. Yet no Current wants the Lurids gone. Lurids go where others will not, and sometimes that is the difference between a disaster anticipated and a disaster devouring a district unawares.

Between all these, a fine balance survives, and into it are braided the daily strange necessities of an ecosystem without plants.

Their “farming” is more choreography than agriculture. They maintain walls of roughened stone designated for colonization by specific chemoautotrophic mats. These mats are almost invisible at first—a faint, mucous sheen that tastes of iron, sulfur, trace carbon compounds. Over time they thicken into textured films that look like nothing much, but a practiced hand can run fingers down a wall and tell from the faint stick, from the tearing resistance of the slime, whether it is ready to be scraped.

They do not eat the mats directly; they are too poor in energy, too laced with unpleasant compounds. Instead, they have bred things to eat them—creatures starting from wild, wall-clinging grazers and refined by centuries of selective capture and tethered breeding.

These grazers, which they call Thrum-backs, are about the size of a large dog in your terms, though their bodies are harder to measure because of the way they fold. Their skeleton is semi-segmented: a chain of stiffened plates connected by flexible cartilage. Each plate sports a ring of limbs with delicate, multi-pronged toes that can grip even overhanging rock. When moving, they shift load from plate to plate in a rippling pattern that, with many of them at once, gives the sense of a low, ongoing vibration—hence the name.

At their front end, they bear a mouth like a rasping disc. They press it to the wall, pulse their powerful throat muscles, and strip the mats down to bare stone. Behind them trails a thin string of waste pellets already half-reworked by bacteria living in their guts, ready to be gathered and fed into other nutrient loops. Their bodies yield milk of a kind—thick, mineral-heavy exudates from glands along their sides, expressed when hand-massaged by practiced keepers. This is concentrated nourishment: the distilled product of water, rock, microbial metabolism, and animal digestion.

Other domestic forms cluster near the grazers’ edges: slender amphibious nibblers that groom their limbs and eat parasites; burrowing mollusk-like things kept in carved pits that concentrate particular trace elements; gliding, mantis-winged scavengers that patrol for carrion and droppings. Nothing is idle. A fallen shed of skin or a discarded crust-flake is noticed, collected, assigned.

Where you might expect market squares, they have confluence-halls: spaces where flows literally meet. Here, narrow water channels are crossed by carved stone lips at different heights, so that some water is diverted, some joins, some falls to lower basins. Around these points, people cluster to trade. A sack of crustacean-meat traded for a bundle of treated hide; a length of sinew-rope given in return for a basket of still-warm Thrum-back exudate; a promise of future rights to scrape a young growth-wall secured by the delivery of a metal tool salvaged from deeper ruins.

The absence of fire changes the palette of their material culture. Metals, when found, are worked cold or with heat from rare vents. They hammer and anneal copper-like alloys patiently, using long cycles of gentle warming near geothermal seeps rather than open flame. Their knives tend to be stone—dark, glassy, knapped to teeth rather than fine edges. Bone is ubiquitous: shaped into hooks, toggles, lock-pins for rope-anchored structures. Fat is precious, nearly sacred, because it is both high-value food and, when anointed on mineral crusts, a means of deterring unwanted colonization.

Their “technology” seems simple until you see its precision. Consider one of their suspension routes: a travel-line between two leaning pillars over a pool too deep to ford.

They anchor the main rope at both ends with horn-like hooks hammered deep into pre-existing cracks. They protect the rope where it crosses any abrasive rock by carving, with great finesse, a half-tube of polished stone that clips around the line, letting it glide through with minimal wear. At intervals, they add vertical drop-lines to hanging baskets of stone rubble, tuned to a particular weight so that if the main line loosens due to shifting pillars, the altered tension in the drop-lines causes a small bone chime to ring. A single traveler, hearing that note, can know the line is unsafe and withdraw, leaving the Currents to come and adjust or abandon.

Language among them belongs as much to hands and shoulders as to tongues. In the damp clarity, vocal sound carries far. So they keep their spoken words low, mostly names for immediate things: “stop,” “slow,” “crack,” “listen.” More complicated ideas are signed.

Hands move in planes you do not ordinarily use: fingers brushing the air just above the stone to indicate time (“this close” meaning “recent,” “hand high” meaning “long ago”); palms turned down with curved fingers to indicate Weight and pressure; palms up, flicking fingers in falling motions for Fall. Sway, when named, is shown not by the hands but by a subtle roll of the feet, a shift of balance as if feeling a tremor.

Their children, trailing along house-walls and route-edges, absorb this language like they absorb the drip’s rhythms. A council of elders can stand in a near-silence broken only by water and still exchange complex positions of law, precedent, and concession in an hour.

What do they fear?

They do not fear darkness, for they have never had it. They do not fear loud sudden sound, because almost nothing in their world does that besides rare collapses, and those they know by other signs first. They fear three main things: thinness, silence, and stillness.

Thinness is the narrowing of a structure to failure—an overstressed bridge, a ceiling that has eroded too close to breakthrough. They train themselves to see thinness in the way a mineral sheet glows just a little differently, in the sharper note when a knuckle taps it. To sleep under thinness is a dangerous thing; they have rituals for it, small and formal, as if to negotiate with Weight for one more cycle.

Silence is the absence of drip where drip should be. When a basin that has always rung with a certain pattern goes mute, hearts tighten. It may mean a distant blockage that will soon burst. It may mean a permanent redirection—a resource lost, a shift in balance among Currents. In their stories, omens come not as new sounds, but as the cease of an old familiar one.

Stillness is the stopping of Fall. Not everywhere—just in a patch, a corner, a vault. When condensation ceases to gather on a certain ceiling, or when the thin fog that always curls along a trench’s lip fails to form, people notice. They have a word for such places: “Holds.” Some Holds are fortunate—zones where dryness allows tools and goods to remain unspoiled longer. Others are ominous—precursors to collapses, vents sealing, underground reservoirs emptying. No one sleeps in a newly formed Hold until they have watched it through many drip-cycles.

And what do they desire?

Not more light; they are saturated. Not conquest; there is nowhere to conquer that will not, in turn, conquer them with its own Weight and Fall. They desire routes. New linkages between previously disconnected basins. Shortcuts that render an old, precarious chain of ledges obsolete. A Current that discovers a safe new connection can rise swiftly in prestige and influence; people and animals will re-route through their sphere, and the trickle of goods will grow.

There is a kind of explorer among them, though the word is too glossy. Call them Path-Finders. They are not heroic loners. They travel in small, well-bonded groups, with redundant tools and carefully calculated weights. They move at the edges of known maps, testing floors with long rods, listening to their own hums reflect off unfamiliar surfaces. When they encounter a new space, they do as their ethic demands: they first listen. How does it drip? Where does the fog gather? What does a knuckle-tap on the ceiling say?

Only after a time will they lay a first human mark—a shallow groove in the floor to show the safest line, a small stone cairn that channels a trickle one way rather than another, a loop of rope left coiled at a promising but dangerous ledge.

They return from such journeys altered, stories folded into their bodies. They have new scars, new memories of sudden shifts and near-falls. They are treated with a cautious respect. Too much time at the edges can give a person a Sway-lean mind, one that finds it difficult to move at the slow pace of domestic cycles. Some Path-Finders cannot settle. They drift from Current to Current, hiring out their skills in exchange for a say in where new routes will be anchored.

Every few generations, a disruption comes that is larger than the usual cracks and surges. An overlying river carves itself an entirely new path and, without warning, empties itself for a season into the underground through some yet-unmapped sink. A distant tectonic adjustment raises one sector of the basin by a handspan relative to another, making old channels flow backward.

For those alive during such times, the world feels as if it is being unmade. But even here, their instinct is not to see endings. They see rawness—newly exposed surfaces, still bright with stored light; new lines of Fall. Disaster, once survived, is also an opening. A flood that scours a market may leave, in its retreat, a network of fresh, mineral-rich walls perfect for mat-seeding, or a stranded lake whose chemistry is unlike any they have yet harvested.

Their history is written not as lists of rulers or battles, but as sequences of such re-routings. Elders will sit in a hall whose center is a shallow depression—the remnant of a long-vanished basin—and with fingers tracing in its dust recall: “Before the Great Back-Fall, this used to be a throat. Water went through, not across. We once had to climb there.” Children, who have only known the gentle incline of the current plain, must imagine a vertical world stood on its side.

There have been, so the oldest tales insist, at least two times when their kind nearly withdrew entirely from this basin. Once, during an epoch when a chain of Holds grew so broad that large stretches of the city dried. Chemoautotrophic mats died back; Thrum-back herds thinned; fog ceased to trace the familiar veins. People followed the water downward, into narrower, darker places, and for generations their narratives shrank to local concerns.

The moisture returned—whether because overlying climates shifted, or because some obstruction failed and Fall re-routed. Those who had clung on in peripheral pockets re-expanded into the old halls, now stranger for having been empty. They found their own ancestors’ work rounded to near-nature; inscriptions faded beyond legibility. They re-founded Currents, sometimes on the memory of names, sometimes purely on geography.

So it is not that one people has lived here unchanged “for longer than any species.” Rather, a succession of related peoples—overlapping, diverging, re-merging—has worn its pattern into the stone. Each left some distinctive trace: a preference for a particular tool-form, a way of scoring stone in parallel lines, a habit of stacking rubble in spirals or in wedges. The present inhabitants read these traces as you might read different scripts.

In one quiet, peripheral neighborhood, for example, there is a sequence of pillars whose bases are encircled by low rings of carefully fitted stones. The living use those rings now as sitting-places, steps, weirs for small drips. But the rings themselves are older than any Current’s archives. Their style—tight joints, inward-leaning faces—is not used now. Children are told that an earlier Current built them as prayers against Sway, hoping that by encircling the feet of the pillars they could anchor the world. Whether true or not, the gesture persists: new constructions occasionally borrow the ring-motif when someone wants to bind themselves symbolically to a piece of stone.

If you stand there, at the edge of such an old ring, and watch the people move through their city of brightness and condensation, you can see how deeply place and person interpenetrate.

A young keeper, skin still almost smooth, shoulders hung with only a few mineralized ridges, walks with a yoke across their back, from which hang twin skins of concentrated exudate. As they pass under a drip, they shift slightly, instinctively, so that the water falls on the back of their neck and not on the precious cargo. They do not look up to do this; their body knows the rhythm of that particular drip.

Near a pool, an elder with heavy crust-plates along their spine kneels to trail fingers in water. The tiny organisms on their skin stir in response to some trace compound; the elder’s face tightens. Without a word, they rise and signal to a child nearby—two fingers flicked downward, then a palm rolled sideways: “The balance of this basin is changing. Inform the keepers.” The child runs off, knowing exactly who, in which Current, needs that information.

In a high hall, Path-Finders return through a curtain of gypsum-needles, shaking fine crystals from their hair and straps. They unroll, not parchment or cloth, but a flexible lattice of bone on which sequences of knotted sinew and attached shells mark the gradients, hazards, and promising drips of a newly-explored section. Councilors bend over it, hands hovering, reading the mesh with a fluency no outsider could match.

Through all of it, the light remains imperturbably bright, without dawn or dusk. Their sleep is not timed to cosmic cycles but to internal ones: bodies that tire after so many tasks, minds that need to dream. They darken their sleeping-places not by extinguishing illumination but by turning away from it: thin, carefully fitted plates of non-luminescent mineral, hoarded through generations, are pivoted or propped to block certain reflective surfaces. A sleep-niche may be as bright as any other place, but its occupant does not see the wider glow; they see only the inside of their own space, close and protected.

Dreams, when they speak of them, are drenched not in visions of other worlds, but in exaggerations of this one: infinitely high pillars with no ceiling; drips that fall upward; stone that is soft as flesh. Occasionally someone will dream of a single, small, intense light that goes out. Such dreams are considered unsettling, even ill-omened, though very few here can articulate why. Some fragment in their collective deep memory still twitches at the thought that illumination could ever be local, fragile, extinguishable.

Who lives here, then?

People who have turned patience into an art. People for whom architecture is conversation with time, and for whom every meal is a chain linking unseen chemical engines to the slow breathing of rock. Their bodies are palimpsests of stone and water, their tools modest, their politics built into channels and claims rather than flags.

With them are the Veils in the water, writing their own intricate currents; the Lurids at the borders, sharp-edged and wary; the Thrum-backs on their walls; the mats and crusts and sacs that spin raw mineral into edible tissue. Over, under, and through all of them move the great abstract presences they recognize as Weight, Fall, and Sway.

They live not in defiance of this bright, wet underworld, but in collaboration with it—restless but not hurried, always adapting, never finished. The city, to an outside eye, is ruinous: sagging, crumbled, endlessly eroded. To them, it is alive precisely because it will not hold still.

And as long as the light continues to have no source and no end, as long as water continues to fall in its countless asynchronous beats, they will continue their quiet, intricate practice of inhabiting: small, upright figures moving with deliberate care through halls that remember more than any of them ever could.
