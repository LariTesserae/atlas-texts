They do not think of themselves as “under” anything.

If they have a concept of orientation at all, it is oriented around gradients—of light, of heat, of wetness, of density of bodies. “Up” is toward thinner air and more violent brightness. “Down” is toward the slow, warm crush of others. Rock is not “ceiling” or “floor,” it is simply the limiting shape that channels the streams of life.

They are not one people. There is no single crown species around which the others orbit. Instead there is an assemblage of lineages, each filling roles that, in worlds with plants, would be shared between roots and trunks and grazers and fungi and flies and wolves. Here, animals have swollen into all those vacancies.

You could divide them, roughly, by how they meet the world.

---

### The Rooted

The “plants” here are not plants at all, but bodies that have made a long wager with stillness.

They begin as mobile things, tiny and active, like seeds with legs. For a time, they roam. They taste surfaces, sample water trickles, brush their sensory cilia along condensation beads. At some point—hard to define except as a convergence of signals: right mineral mix, right moisture pulse, right gradient of faint light or vibrational quiet—they stop and commit.

They extrude a glue.

It is not a simple adhesive. It is a complex interface, a bio-chemical mesh that bites into immature rock and soft clay, that wicks up passing films of water and clamps their bodies flat to stone or into small cups in the sediment. Once this anchoring front spreads, the rest of the body follows, thickening, branching, differentiating.

Those pale, fleshy cones with rings of cilia are one form. The cone is not a single organism, but a chimney of fused bodies, each layer an individual whose feet are sunk into the next, whose mouths face the air or water film. They feed not on light but on the snow of microscopic detritus: shed skins, spore-like propagules, the dust of chitin, bacterial fluff, pulverized feces, and dissolved organic films in the water.

Their cilia comb constantly, not for drifting leaves but for particles and for signals. Touch on one side of the cone sends a traveling wave through it, so that the chimneys can flex as a unit, bowing fractionally toward or away from recurring disturbances. Over time, an entire wall of these rooted filterers subtly rearranges its angles, like a field of sunflowers tracking the sun, but here they track the most reliable layers of nutrient-carrying air and seep.

The flat, many-limbed discs are another rooted guild. They adhere across wide areas, a living veneer, each disc a hub where dozens of pliable arms press into fissures, tasting for ions and bacteria in the damp film. Their backs form an undulating skin that other creatures walk over. They do not resist; in fact, they bend to create minute channels under the tread, guiding runoff and droppings toward certain central pits where the discs concentrate their own digestive chemistry. In effect, the traffic farms them nutrients.

All of these sessile forms are animals frozen in adolescence. They never shed their nerves; they do not relinquish sensation as plants might. They feel everything: the passing of a wing’s pressure wave, the heat of a large body resting against their patch, the change in water’s taste after a ceiling fall exposes new mineral. They have no centralized minds, but each network of connected bodies behaves as a sensing sheet. When stress is sharp—stone shifting, predator burrowing behind—they release abrupt sprays of bitter compounds and vibrational pulses that radiate through the rock and water films: warnings that others can use.

They do not move, but they make the underworld legible.

### The Treaders and Grinders

On the ground—where the notion of “ground” applies—live the ones who convert motion directly into change.

The thumb-length, jointed multitudes are the most numerous. They are not a single species but a complex of related forms, endlessly tweaked to slightly different microniches: some with broad, shovel-like forelimbs for flicking aside silt; some with extended mouthparts that rasp biofilm from stone; others with dense brushy antennae adapted to “taste” the chemical wake of passing bodies.

Their bodies are modular. Segments are repeated in long strings, each packed with muscles, nerves, and a few simple sacs for digesting whatever they encounter. They molt constantly, shedding outgrown armor as the rock itself sheds unstable slabs. Their chitin becomes the finest substrate, broken down and reincorporated by other feeders. Every corridor they frequent acquires their polish: a low, dark sheen from the oils of their skins and the grinding of their feet.

They are not individually intelligent in any mammalian sense. But they are exquisitely tuned to one another. Antenna taps, short trills of mandibles on stone, the exact cadence of footfalls—these encode simple messages: “Obstacle here.” “Predator scent high.” “Water film thickens ahead.” Nothing is stored long; the information is in the wave of moving bodies itself. You could think of the total swarm not as a single mind but as a murmuring of provisional maps, constantly redrawn by the pressure of others.

Larger treaders are rarer but unavoidable. The dog-sized, armored centipede-analogues are the displacement class, pushing through the small ones like ships through plankton. Their plates are thick, some with embedded mineral grains picked up from their environment and fused into their own armor—a form of passive tooling. Their legs are like hydraulic jacks, shortened by evolution to work under the low vaults and tight squeezes.

They feed on bulk: mats of smaller creatures, partially decayed carcasses, the softer tissues of the rooted colonies. But they cannot simply crash through; in such constant crowding, even a big body must negotiate. They do it through force-limited shoving and ritualized strikes of the foreplates: patterned thuds that warn the smaller fauna. The swarms respond, parting just enough. An uncooperative mass that refuses to open can be punished: the big treaders simply drop their weight, grinding a patch flat, leaving a glossy, lifeless smear. It is a crude conversation between density and mass.

Some of them carry.

Along their flanks, tucked between plates, live commensals: tiny, agile cleaners that groom joints, nip incipient infections, and alert their hosts to odd chemical traces by drumming on the plates in specific sequences. These smaller creatures gain transport and access to fresh carcasses when their host feeds; in return, the host gains an extended sensorium and early warning.

Farther down in the sediment, there are burrow-treaders, built like blunt wedges, front end a hardened scoop. They don’t just travel through the soft pockets; they maintain them. As they push, they tamp walls, compacting and smoothing. Their tunnels become the default conduits for fluids and for still smaller things. Their constant passage oxygenates what would otherwise be an anoxic sludge, keeping the underworld’s slow composting from tipping into rot. They are the plows of this place, but with no fields, only passages.

### The Hangers and Listeners

On the undersides of ledges and ceilings cluster forms that have abandoned trust in their eyes.

They are translucent because pigment is no longer useful here. Instead, membranes are thin to pass vibrations, and body cavities are filled with specialized fluids of slightly different densities, creating layers that resonate with distinct bands of sound and pressure.

From each hangs a forest of sensory threads—antennae, frills, fans of hair-thin cilia. Each thread contains tiny crystalline elements, like sand grains grown in place, that flex at specific frequencies. So when a mass of treaders shifts below, or a big plate-creature begins to shove, or a ceiling farther away begins to crack, the hangers feel it as a chord through their bodies.

They rarely move once they’ve chosen a spot. Their limbs, reduced to hooks and pads, fuse into the stone. They feed by casting out fine nets of adhesive filaments, catching the constant rain of organic dust and the occasional drifting larva. When surplus matter accumulates, they retract their nets and press everything into a temporary storage sac, digesting over hours or days.

Their real ecological role is as early-warning stations and as routers of crude information.

They communicate mostly by pulsed vibration. One will shudder at a certain tempo when it detects the deep grind that precedes a rock slump. Others, connected to the same patch of stone, pick up that pattern and repeat it along their own anchoring limbs. Waves of such shudders can precede an actual collapse by precious seconds, enough for the more mobile creatures below to reverse direction, to coil into protective shapes, to brace.

They also read the overarching “hum” of the underworld: the sum of wings and feet and falling drips. Changes in the global soundfield—like the sudden muting when a major light shaft is choked higher up, or the amplifying of a certain corridor as a new void opens—alter the background that they have internalized over their lifetimes. When those signatures shift, they secrete different blends of volatile chemicals, drifting down as scents that the ground fauna can interpret: the closest thing this world has to weather reports.

Some species of hanger enter symbiosis with the rooted colonies. Their anchoring limbs plug into the fleshy cones or mats, sharing nutrients and information. The rooted feel the minute changes in water chemistry; the hangers feel the changes in long-range vibration. Together, they create composite sensing bodies that can anticipate both the far and the near.

### The Fliers and Threaders of Air

Where space allows, the air itself becomes another substrate to be grazed.

The small fliers—rigid-winged, beating in blurs—are hunters of particles and of one another. Their wings are stiff, mineral-reinforced panes that cut through the turbulent air currents with sharp, insect-fast precision. They dart through beams of light not for the brightness itself, but because those columns of warmth and illumination are rising chimneys of convection, bringing upward the richest scents and faintest aerosolized tastes from the crowd below.

They hunt in three-dimensional lanes, weaving between the slow, powerful flaps of larger soft-winged gliders. To the naked eye, it looks like chaos. To them, it is layered order: each species holds to favored bands of temperature and flow, like schools of fish hugging particular thermoclines in the sea.

One species in particular has wrapped its life around those light shafts. Slender-bodied, with long tapering wings that can fold into near-invisible blades, they spiral up and down the bright wells in synchronized swarms. Their bodies are covered in photo-sensitive patches, not eyes but skin that registers changes in illumination. The rapid flicker as they cross the beam’s edge, shadow to dazzle, seems to play into their mating and social codes. Courtship is written in spirals, in choreographies of crossing and recrossing the light until pairs are made.

Their larvae cling in the upper reaches of the shafts, where real sky occasionally appears. There, the larvae feed on what the deeper world barely knows exists: pollen grains and spores and the dried crumbs of leaves that fall from the unseen surface. These are exotic minerals here, foreign dusts that carry different chemistries. The shaft-fliers have in effect become importers, pulling a trickle of “surface” energy into the underworld.

The larger fliers, the ones with soft, damp wings, are less agile but more enduring. Their limbs are strong and jointed; their bodies, protected by soft but thick integuments rather than hard shells, are built for long patrols between widely spaced chambers. They are the main vector-keepers: they carry reproductive packets, symbionts, and hitchhiking parasites (and mutualists) between otherwise isolated pockets.

Many of them navigate by sound. Those short, metallic cries—they are not simply calls of location, but bursts of sonar. The gliders emit sharply timed clicks and read the echoes, building a temporary picture of the hall they cross. Their skulls, such as they have, are honeycombed bones; the returning sound resonates through resonant chambers and along specialized hairs. They can sense the difference between an empty nook and a nook full of bodies by the way it “rings.”

Some have developed loose associations with ground groups. They trail above the routes of certain dense treaders, snatching up the tiny organisms that are scattered and startled by the passage of the mass. In return, they may emit alarm pulses when they detect predators above, or when air from a new shaft bears the high-pitched roar of an impending collapse.

### The Opportunists and Cutters

In a world where every surface is crowded, specialization often means the invention of temporary privacies.

Tool-using species arise where bodies and environment create enough leverage for something like structure. A few lineages have crossed that threshold.

One such is a many-limbed cutter, roughly the length of a human forearm, with a ring of prehensile appendages around a central torso. Each limb ends in a triad of small claws flanking a sensitive pad. Their exoskeleton is patterned with little socket-like pits where they can insert and hold foreign objects: shards of rock, splinters of old carapace, smoothed pebble-disks. They do not manufacture tools from scratch, but they curate their bodies with them.

A cutter approaching a patch of brittle rock will pause, select a sharp shard from a limb socket, and use it to chip at a seam until a piece flakes free. It then pries at the exposed layer, testing for the faint chemical signatures of the rooted, sessile filterers below. If it finds them, it worries the rock apart, opens a capsule of protein-rich flesh, and feeds. The remains are left open for smaller scavengers; nothing is wasted.

These cutters are also the ones who carve those straight rows of shallow pits along busy routes. To a human sensibility, they might look dangerously like writing. But they are more like braille and lamp posts combined. The pits hold a secretion whose luminous component fades quickly but whose texture and taste linger. To those who navigate by tongue and feelers, the pits say: “This way is traveled; this way is currently viable.” When rock slumps and corridors close, the cutters mark new routes. No mind plans the network, but many small choices keep it updated.

Another opportunist lineage meddles with water.

They are small, squat creatures with spade-like anterior segments that can scoop and pile soft sediment. Their mouths extrude a viscous binder, a secretion that hardens on contact with air into a tough, water-resistant crust. With this, they build ridgelets and low dams at runnel edges, diverting drips so that they fall where roots or nurseries need them most.

Their work is not collective in the grand architectural sense—no vast synchronized building, no single design. Each individual responds to hyperlocal cues: a too-dry nursery patch, a drooping cluster of larvae in need of more seep, the bitter alarm molecules of rooted colonies under thirst stress. It piles a bit of clay here, scrapes a groove there. Multiply this by thousands across generations, and the underworld’s hydrology bears their handwriting: trickles slanted toward certain hollows, vertical water sheets made thicker where soft cones cluster.

When their own life cycles demand safety, many tool-using opportunists wedge constructs into rock. Simple doors—a smoothed stone disk sized exactly to plug a favored crevice, held and pushed into place by a muscular body—turn a random crack into a defendable den for molting or incubation. Some carry these disks with them, nestled under their bodies, until a threatening scent sharpens in the air; then they retreat into the nearest viable gap and slam the disk into place, their limbs splayed to anchor it.

This is technology at the scale of a hand or mandible, scattered everywhere but never aggregated into cities.

### Predators and the Hidden Violence

Where there is so much flesh, there must be things that specialize in ending it.

Not all killers are obvious. The whip-limbed predators that strike from crevices are elongated, flexible creatures, laterally compressed like living blades. Most of their length is muscle wrapped in stiffened but flexible cartilage; their skin bears rows of vibratory sensors that taste the currents of air churning through a corridor.

They wait in slits in the rock, bodies folded, limbs retracted. When a suitable density of passing bodies reaches a certain threshold near their hiding place, they extend a single whip-limb in a lightning-fast lash. Hooked spines at its tip snag a creature from the passing swarm. If it is small, it is yanked whole into the crevice. If larger, the limb’s barbs hold while the predator extends more appendages to dismantle it piece by piece, pulling the dismantled parts in.

They are aided by the very crowding that sustains them; the swarms flow around the gap made by the disappearing prey, filling it a moment later. Predation here is often invisible beyond a brief contraction in the living carpet.

Ambush burrowers are the subterranean analog. They live within the thinner, loosely packed sediments, their bodies encased in flexible shells armed at the front with concentric rings of teeth-like projections. They monitor the density and rhythm of the feet overhead; when the pattern signals a thick vein of bite-sized bodies, they erupt upward in a lunging cone, ingesting a mouthful of whatever happened to be above them. The opening collapses behind them as they re-submerge, leaving only a briefly deformed patch of moving creatures.

There are apex predators of sorts: larger, heavily-plated forms that graze less and hunt more. Some glide low over the swarms on folded limbs, their undersides lined with reversible hooks. They drop suddenly, pincering and pinning larger prey against the rock, then injecting paralytic fluids before feeding.

Yet, “apex” is misleading. Nothing is solitary or secure for long. The carcass of a would-be top predator becomes, within hours, a buzzing, crawling center of attraction. Delicate-limbed piercers arrive first, tapping soft tissues and sucking out fluids. Then chunk-biters peel away plates; borers tunnel into bone-like supports; larvae of many kinds are laid in crevices, to hatch into a storm of mouths. Within days, only a thin residue of chemically stubborn material remains, often spirited away by builders as structural reinforcement.

Hunting is less a clean event than a redistribution of matter from one turbulent eddy in the crowd to countless others.

### Nurseries and the Practice of Care

In a realm with so few empty spaces, a truly sheltered pocket is immensely valuable. It is in these recesses—behind bulging walls of half-translucent stone, within the angles of partially collapsed ceilings—that something like tenderness appears.

Many species, even those that elsewhere appear clumsy, become deliberate and surprisingly gentle in their nurseries. The gelatinous larval masses hung under shelves are assembled with care: first a scaffold of hardened secretion, textured so that droplets cling and distribute evenly; then a layering of jelly, rich in dissolved nutrients and microbes. Adults may coat their own bodies in a fresh film of nutrient secretion and brush against the larval clumps, renewing their stores.

Certain small builders specialize as nursery tenders. They are attracted to the specific chemical bouquet of larval jelly. Instead of feeding on it, they adjust the micro-hydrology: moving pebble-grains to redirect drips, scraping away fungal films that could overgrow and suffocate the mass, excavating tiny drainage channels so that waste fluids do not stagnate. Whether they understand what they are doing in any conscious way is irrelevant; evolution has made them respond to particular cues with these maintenance behaviors, and the result is care.

Parents, in some lineages, maintain direct contact. A brood of hanger larvae may be connected by thin cords of tissue to the bodies of their adults, sharing fluids until they are large enough to detach and harden. Big treaders often leave their young in the calmer recruitment chambers while they push into the denser currents to feed, returning with boluses of half-digested mash they regurgitate into communal pits. Here, larvae of multiple clades may mingle, but their parents differentiate them by minute scent signatures, nudging their own closer to the richest lumps.

Failure of a nursery—sudden drying, rock shift, predator intrusion—reverberates. Adults already overloaded with sensory data still allocate attention to these off-route pockets. The quiet rustle of a healthy nursery becomes, in their neural nets, a known motif. When that motif changes—becomes too still, or too shrill with alarm—the tending individuals may alter their usual circuits, break patterns, arrive in numbers. Their ability to act is limited: they cannot lift boulders or command the multitudes. But they can shore a crumbling edge, plug a crack with their own bodies, or, failing that, cannibalize the failed clutch to reclaim nutrients for the next attempt.

In this way, despite the press and churn, lineages persist locally. The crowd never becomes a pure soup of interchangeable bodies; family lines leave faint statistical signatures.

### Bioluminescent Weavers of Dark

Where the daylight shafts cannot reach, other forms of light become currencies.

The glowing patches on walls and nests are not decorative. Many are colonies of symbionts: bacteria or tiny multi-celled organisms living within the tissues of rooted cones or discs or within specialized skin folds of hangers. They convert certain waste products into low-level light. In return, they gain reliable habitat and nutrients.

The larger animals that glow—the millipede-like chains of greenish dots—are signalers. Their luminescent organs are under nervous control. A line of them moving in file through a deep corridor can flash patterns: long pulses indicating a clear path; staccato bursts marking a sudden obstruction or predator scent. Their following kin read and respond, adjusting speed or direction.

These light-lines are not altruists. Their glow makes them more visible to predators. But the patterns they use can also confuse and deter attackers. A sudden, synchronized flare from many individuals may mimic the brief glare of a shaft opening above, startling light-averse hunters. More importantly, by leading conspecifics along safer or more efficient paths, they collectively reduce the per-individual risk—a primitive networked navigation.

Glow is also used in courtship. Certain small, otherwise inconspicuous species of ceiling-hangers extend flexible stalks tipped with luminous bulbs. In the darkest recesses, they pulse in sequences that prospective mates recognize. Successful sequences result in bodies drawing near, limbs interlacing to exchange gametes before parting again to their solitary perches.

The underworld’s few larger cavities devoid of any direct sunlight can, at certain times, feel like the inside of a slowly breathing constellation: soft pulses along walls, drifting dots of moving glowers, all superimposed on the steady mechanical chorus of wings and feet. To eyes evolved in darkness, this is as rich and informative as daylight.

### Perception and “Mind”

Do any of them think, in a way we might recognize?

No single species here writes, stacks stone into grand geometry, or weaves myth. Their tools are few, their constructs small, their memory mostly chemical and procedural. Yet there are glimmers.

Some cutters remember particular routes, favoring them over others even when immediate cues like scent have faded—suggesting some retained inner representation of space. Large gliders recognize individual treaders with which they have long mutualisms, adjusting their flight to shadow them specifically. Hangers and rooted colonies, though without centralized brains, display plasticity: their responses to certain vibration patterns shift if those patterns are repeatedly decoupled from actual collapses, attenuating false alarms.

More intriguingly, the collective behaviors of multiple species form emergent patterns that, to an outsider, might feel intentional.

Consider a newly opened chamber: fresh rock, a fall from above, a void where stone was. Within hours, scouts from several clades arrive: small fliers testing air currents, ground swarms sampling minerals, burrowers probing sediment depth. Their findings propagate indirectly—through altered scent trails, through where the swarms thicken or thin, through the faint chemical tags bored into nearby rock.

Within a day, water is subtly rerouted by builders so that drips fall into the chamber’s most sheltered alcoves. Rooted larvae drift or are carried in, adhere, and begin to anchor. Hangers settler the stable arches, creating a sensory canopy. Predators test entry points, staying in the shadows where traffic patterns favor ambush. Nothing commands this; yet the result is a self-organized, functionally zoned habitat.

This happens over and over, wherever new space appears.

If there is a “mind” here, it is distributed, slow, and blind to its own existence. It resides in the soft biases and hard-won reflexes of countless bodies, and in the literal channels and textures they etch into stone and water flow. The underworld is not merely inhabited; it is being written into, pupated within. The rock is a draft manuscript, constantly being overwritten by footfall and secretion.

### The Upper Hints and Their Indifference

From above, seeds and dry plant fragments fall—messages from a world that has solved life differently.

Here below, they are not ignored; everything organic is food first. Some grinders and cutters show a slight preference for that unfamiliar tissue: the fibrous crunch of dried leaf, the sudden sugar spike from a seed. A few lineages have even evolved specialized mouthparts to exploit these rare windfall resources more fully, cracking tough seed coats, grinding lignified tissue.

But there is no upward gaze of longing. The shafts are gradients of danger as much as of opportunity: brighter means less cover, more erratic falls of debris, sudden changes in temperature. Those shaft-fliers that use the uppermost reaches are themselves hybrids of worlds: their larvae tasting pollen, their adults bringing the surface’s signature molecules down on their wings.

Their existence hints at future possibilities—more sustained flows of surface energy into the underworld, perhaps the eventual domestication of certain falling plant forms as predictable resource. But for now, the falls are random and small. The crowd does not depend on them. Life here is built primarily on the recycling of its own flesh and the slow leaching of inorganic gradients from newborn stone and trickling water.

This inward, autarkic focus explains the underworld’s indifference to the idea of a sky. The few glimpses of blue or blasting white from an open shaft are simply another kind of light, with its own risks and cues, rather than a symbol of elsewhere. For those beings whose entire world is gradients and contact, an empty spanning distance would be an unthinkably barren place.

### What It Is, Altogether

So what lives here?

A chorus of bodies that have learned to live without solitude. Organisms that have ceded the fantasy of stable ground and still air, and instead made the flux itself their element.

Rooted animals that play at being soil and plant, turning water films and dust into biomass while serving as the world’s nerves. Swarms of treaders that grind stone toward soil and keep organic matter in constant circulation, their rivers as integral to the landscape as any rock rib. Hangers that listen to the whole vibrating mass and shudder the news through stone. Fliers that turn shafts of light and air into highways and mating grounds, threaders of a three-dimensional maze. Builders and cutters who take what the world offers—shard, drip, crevice—and leverage it just enough to create temporary edges against the press. Predators that flicker in and out of visibility, spikes in a continuous background of feeding.

They are not a people in the singular. They are a condition: a volume of space where geology is newborn and has not yet settled into its own hardness, where the very youth of the stone leaves room for life to insert claws, root-bodies, and tools.

They live as the rock cools and cracks, as water writes its first sentences through it. In that window—before the tunnels close or widen into clean caves, before plants above can send down roots thick enough to claim the stone—they have filled the volume as thoroughly as they can, an animal bloom in the planet’s fontanelle.

Someday, perhaps, the crowding will ease. The rock will harden and fracture; voids will become stable, plants may snake in, new sources of energy may arrive. If intelligence of a more recognizable kind emerges here, it will emerge from these lineages: from tool-touchers, route-markers, nursery-tenders, vibration-readers.

For now, what lives here is everything that can, all at once—an underworld body composed of a million smaller lives, forever in the act of pressing itself into being against the soft, forming stone.
