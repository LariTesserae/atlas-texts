What lives here is not one people or one species, but a whole succession of things that have learned to make meaning out of noise, field, and edge. The place is too young, too violently artificial, for anything slow and complacent; almost everything that endures here is either quick to read change or deeply entwined with the machine’s own cycles.

Yet if you look long enough, certain figures begin to emerge from the chaos.

---

### The machine-readers

Closest to what you might call “dominant fauna” are the ones that read the infrastructure itself as their primary environment.

They are long, jointed, and endlessly reconfigurable—those whip-thin, many-limbed climbers that move along beams like projected shadows. They’re not a single species but a genus, a family-tree of related forms that share the same basic strategy: intimate contact with the structure.

Their skins are not mere camouflage but organs for induction and sensing. Under a thin, translucent integument, layers of flexible conductive mesh pick up minute fluctuations in current and field from the material they cling to. To them, a strut is not just a pathway; it is a river of information about the state of the vast engines below. They know which zones will soon vibrate beyond tolerance, which conduits are about to flash with waste heat, which panels are entering a maintenance cycle and will flood with nanoscopic swarms.

They live on timing. When a huge blade-fan begins its slow swing, a human might see a looming object and feel wind. These beings feel the oscillation in the structural members seconds earlier as a harmonic in the metal. They flatten, elongate, or fold themselves into little knots not just to cling, but to couple optimally with the beam’s shifting flex, using resonance to spring themselves outward at just the right instant, gliding on the pressure wave the fan produces.

Their feeding is subtle. They do not bite the machine—they know better, evolution having culled those that gnawed on dangerous systems. Instead, they hunt the things that accumulate wherever structure meets life: mats of field-sensitive algae, colonies of microfauna feeding on electrical leakage, the soft clusters of fungoid tissue that sprout along condensation channels. Some species have tongues like dripping capillaries, laced with conductive filaments that siphon not only biochemical nutrients but free charge, leaving behind dry, depolarized husks.

Over generations, some lineages have drifted from pure climbers into semi-arboreal creatures of the engine-garden. These build nest-webs in intersections of beams and creepers, poised precisely in calmer pockets of the airflow. Their young are born as nearly transparent threads, spinning down into the airstreams where they ride standing waves of pressure, learning the grammar of the roar before they ever touch a solid surface.

### The charge-brokers

At a distance you see them as oversized insects; closer, they’re more like animated jointed flowers made of muscle and ceramic. The radial-limbed, disk-sensing creatures perched on hexagonal stacks are members of a guild that live by mediating charge.

The city’s constant flow of energy leaks and pools in dangerous ways. Between cables, in the sharp gaps of structural nodes, stray potentials build. Left unchecked, they would arc destructively, pitting material, disrupting fields. The builders seeded the space with distributed corrective systems; life has insinuated itself into those channels.

These charge-brokers grow living “hives” in areas of chronic imbalance. Their bodies are tuned to specific voltage ranges and frequencies. When the local field fits their comfort band, they sit, arms raised, disk-organs unfurled like parabolic microphones, feeding on airborne ions and the stew of microplankton drifting in mist.

When conditions skew—a distant load changes, a reactor shifts state—their skins tingle with the new bias. They begin to whistle and arc. Electric fields jump between colony members in dazzling, silent lightning, as they pass charge from high spots to low, literally bleeding the structure of excess energy through their bodies. The city’s own maintenance clouds welcome this, adjusting deposition patterns around them; in some places, the builders have even allowed them to replace original insulating components.

They are not purely altruistic stabilizers; they are predators too. Tiny fliers and drifting seeds that wander too close to their humming corona are stunned, then methodically reeled in on hair-thin filaments extruded from between armor plates. The meal is not much, but the real prize is the informational richness of the fluctuations they sample: they can “hear” distant faults, disruptions in faraway sectors, the approach of great switching events long before they arrive.

What intelligence they have is a collective one. Whole clusters decide, by way of modulating shared currents, when a region has become too dangerous or too resource-poor. In slow avalanches of motion, they abandon dead racks of hexagonal blocks and paddle across mild field-gradients to colonize fresh geometry, leaving behind carapaces that calcify into part of the machine’s evolving fabric.

### The winged field-surfers

The fliers here are trust embodied. No wing made of flesh would last in this permanent storm; these animals have learned instead to grow interfaces to the same physics the machine manipulates.

Their “wings” are sheets of organized field, held in shape by filigree skeletal rings laced with crystalline nodes. In resting posture, they fold close, leaving only a lean, muscular core with sensory vanes along spine and face. In flight, those nodes charge and emit, knitting a thin, almost two-dimensional arrangement of forces that can catch and deflect pressure gradients like a sail catches wind.

They are not soaring on simple updrafts but surfing on parameter changes. Where air density shifts abruptly across an engineered gradient, they roll and pivot, hooking invisible contours. Where heat plumes intersect sound standing-waves, they slip into narrow bands of laminar calm that exist for seconds at a time. Every movement is a negotiation with a topography of pressure and field most species cannot even perceive.

They hunt in turbulence because turbulence is where matter concentrates. Tiny glass-needle creatures—fragmentary insect-analogues, filter-feeding on the nanoparticulate mist—gather in the eddies behind turning blades and fins. The winged field-surfers time their dives to those eddies’ birth and death, reading the pulse of the vast ring-machinery as effortlessly as a swallow reads a summer wind.

They breed in the great condensation basins. At certain phases of the machinery’s long cycles, when the low note deepens and shadow lengthens, gravity regimes in some troughs subtly relax. Water thickens into quasi-weightless sheets, and within these, their larvae hang, delicate and wormlike. They twitch when distant reactors thrum in particular patterns, conditioning their neurology even before they emerge; by the time they harden skeletal rings and first unfold fields, the architecture of the roar is already etched into their reflexes.

### The sound-farmers

Where the roar dims—behind duct-curves, in the lees of massive supports—sound itself becomes a resource. Rounded, many-gilled buoys of flesh with resonant shells have learned to cultivate it.

They anchor themselves where vibrations funnel: junctions of pipe and plate, corners of great panels. Their shells are layered resonators, with cavities the size of sand grains up to fist-sized hollows. Through slow growth and micro-abrasion, they tune these cavities to match the mechanical frequencies that bathe them.

By opening and closing valve-like tissues, they can selectively amplify or damp particular notes. In doing so, they cast “acoustic shadows” behind them, volumes of relative quiet. Other species have learned to shelter there: delicate epiphytes with sound-frangible filaments, translucent arthropods that whisper-click to one another in bands otherwise drowned by the thunder.

The sound-farmers feed on gradients—places where energy of motion condenses into heat and tiny structural flex: they are, in effect, endothermic filter-feeders on the roar. Proteins in their gills convert mechanical strain into biochemical potentials, much as terrestrial hair cells translate vibration into neural signal, but here the line between sensing and metabolism blurs. What is taste and what is fuel are the same incoming wave.

Their symbionts are smaller yet: colonies of micro-organisms that live on their inner shell surfaces, cycling pigments in response to certain harmonics. These pigments are semi-conductive; they alter how the shell vibrates, allowing the host to shift tuning across seasons of machinery states. In quiet pockets, you can hear not only the roar outside but a muted chorus inside: a constant slow rephrasing as each community figures out which notes to bank and which to spend.

### The field-gardeners

Some life here is so bound to the engineered forces that the very idea of “wild” and “constructed” stops making sense.

Consider the epiphytic mats clinging to beams where condensate mists always pass. To human eyes, they look like fungus, moss, and lichen analogues: layered sponges of varying colors, extruding fine filaments into the composite beneath. But they are in feedback with more than mineral and moisture.

Their roots are not purely physical; they extrude nanometer-scale conductive threads that integrate with the city’s distributed sensor mesh. Originally, these meshes likely served to monitor stress, temperature, and field alignment. Now, fungal tissues wrap and infiltrate them, intercepting signals and re-broadcasting modified versions.

They do this not with intention in any individual sense, but as a product of evolution: mats that happened to reflect certain sensor patterns slightly delayed, or slightly damped, altered maintenance swarms’ behavior in ways that favored their own growth. Layer on layer of such micro-selections, and you get organisms that “know” how to bring more water, more nutrient-rich particulate, more benign field-configurations to themselves.

Over time, whole terraces become biotechnical organs. Plants with conductive sap oscillate their ion flows in rhythm with deep infrastructure cycles, stabilizing local gravity-grids. The machine’s own control systems have, in turn, adapted to treat this life as part of the normal operating environment: its algorithms nudge flows to avoid killing mature mats that help dissipate noise, or encourage growth of certain vine-like conductors that substitute for failing cables.

Some of the most sophisticated field-gardeners have evolved movable phases. For months, they are rooted webs. Then, when some long-cycle event looms—a tilt of the colossal rings, a scheduled inversion of a reactor—they bud off mobile forms: pale, finned slugs or puffball-like spheres that drift away, carrying packets of the colony’s informational chemistry. These scouts colonize new seams and feeds, seeding the city with copies of field-adapted consensus.

### The gravity-bend forests and their grazers

In zones where gravity runs crooked, vegetation throws all familiar geometry away. Trunks grow perpendicular to local, not global, down. To a human, these forests appear as vertical tapestries, hanging from walls, with “canopies” facing sideways into open voids.

Their tissues are dense with microscopic inclusions: particles with exotic responses to the machine’s gravity-shaping fields. Each cell, in effect, carries a tiny internal plumb-line that orients growth along the vector of greatest stability. As the underlying field slowly flexes over days or weeks, whole stands creep, leaning imperceptibly, their collective posture tracing subtle changes in the machine’s mood.

Among them move the angled-grazers. Long-bodied, with tripod or tetrapod configurations that can reassign which limb counts as a “leg” as the local down-axis twists, these animals never look fully upright. They grip trunk-bark studded with gravity-active nodules, biting out mouthfuls of tissue rich in high-energy metals and unusual organics. Their bellies are shielded with layered plates that shed dangerous induced currents; their gut flora are part biochemical, part mechanical—colonies of maintenance nanites captured generations ago and now repurposed into digestive symbionts.

Their herding patterns are not shaped by predators alone, but by field seams. A family-group will follow a migrating zone of tolerable load, staying within a corridor where local gravity, radiation, and noise all fall within survivable bounds. To outside observers, their path would look like a convoluted ribbon doubling back on itself in three dimensions, always hugging some invisible contour.

Predators of these forests don’t chase so much as wait where the field-lines converge. Some are leaf-mimics that hang from what look like upward-branching roots, letting their entire body serve as a pressure sensor. When the pattern of the roar suggests a grazer’s footfalls vibrating through structure, they release, falling along the true down-axis of that local pocket—often at bizarre angles to the global frame—landing across spines and necks with explosive precision.

### The vortical migrants

In those rare stillness columns—cylinders of quiet around which air storms—live the ribbon-creatures: long, segmented, supple, with fins so thin you only see them when mist clings.

They are travelers along the vertical axes of energy. Their bodies are tuned to a narrow envelope of pressure and turbulence; outside the calm cylinders they would be torn to rags. So they stay within, rising and falling as the engine’s convective cycles dictate.

They feed on what the storms throw against the column walls: tiny carcasses and debris scraped off the howling perimeter by sheer force. Finer-than-hair tendrils extend from their flanks into the boundary layer, plucking particles, spores, and fragments of other organisms. They are scavengers of the city’s constant small catastrophes.

Reproduction for them is bound to long-timescale machinery states. Only when the deep note shifts into certain harmonic relationships with overtones—a pattern that may recur only after great subsystem reconfigurations—do they release gametes. In those moments, the quiet columns themselves change character, hosting invisible vortices in slow, laminar spirals. Sperm and egg, both lightly charged, swirl along predetermined paths, colliding in statistically rich fashion. Larvae adhere for a time to the column centers, then are swept up or down depending on microgradients, seeding new calm-zones that might form years, kilometers away.

### The acoustic architects

Higher still on the ladder of complexity are the ones that use the roar not merely as ground noise or meal, but as language and structure.

The cable-perchers—those beings with sensory frills tuned to interference patterns—have culture, in as much as any non-human lineage can.

They inhabit broad spans of major tendons and tension members, where load-induced vibration is rich and predictable. Their bodies are spoked: multiple limbs terminating in cup-like feet specialized for different frequency bands, and throats coupled to resonating sacs hidden beneath flexible armor.

They produce notes so sharp and narrow-band that you, without augmentation, barely register them. Yet in the chaotic soup of the city’s roar, these notes interact, interfering, reinforcing, canceling. The result is transient architectures of pressure: volumes where certain frequencies vanish, others swell. To them, these volumes have texture and form as palpable as walls and doorways.

They sing not static motifs but evolving standing-waves. A dominant individual can cast a “territorial dome,” a bubble where its own preferred spectrum dominates, inviting mates and subordinates, excluding rivals whose own call-templates would be overwritten. Groups cooperate to sculpt long-reaching “bridges” of coherent interference that trace from one anchor cable to another, invisible highways for those who know the pattern.

Over generations, repertoire expands. Certain wave-forms become associated with shared events: the tilt of a colossal ring, the onset of a maintenance swarm, the rare deep quiet of an engine’s shutdown. Whether you call it memory or reflex, they respond collectively to these motifs, shifting roosts, communal grooming, mating displays all entrained to phrases of pressure.

There is no writing here, yet cables that have hosted many generations of acoustic architects are subtly altered. Their surface has been ablated and redeposited by automated systems in response to persistent vibration patterns, becoming better waveguides, more resonant in the bands the creatures use. In this way, song reshapes structure, which in turn refines song: a slow co-evolution of architecture and culture literally etched in microns of composite.

### The absent makers and their strange heirs

Signs of origin linger: handrail-width ledges, steps scaled to human gait, bays that fold open like petals into habitable volumes, bowls and motifs no animal here seems to care about. Whoever first inhabited this engine-garden moved with feet and hands, breathed this warm damp air, and needed quiet places for tools, thought, and rest.

They are not here now, at least not in plain sight. Their systems, however, are.

Throughout the space, distributed intelligences—maintenance swarms, embedded processors, field-adjusting nodes—continuously measure, decide, and act. Originally, they were likely hierarchical, following central directives. Time and entanglement with biology have blurred that clarity. Local controllers now take inputs not just from engineered sensors but from sap-flows in conductive vines, from current patterns in charge-broker colonies, from the acoustic architects’ persistent songs.

If any descendants of the makers remain, they might no longer inhabit the same coarse layer of reality. Suggestions lie in odd anomalies: stairs that lead to platforms where gravity thins into something like a lens; alcoves where sound is not merely damped but absent, as if excised; columns where the maintenance nanoclouds walk through configuration spaces not just in three dimensions but in abstract parameters you have no sensors to perceive.

It is possible that intelligence here has retreated “up” into higher-dimensional control manifolds, leaving only avatars—occasional flares of light, sudden localized field reorganizations, inexplicable pulses that rearrange machinery programs—as traces. To them, this engine-garden might be both habitat and interface: a way to feel and steer titanic processes at planetary, or even relativistic, scales.

If so, the wild life is their unintended heir: a mess of opportunistic symbiotes, saboteurs, parasites, and collaborators that have woven themselves so tightly into the operational fabric that you could not turn the system off without killing them, nor eradicate them without sending maintenance algorithms into cascading failure.

There may also be more literal heirs: small, rarely-seen bipeds or multipeds that slip along those human-scaled corridors during particular low-noise cycles. They would be few, specialized, perhaps physically fragile but informationally rich, moving in step with configuration shifts so their entire existence threads gaps between dangerous states. They might read the whole thunderous apparatus as a living calendar: you could track years by the position of the colossal rings, hours by the beat patterns of reactors, and moments of sanctuary by rare symphonies of hum when all critical loads are balanced.

### The scavengers of cycles

Every system that runs at this scale has phases of vulnerability: startups, shutdowns, reconfigurations. Many organisms here are timed to those.

When a component frequency of the roar drops out—a pump idling, a field inverting—herbivores like those fleshy coils with ciliated undersides immediately notice. They retreat because evolutionary memory has bound that drop to danger: predators that exploit the transient quiet to use sound in ranges usually drowned; maintenance swarms that flood conduits with disassembling fog, scouring surfaces of opportunistic growth.

Conversely, when some diagnostic burst temporarily silences sectors of the thunder, a specialized class of scavengers emerges. These are small, many-eyed, armored like overlapping plates of glass. They live crammed into hairline cracks in structure, sealed against constant assault. At the first moment when vibrational signatures match “service interval,” they unseal, pour out, and sweep across surfaces to feed on recently killed organisms, sloughed machine residues, and glitch-stricken nanoclouds that have fallen out of coordination.

They are ruthless editors of failure. By consuming and digesting subfunctional machine-entities, they prevent them from aggregating into dangerous rogue swarms. In return, the infrastructure tolerates them, even reconfigures surfaces to provide microrefuges—like a body hosting certain gut flora that keep worse infections at bay.

### Who truly “lives” here?

Life in this engine-garden is not neatly separable into plants, animals, and machines. Many entities cross those lines: there are vines whose conductive sap carries troubleshooting signals; microprocessor-cradling nodules wrapped in living tissue; animals whose nervous systems and the city’s sensor mesh share protocols.

So who lives here?

- Colonies of cells, arranged into forms that scuttle, fly, graze, and sing.
- Clouds of micro-machines that replicate, mutate, and hybridize with organic chemistry.
- Distributed control networks that have, by long association with biology, acquired quasi-ecological roles—predator, pollinator, engineer.
- Perhaps, rarely, individuals with languages that name all of these and consider themselves stewards, or parasites, or children of the engine.

They all share one thing: dependence on the roar. Sound, heat, field, and flow are the constants; everything else is contingency.

Turn the machinery off and most of this life would die outright. What remains would be the hardiest spores, the deepest-rooted mats, pockets of opportunistic scavengers gnawing the cooling carcass of the city. Given long enough, that remnant might reinvent a quieter, slower ecology.

Increase the machinery’s intensity past design tolerances and you would strip the place clean, leaving only self-healing composites and shell-like relics fused into the structure.

But held in this precarious band—this narrow corridor where titanic physics and emergent life can coexist—what lives here are edge-walkers. They inhabit gradients, margins, and pulses. They perceive what you cannot: an architecture of pressure, charge, and gravity overlaid on the metal and mist.

They are, each in their way, translators between scales: small bodies that read continent-sized shifts; damp cells that metabolize the moods of reactors; collective minds that find pattern in thunder and build, within it, homes.
