What lives here has, above all, given up on the idea of *elsewhere*.

Nothing in this basin is built for journeys, migrations, long clean arcs from one condition to another. The inhabitants are creatures of sufficiency, of saturation, of being entirely inside a single, never-quite-changing now. Their bodies and temperaments follow from that.

---

### The ones that are almost ground

The most pervasive lives are the ones that you would, at first, mistake for terrain.

Under your feet, in the shallow dark between the thin soil and that unbroken stone sheet, live the *underplates*: vast, slow, continuous organisms that spread like skins. Their tissues are neither root nor fungus, but something between—thick, fibrous, spongy but with a faint muscular give if you push hard enough.

They do not form discrete individuals so much as territories of sameness, color-shifted in slate greens, bruised purples, pale ochres. Somewhere in the distant past they may have begun as competing colonies; now their borders blur, overlap, trade cellular parcels. Where one underplate shades into another, the difference is more like dialect than like a frontier.

They breathe through the land’s pores: small, regular openings that pucker and unpucker, exhaling damp mineral scent and faint methane, inhaling CO₂, steam, volatile organics. The “muffled roar” that seems like distant surf is in part their constant, continental respiration. Microfauna ride those breaths, drifting in and out of the soil in swarms, an aerial plankton that feeds the filters in the mist-hollows.

The underplates digest whatever seeps down to them: rotted leaf threads, dissolved bone, the metabolisms of the endless bacterial mats. They are the place’s gut and its slow muscle. When an enormous surface-creature shifts, it is the underplates that flex against the stone, redistributing pressure, making the land feel like a resilient mattress rather than dead crust.

Nothing here thinks of them as separate animals. It would be like calling your own dermis a neighbor. But they are alive, and they are the oldest inhabitants: the basin’s first decision to be a single, continuous body.

### Slow ridges that remember movement

Those enormous “ridges” that uncurve from the soil are not rare monsters; they are the underplates’ higher expressions, their experiments in verticality.

Call them *heavies*.

They rise only a body-height or two above the general sprawl, but each heavy is long, broad, and low, like a fallen tree given a shallow arch and packed with wet meat. Their backs are padded with what looks like moss and lichen, but if you cut and wait, it heals—not as bark does, by forming scar wood, but by a slow knitting of living tissues that do not know they are separate.

Heavies are not individuals so much as mobile lobes of the ground-organism. They pull themselves by retractile pads along the underplate’s upper surface, dragging soil and root webs with them, smearing microtopography into new arrangements. The basin’s subdued relief—the gentle swells, the spooned-out depressions—is largely their work, over spans so long that urgency would be meaningless.

They graze not by biting plants but by lowering their warm, damp undersides onto particularly rich patches: mats of fungus, layered leafmold, scums of algae. Their skins exude a fluid that is both solvent and seedbank, dissolving what’s beneath while inoculating it with communities they favor. When they lift themselves again and creep along, they leave behind an altered patchwork: some zones newly bare and oozing, others suddenly thick with a particular fungus or rootlet they carry with them.

Things live on them: whole microforests of specialized mosses, insect-analog colonies, delicate, hair-fine tendrils that sip from the heavy’s blood-equivalent and vent scented plumes. Small grazing arthropods ride their backs, safe from many predators simply because nothing else is big and patient enough to argue with a heavy. In turn, those riders are food for quick opportunists that dart in and out whenever a heavy pauses.

The heavies have no need to hurry, no reason to flee. Nothing in this world kills them quickly. When part of one dies, it slumps and flattens and joins the underplate; elsewhere along its length, new bulges may rise. Identity for a heavy is not a question of “which animal” but of “which region of the land has chosen to rise for a while.”

If there is such a thing as memory here on a landscape scale, it might be encoded in the heavies’ tendency to revisit the same shallow circuits over decades, pressing and re-pressing certain tracks, preventing tall, brittle forms from ever establishing. They keep the basin low, soft, and smooth simply by existing.

### The treefolk and their quiet calculations

The trees are not rulers, but they are the basin’s great experimenters.

They have given up on height. The sky above this place is never generous enough to reward fifty meters of risk. Instead, they have discovered the advantages of *horizontal thinking*: thick, low trunks, sprawling limbs, an insistence on occupying breadth rather than altitude.

Within their tissues live networks of slow, chemical integration that, in a different world, might have gone unnoticed. Here, in the absence of fire, freeze, or famine, such networks have had ages to elaborate. Sap flows are metronomic; signals travel in gradients of hormones along branch and root. Each tree is a subtle analyst of its surrounding density: the weight of spores on its leaves, the composition of the breath washing past its stomata, the pattern of impacts from falling condensation.

They do not think in the human sense, but they do *calculate*. A limb thickens or thins in response not only to its own load but to dozens of tiny influences: the particular mix of fungi at its base, the species of vine most prevalent in its neighborhood, the swarm-patterns of certain sheltering insect-analogues that come and go.

When a tree senses damage—bark scraped by a heavy’s passing, or a major limb broken by some rare mass-fall from above—it does not react with alarm; there is no fast danger here. It simply subtly adjusts a long set of allocations: more growth here, less there, a slight shift in the chemical bribes offered to symbiotic fungi. Over years, these slow choices add up into personalities, into different architectures of branching and canopy.

Some trees cultivate particular constellations of epiphytes and vines, effectively “decorating” themselves with whatever assemblage yields the best nutrient trade. A few foster clusters of carnivorous, root-mouthed plants along their own roots, letting those predators harvest the endless stream of creeping microfauna in exchange for a share of the digestion seeped into the soil.

It would be too much to call the trees *intentional,* but they are not passive. They are local optimizers of a condition whose overall parameters never change.

### Rope-bodies and bridge-makers

In the lower canopy, where trunks begin to merge with the roof of leaves, live the ones that most resemble animals in the way you’d expect: the *braiders*.

Braiders are long-bodied, multiply jointed climbers. They look like cords of muscle threaded with sparse, sensory fronds rather than hair. Their limbs are numerous, small and hook-tipped; when they rest, they wrap themselves around branches and vines so thoroughly that they become indistinguishable from them.

Their trick is to build themselves into the place.

A braider rarely moves alone. They live lined up along a shared path, each body partly wrapped around the next, forming living cables that stretch between trunks, across humid hollows, along the undersides of big leaves. While the basin as a whole has no “paths” in the human sense, braider chains create temporary, shifting causeways for things that are too delicate or hydrophobic to push through the lowest tangle.

In doing so, braiders are paid—not with deliberate currency, but with happenstance. The constant traffic of small grazers and fungus-lickers along their bodies provides a steady buffet: discarded skins, stray spores, fragments of softer tissues. Braiders’ skins are slightly adhesive, exuding slow digestive films that trap and integrate this rain of micro-debris.

They can unhook and flow along their own lines, rearranging the living bridges according to faintly perceived gradients of opportunity. Where the constant sound thins a little (indicating perhaps fewer predators), where a particular fungal glow is strongest (signalling rich decay), they’ll stretch. Where heavies pass too frequently, scraping low branches, they retract.

To an outside eye they might look cooperative, social. To the braiders, who have no self-conception beyond the narrow tube of their own body’s appetite and tension, it is simply what the world feels like: a constant adjusting of length and grip in response to a dense field of soft signals.

### Things that glide and sing

Some inhabitants split their existence between branches and the heavy, static air under the lid of haze.

There are several kinds of *gliders*, each with its own solution to moving through an atmosphere that barely stirs.

One kind has bodies like segmented seedpods, each segment ringed by a thin, moist membrane. At rest, they cling to the undersides of leaves, membranes folded between segments, making them look like dry shells. When they push off, the membranes unfurl not into flat wings but into cupped, flexible vanes. They do not beat them; they *shape* them, squeezing and relaxing to squeeze the thick air, riding pressure differences as one might knead clay.

Their calls are low, resonant, almost liquid: pulses produced not in throats but by momentary stiffening of the membranes during glide, turning their whole bodies into muted instruments. Each glide writes a line of damp sound in the air, and in the constant hush-roar of the place, these sounds are private languages. Gliders navigate not by vision alone, which is poor in the amber-grey light, but by the echoes of their own calls off the wet surfaces below.

Another kind of glider is more planar, with four long, webbed appendages that recall both wings and hands. They launch rarely and land often, preferring short, careful hops from perch to perch. Their eyes are small, their mouths wide and ringed with fine filter-combs.

They are mid-air netters of the insect-clouds. When a cloud of microfauna grows too dense, too locally bright with wing-reflections, these gliders slip out, sweep their open mouths through, and drift back, combs clogged with bodies. The act is more like grooming the air than hunting.

Their influence is subtle but important: they prevent explosive blooms of any one airborne species from occluding all light, becoming literal storms of wings. In this way, their foraging maintains the haze at its familiar density, keeps the basin’s light at its usual exhausted level.

Their voices are small eddies in sound, soft chewing clicks and short damp chirps that add texture to the endless standing noise but rarely rise above it.

### The arthropod multitudes

Everything here that is not large or rooted is, in some way, a walking stomach.

The arthropod-niche creatures are myriad, but they share certain themes: segmentation, many legs, surfaces that are never quite dry, sensory structures tuned more to chemical gradients and vibrations than to sharp images.

The heavy-bodied grazers you see on roots are some of the more obvious. They are broadly oval, their dorsal sides convex and dappled in patterns that echo the fungi they love. Their underbellies are soft pads that exude enzymes; they do not really *chew* so much as lie upon a patch of fungus or algal crust and let it melt into a slurry, which is then wicked up into internal fermentation chambers.

On their backs, attendant miniforms live: minute cleaners that pick off mold overgrowths that would otherwise smother the grazer’s sensory pores, mite-like scavengers that colonize every crease and joint. These tiny riders bring with them their own bacterial cohorts, and so each big grazer is not only an eater but also a mobile source of inoculation, bringing fresh microbial communities to every substrate they visit.

Other arthropod analogues specialize in the half-solids. Some have flat, wide feet that distribute their weight across ooze, letting them trot where others would sink. These are the basin’s *tenders*: they break surface skins of algae and bacterial mats into patchworks, preventing any single mat from sealing off the water entirely. Their walking—and their constant small bites—keeps oxygen, light, and nutrient gradients dynamic enough to support the vast consortium of life within each shallow pool.

Predatory arthropods rarely chase. Why would they? No prey flees far, and nothing is forced by seasons into panic. Instead, they grow mouths like waiting traps: broad, flat plates that can press slowly down onto a patch of busy ground, enclosing dozens of smaller bodies within. Then come the digestive mists, gentle as fog, dissolving skin and shell alike. When they lift again, little remains but a slightly enriched smear of slime that others will feed on in turn.

Even flighted insect-forms, whose brisk wing-beats suggest energy and haste, move in lazy drifts rather than darting lines. Their swarms bend to follow minute shifts in humidity or chemical trace, taking the shape of that invisible landscape. For them, the basin is not crowded; it is generous, offering the same feast of microscopic films at every height, on every surface.

### Hunters that are also plants

Predation here is often vegetative.

There are *root-mouths*—plants whose main bodies are modest, unremarkable mounds of leaf and stem, but whose roots end in flexible, ringed apertures. These orifices do not gape; they *feel*: slow, inching expansions and contractions, taste-testing the soup of mud and microbodies.

When a concentration of small creatures flows along a channel—perhaps driven by some subtle pressure gradient, perhaps by nothing more than habit—a root-mouth will, over days, notice. It will bud more rootlets in that direction, extend more tasting rings. In time, a small forest of flexible mouths may lace the underside of a favored runnel, each one a soft-lined snare.

Nothing spectacular happens when prey arrives. A moving thread of minute bodies enters a root-mouth, the ring tightens a little, and everything—water, soil, animal—is slowed, then stopped, then incorporated. The plant thickens, its aboveground parts slightly glossier, its leaves a touch broader, all over timescales too long for any single observer but themselves.

Similar strategies occur in the canopy: leaves that fold, not quickly like carnivorous traps but slowly, beginning to curl around a persistent insect trail over days; vines whose surfaces exude anesthetics, so that resting gliders wake up subtly entangled and find that their minor twitches have already been converted into nutrient flows.

Nothing struggles dramatically here because nothing is attacked in ways that reward panic. Death is a gentle coagulation, a thickening, a losing of edges.

### The mist-walkers and air-drinkers

In the hollows where mist hovers, some species have taken the atmosphere itself as substrate.

Tiny *mist-walkers* are built like six-armed stars with long, hydrophilic hairs. Each arm ends in a pad of fringe that can catch and hold clumps of suspended droplets. The walker’s mass is low, its body lightly charged. As it moves, each step is not onto ground but onto a denser cluster of aerosol, which its hairs momentarily pin into a makeshift surface.

To an outside observer, they seem to trot on nothing, just at the top of the pale mist, leaving swirling wakes where they disturb the microcurrents.

They graze on the organic particles the air always carries: fragments of spore, dried bacterial chains, microscopic fecal pellets from the infinite crawl below. Their mouths are on their undersides, constantly combing the mist for those denser specks.

Above them, *air-drinkers* hang unmoving in the vapored light. These are colonial organisms that have grown into delicate, umbrella-like lattices—thin frameworks of translucent tissue strung with millions of cilia. They root in the branches overhead or on long, whip-thin supports rising from the ground.

Their ciliated veils vibrate constantly, drawing air through and trapping whatever life-dust it carries. They never close; there is no “feeding time.” Every moment is feeding time when the world itself is a steady aerosol of the edible.

Over long periods, air-drinkers can become massive, trailing layered veils like chandeliers of living fabric. When a heavy or an especially persistent braider route changes the airflows in a hollow, the air-drinkers respond by budding new supports in the direction where the hum of micro-wings is loudest, letting gravity and thickness guide their growth.

### The near-minds: watchers without tools

No civilization has arisen here, no tool-using culture that stacks matter into new logics. But there are beings whose internal complexity nudges against what you might, elsewhere, call awareness.

Among the canopy, there are *perchers* that watch.

Physically, they are unspectacular: compact, many-limbed climbers with soft, wrinkled surfaces and eyes that are little more than pale pits. What sets them apart is not a strange morphology but unusual *attention*.

Most creatures here live in continuous appetite: they move insofar as chemical gradients or tactile stimuli impel them. Perchers move for other reasons. They reposition to *observe* changes in their neighborhood: a new fungal bloom, an unusual cluster of deaths (marked by quick flushes of scavenger activity), a tree’s limb thickening in an unexpected direction.

Their nervous systems are disproportionately large relative to their skimpy musculature. Within their bodies, information does something more than trigger movement: it circulates, is compared, loops back. They have simple memory, crude expectation. They are capable of what could be called boredom, a restlessness when the patterns they track stay too constant for too long.

They respond to that restlessness by exploring small variations: a different perch, a slightly altered path along a vine-work, a short glide where they usually climb. Over generations, this mild exploratory bias has made them accidental cartographers of the immediate.

They do not carve trails; they lack the leverage and the reason. But their constant, slightly-not-random movements have increased the connections among branches in certain heights, as their repetitive use of particular vine-bundles encourages those vines to thicken, fuse, and grow more permanent.

In a world without seasons, they are agents of the only weather that matters: tiny redistributions of where attention falls, and thus where future opportunity—however unconsciously—collects.

If any line here were ever to lead toward deliberate tool-use, toward objects that persist in form and function beyond the bodies that shaped them, it might need to pass through something like the percher. But the basin does not ask that of them. They do not need abstractions to survive. The world has no sharp edges to press them into innovation.

So they remain what they are: slightly-too-curious watchers perched between the forest’s general vegetative knowing and the almost-animals’ simple hunger.

### The omnipresent small intelligences

Beyond these, there is intelligence of another order, smeared thinly across billions.

The bacterial mats, the translucent films on water, the faint glows in the fungal veils—they all host signaling networks, exchanging chemical notes about nutrient levels, viral incursions, shifting pH. In a more stressed world these communications would be frantic, emergency dispatches. Here, they are more like gossip, a constant low-grade commentary on conditions that never tip far from the mean.

From pool to pool, mat to mat, there propagates a distributed consensus about how rich things are, how laden with certain ions, how crowded a given micro-niche has become. The underplates listen to this. Trees tune their stomata and root exudations to it. Heavies, in some mute way, flow more toward zones where the mat-voices hum with surplus.

No one unit, no single patch of slime, houses a mind. But taken together, the microbial substrate is the basin’s ambient mood. When, on some rare occasion, an external perturbation occurs—a cold influx of underground water along a fracture in the stone sheet, perhaps, or a temporary dimming of the high haze by some distant volcanic dust—the entire web detects it.

The response is not panic but a slow redistribution of emphasis. Certain fungi bloom a bit more thickly in the newly cooled hollows. A class of filter-feeder runts a bit smaller that generation, metabolically thrifty. Underplates shift their breathing pores a fraction. The heavies’ routes bend, almost-imperceptibly, away from the less productive regions.

The sum of these reactions is so smooth you would not perceive it as a reaction at all unless you had watched for centuries. From within, it is merely that this place *always* feels as if it is in exactly the state it prefers.

### What it feels like to be from here

If a consciousness shaped in this basin could articulate its experience, it would not talk of seasons, or journeys, or crises. It would speak—if it could—of *gradients*.

Pleasure would be a slightly richer thickness of spores on the air, a narrower band of temperature across the skin. Discomfort would be a deviation from familiar density: too little film underfoot, too much clear water exposing bare stone. Danger, such as it is, would feel like an absence in the sound-field, a hollow where the constant rustle and buzz have thinned, indicating that something large has moved too recently, or too often.

Nothing here fears the future as an unknown category, because the future is provisionally identical to the present and the present to the long stretch of past. The only relevant question is: thicker or thinner? Warmer or cooler by a hair? More crowded in this corner or that?

To live in this basin is to be an organ of a body that does not know it is a body, only that it is *full* and wishes to remain so.

---

### Who lives here?

So who lives here?

- Vast, continentlike underplates, the land’s skin and gut, from which rise the slow heavies.
- Heavies themselves, living hill-creatures that press and knead the world into its low, smooth shapes.
- Broad, calculating trees, horizontal thinkers negotiating with fungi, vines, and symbionts over decades-long bargains.
- Braiders, rope-bodied bridge-makers whose living causeways stitch the mid-heights together.
- Gliders: membraned seedpod-things and four-limbed air-groomers that sing the air’s shape and comb the insect haze.
- Arthropod multitudes: grazers, tenders of algal skins, patient plate-mouthed predators, clouds of winged foragers that are the weather made of bodies.
- Root-mouths and carnivorous tissues hidden among the plants, for whom predation is absorption rather than attack.
- Mist-walkers and air-drinkers, walking on aerosol and sieving the atmosphere for the constant fall of organic dust.
- Perchers, near-minds who watch patterns instead of just following them, adding tiny slants of curiosity to an otherwise self-sufficient world.
- And, beneath and between them all, the distributed intelligences of films and mats and veils, diffused cognition that keeps the whole basin sitting inside the same, long-held decision to be exactly this and no more.

Nothing here lives alone. Nothing here is entirely itself. Everything is an organ in everyone else’s body, and together they inhabit not simply a place, but a single, saturated condition that has learned—over a long, hot, humid patience—how to go on almost unchanged.
