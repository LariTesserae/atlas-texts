Those who live here are people who have decided, somewhere far back in their history, that being loud in the world is a kind of error.

They are small in footprint more than in stature. Adult bodies are compact rather than tall, built for bending, reaching, stooping: strong in the back, in the thighs, in the hands. Their skin, regardless of its underlying tones, tends toward the color of things often seen in this light: the browned white of dry roots, the dim gold of dusted stone, the muted olives and umbers of old leaves. Hair runs dark or pale, but never vividly so; it’s worn close or braided in modest cords that won’t snag on jutting roots and beams.

Their faces are the most expressive part of them, because voices are held in check. Brows and eyes and the set of the mouth do the work that elsewhere would be done in exclamation. Their gaze is attentive in a way surface-dwellers might find unsettling; they look as if they are listening with their eyes, watching your breathing, the way your shoulders shift, the place you put your feet.

You would call them quiet, but “quiet” for them is not absence; it is a practiced craft.

They rarely walk heel-first. Feet come down as though on the back of some sleeping animal they mean not to wake: broad-soled, usually bare or wrapped in soft, thickly woven fiber that grips and muffles. When they climb the carved steps, they know which bowl of stone catches sound, which spills it; they choose the spill-less paths by habit. Children learn their own labyrinth not as a map of where things are, but as a choreography of where sound goes.

No one living remembers a time when their homes weren’t already rounded by use, when the steps weren’t already cupped. Their stories begin long after the dramatic collapses and upheavals. The world as they know it is a found thing, something they arrived in—whether their ancestors climbed down from an outside forest, or stumbled inward fleeing some forgotten danger, or awoke here in a tale they do not tell. They do not claim to have made this place. They say instead that they have “helped it remember.”

They call themselves—if translated loosely—Attendants, or perhaps Keepers. Not keepers in the sense of ownership, but the way one “keeps” a promise, “keeps” a fire: tending something that precedes you and should outlast you.

Their dwellings are not “houses” as separate abstractions; each family cluster or pairing lives in a curve of the overall form. A woven wall defines a sleeping hollow. A low platform, smoothed by generations of sitting, becomes a central place to work, tell, mend, think. Curtains of fiber stand in for doors, but they are more for draft and sightline than for privacy: sound, as always, is attended to as a shared element.

They have no written script in the sense of ink on portable surfaces. Writing, to them, is something carved into the stone that holds the air and the water. A mark not worthy of affecting condensation is not worth making. The glyphs that gather moisture—those geometric channels and ridges that coax water from the thin cold breath of this place—are their oldest inscriptions. No one alive first made them; each generation rediscovers or re-cuts a fading groove, repolishes a ridge the water has grown shy of.

Some say those first glyphs were found already working, etched into stone long before the Attendants. Others insist that some early ancestor discovered how to speak with water by trial—scratching patterns, watching where the first bright beads chose to cling. The disagreement has never been settled, because the older truth is not their concern. What matters is that the glyphs function, and functioning things are treated with reverence.

Every child, at a certain age, is given a small facet of unmarked rock in some marginal, dry corner and told to call water there. It is both craft lesson and rite. The child carves shallow lines with an inherited stone tool, guided by a patient elder. Then they come back day after day to see whether the air has listened. Often, for weeks, nothing happens. Then one morning a shine appears: a dampening along one channel, a first slow drop that creeps where before the stone was dull. The success is not shouted; it is acknowledged with the same subtlety as sun returning to a familiar ledge: a hand on the shoulder, a word spoken close. The stone remains, its channels deepened over years. Eventually, it will join the great, established patterns that feed the basins and terraces.

Their language is full of verbs about listening to things that do not make sound. To “hear the water” is to read condensation trails, to see whether the patterns of beading follow last decade’s memory or have edged somewhere new. To “listen to the stone” is to watch the way dust settles, to notice hairline cracks before beams complain. To “attend the air” is to know which shafts are sending colder breath, which warmer, and adjust plantings and hanging mats accordingly.

Light to them is not an abstract quantity but a kind of slow-moving creature they shepherd.

They keep meticulous mental and oral maps of where the sun will fall on a given day of the year, at given hours, through each opening above. Elders remember thirty-year patterns of slightly shifting shafts as roots thicken, as a far-off rockface succumbs to moss. When a ledge that once grew an herb requiring bright reflection begins to dim, someone patiently relocates both herb and mirror-stone to where the light now runs, amending the city-as-lens.

There is a small lineage among them—fewer than a dozen alive at any time—who specialize in this: the Light-Binders. They are the ones who polish and place the reflective slabs and plates, who make minutely adjusted wedges of stone that tilt an incoming brightness a fingers-width one way or another. They carry in their heads an understanding of the sun’s invisible path through the labyrinth, but they do not draw diagrams; they walk the city with their eyes narrow, recalling past seasons: “Here the edge of the light used to fall at planting time. Now it stops before this crack. Move the berry-bushes down one ledge; bring the moss trays up.” Their word holds weight. To ignore them would be to waste light.

If there is a priesthood here, it is divided among the Water-Shapers, the Light-Binders, and a quieter group yet: those who work directly with the animal presences.

They do not keep pets in the way other cultures might. The gliding mammals, the pale nesters in the beams, the translucent-bellied lizards—these are not owned, but cohabited with. Yet the Attendants watch them with an almost studying affection. Certain people among them—call them Sympaths—are adept at reading the language of these animals’ motion. The way a beam-nester pauses at a crack, licking there more frequently than before, may tell of a new seep. A lizard that changes its patrol route might be tracking a subtle bloom of algal film, indicating a shift in the moisture’s chemistry. The Sympaths are also the ones who decide which corners of the city remain undergrown by humans, left as havens where animals can breed and feed with minimal interference.

Food for these people is a continuous, small tending rather than a grand, seasonal spectacle.

They farm in beads, not fields: pockets of rich soil layered into stone basins and root-bound troughs. Leafy greens carefully chosen for their affinity to cool temperatures and diffuse light. Tubers that swell slowly beneath the moss, drawn forth without ever stripping the covering entirely. Mushrooms trained to particular stones, each variety paired with its preferred blend of dimness and humidity. Seeds are not sown in broad scattering; they are pressed one by one into pre-warmed hollows, each remembering the hand that placed it.

Protein comes from many small sources: mushrooms with higher nourishment, insects bred in hidden loam-troughs, a few carefully managed colonies of the rounded grazers that feed on algae. Larger mammals are never slaughtered wholesale. A gliding creature found dead of old age is used reverently: hide tanned with gentle mixtures, bones polished into needles or toggles, meat shared. On rare occasions, a planned culling is done of older, non-breeding individuals, but it is managed quietly, almost formally, and every part of the body is used. They do not like to introduce violence into their soundscape; even necessary death is done muffled, concealed from echo.

Their tools are the accumulated patience of centuries.

Every stone blade in use has a lineage; each has a name, not out of sentimentality, but because its properties—sharpness, angle, slight chips—must be known to everyone who might use it. “Borrow Stream’s-Edge for the vine-thick beams; its bite is kinder to sappy wood.” “Take Old-Rain; its weight knows the gutter stone.” When a blade finally wears too thin to hold an edge, it is not discarded; it goes to the children as practice stone, or is set into a niche where condensation runs, becoming part of a tiny, elaborate shrine to the way things wear and are remade.

They work bone with a precision born of long habit. A rib becomes an awl, its point so fine it can pierce fiber without fraying. A knuckle-bone is drilled and strung as part of a counting-string: a tactile record of something that happened, a planting, a repair, a birth. Hard seeds, smoothed by fingers, join these strings. To run your hand along one is to read a story not in symbols, but in sequence and texture: smooth, rough, thick, thin; dry, slightly damp; warm from handling, cold from long neglect.

Metal, when it appears, is like a voice from another age. They have a few pieces: copper beaten until it is almost translucent, a bronze needle held in a wrapped-cloth case and touched only with oiled fingers. They do not smelt; what they have was found, or traded long ago with some people of the Outside. Those stories are told with a mixture of awe and unease. The Outside, from which comes metal and wind and storms, is a place of great power and greater waste, they say. The Attendants’ task is to take as little from it as they can.

Not many of them go up, above. Those who do are changed by it.

There is a small, rotating group — the Uplookers — who climb the convoluted shafts and narrow canyons to the forested lip above the sinkhole. They go in pairs or threes, wrapped more heavily against a wind they never quite get used to. Their job is to watch the health of the overstory: the ancient trees whose roots knit the ceiling, whose fall would be catastrophe; the patterns of rain and drought; the wandering of any surface-dwellers who might stumble too near the openings.

They speak of color differently, having seen it unfiltered. To them, the dull berry-reds and leaf-greens below are echoes. They carry back tokens sometimes: a leaf dried to translucency, a stone with sparkling inclusions, a feather from a bird that never descends. They are listened to with a kind of careful curiosity, the way one might listen to someone who has spoken briefly with a dangerous god and returned mostly unharmed. Their words shape long-term decisions: whether to thicken a root-wall here, to draw down more moisture there, to adjust for an oncoming shift in the outside climate.

Children here grow up surrounded by so much continuity that they must be deliberately taught to notice change.

There is a ritual, recurring every few years, where an elder escorts a child along a particular path and asks, at intervals: “What is not as it was?” The child points out a missing basket, a thicker curtain of vine, a new gutter etched beneath a condensation track. If the child fails to see something significant—a beam newly reinforced, a seep that has dried—the elder does not scold. They bring the child back later, after the repair has quietly progressed, and show them the “sound” of what they missed: “Hear how stepping here is duller now? There is fresh wood behind that wall. Remember its old sound, and you will feel the change next time before you see it.” Memory, for them, is auditory, tactile, textural.

They tell stories, but they do so close-in, voices barely higher than breath, and often accompanied by gesture more than drama.

There are myths of the time before condensing glyphs, when water fell only where it chose. Tales of a child who carved against the flow and angered the stone, causing days of dryness. Morals about never trying to hoard what must circulate. Another cycle of tales centers on light: an ancestor who polished a stone so mirror-bright that it burned a young fern, and how, overcome with guilt, they dulled all surfaces for a season until persuaded that careful guidance is not the same as violence.

Their weirdness is not something they think of as “magic.” It is etiquette between them and the slow agencies of stone, water, and light.

They do believe the place is alive—not in a simplistic, animist personification, but as a large, extremely slow organism they are partly inside and partly made of. They speak of the city as “the Hollow,” “the Lung,” or simply “Within.” When rock shifts with a deep, resonant creak, they’ll pause and place a palm against the nearest wall, saying a phrase that might translate as, “We hear you.” When a condensation path changes after a long unbroken flow, they talk about it the way agriculturalists elsewhere talk about weather: “The Hollow has turned its face a little.”

Illness among them is rare in the dramatic sense. The constant cold and damp invite certain ailments, and they have herbs and fungi for those. But diseases that rage and burn through populations cannot find much purchase in a people who live spaciously within a small volume, whose contacts are many but rarely chaotic. They are intimately close, but more in time than physical crowding: each shared surface is used by many, but slowly, in sequence.

Births happen in sound-insulated hollows, lined with layers of fiber and moss that will later be carefully composted, returned to soil. The newborn’s first cry is one of the few loud sounds the Hollow expects and welcomes. Neighbors pause, tilting their heads: another voice added to the low chorus. Deaths are marked differently. There is no place to bury bodies intact, and the soil is too precious for simple graves. The dead are returned in pieces: bone taken for tool and token, flesh processed in ways that vary by family tradition—some dried and crumbled into the substrate that feeds mushrooms; some given to insects and tiny grazers in a hidden corner, transmuted back into the cycle that will eventually feed everyone again.

The idea that something of the person persists in those cycles is not questioned; it is observed. “Your grandmother tastes sharp in this crop of fungus,” someone might say, with no more irreverence than if they said, “The shade has shifted; the caps are narrower this year.” Ancestors are present in the enduring layout, in the curve of a well-placed beam, in a particularly efficient gutter. To touch a perfectly cupped step is to touch the wear of thousands of feet, many of whom have names still spoken.

Conflict does exist among them, but it tends to be long, low disagreements rather than flare-ups.

Two groups may argue, for years, about whether to widen a condensation gutter that might steal a little dampness from a neighboring moss-field, benefiting some crops while endangering others. They will gather, speak in tight circles, use pebbles on a smoothed patch of clay to model the flows. Children watch, learning that “argument” is not a raised voice but a rearrangement of shared pictures. In the end, if consensus cannot be reached, they defer to the slowest voices: the elders who remember similar situations, or the perceptible preferences of the Hollow itself. “The water never lingers here,” an elder says, touching a stone. “We have tried to persuade it before. Let the moss have its way; find another path.”

Clumsiness and carelessness are among their few real sins. To drop a vessel, to break a beam by failing to inspect it—these are failures not because of the lost object, but because of the wasted pattern: the sound of the shatter, the unexpected repair such a mistake demands from a system calibrated to slow change. A child who throws something in anger will find themself gently but firmly isolated for a time in a dim, echo-rich cul-de-sac, listening to their own breaths and muffled sobs. When they emerge, someone will be waiting with a small, tedious task—re-plaiting a frayed rope, mending a torn basket—reminding them by example that mending is always slower than breaking.

They have almost no concept of spectacle. Celebration, when it happens, is distributed.

There is a planting-peak time, when the light and moisture together are at a delicate balance. During those days, certain foods that are otherwise rationed appear quietly on many platforms: a tart berry mash, a rare sweet made from long-kept honey that surface bees once produced. There is a coming-of-age night where the youths of a certain age are allowed to walk unaccompanied through the Hollow in total darkness, no torch, no polished stone tilted to help them, trusting touch, breath, and the faintest grays of ambient light to guide them. At the end of this walk, they are given not a knife or a tool, but a small, unmarked piece of stone: their first invitation to carve a new path for water.

Visitors from outside, when they appear—and they do, occasionally—find the Attendants almost disconcertingly unhurried.

An outsider might stumble in through a shaft after a forest misstep, breath heaving in the cold, eyes adjusting to the diffuse glow. Someone from Within will appear, not with alarm but with measured curiosity, offering a woven wrap, a drink of near-icy water. Questions will be asked, but in a sequence that prioritizes the Hollow’s boundaries: “Were others with you?” “Did you mark your way?” “Did the fall break anything in the roots above?” Only later: “Where do you come from? What is your need?”

To the Attendants, all strangers are momentary disturbances in a much longer equilibrium. They are compassionate but not dazzled. A merchant bearing glinting metal will find their goods examined for texture, not shine. “This will ring when struck,” an Attendant might say, frowning slightly. “We use little that calls attention to itself in that way.” They might accept a metal pot if it can be muffled in moss, if its usefulness outweighs its sharp sound. They might offer in trade not gold or gems, but a carefully cultivated fungus that draws moisture in a precise pattern, or a woven cloth that insulates better than anything the trader has seen.

The weird regularity of water here—the way it obeys carved glyphs—is something outsiders sometimes call magic. The Attendants tilt their heads at that, as if “magic” was too hurried a word.

For them, the regularity is the whole point. Why would you trust a world where patterns didn’t hold? The few times they glimpse true anomaly—condensation suddenly thickening on a bare stone, or a beam rot appearing faster than it should—they respond not with ritual only, but with diagnosis. Has something in the airflow above changed? Has some creature gnawed a hidden root, altering the structure? If they cannot find a mundane cause, they fold the event into their understanding of the Hollow, a new behavior in their companion creature’s long life.

There may be, at the very center of their culture, a single story of rupture—a time when the balance failed, when a flood or drought or disease nearly unmade everything. They refer to it obliquely, as “the Year of Harsh Echoes” or “the Time of Two Voices.” Few details remain in common speech; perhaps more are preserved in low recitations shared only at certain ages. What remains evident is the lesson they took from it: that any attempt to dominate the Hollow—to push its fluids and lights entirely to their will—invites backlash. Thus their rules: don’t block condensation paths; don’t feed fire with wild zeal; don’t dam sound in a way that makes it build up and crash.

So, who lives here?

A people who wake each day inside a negotiated truce with stone and root and water. A people whose wealth is measured in how many things can keep doing what they were doing yesterday. A people who have turned slowness into skill, subtlety into architecture, and attention into belief.

They live as if the whole place were one immense, slow-breathing animal and they the cells lining its lungs: exchanging, filtering, keeping the passageways open. They do not think of themselves as its masters. They think of themselves, quite literally, as its residents—those who sit down within it, who attend its long inhalations and exhalations, who expect to be there still when this stone has rounded itself a little further, when a new child presses a first shallow glyph and waits, very quietly, to see if the water will answer.
