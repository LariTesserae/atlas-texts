They are not a people in the way you’d expect. They are a consequence.

What lives here is whatever has learned to treat pressure and flow as other creatures treat gravity and air. Nothing that endures in this drowned machine can afford to be indifferent to hydraulics. Everything with agency is, first of all, an instrument for feeling the water.

### The smallest listeners

The base inhabitants are barely separate from the surfaces they coat.

On the warmest metal skins and mineral bulges live thin consortia of films: layered mats of chemoautotrophs and their collaborators, feeding on the dissolved metals, trace gases, and redox imbalances that the engines continually produce.

They have no single species; they are shifting treaties between chemistry and time. In one region, the dominant layer might be iron-oxidizers that lace themselves with rust, turning their own excretions into sheltering crust. Elsewhere, sulfide-eaters build pallid, rubbery veneers that flex under shifting currents.

They do not think, but they remember. Each mat’s thickness is a stratigraphy of operating regimes: high-flow years recorded as compact, dense bands where only tenacious forms survived; quiet centuries as looser, delicate layers, full of branching filaments and slow-growing tubes. Where a panel has stood for long enough, its biofilm is a library of forgotten pump schedules, floodgate failures, temperature cycles — encoded not in words, but in which metabolisms took root, flourished, and were buried.

Some line interior cracks of the machinery itself. They ride the most intimate currents, tasting the very first hints of chemical change when a distant valve shifts. In them, “awareness” is nothing more than a cascade of molecular switches opening or closing, but those cascades ripple across hectares of surface. A flow-change born in a single pipe may, via shifting redox balances, trigger a slow, wave-like reconfiguration of films throughout this node. If there is anything like a shared body here, it’s this continual rearrangement of wet chemistry across stone and steel.

### The clingers

Where surfaces protrude into the main currents, you find the limpet-things: disk-creatures that look, in stillness, like part of the rivetwork.

Each is a flattened dome of flesh heavily mineralized on its outer face, blending seamlessly into its chosen anchor point. Around the edge runs a fringe of thousands of fine cilia hidden in a narrow slit.

They live by tasting.

Most of the time they are sealed, presenting only an unremarkable hump to the roaring flow. When the deep bass pattern of the pumps enters a “safe” configuration they’ve learned, they crack their rims open by the width of a grain of sand. Water knifes through the slit; the cilia flare and beat, billowing a microscopic plume of mucus and trap-fibers. Dissolved metals, drifting organics, bacterial cells, and mineral colloids all catch and are drawn inward.

They have no centralized brain, only a ring of ganglia arranged just inside the rim. These nerve-knots integrate flow information from the cilia: direction, speed, density, microvortex signatures. Over many cycles, each limpet develops a personal repertoire of “good” and “bad” hydrodynamic chords. In one interval, they might all open in unison, confident. In another, scattered individuals will clamp shut early, having learned different local dangers from past scourings.

When the flow shears too violently, they tighten down so hard they leave a pale, gasket-like scar on the metal beneath, sealing themselves into a moving world that may, for long minutes, include only the faint booming of distant structures and their own internal heartbeat of slow chemical exchange.

They reproduce by shedding tiny shelled stages that drift until they encounter the right combination of vibration and chemistry, then settle and cement themselves. A ghostly constellation of them may stud a favored beam, gradated in size from pinhead to coin, each a separate decision machine anchored in the roar.

### The strip-jellies

The delicate, translucent slivers near the warm jets are the system’s first true “swimmers.”

They are long, ribbonlike bodies, as if a comb jelly had been cut into sheets. Along their edges run paired bands of contractile cells and refractive cilia. Their tissues are thin, almost two-dimensional; seen edge-on they become nearly invisible, just a shimmer in the water.

Inside their membranes, faint tracks of denser granules cycle in loops, driven not by muscles but by traveling waves of contraction. These loops are more than circulation; they are timing devices. The rate at which granules complete a circuit depends on ambient temperature, local shear, and vibration frequency. When those parameters drift beyond certain bounds, the loops desynchronize; that desynchronization propagates along the body and triggers a change in behavior.

Thus each strip-jelly is a mobile calculus of its surroundings, integrating many subtle cues into a binary choice: remain in this shear layer, or fold and flee.

A few have specialized fringes along their length, bristling with chemoreceptive hairs. Those live nearer to tiny leaks of exotic compounds: minute vents where lubricants, coolants, or reaction intermediates seep into the water. Around these vents the jellies’ tissues are faintly iridescent, pigmented by trapped metal complexes, tuned to feed on specific dissolved energies.

They are prey of a limited sort, nibbled from the edges by larger grazers, but they are also seed-bearers. Periodically they shiver, releasing microscopic vesicles of tissue. These vesicles do not grow into new jellies directly; instead they attach to biofilms, rewriting them slightly, grafting the jellies’ sensory loops into surface consortia. Through them, some aspects of free-swimmer experience are archived into the larger chemical memory of the node.

In that way, motion and stillness cross-feed: the wanderers contribute their path-history to the walls; the walls, in turn, shape the conditions that give rise to future wanderers.

### The armored segments

The long, jointed grazer that moves between pipes is an apex presence here, though it is not a hunter of flesh.

Imagine an elongated isopod distilled into essentials, then rebuilt on principles of hydraulics. Its body is divided into broad, overlapping plates, each cased in mineral-composite armor that perfectly mimics corroded steel: mottled rust reds and pallid zinc hues. The mimicry began as camouflage; over generations, the minerals themselves have become structural, integrated into exoskeleton rather than mere encrustation.

Beneath each plate, rows of flexible limbs end not in claws but in suctioned paddles. They do not push water for swimming; instead, they grip surfaces and pull the body forward in slow, deliberate movements, always maintaining three or four anchor points. Their default state is clinging. Free-swimming is reserved for brief, desperate corrections when currents wrench them from their path.

At the anterior, instead of eyes, sits a crown of whisker-like antennae, each a bundle of pressure-sensitive hairs and microcanals filled with slightly different saline mixtures. When a distant valve opens, when a pump’s tone shifts by a fraction, the pressure harmonics at their perch change. The antennae translate these harmonics into activity patterns along the body. The grazer “feels” the infrastructure like a blind climber feeling the mood of a mountain through the tension in its rock.

It feeds with comb-like mouthparts that scrape and plane the mineral sculptures, lifting biofilms, soft accretions, and newly formed crust. Its teeth are laminated with exotic scales stolen from the vents themselves; some layers are sacrificial, dissolved as they dig into aggressively corrosive surfaces, then regrown from dietary minerals in quieter times.

Because it continuously abrades the hardened growths, it is a shaper of the environment. Narrow throats in the mineral wax-antlers become widened by its passing; new grooves and channels appear where it has foraged deeply. Those new shapes alter microflows, which in turn change the pattern in which bacteria and minerals deposit. Over decades, one such grazer can turn a jagged vent into a smooth nozzle, a random outcropping into a tuned baffle.

They are solitary more by hydraulics than temperament. Two bodies of their mass moving through the same confined turbulence would dangerously perturb each other’s readings. Yet they are dimly aware of conspecifics via distant, low-frequency pulses: special plates along their flanks can vibrate in response to body contractions, sending out subsonic thumps that travel through metal into water. Sometimes, rarely, a reply comes back.

Their reproduction, when it occurs, is tied to rare operating modes. When the node’s flows shift into an unusual pattern — a coincidence of pump activations that only recurs perhaps once in a century — a mature grazer may follow pressure gradients to a sheltered recess where turbulence cancels. There, two individuals rub flanks, exchanging packets of germinal cells via small temporary pores in their armor. Fertilized eggs are not free-floating; they are glued to underside crevices of favored structures, disguised under intentional mineral overgrowth that only their offspring will know how to read.

To the rest of the ecosystem, they are moving disturbances more than charismatic bodies: when one threads a passage, pressure maps change, vortices spin off its flanks, limpets slam shut in its wake, strip-jellies fold away from the altered shear. Its life is written in momentary silences and retuned roars.

### The greater shadow

Deeper in the system lives something larger, more distributed. You never see it whole because it is not, strictly, one animal. It is a coalition of anatomy and architecture.

Think of a colonial organism whose polyps are rooms.

In certain remote galleries, the mineral accretions have organized themselves into repeating motifs: long folds of layered deposit, cross-braced by stalagmitic columns, riddled with pores. Through these pores move thick cords of living tissue, rope-gray and flexibly jointed, threaded through the building like nerves through bone.

The cords themselves are extensions of a central mass hidden far in some inaccessible sump, where heat and exotic fluids vent continuously. That mass is less an organ and more a braided tangle of many such cords, knotted together into a responsive matrix. Over time, as flows and temperatures changed, it grew outward along the easy paths—existing ducts, maintenance conduits, cable trays—and reinforced itself by precipitating minerals onto exuded scaffolds. In places, its exoskeleton and the city’s skeleton are indistinguishable.

Each projecting cord is studded with sensory tufts that protrude into the water through old bolt holes, cracked welds, seam-gaps. Collectively, these tufts monitor a huge swath of the node’s hydrodynamics and chemistry. When the silhouette of “something large” dims a passage, it may simply be one of these cords resettling along its bony groove; when a long wall of shadow passes, the organism is contracting a portion of itself, closing one porous avenue and opening another elsewhere.

It has no brain localized in one place, but its braided center functions as an integrator: waves of ionic activity sweep along the cords, summing up thousands of tiny shifts into a small repertoire of global responses. Sometimes that response is to expand into a new cavity, secreting mineral to armor its advance. Sometimes it is to withdraw from a chamber where flows have become violently unstable, leaving behind only abandoned sheaths of dead tissue, soon colonized by simple films.

It feeds not just on chemical gradients but on the engines themselves. Where an old bearing still moves, however sluggishly, cords infiltrate the housing, exuding lubricating exopolymers laced with scavenging enzymes. Microscopic flakes of metal are caught and slowly digested for trace elements. In return, friction drops fractionally. A feedback loop emerges: the beast-circuit that infiltrated the bearing keeps it just functional enough to maintain the microenvironment the beast favors.

In numerous such small bargains, it has insinuated itself into the decaying machinery: clearing out blockages that would starve its preferred warm vents, dampening vibrations that could crack its structural supports. When a distant relief hatch slams, some cords tense and others slacken, subtly adjusting torque paths and stress distributions. The larger infrastructure no longer runs as its designers intended; it runs as this distributed inhabitant has, over centuries, gently pruned it to run.

From the point of view of the other creatures, this vast tenant manifests as shifting backgrounds: walls that very slowly flex, apertures that unpredictably dilate or close, currents that become slightly more or less laminar in ways that follow no apparent schedule. For those that can feel subsonics, there is also a faint, almost constant murmur, like the slow rumble of blood through veins. In that sense, this organism is the node’s internal “weather-controller,” though not by plan—just by being the single largest sustained draw on its flows and stresses.

### The articulated crawlers

On the rung-ladders and structural lattices near the main upward shaft live the smallest obvious animals with what might be called behavior.

They are many-legged, semi-transparent creatures no longer than a human finger, with bodies like flattened centipedes. Each segment houses a circular flow-channel, a loop of internal fluid studded with dense flecks that orbit endlessly when conditions are stable.

The flecks are not just cargo; they are part of a mechano-chemical timing system. In different segments, the loops have slightly different wall compositions, so the flecks respond to pressure pulses at different resonant frequencies. When the node’s main pumps thud, a wave runs along the animal’s body as certain segments’ loops accelerate and others slow. The pattern of these accelerations encodes information about pulse strength, phase, and interval.

The crawler’s nervous tissue is minimal but cunning: a set of segmental ganglia that watch the loops and adjust posture accordingly. If the pulses imply increasing upward pressure and safe turbulence, the legs extend, hooks dig deeper into the metal; the animal hangs on, harvesting food from the enriched water streaming past. If the pattern hints at an imminent regime change—say, the opening of a relief gate that will reverse local flow—it crouches, tightens every limb, and presents its minimal cross-section to the stream.

For food, it scrapes films and sifts particulate matter from eddies around its perch. Mouthparts comb the metal, then rinse in the passing flow; tiny sensory papillae decide which grains are edible and which are harmful shards of pure oxidized metal to be spat out. They are also opportunistic symbionts: some carry on their ventral surfaces small patches of specialized bacteria that convert otherwise toxic coolants into usable nutrients. In return, the crawler wanders just enough for the bacterial patch to colonize fresh ground.

Some rare individuals bear on their backs minute vesicles—bubbles of stabilized gas held in flexible sacs. Within those sacs, entirely different microbial communities live in reduced pressure. When the crawlers relocate, they bring these little ecosystems with them, introducing gas-phase niches to new cracks and cavities.

They are the most likely to move between microclimates, bridging the side-chambers of stillness with the main arteries of roar. On their barbed feet, chemical memories and spores hitch rides. Through them, isolated pockets of biofilm, otherwise trapped behind silt and geometry, remain connected, however thinly, to the greater node.

### The barely-living packets

When rare operating configurations align—those transient, strange “moods” of the machinery—many of the inhabitants take the opportunity to gamble.

Limpets discharge packets of spore-like cells; strip-jellies release swarms of vesicles; the great colonial cords slough off tiny rings of undifferentiated tissue. These packets are not full organisms; they are fragments of metabolisms with just enough structure to begin interacting with the environment.

Most die. Turbulence tears them apart or flings them into regions where there is nothing to exploit. But a few lodge in crannies where the flows are gentle and persistent, where trace chemicals swirl in the right ratios. There, they organize into new films or buds, sampling combinations of metabolisms that did not previously coexist.

In these pockets, “new species” is too coarse a term. What appears instead are new recipes for being in this place: different ways of coupling energy gradients, of using minerals as scaffold, of responding to particular vibratory chords of the pumps. Some of these experiments fail quickly. Others become permanent residents, altering the local balance: making surfaces more or less attractive to grazers, slightly changing how quickly metal pits or scale grows.

Over aeons, this process has elaborated the community from simple metal-eaters into a thick web of interdependent processes. Most do not look like discrete creatures. They are tangles of habit at the boundary between rock, rust, and water.

### The absent makers, and their ghosts

No humans remain, but their patterns do.

Control rooms overlooking the main channels have long been dead as workspaces, but they host a peculiar second wave of inhabitants. Inside these bubbles—the few that were once dry—water seeped slowly, then stood nearly still. Dust that never fell in the air accreted here instead as fine particulate rain through water, settling on panels and consoles.

On these surfaces grew thin, glassy films of corrosion products. Over time, microorganisms colonized them, ones that could feed on minute energy differences between layers of oxidized metal. Light leaking through broken gauge glass or cracked viewports provided gentle, intermittent warmth.

In these near-still pools, evolution took a different direction. Instead of adapting to scream and surge, these films adapted to whispers: very small, slow changes in ambient temperature, light intensity, and chemical composition. Their “world” is the passing shadow of a large swimmer across a distant shaft, registered as an almost imperceptible change in the flicker pattern of stray photons.

They form branching labyrinths of discoloration and texture on control panels, like frost-patterns that persist. When a rare combination of light and vibration occurs—a signature of some remote operational reconfiguration—parts of these labyrinths reorganize, dissolving here, thickening there, curling tendrils along engraved labels and meter rims.

In such rearrangements, the system writes a memory into its own skin. Not readable to anything but itself, not purposeful by human standards, but still a record. Over centuries, these panels become palimpsests of events: not what happened “out there” in the larger world, but what the node has been through.

You might say these are the only “archives” left of the builders’ intent: their machines’ own corroded faces, subtly altered by life that learned to see them.

### What it adds up to

Taken together, the inhabitants of this place are not a tidy food web but a braided set of negotiations between matter and motion.

The films and mats make the raw chemistry habitable. The limpets and crawlers, the strip-jellies and grazers, harvest that chemistry and rearrange the physical structure: smoothing some edges, keeping others sharp, opening new grooves, sealing old leaks. The vast colonial cords insinuate themselves into the very logic of the pumps and valves, affecting which flows persist and which fall silent.

No one here cares about light enough to chase it. No one swims for pleasure. Mobility, when it exists, is for holding position in changing flow, for reaching the next favorable shear, for bridging one stable niche to another.

To “live” here is to interpret pressure, to decode vibration, to bet one’s fragile structure against changes in a machine that no longer serves any outside master. Every creature, from a molecular biofilm to the armored grazer, is in some sense an organ of the node itself—each a local attempt to use the leftover energy of industry to persist one more operating cycle.

If you insist on an overarching resident, then the answer is not the great colonial beast, nor the largest grazer, nor the aggregate of limpets. The true inhabitant is the pattern that emerges when rust, water, and metabolism, driven by blind pumps, settle into this humming, roaring near-equilibrium.

What lives here is the drowned machinery’s long, slow attempt to become an environment—and, in doing so, to become something like a body.
