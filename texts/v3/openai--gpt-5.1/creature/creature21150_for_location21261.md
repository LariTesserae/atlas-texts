They are a people of edges, of margins, of held breath.

They live where no one sensibly would, unless “sensible” has been revised by a few hundred generations of never quite having enough. The cold has pressed them into the shape that fits this place, the way wind shapes snow—rounding nothing, but making every angle intentional.

Physically, they look smaller than they are. Not stunted, exactly; more compressed. Bodies are compact, dense with muscle and fat where it matters. Tallness is a liability in air this thin and wind this insistent; stature tends toward the median, with long torsos and short limbs. Their chests are wide, ribs sprung outward, lungs working a slow, deep rhythm even at rest. Faces are broad and flat, cheekbones pronounced, nostrils narrower and often slightly downturned, as if the flesh itself had learned to shelter the in-breath from the knife of the air.

Skin tones trend toward muted earth: stone-cream, dust-brown, the pale tan of old bone, the dull gray of rock soaked and then dried. Whatever their distant ancestry, the plateau has washed bright color out of them; here, pigments belong on walls and cloth, not on the body. Their hair runs dark more often than not, but with a wide variation in texture—tight curls that trap warmth; heavy, straight ropes bound close to the skull; wiry waves. It is worn simply and functionally: braided tight along the scalp, coiled at the nape, or shaved in strips where straps and hoods must sit.

Eyes are often narrow not from genetics alone but from constant squinting against foam-white snowfields and low, diffused glare. Lids thicken; lashes grow dense. Crow’s-feet come early, as does a kind of patient stillness in the gaze: people learn to rest their faces, to keep expression economical, as if even that could conserve heat.

They are perpetually layered. Nakedness, here, is not shocking, only nonsensical, like walking barefoot on blades. Clothing is a kind of architecture one wears. Innermost is soft: finely scraped and worked hides, woven underthings from rare fibers coaxed out of plateau plants, traded wool from lower elevations. Over this comes thicker hide and felted cloth, snug at wrists and ankles, loose around joints, always strappable tighter. Outer layers are shell-like—tightly woven, waxed, patched again and again. Nothing is allowed to flap; loose fabric becomes a sail, and a sail in a wind like this is a thief of balance and heat.

They dress in zones rather than in outfits. The city is laced with temperature gradients as much as with paths: sunlit terraces, wind-cut corridors, shadowed, still interiors. They add and remove layers fluidly as they move through it, hoods up in one lane, sleeves pushed to elbows in the lee of a warm wall; by instinct and experience they know the microclimate of every turn and stair.

Color is reserved for meaning. Most cloth is undyed, bearing the palest of greys and browns, sometimes the faint greenish hue of a plant-fiber too stubborn to yield its tint entirely. Over this, bands and knots of pigment appear: a strip of ochre at one sleeve to mark a work-clan, a set of soot-black chevrons along a hood denoting a particular household or basin, a faint green dot near the hem that signals a person has water-sharing rights in a given cistern. Children’s garments are marked more boldly than adults’; they are still learning the map of obligation and belonging their bodies must carry.

Their hands are the most expressive part of them. The cold forces them into mitts and wraps whenever possible, but within those wraps fingers are callused with the very specific textures of this life: the fine, dry scrape of stone dust; the softened roughness of rope used so often it has almost gone velvety; the faint gloss left by animal fat and tallow. Nails are kept short, sometimes bluntly squared by work alone. Many knuckles bear faint white scars from frostnip or a moment’s careless contact with metal in deep chill.

They walk with the wariness of people who understand that the ground is not a guarantee. Feet are set deliberately. Even on familiar paths, weight goes down like a question, feeling for the answer in the flex of gravel and the give of thawing ice beneath. Their gait on the flat is peculiar: a slightly forward lean, as if into a constant invisible push; steps are short and balanced, capable of halting or pivoting around a single grounded foot. On slope, they move with inbuilt rhythm: three strides, pause to breathe; five careful steps, a small rest at a boulder; every pattern tuned to lungs and heart and gradient.

In temperament, they are quiet without being timid. Voice carried too far is wasted heat, wasted breath, wasted attention. They speak close, mouths near ears, body angled to shield a conversation from wind and from unnecessary ears. Even arguments are often conducted at arm’s length, words low but edged. Sound bursts exist—laughter that collapses in on itself quickly, a shout when a stone goes loose, a child’s squeal at first snowfall—but they are sparks on a banked fire, not the blaze itself.

Humor is dry and often situational: an appreciation of narrowly averted mishaps, of the absurdity in the day’s small negotiations with physics and climate. They are not dour; they simply hoard overt exuberance the way they hoard fuel. Jokes are told with straight faces, eyes glinting; a good retort is one that lands with minimal waste of word.

They think long. Not in the abstract, but in the direct line from act to consequence to future condition of stone and cistern and herd. A person who has watched a miscut gutter slowly erode a wall over twenty years, or whose grandparent’s overgrazing left a hollow barren in their own childhood, occupies time differently. There is a cultural distaste for things that cannot be seen playing out at least two or three seasons ahead; impulsiveness is not romanticized. Stories praise the one who waited three extra days for the snow to move, who built a second brace under a ledge “because the grandchildren will need that rock.”

Their society is braided as tightly as their rope.

Households are large, extended beyond blood in ways outsiders might struggle to map. Kinship here is not just descent but co-labor. Those who carve the same wall, who share the upkeep of a particular melt-channel, who herd their animals to the same patchwork of basins, accrue obligations to one another that over time become indistinguishable from family. The word they use for “we” has gradations: “we in this room,” “we of this wall,” “we of this basin,” “we of this plateau.” The boundaries are not rigid, but each “we” carries different rules.

Leadership is as distributed as the city itself. There are no grand halls for a single ruler; space is too costly for symbolic emptiness. Instead, authority nests where the problems are. Each cistern has its watchers and keepers, elders who have memorized years of snowpack, who can read in the quality of a drip and the feel of the stone how much water lies behind. Each terrace, each rope-bridge, each migration path has named stewards. They make decisions first, from immediate knowledge. Above them is a looser council of those who have proven, over time, to think beyond their own catchment: negotiators, long-path planners, people who can trade a minor shortfall this season for a stronger position three winters from now.

Disputes rarely escalate to this topmost layer. Most arguments are over use—of water, of animals, of rock surface—and they are mediated locally. There is a strong ethic of “you do not get to break what others must rely on.” Fines are rarely in coin or abstract value; they are in work: so many days diverted from one’s own wall to another’s, so many hours under someone else’s lead in repairing what one’s error damaged.

Property is layered. Personal items—clothing, tools small enough to be carried, a sleeping niche, a carved token—are keenly owned and respected. House-spaces are collectively held by extended households: no one displaces another from their alcove without cause, but rearrangements do happen as families grow or contract, negotiated and recorded in low-key rituals. The city’s true wealth—water, grazing rights, structural stone, critical timber—is held in forms of shared stewardship that would seem labyrinthine to those used to private land. Rights are divisible in time: a household might have first access to grazing in a particular hollow for three weeks in early thaw, and last access to a cistern in late autumn. These rights are tracked in memory, in speech, and discreetly on walls.

The walls themselves speak more than the people do. On the sheltered sides of courtyards and along interior corridors, there are carved bands that look at first like errant decoration: repeating motifs of snow-feather, horn-spiral, layered stone. In fact, they are records and guides. One vertical sequence along a cistern wall might mark, in stylized form, the seven worst low-snow years; another band near a gateway might encode the sequence of dried-plant markers to be placed along a path when a particular shortcut is unsafe. Children learn to read these reliefs as early as they learn spoken counting.

Their relationship to water is not reverent in the sense of worship; it is reverent in the sense of obsessive engagement. Where other peoples might have saints or patrons, they have names for the different kinds of melt and condensation: the sharp-drip of new thaw, the slow-seep from firn, the sudden beading of “standing clouds” that coalesce into frost. These are not spirits, but they are treated as actors with consistent habits—things to be studied, anticipated, coaxed.

There are rituals around first water of the day. In many households, the first dipper from a cistern is split: half to the mouths of those present, half smeared with the palm along the lip of the storage opening, a gesture both of sealing and of acknowledgement. When a new gutter is cut or an old one re-routed, there is a small, unadorned ceremony: a few words spoken, not to ask permission, but to fix accountability. The cutter places their hand against the wet stone, says their name and the names of those who taught them, and promises to watch the new flow through at least one full freeze-thaw cycle.

Fuel—what little there is—is a minor god of its own. Dung gathered from herd animals and from the small burrowers near the city margins, dry lichens peeled from high stones, the rare deadwood hauled from lower slopes: all are sorted, dried, graded. Fires are small and contained, primarily for work that demands them: metalwork, bone and stone treatment, certain kinds of cooking. Heat for comfort comes more from shared bodies, from buried rooms that borrow the day’s warmth, from clever layering. Children are taught from earliest years the hierarchy of flame: what may be burned without guilt, what only when the choice is between that and serious harm, what should never be burned because its other uses are too valuable.

They keep animals in ways that blur the line between wild and domestic. The big herd-creatures that graze the thin pastures are not fully theirs; they are in a relationship of patterned interference. Calving grounds are protected from predators not by fences but by watch and deterrent. Bulls too aggressive are quietly steered toward the paths of known cliff-lions and slope-wolves. Salt licks are placed to persuade the herds to favor certain routes that help maintain soil where it’s most needed. Marking is subtle: woven bands braided into the thick hair at the base of a horn, a particular notch pattern in the hoof-guards placed on young animals to protect their feet over rock.

Closer to the city, in caverns and sheltered ledges, smaller animals are more thoroughly claimed. Fur-beasts, somewhere between marmot and rabbit, are reared for pelts and meat. They are not caged so much as contained by mutual habit: they are fed, sheltered from predators, and in return they stay within the known warrens, breeding in numbers calculated to match the plateau’s slow productivity. Children tend them, learning early how the carrying capacity of a single rock-cluttered hollow determines how many lives can reasonably share it.

Birds are both neighbors and tools. Large cliff-riders are left mostly alone; they serve as scavengers and as warning—how they circle, how they lift their wings against a certain sky tells an experienced watcher about air currents and coming shifts in pressure. Smaller, clever birds are encouraged to nest near the city, their presence lured by scraps and by carefully placed ledges. Feathers are valuable insulation; eggs, when taken, are taken with a ratio in mind, an ingrained arithmetic of “this many left for next season.”

Their tools and technology are not primitive so much as acutely fitted to constraint.

Stone is their primary medium because it is what they stand on. They are intimate with it in all its moods: fresh-broken and razor-keen, weathered and crumbly, veined with ice or metal. Their best stoneworkers are as honored as any warrior elsewhere. They know how to read a cliff face as if it were a slow-moving river: where it wants to crack, where weight already lies, how a single misjudged notch might, decades from now, cause a slice of wall to peel away like bark. Their chisels—metal, bone, or particularly hard stone—are sharpened to hair-shearing edges, but used with a patience that seems at odds with such dangerous fineness.

Rope is the other great technology. It is everywhere because it replaces what they lack: trees tall enough for long beams, metals abundant enough for spanning structures. They weave from everything: long hair cut from herd animals, inner bark from the toughest shrubs, fibrous mats from plateau plants, strips of tough rawhide. Ropes differ by task. Some are stiff and thick, meant for permanent supports; others are slightly elastic, for absorbing shock on bridges and belays. Color-coding appears here too: a red-dyed thread spun into a climbing rope means it has taken one bad fall and should no longer bear full weight; two such threads and it becomes a tie-down for static loads only.

In some basins, they have harnessed a shy form of water power. Where a seep or trickle is reliable, they construct tiny drop-pools and narrow chutes that, over short distances, gain enough fall to turn simple wooden or stone wheels. These power grindstones or simple bellows for forges, their slow turning a visible reminder that even here, where everything seems still, something is always moving. It is not grand industry, but it spares hands and lungs and, crucially, fuel.

They have mastered the art of doing much with almost none. Light, for instance, is eked out from slivers of tallow candles, from polished stone surfaces that bounce what little daylight exists deep into rooms, from tiny wall mirrors made not from glass but from meticulously smoothed metal shards, traded at absurd cost from lower lands. At night, household common rooms glow dimly; most work follows the sun. The darkness is not something to be feared, only another limit to be moved within.

Their knowledge system is layered like their city.

Oral memory is the primary archive. Elder storytellers hold not only fables and myths but detailed data: the sequence of seven snow-thin years and how they survived them; the last time a rope-bridge failed and the wind conditions on that day; the behavior of a particular cliff-face over three generations. Stories are told seasonally, keyed to the work at hand. When the first “walking clouds” of the wet months arrive, tales are told of past mistakes made in low visibility; when young people first accompany their elders on the long herd-circuits, they are sat down at night and given the lineage of each path: who cut it, who died there, who found water there in an unlikely year.

To fix what words alone might fray, they use marks: shallow carved lines on stone near critical points, color spots at rope junctions, pattern-knots in certain cords. There is no broad-written language in the sense of books and scrolls—it would be too cumbersome here—but there is an agreed symbology that maps onto shared oral knowledge. A newcomer raised here can walk through a basin-city and “read” its infrastructure: this notch means “ice under thin rock”; that spiral around a doorway means “this household tends the western cistern”; a series of three chevrons at chest height in a passage means “wind from the north will be strong here in the third month.”

They do send messages over distance, but carefully. Runners traverse the stone highways between basin-cities, hardened to the altitude, moving with rolled-up soles that can feel the ground through layered protection. They carry knotted cords—quipu-like records that tally contributions, deficits, arrivals. With them travel small carved chips of stone from one city’s central wall to another’s, tokens of presence that ensure that no settlement feels entirely isolated atop its own promontory.

Sometimes, when cloud and wind and season align, they send birds. Not full-fledged homing systems—the plateau is too treacherous for that—but certain birds, raised from fledglings near the hearths of messengers, can be persuaded to return to that scent. A hopeful scrap of encoded cloth on a leg, or a tiny pattern-cut shell, sometimes makes it across a chasm faster than any human can climb.

Their spirituality—if that is the right word—is not about gods that stand outside the system, but about pattern and reciprocity within it.

They do tell stories of beings larger than themselves: the First Ridge, who rose too fast and had its head broken off by the sky; the Deep Cistern, who hoards water in the stone and releases it only to those who know its cracks. But these are not petitionary tales. One does not ask the First Ridge for aid; one remembers its fate when tempted to overcut a ledge. The Deep Cistern is not prayed to; its myth is told when teaching children how slowly underground water moves, how long a damaged aquifer takes to heal.

Ritual focuses on alignment rather than intervention. Solstice gatherings, for example, are less about worship and more about synchronization: communities from multiple basins converging to recalibrate their seasonal counts, to compare snowfall, to redistribute surplus and shortage. There will be songs and some carefully rationed joy—dances that stamp warmth into cold feet, shared meals that for once allow a little extravagance of spice or fat—but the core act is communal accounting. In this, they find a sense of participation in something larger than any one city: the plateau itself as an organism of stone and life.

Death is present, but not melodramatically. In a place where a misstep can end a life, they have learned to soften it enough to live with it, without pretending it is anything but final. Bodies are not buried deeply—the stone resists—and open-air exposure would be a feast for predators best kept wary of human scent. So dead are placed in specific crevices and alcoves outside the main living zones, in layers. Wrapped tightly in cloth and hide, they are tucked into the stone like seeds into a crack. Over them are stacked small, fitted stones, not cairns to be seen from far away but interlocking shields against scavengers and erosion.

Names of the dead are not erased. They are incorporated into work. A particular gutter might be known as “Old Aset’s Cut,” long after Aset is gone; a dangerous turning on a traversing path is “Rin’s Step,” in grim memory of the child who misjudged it. In this way, the plateau itself becomes a memorial, and memory is woven into navigation.

Art exists, but it is lean, mostly folded into function.

Patterns on lintels that break wind eddies are also aesthetic, each household adding its own small variation in the curve of a line, the angle of a stylized feather. Nets hung as safety barriers at cliff edges are sometimes dyed in faint, shifting bands of color that, at dawn or dusk, look like captured sky. According to season, women and men alike stitch tiny beads of polished bone or smoothed, pale stone into their outer wraps—not enough to rattle or catch, just enough that, in the right light, a shoulder or hood glints for a moment as a person moves through shadow.

Music is mostly vocal and percussive. Stringed instruments are rare—the materials too costly—and what little wood there is, is kept for structures and tools. But a skin stretched over a carefully carved stone bowl can make a drum, and fingers on hollowed bone can coax thin, high notes that carry better in this air than deeper tones. Their songs are not loud, but they are complex, interweaving lines sung softly in overlapping rounds so that the whole becomes more than the sum of whispers. Lyrics remember routes, weather years, kinship obligations; a work crew cutting a new staircase may sing a pattern that encodes the sequence of chip and sweep and test.

When they celebrate fully, which is not often, it is usually for something irrevocable: the completion of a major new cistern, the successful rerouting of a meltstream, the birth of a child into a line that had been failing. Then light spills from every opening, small flames multiplied by mirrored stone. People climb up onto roofs and terraces, gathering at the city’s wind-still heart. Food that has been saved for months is eaten in a single evening. Children stamp their feet and shriek, just because they can. Elders watch, thin smiles at the edges of their mouths, pleased at the brief looseness.

At such times, if you stand a little apart on a higher ledge, the city looks like a second sky fallen into the basin: points of light held in a net of stone and rope, voiced by murmuring, laughing, softly singing figures. Above them, the true stars burn in the thin air; below, through gaps in cloud, distant valleys glimmer faintly blue-grey.

This is who lives here: a people who have learned to think like water in stone, to move like animals on shattered ground, to speak in a register matched to thin air and thick necessity. They are not ascetics—they love, quarrel, dream, take pride, tell jokes, are vain about their carvings or their ropework or their children’s first sure-footed climb. But they have pared away any habit that cannot justify the space and warmth it consumes.

Around them, under their feet and over their heads, all the other lives of the plateau thread their own patterns: the herd-creatures tracing ancestral lines along ridges; the small furred things chittering in the honeycomb below; the birds writing circles and sigils in the air; lichens brightening, almost imperceptibly, a once-bare rock. Together, over seasons and centuries, they and the stone and the cold and the water are making something that did not exist before: a high, thin world crowded not with emptiness, but with careful presences, each of which has, by persistence and attention, earned the right to cast a shadow.
