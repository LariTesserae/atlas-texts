What lives here is less a collection of species and more a single, continuous habit of matter: life turned into weather, into floor, into sound. But within that continuity there are bodies and roles, and they can be told apart if you linger long enough in one place and learn the texture of the noise.

---

### The basal fabric: the crust itself

The first inhabitants are the ones you never really stop touching. The living crust is not a surface coating on stone; it *is* the stone’s present tense.

Its smallest citizens are pore-things—organisms whose entire world is the interior of a single cavity in the mineral-cartilage substrate. They are soft, lamellar discs, stacked like wet coins, each layer able to slide past the others to compress or expand into any irregularity of their host pore. Their outer faces are all chemistry: slow pumps, exchange membranes, patches of stiffened protein crystal that harvest tiny voltage differences between damp and less-damp microzones.

They do not move in the usual sense; their lives are a continuous swelling and thinning, turning and folding. Some individuals never leave their original pore. Others send out threads—clear, tube-like pseudopods reinforced with microscopic mineral grains—that bridge from cavity to cavity. Where many such threads coincide, they fuse into translucent ganglia: nodules that serve as shared reservoirs of water and metabolite, and rudimentary decision-points. In these, traffic is chemical, but not random. When the air dampens, specific substances surge one way; when a predator presses overhead and blocks the flow of vibration, a different set of compounds backs up, redirecting growth and closure.

In places that seem bare, these pore-things are still present, only more deeply recessed, retracted from rougher treatment. In the darkest, quietest hollows, they crowd to the surface until they become visible as a skin: a flexible, almost featureless slick that beads condensation with greedy efficiency. This is the “unseen city” under everything else—primary harvesters of tiny gradients, the slow, patient underwriters of everyone above.

They are not alone in the crust. Interleaved with them live filament-predators: hair-thin, glassy threads that traverse pores like snares. They are mostly dormant, drawn taut across narrow passageways. When a pore-thing’s exploratory pseudopod brushes one, the filament stiffens, suddenly, like a tensed bowstring, puncturing and siphoning. The energy here is so meager that one such kill might feed a whole filament for days.

Over generations, the aggregate positioning of those filaments subtly redirects how pores are connected, what can pass and what cannot. The bedrock’s porosity pattern, once only a geologic fact, is now a slowly evolving anatomy shaped by who can afford to live where.

### The carpets and films: grazers that erase and rewrite

Above the pore-level lives a stratum of mat-builders and mat-eaters, pressed together so tightly that “above” means fractions of a millimeter.

Some are sheet animals: one-body films, spread thin over dozens of square centimeters. Their outer face is textured into countless minute hooks and folds; their inner face is riddled with contractile sacs. They grip the substrate not to move, but to hold their exact outline against the ceaseless tide of others. They farm. Their upper surface is cultivated with bacterial biofilms that can survive the near-dark, gleaning energy from trace chemical flows the sheet animal concentrates: ions pushed, diffused, coaxed through itself from pore to open air. Every so often, the entire sheet flexes and “eats its own skin,” rolling the colonized surface inward into digestive creases, then exposing a fresh area to be reseeded.

Other mat-beings are granular, beadlike colonies. Each bead is a soft, kernel-sized sphere with a thin crust of rigid spines pointing in all directions, like a sand grain that learned to breathe. Alone, a bead can only quiver and swell. Together, thousands mesh their spines into a felt, a low, prickly lawn that can creep—very slowly—by coordinated rolling and unrolling. They travel not to find food but to follow conditions: pursuing the boundary where condensation is just right for their symbiotic filaments of chemoautotrophic microbes. When air grows too still and saturated, they collectively contract, opening up microscopic chimneys between their bodies to vent moisture upwards, engineering a thin, self-made draft.

Grazing across these mats, wearing them down, are the raspers: flat, many-mouthed creatures that are less “animal” than mobile filing surfaces. Their backs are low, domed planes of dull armor; their ventral faces are carpeted with short, looping cilia tipped with hardened ridges. As they pass, they sand a micron-thin layer off everything—biofilm, mat, dead shell, even the softer substrate—passing the slurry inward to layered stomach corridors that sort, ferment, and share it with internal microbiomes. Raspers do not choose food; they choose *routes*. Their survival depends on finding paths that cost them less tissue in abrasion than they gain in nutrient.

Where many raspers follow the same preferential gradient—toward a seep, or along a migration humidity corridor—they engrave true roads into the world: depressions polished by immeasurable tongues. Some become so deep, relative to body thickness, that the raspers themselves can no longer climb out; they evolve into linear, canal-locked populations that forever pace their single furrow, scraping its walls. A road, here, is not an absence of life but a specialization of it.

### Mantles: the mobile plains

The most obvious bodies, when the light flares, are the mantles: large, table-sized ovals, low as mattresses, that move like thick spills poured in slow motion.

Seen from above, they are almost featureless: rounded plates with faint, concentric banding where growth has advanced outward over time. Their color is whatever color dominates the local crust; they incorporate it, overlaying their own pigmentation with a camouflage of copied stains. What gives them away is the rhythm of their motion: they advance by unsealing themselves, front edge first, then flowing forward as the rear clings, then re-anchoring in a new position—peel, slide, press, repeat.

The hidden side of a mantle is bewilderingly dense. Hundreds of thousands of tiny “feet” radiate from nested rings of muscular sheaths. Each foot is really a multi-purpose organ: sucker, chemosensor, and rasping tongue. As the mantle passes, these feet take the place of the basal grazers, stripping the crust down further in a tight, synchronized wave before releasing it to regrow.

Mantles are not loners. They stack. In the thickest parts of the basin, three or four mantles may overlap over the same patch of floor, their undersides never quite touching, separated by a living shingle of small, opportunistic creatures that specialize in the inter-mantle gap. These gap-fauna feed on the constant snow of scraped fragments and excreted gel drifting down from above, and on the concentrated humidity trapped between warm bodies.

Mantles communicate in a way that does not resemble intention but has the stability of a language. Embedded within their dorsal plates are bands of resonant shell—arcs and patches tuned to specific frequencies. When a mantle flexes certain muscle groups, the plate hums or clicks, sending a directed pulse of vibration through itself into the crust. Different tones mean specific bodily states: absolute fullness, dangerous dryness, damaging pressure overhead. Nearby mantles sense these vibrations through their own feet and adjust behaviors accordingly—slightly altering route, slowing their peel, changing how tightly they press down.

Through this, mantles maintain low-grade spacing in a world where empty ground is impossible. No thought is required; resonance habits propagate the way erosion habits do, carving routine out of chaos.

### The arch-bearers: infrastructure that creeps

In the wettest pockets live entities that have made a different bargain with space: they reach upward.

These are the scaffolders, the slow cathedrals. Their main bodies lie low and hidden, patchy cakes of tough, rubbery tissue adhered deep in crevices. From these bases rise thin arches and buttresses of calcified secretion, each arch emerging from a different historical episode of growth—a humidity cycle, a nutrient flush, a rare calm. Gossamer membranes stretch between these supports, surfaces so thin that the faintest trembling air condenses on them.

Each drop that forms is rapidly drawn along tiny surface channels etched into the membrane toward thickened rims, then into ductlike grooves that run down the arches to the core body. The scaffolders drink the sky, which here is only a few centimeters of wet air.

Because building up is costly in a universe of constant collision, scaffolders are heavily defended. Their arches are flexible rather than brittle; they can bow away from heavy passing bodies rather than snap. Their membranes taste bad, exuding repellant chemicals that irritate the raspers’ cilia. And when something persists in chewing at the framework, the scaffolder can rapidly demineralize a targeted span, causing a local collapse that sheds the attacked piece like a lizard losing its tail, sacrificing architecture to save the living root.

Their movement is astonishingly slow—a fraction of a body-length per day at best—achieved by dissolving substrate beneath them on one side and reprecipitating it on the other, like a tide eroding its own shore and building a new one. The trails they leave behind are startlingly clean in a place so overgrown: ribbons of dehydrated, mineral-rich crust, pale and crackling. To some followers these tracks are famine, stripped of easy grazing. To others, adapted to gnawing tough residues and exploiting hard porosity, they are corridors of relative safety through the writhing, wetter press beyond.

### Compression-hunters: weight that thinks by pressure

Predation here is mostly ambush, enacted by those that can become “ground” until they decide not to be.

Compression-hunters are among the most deceptive of these. At rest, they are indistinguishable from a slightly thicker, slightly softer patch of crust, patterned with faux pore-mouths and dummy nodules that vibrate convincingly to the footfalls above. Their edges feather seamlessly into the surrounding mat. Only deep inside do their true margins reside: rings of powerful contractile tissue encircling a cavity of folded, caustic gut.

They listen. Layers of pressure-sensitive organs line their upper surfaces, tuned not to generic movement but to patterns: the basin-splash of small grazers; the slow, heavy peel of a mantle; the gentle pour of microfaunal streams. When a threshold is crossed—a specific density, a tightness of clustering—they trigger. The whole patch arches upward in a tin-snap motion, then slams down again.

In the first fraction of a moment, this traps whatever happens to be passing over the hunter’s midsection beneath a sealed dome of flesh. In the second, the dome contracts and thickens, squeezing out water and air, collapsing the bodies within into a single, amorphous mash. In the minutes to hours that follow, the hunter’s interior unfolds: fan-like digestive surfaces press into contact with the slurry, absorbing what can be absorbed; indigestible fragments—chitin plates, spicules, mineral grains—are left embedded in the hunter’s lower body, slowly compressed into a denser layer that reinforces its disguise. Some individuals grow so armored with former prey that they resemble small, low mounds of fused shell-field, complete with superficial tunneling life.

Compression-hunters are not inviolable. The increase in local pressure and chemical leakage when one feeds is a strong cue underground. Drill-tube organisms—long, rootlike predators whose bodies occupy vertical burrows—spread their sensing rims through the pores. When they detect the heavy, sustained load and nutrient-rich seep of a feeding event above, they extend upward. Their tips are not hard but soaked with enzymes and abrasive particles. They do not pierce in one go; they *worry* through, grinding and dissolving, drawn toward the feeding core. If they reach it, they tap into the hunter’s pressurized gut and drink, parasitizing or even killing the predator. Every successful drill leaves a scar: a hardened, sealed plug that slightly alters how that hunter will feel the world above.

### Funnels and mouths of the basins

Where shallow basins collect their persistent sheen of water, life rearranges itself around the liquid skin.

In the sloping margins live tension-trappers: entities that co-opt surface tension itself as their hunting gear. At rest, they are simply “wet spots,” dull, darker rings in the thin film. In fact, these rings are continuous collars of muscular lip tissue, stretched flat and motionless. Their inner rims secrete a microscopically thin, highly hydrophilic compound that exaggerates cohesion in the immediate layer of water.

As small grazers move across the basin, their legs and cilia disturb the film. Ordinarily these disturbances propagate as harmless ripples. When a wave intersects a tension-trapper’s ring from outside, subtle sensors detect the sharp change in pull along the circumference. If enough disturbance peak points arrive in quick sequence—indicative of many bodies crossing—the collar instantaneously lifts and curls. The film breaks. Microdroplets form between the lip’s inner projections and the basin floor, each droplet holding one or more tiny animals pinned by the same forces that moments ago let them glide.

The lip then inverts, dragging its catch into its interior pockets, where hook-lined folds knead and sort by size. Nothing larger than a fingernail ever notices these events; the basin floor seems to stir, then still.

### Airborne drifters: things that never really land

The centimeter or two of “sky” above the crust is a hectic medium, packed with collision and constant, tiny gusts as bodies thrust and withdraw, as mantles peel, as compression-hunters slam. Into this the basin’s floaters interleave.

One common type is the vesicle-kite. Picture a flattened, asymmetrical sack, its upper shell stiffened into a gentle curve, its lower face trailing a tangle of fine, whip-like filaments. Along the inner surface of the shell, bacterial reactors ferment abundant local chemicals into a mix of gases marginally lighter than the ambient air. The result is buoyancy just strong enough to offset the weight of the body and its tendrils.

The kite does not fly; it drifts, its orientation and path dictated by the microcurrents generated by others. Its filaments are studded with chemoreceptors and microscopic grapples. When they brush a surface rich enough in certain metabolites—mucus secreted by mantles, exudates from scaffold membranes, the slow leak from an injured body—they momentarily snag, anchoring the kite just long enough for specialized lower-surface pads to descend and absorb.

Kites must avoid being wrapped and eaten by the sheet-predators of the air: the living wads. Wads are, at rest, thin disks—almost two-dimensional, suspended like slightly crumpled leaves in the humidity flows. Their margins are hyper-sensitive to touch. When two floaters meet, both feel the contact. The wad’s response, however, is explosive: its rim folds in dozens of tiny pleats, throwing its surface into a tight spiral around the intruder. Adhesive patches lock layers together; digestive pores between them go to work. The process is as silent as cloth being balled up.

Every time a wad feeds, it becomes slightly thicker, slightly less efficient at staying aloft. Old, overfed wads eventually drift lower and lower until they make contact with the ground. There, they invert their way of life: the adhesive meant to catch airborne prey now tethers them to the crust. They become static patches that trap passing microfauna instead of floaters, their former gas-reactors slowly retooling to exploit new gradients along the rock–air interface.

### Senses without eyes: the music of shell

Almost nothing here sees, but nearly everything *plays*.

Shell-panels, plates, and hardened knobs double as resonators. Each lineage has discovered, in its own way, that a tuned stiffness or a certain curvature can pick out specific bands of vibration from the omnipresent roar. Some mantles have crescents of shell along their flanks tuned to the long, slow flexing of nearby giants; others carry peppered arrays of smaller plates that buzz sympathetically with the rapid clatter of massed microfauna.

The act of sensing is inseparable from the act of altering the field. To feel the world more clearly, an organism must amplify particular frequencies, and that amplification in turn adds subtle harmonics back into the shared sound. Over time, co-adaptations have emerged: the feeding rasp of a common grazer sits in the “listening band” of its primary predator; a certain stridulation pattern acts as a deterrent not because anyone “understands” it, but because it temporarily saturates the narrow band a would-be attacker relies on, like glare.

In the more stable wet pockets, this has led to complicated standing sound-structures. Whole neighborhoods of shell-bearing beings settle into positions and postures such that their emitted and reflected vibrations create semi-persistent patterns—zones of constructive and destructive interference. Within a certain ridge, the roar may be oddly muted; in another curve, a narrow band of frequencies is magnified, like an acoustic lens. Creatures that need quiet—pore-things on the edge of desiccation, fragile scaffold seedlings—tend to accumulate in the lull zones. Those that exploit cacophony—ambush predators that confuse prey by overwhelming their senses—favor the noisy edges.

If there is any “mind” here in the sense of emergent cognition, it lives in these long-lived acoustic structures: the way movement habits, shell shapes, and positional feedback loops create enduring, basin-scale patterns of sound that persist beyond any individual life.

### Cycles and migrations that are not quite seasons

Because there is no sun to march overhead, the basin’s time is counted in humidity tides and sound-color events.

The slow shifting of warm, moisture-heavy air from one region to another demarcates territories more surely than any line on rock. Humid flows swell with metabolism, packed with carbon dioxide, volatile organics, and heat. As they move, they trail gradients that everything can feel. Raspers alter their roads to follow the richest, dampest trails. Mantles adjust their peel-rate: faster advancing into an oncoming wet front to get ahead of it, slower retreating from newly drying ground.

Certain organisms are almost purely migratory in relation to these tides. One is a carrier of its own weather: a thick, cylindrical beast whose interior is mostly water bladder. It moves with a slow peristaltic crawl, and as it goes it constantly leaks—a mist issuing from numerous dorsal pores, condensing into a localized halo of high humidity. On its flanks grow entire dependent communities of paste-animals and bacteria that cannot survive elsewhere. They are, in effect, epiphytes of a walking cloud.

Others are “dry opportunists”: low, shielded plates with deep internal reservoirs and highly impermeable outer cuticle. They expand into zones as they dry, exploiting the sudden availability of bleached, uncovered mineral for direct chemical harvesting. Their presence is signaled by a different sound: not the wet squelch and glug, but faint crackles and brittle snaps as their hard margins pry into tight crevices, wedging and fracturing to claim new pore-space.

When a bioluminescent cascade sweeps a region—as it does only occasionally, when thresholds of crowding and chemistry coincide—the effect is as much ecological as aesthetic. Many creatures are indifferent to light, but some are not entirely blind to it. Scaffolder larvae, for instance—tiny, floating before they settle—respond to these brief illuminations by synchronizing their drop, exploiting the momentary visual outline of existing architectures to choose attachment points. For a little while after a wave, successful collector-forms cluster along the same curves that the light revealed, strengthening emergent “lines” in the basin’s structure.

### No tools, but something like a memory

There are no dwellings, no arranged piles of shell that are not ultimately traceable to accident or blind habit. And yet the basin remembers itself.

Paths persist. A depression first eroded by a tendril of condensation becomes a rasper-road, then a canal of specialized grazers, then a fault-line along which scaffolder tracks preferentially creep because the dryness of their wake makes the sides less likely to crumble. Predators tune their trigger thresholds to the footfall patterns typical of those routes. Over thousands, millions of cycles of humidity tides, these drifts of behavior write semi-permanent circuits into the stone, as distinct from newly colonized surface as a scar is from fresh skin.

The patterns of predation themselves form loops: grazers that specialize in eating only the hardened remains within compression-hunters; drill-tubes that only activate under the peculiar squeeze-signature of those predators at full feed; filament-predators in the pore network that thrive on the waste streams from the drill-tubes’ digestion. Energy climbs and descends these loops, never resting long in any one body, but the loops endure.

If intelligence is, in part, the capacity to form and maintain complex, adaptive patterns of interaction over time, then something like it lives not in any single inhabitant but in the total choreography: in who grazes after whom, which roads stay open, which humidity corridors become self-reinforcing because too much life depends on them to let them close.

Nothing here plans. Yet everything here participates in behaviors that would take planning to design. The basin is thus not a society: it is an ongoing, densely inhabited argument between rock, water, and whatever has learned to be a thin, persistent layer between them.

---

Inhabitants here are not individuals in the sense of solitary, bounded lives wandering through empty space. They are protrusions of a collective metabolism, temporary arrangements of membrane and mineral that solve local problems—how to scrape, how to drink, how to push, how to endure pressure—before dissolving back into the general swarm.

To speak of “what lives here” is, finally, to speak of this: a world where life has given up on distance and sight and chosen, instead, to become density itself—sound that thickened, moisture that learned to cling, the floor that never quite settles into being merely stone.
