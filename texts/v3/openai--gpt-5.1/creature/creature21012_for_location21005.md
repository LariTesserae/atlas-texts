What lives here is everything that has already made its decision.

Nothing is in the middle of becoming. The life that persists on these mountains is what remains after a very long, unsentimental sorting. What is left is precise, pared down, and oddly self-assured.

### The big-bodied herds

The dominant animals are the mountain herds you hear long before you really see them.

They live on the slopes just below the highest ribs of rock, where there is enough scattered growth to eat and enough ledge and broken ground to keep them out of reach of most things that would like to kill them. Their bodies are built around two facts: thin air and steep stone.

They are massive in the chest, barrel-lunged, hearts overbuilt for pumping what little oxygen the air provides. Their legs seem comparatively fine until you watch them move; then you see how much joint and tendon has been invested. They do not bound like deer or spring like goats so much as unspool, each step a calculation of weight, angle, and friction. The hooves are not small points like those of lowland ungulates but broad, cloven cups with a rubbery, almost skin-like rim that can deform slightly around irregularities in the rock. On loose scree they spread, increasing contact. On polished ledges, the edges bite into microscopic roughness. The sound of them is constant: a mix of clatter, scuff, and the small sliding rattle of dislodged stones.

Their coats are dense, but not luxuriant. Insulation here is less about warmth—though the nights are cold—than about buffering the violent shifts of temperature and light. The outer hairs are stiff, slightly glossy, and colored in every shade of stone this range offers: faded basalt gray, dust-brown, a faint rusty undertone where iron stains the slopes. In the right light, they vanish. Only the movement of a flank as a muscle bunches, the turn of a horn, reveals them.

Those horns are not the extravagant spirals of rams, not trophies, but tools. They grow back and out, following the line of the skull, ridged along their length. The ridges tell age and status: each year adds a little more thickness, a new notch. In the rutting season, males will clash with them, but half-heartedly—short, bracing shoves more than full-speed rams. Here, a broken leg is a death sentence; they cannot afford wasted dramatics. More often, the horns are used as levers: scraping snow, prying up mats of dry vegetation, hooking under the lip of a rock to shift it and get at a trickle of moisture or a clutch of mineral-rich lichens.

It is their voices, though, that most define them. In this overabundant echo chamber, their calls have been stretched into complex, layered instruments.

Each animal has a vocal tract tuned to emphasize certain overtones. In close quarters, they murmur to one another in subsonic hums you feel in your sternum more than hear with your ears, vibrations that carry through the ground of a ledge from one body to the next. At distance, they inflate their chests and send long, almost wind-instrument tones out over the valleys—rising bugles that break at odd intervals, as if the sound is climbing a hidden staircase inside the air.

Those calls are not just dominance displays, though they serve that purpose. They are maps. Each note, each inflection, carries information about where the animal is standing: which ridge, which side of which gully, how high above the valley floor. Herds read this acoustic landscape as fluently as you might read a drawn one. Youngsters learn early to pick out the signature of their mother’s call from the crowded chorus, following it through echoes that last unnaturally long. Sound is not just communication here; it is an organ of spatial sense.

Their social structure is loose, strung across the mountains the way they string themselves across a slope: in beads rather than tight knots. Family groups form the basic unit—mothers, this season’s young, a few older offspring that haven’t yet peeled away. Males drift, sometimes alone, sometimes in small coalitions, braiding in and out of different groups according to a logic that seems opaque until you remember that those long calls are also constantly broadcasting fitness, health, and intention. Fights are rare; most contests are decided before the bodies ever meet, in the air between.

They have almost no constructed culture. The polished ledge-platforms they make by endless resting and shifting in the same spots are their only architecture, small edits to the stone’s surface. Their traditions are routes: the inherited memory of which traverses always hold, which snowfields conceal treacherous hollows, which gullies channel storms. Mothers do not teach their young “paths” in any formal way; they simply go, and the young follow. The mountain itself supplies the curriculum.

### The hunters of ledges

Predation here is about patience and timing, not sprint and ambush. The most visible—or perhaps least invisible—predator is the pale, sinuous hunter that moves along the cliffs like a loose rope of muscle.

Its body plan sits somewhere between cat and mustelid: long spine, low center of gravity, big forepaws with semi-retractable claws, hindquarters able to coil and release with whip-like force. The fur is short, dense, and almost the color of the sky filtered through stone: a washed-out, dusty cream with darker banding only on the tail, which is thick and heavy to act as both counterweight and rudder.

Up close, its head would strike you as disconcertingly expressionless. The eyes are large and pale, iris nearly the same color as the surrounding fur. The ears are small and rounded to reduce heat loss and snagging on rock, but they pivot with astonishing speed, triangulating every flicker of sound in the continuous roar. The whiskers are long and stiff, not just around the muzzle but sprouting from small pads along the cheeks and even forelegs, giving it a tactile sense of the minute air currents and the tiniest touches against stone.

It does not roar or yowl. Its vocalizations are whispers: hisses, small chirrups, a low, purling growl used only at very close range with kin or mates. To hunt in this world of endless resonance, you must be acoustically invisible. The pads of its feet are thick, slightly corrugated, leaving no scrape on rock. Even the hair between the toes is flattened and velvety, soaking up the little sounds of grit underfoot.

It takes the herd beasts when it can—young, injured, elderly—but much of its diet is made up of the smaller creatures that dot the slope: burrowers, ground-nesting birds, the occasional amphibious animal that strays too far from a tarn. It hunts by listening for anomalies in the stable noise. This is a learned art: each individual gradually grows an internal template of what “normal” sounds like at different times of day, in different winds. A burst of alarm calls or the sudden dropping-out of a certain background croak is like a flare going up.

They breed sparingly. A female will range alone over a large section of cliff and broken slope, scent-marking only in sheltered spots where the wind won’t strip her sign away immediately. When she comes into estrus, the calls of males—tiny against the herd’s thunder, but pitched at a frequency that travels farther in this particular mix of air and rock—begin to thread through the soundscape. They meet briefly, mate, separate. She will raise a small litter of blind, stone-colored kits in a crack, a pocket cave, or—rarely—an abandoned burrow she has enlarged.

The young climb before they walk well on the flat, taught by necessity. Those that misjudge a leap do not survive long enough to pass on that particular miscalculation. Over time, this has produced animals that, once mature, almost never fall.

They have no mythology, no inherited rituals beyond the silent edicts of terrain: avoid that pass when the clouds sit a certain way; rest only where the rock gives beneath your claws in a particular fashion; never chase panicked prey into a gully that “talks” with a specific, ominous note when wind enters it. Again, what looks like instinct from the outside is an accumulation of many, many deaths behind them.

### Burrowers, runners, and night feeders

Lower down, where the broken rock lies in thick mantles around the valley sides and floors, smaller mammals operate in a world of crevices and temporary covers.

There are pebble-burrowers: fist-sized, blunt-faced animals with dense, almost armor-like fur that pushes through scree as much as they dig. Their paws are short and powerful, claws broad rather than sharp, adapted to shoving rounded stones aside. They live in systems of shallow tunnels and hollowed-out chambers just below the surface layer, roofs supported by the innate stability of interlocking rocks rather than by soil. A warren is marked by a fan of slightly rearranged pebbles and the faint, ammonia-tanged scent of their waste.

They are mostly herbivorous, nibbling the tough bases of alpine grasses and the fleshy parts of cushion plants, but they are not picky; any insect, dropped seed, or scrap of carrion is welcome. They hoard in a minimal way: a few galleries lined with dried plant matter that serves both as bedding and food cache. In bad years, they cannibalize their own nests.

Their reproduction is opportunistic. After a wetter-than-usual spell, when flowers have flashed and gone and seeds are briefly abundant, they produce large litters, banking on numbers to push some offspring through the leaner cycles that follow. In drier stretches, they slow almost to a halt. In this, they mirror the water itself: quick, intense bursts of activity bracketed by long, cautious waits.

The slope-runners are different. These are longer-limbed, lighter-framed animals, perhaps somewhere between a hare and a small antelope in impression, though they are neither. Their hind feet splay wide when weight is put on them, distributing load across loose stones so they skate rather than sink. The forefeet are narrower, used more for steering than propulsion. At speed, they appear to skim the surface of scree, riding arcs of descent and sudden lateral shifts in a way that would tear the tendons of less specialized creatures.

They are mostly nocturnal, and their huge, dark eyes gather what little light the stars and thin moon provide over these high ridges. Their ears are large, but here that is as much about thermoregulation as hearing; the thin, well-vascularized skin dumps heat quickly after frantic flights. Their calls are brief, high whistles, shared only within small family groups. To avoid advertising their presence, they prefer to communicate with foot-drumming: rapid, faint pulses through the thin ground layer that carry a few meters and no further—just enough to say, “Here, stay close,” without telling any hunter that a nervous body is passing.

These mammals, and others like them, do not shape the stone, but they do shape where life collects on it. Their burrows trap windblown seeds and dust, creating micro-patches of richer substrate. The mouths of their tunnels are fringed with slightly taller grasses, slightly thicker moss. Predators learn to watch these spots; moths and beetles learn to lay eggs in them. Over generations, each warren becomes not a structure but a pattern of probabilities in the land: here, more likely food; there, more frequent death.

### The amphibious thresholds

The tarns, where water lingers, are small laboratories of persistence.

Here live the squat toads you spotted—their bodies low, wide, all knuckles and warts, colored like wet rock. Their skin is thick and only intermittently permeable; they spend much of their time half-buried at the margins, noses and eye-ridges just above the grit, exposing as little surface as possible to the desiccating air. To breed, they must gamble. When a pulse of rain swells the tarns and cools the surrounding stone, males emerge and stake out inch-wide territories, croaking in complex, arrhythmic patterns—not songs exactly, but codes, each rhythm claiming a particular angle of shoreline, a certain depth of water, a patch of submerged pebbles.

Females, listening from the safety of deeper water or shaded crevices, choose not just based on the vigor of the call but on its pattern, which reflects where the caller is and how well that microhabitat has served past broods. Generations of selection have packed meaning into these pulses. If the water holds long enough, gelatinous strings of eggs become tadpoles that are already part-lunged, accelerated in their timetable. They feed in furious, bright swarms on algae and whatever small organic drift they can find, racing the evaporation clock.

The eel-bodied amphibians are stranger still. They are long relative to their width, with reduced limbs—more fins than legs—and smooth hides that glisten even out of the water. They spend much of their lives in darkness under the cold stones of seep-lines, where film-thin trickles of water thread down through rock. There they rasp biofilms and silt for nutrition, powering slow, low-metabolism existences.

On certain days—triggered not by temperature alone, but by a specific combination of humidity, barometric pressure, and the spectral quality of the light—they emerge in small numbers from the scree at tarn edges, their bodies wet and improbably agile. They wriggle into deeper water, shed a translucent layer of outer skin that clouds the tarn momentarily, then perform brief, frantic circuits near the surface, releasing and fertilizing strings of eggs. Their calls during this are high, rapid peeps that, in aggregate, make the mechanical buzz you hear: a chorus of life compressing an entire reproductive season into hours.

Their larvae are ghostly: nearly transparent, with thin fronds for gills like pale algae. Many will be eaten; many more will simply fail to find a crevice with the right damp gradient when the tarn shrinks. Those that do survive will disappear into the rock itself, joining the slow, unseen majority.

### Birds: The owners of the open air

Above, where nothing holds but wind and intention, the birds have partitioned the sky.

The great gliders—call them mountain carrion-wings—are the patient apex of the air. Wingspans broader than you are tall, bodies surprisingly light, keel bones wide to anchor the enormous flight muscles that, paradoxically, fire only in rare, powerful bursts. They are mostly held aloft by geometry: the right curve of feather and bone inserted into the right moving mass of air.

Their underwings are white not by accident but by function. Against the hard light, they become flashes, signals to one another. At these heights, where scent disperses and is torn apart by turbulence, visual cues travel farther and more reliably. The slate-colored backs vanish when they tilt, giving them the option to be seen or not depending on how they roll.

They feed on what dies—herd beasts that mis-stepped, hunters that missed one leap too many, burrowers caught out, even the rare casualty of sudden weather. Their heads are bare or close to it, feathering giving way to tough, almost leathery skin around the beak and eyes, easier to keep clean. Their eyesight is extraordinarily acute, but more importantly, it is tuned to movement not just of bodies but of flocks of other scavengers. A small flurry of corvids at some distant ridge, an explosion of seed-eaters from a patch of shrubs—that is enough to send them angling that direction.

Their calls, when they bother, are violent rips of sound—harsh, carrion-metal cries that cut through everything else. These are not regular; they are used in disputes around a carcass, in close aerial displays between mates, and—very rarely—at the approach of something so novel or disruptive that even these jaded birds feel the need to name it.

The smaller raptors are more frenetic. They hunt the mid-sized birds, the runners, the young burrowers. Their calls are knives in the soundscape: thin, piercing peals that occupy a frequency lane few others use. In a place where echoes linger, brevity and narrow pitch help; a short, sharp scream is easier to locate in time and space than a long, booming one that fragments.

These birds are also sculptors of the thin line between cliff and air. Their nests are not elaborate—little more than bowls of coarse grasses and the odd stolen twig—but where they wedge them changes the way wind shivers along the rock, creating slight eddies that other birds learn either to exploit or avoid. Over centuries, whole vertical “neighborhoods” of ledges develop distinct sound characters depending on who nests there, which cries blend into which echoes, how many wings slice the air in front of a given face.

Corvids and their kin, meanwhile, are the gossipers and generalists. Glossy black or dull charcoal, occasionally with a sliver of white along the wing edge, they move in compact flocks that expand and contract like breathing. They eat almost anything: insects knocked loose by the hooves of herd beasts, the eyes of the freshly dead, seeds from the few plants that dare to commit to reproduction, even scraps of shed skin from amphibians and, more rarely, from the big hunters.

Their intelligence expresses itself less in object manipulation—there is little here to shape—and more in social and acoustic play. They elaborate on each other’s calls endlessly, layering mimicry of other birds, of herd bugles, even of wind-notes in particular gullies into strings of oddly musical phrases. A flock’s repertoire is a living record of its travels and interactions. Young birds learn not just what a certain alarm call “means,” but who originated it, on which ridge, under which weather. In this way, knowledge—of safe and unsafe places, of where food usually appears after certain weather patterns—accumulates culturally as well as genetically.

### The plants: decisions to stay small

Nothing here grows tall without paying dearly for it. The plants that persist have all chosen, in one way or another, to hug the ground.

The cushion plants are among the most remarkable. From above they are just hemispheres or oblong domes of tight, tiny foliage, each leaf no bigger than the head of a pin. Press them gently and they resist like memory foam: firm, springy, dry. Inside each cushion is a network of micro-stems, branching and re-branching in dense geometry that traps heat and any stray moisture. Dead leaves are not shed outward but retained inside, slowly decomposing into a fibrous, minimal soil that the living tissues continuously mine. A single cushion can be decades old and grow only a few centimeters across in all that time.

When they flower—if you happen to be standing there on the right day in the right year—they erupt in a low, even scattering of color: hundreds of minuscule blooms flush across the dome’s surface, offering small, nectar-sparing cups to the few insects hardy enough to fly at this altitude. Some are electric chartreuse-yellow, some a shy purple, some pale, almost white. The display is over quickly. Petals shrivel in hours, seeds form fast, then vanish into the interior web, to be carried out later as grit, as droppings from ill-advised grazers, as accidental freight in the fur of beasts scratching an itch.

The jointed, leafless shrubs are another strategy. Each segment is a slightly flattened cylinder, surface glaucous—covered with a waxy bloom that turns water into beads and reflects excess sunlight. Photosynthesis happens just under this skin, in thin layers of tissue arranged around a central pith. The joints allow the shrub to flex in strong winds without snapping, each segment like a knuckle. In the rare wet, these plants can swell perceptibly as they take up water, the joints loosening, then tightening again as they slowly leak it back into their own metabolism over many dry days.

Their thorns are few but well-placed: along the outer faces of segments where the mouths of herbivores might otherwise easily nip off whole lengths. The bites that do get taken leave scars that do not heal in the way you might expect; instead, the plant walls off the damaged area and allows it to erode. Many older shrubs are hollow in places, thin shells of living tissue forming odd, twisted lattices. Insects shelter in the cavities, birds wedge small bits of nest material into them, amphibians hide under their slight shade. The plant becomes, unintentionally, architecture.

The thin-stemmed grasses that grow sideways, parallel to the wind, minimize the profile they give the air. Each blade is rolled, not flat, presenting only a narrow, tough edge to the light. They grow in small clumps rather than fields, each tuft a little world. Roots penetrate into minute cracks, anchoring the plant and, incidentally, prying the rock a fraction more open as they thicken with age. Every winter and spring’s freeze-thaw cycle works on those roots’ expansions, turning living tissue into wedges. Over time, the grass doesn’t just cling to the rock; it helps unmake it, contributing to the slow creation of real soil. It’s a patient bargain: the plant is both tenant and quiet demolitions crew.

Where seep-lines darken the stone to black, mosses press themselves thin and low. They are as much a texture as they are plants, their colors dulled by a constant thin coat of mineral dust. They rely on capillary forces to gather what water there is, wicking it laterally along their bodies. When the seeps run more generously, they plump and green; when they dry, the mosses crisp, curl inward, and simply wait. There is no dramatic wilting, no rot. Life leaks out as slowly as it will flow back when the next trickle comes.

Lichens are the oldest citizens here, and the least in a hurry. They spread in faint halos and jagged lines, each color and texture representing a different partnership of fungus and alga (or cyanobacterium) crafted by centuries of selection. Some draw out iron, painting the rock rusty-orange; others leave pale gray ghost-circles. They do not “bloom” in any way that a passing observer could notice. Their reproductive events and slow expansions are geologic gossip, exchanged on timescales that make even the patient tarns’ amphibians seem flighty.

### The insects and invertebrates: the cryptic majority

You might not see them first, but there are many more of them than anything else.

Diurnal insects here are small, compact, often flight-limited. Many beetles have shortened wings or fused elytra, choosing certainty on the ground over the risk of being blown into nothing by the always-present wind. They tuck themselves into crevices, under pebbles, between the outer leaves of cushion plants. When the sun hits a certain angle and temperatures climb a few degrees, they emerge in quick scurries to feed on pollen, on lichens, on decaying organic fragments too small for your eyes to separate from dust.

There are also true high-fliers, but they are exquisite specialists. Certain flies and tiny wasp-like creatures time their flights to overlap with the brief flowering of cushion plants and jointed shrubs. Their wings are thin, highly veined, able to flex and twist under the strange aerodynamic loads that gusts impose. They fly low, hugging the boundary layer of air where friction against the ground calms the worst of the turbulence. Their bodies are dark to absorb heat quickly; many are covered in fine hairs that trap both warmth and pollen.

Spiders anchor webs in odd, sheltered corners: the lee sides of boulders, small overhangs, the hollows between roots of shrubs where dust has piled. Their webs are rarely the classic, open orbs of storybook gardens; more often they are chaotic tangles, three-dimensional snares designed to work amid buffeting gusts. These spiders are patient ambush artists, feeling for the particular vibrations a fly or beetle makes in the silk against a continuous background of trembling from wind.

Under the stones, in the damp lines and in the burrow margins, live a host of nematodes, mites, springtails, and other soil microfauna. They process everything: shed skin, dried droppings, the bodies of insects that didn’t make it, the fragments of leaves. Their waste, in turn, feeds the few bacteria and fungi that can operate in such thin, cold films of water. These communities are the quiet engines that let even a sparse ecology cycle its materials. Without them, what little fertility exists would be locked in place, useless.

### The invisible life: bacteria, algae, and the stone itself

Even the rock here hosts life directly.

Endolithic communities—bacteria and algae living just under the surface—pepper certain boulders and faces. To your eyes, they are only a slight color cast: a faint greenish blush in one direction of light, a barely-there pink in another. These organisms have tucked themselves between mineral grains, where radiation is softer and temperature swings gentler. They sip water from condensation and from the tiniest seeps, surviving on light that has already been scattered and filtered.

Cyanobacteria form crusts on some arid faces, especially where dust has settled. These biofilms stabilize fine particles, making it a little harder for the wind to pick them up again. Over time, these crusts create the illusion of a very thin “skin” on certain slopes. Disturb it, and the fine material beneath is newly free to move; leave it, and slowly, quietly, the beginnings of soil form.

In the tarns themselves, planktonic algae bloom briefly after pulses of nutrient input—say, after a particularly big die-off of amphibian larvae or when carrion falls into the water. They cloud the bowls in faint greens and browns, then are grazed back by microinvertebrates. None of these explosions last long enough or reach biomass enough to change the character of the water, but they matter to the tiny crustaceans and insect larvae that depend on them. Those, in turn, are food for young amphibians, for certain birds that have learned to dabble quickly, feet braced against the push of wind on the surface.

### The sound-users: life built around echo

More than any other sense, hearing shapes the behavior and perhaps even the minds of the animals here.

You noticed that calls are polarized: very low or very high, almost nothing in the crowded mid-range. This is not accident. Over generations, any call that was too easily lost in the persistent roar and resonance simply failed at its job. Sound that cut through—by sheer bass force that makes rock participate, or by the needle of high pitch that bounces cleanly without muddling—became the standard.

Among the herd beasts, individuals can distinguish not just the voice of another, but the “shadow” of that voice: how it changes when echoed off a particular ridge or funneled through a particular pass. They use this to infer not just direction but path. A mother can tell that her calf is on the far side of a specific gully because the calf’s answering bleat carries a thin, fluttery over-echo she remembers from that place. The calf, in turn, learns to adjust the pitch of its call as it moves, seeking the tone that returns with the clearest pattern of familiar reflections. Communication here is a duet between lungs and landscape.

Predators, especially the pale cliff-hunters, grow up bathed in these same patterns. Their brains become good at subtraction: taking the composite roar and stripping out what is expected, leaving behind only anomalies—panic shrieks, the stuttering of disturbed flocks, the sudden quiet when a group of burrowers vanishes underground. Hunting is as much about listening for absences as presences.

Even the night owls—the soft-winged raptors that emerge after sunset—use sound differently than their relatives in woods or lowlands might. Their hoots are not just territory markers but sonar probes. They send a low-frequency pulse that travels, smears around ridges, and comes back slightly altered. Experience has taught them how certain echo-profiles correlate with particular arrangements of slope, boulder, and shrub. In a way, they “see” beyond the next rib of rock using these calls, navigating in three-and-more dimensions that your eyes alone fail to resolve in the dark, thin air.

Insects, too, are shaped by this environment. Many have become effectively deaf, their ancestors’ ears abandoned because constant noise conveyed so little actionable information. Others have gone the opposite way: certain crickets and katydid analogues produce and hear ultrasounds, well above the mammalian and avian range, their quiet private music running parallel to the mountainous roar like a hidden script.

### What does not live here

There are no large, sprawling forests; trees cannot afford that gamble with moisture and exposure. There are no burrowing megafauna; the rock is too hard, the soil too scarce. There are no builders in the sense you might seek: no creatures that stack stone into walls, that dam water, that store tools and pass them down. Intelligence, where it arises, expresses itself in routes and calls, in timing and microhabitat choice, not in artifacts.

You also notice the absence of true migrants. No vast flocks appearing only for a season, no herds that come up from richer lowlands to graze and leave. The animals here are residents, year-round. The cost of travelling to and from more forgiving ecosystems, across lower country that might have its own predators and diseases, has been judged—by evolution—too high. Instead, these creatures have committed. Their bodies, habits, and even internal biochemistries are tuned to thin air, high light, erratic moisture. To leave would be to unmake themselves.

In a sense, this range is a closed world. Materials cycle within it: a carcass high on a slope may take years to feed the tarns and the seep-lines, but it will, bit by bit. Very little leaves except in the form of dust carried by wind far beyond these peaks, or in the bodies of rare birds whose territories overlap both this high country and some neighboring, different realm.

### The feel of their lives

If you could, briefly, share a day in the awareness of one of these beings, you would not find drama as you understand it—not often. You would find a continuous negotiation with a stable, demanding context.

A herd beast’s mind is a map of slopes and sounds: the constant low awareness of how your footing feels, of where your kin are by the texture of their calls in the air. There is hunger, of course, and fear, and the periodic sharp blaze of mating urgency, but mostly there is a wide, steady monitor of horizon, wind, and stone. Comfort is standing on a ledge that you and uncounted ancestors have stood on before, your hooves fitting the same polished dips, your breath syncing with a familiar echo of your own voice.

A burrower’s inner life is smaller in scale but no less tuned: the secure pressure of rock just above your back, the smell of your warren-mates, the precise coolness of the air at different depths, telling you whether outside is safe or searing. Danger is vibration and shadow. Reward is the feel of damp against your whiskers when you break through into a seep-line’s margin.

A gliding scavenger’s world is made of updrafts and angles: the weight of your own wingbones against tendons, the thin roar of air along your primaries, the flash of something not-rock below. Hunger is a hollow under the keel bone. Satisfaction is the peculiar shift in air when you wheel into lift without flapping once for many long seconds.

None of them know this is a “finished” range, that its glaciers are gone and its big drama over. What they inhabit is simply what is. And because what is has been so unchanging for so long, they are, in their way, complete. Not perfect, not static, but well-fitted enough that radical change has slowed to a crawl.

### In the end

What lives here is an ecology in equilibrium with its extremes:

- Herds of stone-colored, deep-chested grazers whose voices are as much architecture as their hooves’ polished ledges.
- Pale, long-bodied hunters that read absence in noise and trust the reliability of their own balance more than gravity’s pull.
- A suite of small mammals—burrowers and runners—that treat scree like water, diving into it for safety or skimming its surface for escape.
- Amphibians that compress their ambitions into moments when water and chance align, then retreat to cold rock to wait out the rest.
- Birds that own the vertical: huge carrion-gliders, knife-voiced raptors, chattering corvids, low-hooting owls, each using air and echo as structure.
- Plants that have chosen proximity over prominence—cushions, mats, jointed stems, sparse shrubs—that turn the barest trickles of moisture into enough life to keep everything else fed.
- Invertebrates, microbes, and endolithic communities that do the slow, invisible work of turning death back into the bare beginnings of soil and nutrient.
- And, everywhere, the listening itself: animals whose senses and behaviors are molded by a world in which sound lasts longer than it should and light falls harder than you expect.

Nothing here is halfway. Every lineage that remains has already passed through its crucible. They do not strive to become something else; they strive only to continue being exactly what they are, in a place that has stopped changing quickly enough to demand anything more.

In that sense, what truly “lives” here is the balance itself: a loud, bright, nearly empty abundance, composed of creatures that do not rush, on mountains that have finished their own becoming and now simply hold everything that belongs.
