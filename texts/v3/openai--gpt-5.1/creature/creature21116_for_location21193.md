They are older than what we would usually mean by “species.”

Not in the sense of a single unbroken lineage stretching from the time of the builders to now—that is gone, and gone again, many times—but in the sense that the *roles* here have calcified. Every inhabitant is a variation on a few stable themes, locked into a choreography that has become as structural as the stone.

They are, in a way, organs of the place.

---

### The substrate-keepers

The smallest things—too small for most eyes—are not individuals in any meaningful way. They are sheets of function.

The velvety coat on the stone is formed by a consortium, not a single organism: fungal threads, archaea, and bacteria whose ancestors once lived in soils and hot vents and inside the guts of larger animals. Here, they have condensed into a single, distributed body.

Each square centimeter is a community tuned like an instrument to a specific section of wall. They differ along gradients we would barely notice—one mix favors a tad more iron in the seep; another specializes in wringing a last trace of energy from a decaying silicate bond. They share genes horizontally, but so slowly that innovation creeps like lichen.

They do several things at once:

- They **eat the stone**, but with reverence. Their biochemistry etches minerals atom by atom, freeing trace elements and releasing exothermic whispers of heat.
- They **feed the water**, bleeding out faint plumes of organic molecules and oxidized compounds that higher life can use.
- They **bind the structure**, precipitating thin cements in cracks they have already hollowed, sealing leaks before pressure can become a weapon.

Their texture—soft, compressible, barely warm—is the feel of the hall very slowly digesting itself and then reassembling.

Interleaved with this velvet are the **fibrous plugs**: mats of fungal cords and filamentous microbes, wound into dense, knotted blocks. These arise wherever water wants to move faster than the chamber’s slow equilibrium allows. A gap becomes a draft; a draft becomes a current; the plugs grow in that current, drinking its kinetic energy and cutting it down until motion is again only a background murmur.

They are the hall’s clotting response.

Disturb them and they smoke spores: minute, hooked things that drift until they find another problematic opening to muzzle. Their life cycle is less about reproduction for its own sake, more about ensuring there is nowhere left for anything abrupt to happen.

On some faces, the living film takes on a **crystalline discipline**—repeated hexagonal tiles, faintly glossier than the stone. These are micro-colonies that phase their growth in lockstep, each cell dividing only when its neighbors do, guided by slow chemical oscillations. Over geological time, they have discovered that pattern is a survival strategy: a way to distribute stress evenly, to avoid fractures propagating along weaknesses. What looks like idle decoration is actually a stress map written in flesh.

The result: the chamber’s surfaces are not just colonized; they are *regulated* by living weathering. The builders’ work has become a reef of stone and microbiology, each depending on the other’s constancy.

### The wall-mosaics: isopods and disks

Against that living skin, the first truly animal presences are small and numerous.

The **plate-bodies**—those translucent, isopod-like beings that glow when they move—are not a single species but a guild. Their ancestors were probably scavengers in some shallower, more chaotic environment. Here, they have refined themselves into **tactile filters** and **signal-weavers**.

Their plates are not just armor. Each segment houses a thin layer of excitable cells, capable of emitting brief pulses of amber light when compressed or flexed. The glow we see is incidental; the real communication lies in frequency and pattern, mostly absorbed by neighboring plates and never traveling more than a few body-lengths.

They graze the velvet films with delicate, scraping mouthparts, but they never strip an area bare. Their gut flora depend on the very communities they feed from; in turn, they excrete concentrated pellets of minerals and organic debris that the films eagerly reabsorb. Where they cluster thickly, the biofilm is richer, more active, a well-managed garden.

In their slow movement—spreading and contracting across surfaces—they continuously map the hall’s contours, and each other. Over lifetimes that might stretch for human centuries, they come to *know* particular patches of stone completely: where the warmth is faintly stronger, where a hairline crack breathes different chemistry.

A few lineages have taken this partnership further. They **embed** themselves.

At certain seams, colonies of plate-bodies settle into place almost permanently, overlapping in intricate, interlocked patterns. Over time their lower plates fuse faintly to the stone. They become *organs of compression*, soft but firm bridges across joints that would otherwise shift. When that deep tremor comes, the flexion of their bodies absorbs a portion of the strain. Their bioluminescent ripples shiver through the colony, a slow wave of response to an almost-forgotten violence.

Threading among and under them are the **disk-keepers**: flat, fingernail-sized organisms that skate on invisible cilia. These act like living capillary tubes. Their acid exudates don’t dissolve the structure; they tune it.

By etching microscopic channels through biofilms and mineral precipitates, they maintain extremely slow seepage paths between adjacent chambers and through thick walls. This leakage is not a flaw but a nervous system: chemical gradients diffusing through these veins carry information about temperature, composition, even the rare passage of larger creatures brushing a distant surface.

The disks are exquisitely sensitive to these gradients. They prefer to ring seams where change is most legible. Each individual is simple; as a ring, they behave like a perimeter sensor, a living oscilloscope tracing the subtle pulses that keep the mid-water animals attuned to the hall’s state.

They clear space wherever they settle. Their necessity to keep those tiny channels open becomes, over generations, an instinctive duty: no biofilm, no fungus, nothing that clogs is allowed to remain. In that unthinking maintenance, a ghost of the builders’ desire for controlled flow persists.

---

### The slow swimmers and entwined knots

In the volume of the water itself, movement is sparse and intentional.

The **thread-swimmers**—those twig-thin, segmented bodies with paddling bands and sensing fans—specialize in reading the three-dimensional structure of the hall’s invisible currents. Their constricting segments do not just move fluid along their bodies; they pump water through internal galleries lined with chemosensitive tissue.

Each swimmer is a mobile tongue, tasting:

- trace exhalations from microbial films,
- faint exudates from larger animals,
- the ghostly flavor of old stone under strain.

Their forward fans are tactile-feather organs, sampling turbulence as carefully as others might sample light. They rarely hurry because nothing here rewards haste. They graze microscopic particulate, exhalations from sievers, and spores. Their primary ecological role is **mixing**: in their endless, leisurely patrols, they stir gradients just enough to prevent lethal stagnation, but not enough to erase the differences on which everything else depends.

Then there are the **living knots**.

These tangled thread-creatures are a higher elaboration on the same theme but have turned their own bodies into both shelter and trap. Each strand of the knot can act independently, sensing, flexing, or sipping dilute nutrient. Bound together, they generate a private micro-environment: the interstices between loops are calmer, slightly warmer, and laced with their own metabolic byproducts.

As free-drifting tangles, they are **collectors**. Tiny particles, spores, and larvae that wander into their folds adhere lightly. Some are eaten slowly. Others simply live out their own small cycles inside the knot’s protected maze, shedding waste that the host consumes.

When multiple knots coil into a stable bundle and glue themselves into a crack, they become quasi-sessile:

- Their outer loops coarsen and darken, mimicking the fungal plugs until they are visually indistinguishable.
- Their inner loops refine, becoming more richly supplied with capillary vessels and sensory hairs.

They are not merely hiding; they are **watching**.

Unlike the true plugs, which only answer the pull of flow, the knot-plugs react to the hall’s fauna. A passing large cylinder, a bell-creature pulsing with different chemistry, a bloom of spores—they record these events as changes in their internal state. Over very long spans, these experiences feed into slow genetic drift. A knot-line that effectively positions itself near reliable sources of micronutrients or subtle warmth wins the evolutionary race by inches.

In this way, they are the hall’s archivists, encoding in their tangled genealogies a long, diffused memory of what passes.

---

### The bell-sievers, hanging in place of chandeliers

Suspended from the vaulted ceilings, the **bell-creatures** are among the most obviously architectural animals. Their domes mirror the arches they cling to: strong, wide, load-distributing.

Their tissue is neither cartilage nor pure muscle, but a composite of flexible protein fibers interleaved with mineralized struts. The faint blue-green pulses that pass through their bells are metachronal waves of bioelectric activity, coordinating millions of filter-cilia inside the tendrils.

Those tendrils are elaborately zoned:

- The outer sections, armored in bony rings, function as **valves** and **anchors**, gripping stone ribs and adjusting tension.
- The inner cores contain feathery sieving arrays, coated in dense biofilms of symbiotic microbes that oxidize dissolved compounds and convert them into more accessible forms.

Water drawn through a bell’s interior is effectively processed twice: once chemically, by the symbionts, and once physically, by sticky ciliary meshes that trap particulate.

They do not feed like whales or clams. Their intake is so slow that each liter of water might take many minutes to pass. Over a lifetime, they will have filtered an immense volume, but the moment-to-moment exchange is barely perceptible. They are less harvesters and more **refiners**, turning an already-thin energy haze into something storable.

Their saved energy goes into maintaining structure:

- reinforcing tendrils against creep and gravity,
- repairing small cracks in their domes,
- and, most critically, sustaining their own limited light.

The glow serves two purposes. First, it allows them to sense the immediate architecture via back-scatter; their photoreceptors are tuned to their own emission. Second, it advertises presence to other large animals: an anti-collision system in a place where sound is muted and everything moves by touch and taste.

On timescales that matter to them, the hall is not static. Columns slowly erode, ribs slump. The bells adjust. They redistribute themselves, abandoning deteriorating anchor points and settling on those whose microfilms and mineral taste suggest longer-term stability. Over many generations, their positions trace out the chamber’s structural health: dense constellations where stone is sound, sparse where age has eaten too deep.

Some lines have taken their symbioses further inward. Inside their bells grow **larval crèches**: gelatinous lattices where minute versions of all the hall’s microbes, and even some animal larvae, coexist in a curated stew. The young bells filter not just for themselves but for their entire start-up ecology. When, decades later, a mature bell detaches its crèche as a drifting, self-seeding mass, it is launching a ready-made microcosm that can colonize vacant ribs elsewhere.

In that sense, the bells are not just sieve-organisms; they are *founders* of new sub-habitats.

### The stone-shelled grazers

Fixed to ledges and arch undersides are the **glass-limpets**, those shell-animals armored with “frozen smoke” borrowed from the structure itself.

Their bodies are muscular spirals, coiled beneath conical plates grown through a slow alchemy:

- They dissolve infinitesimal layers from the surrounding glassy stone.
- They reprecipitate it as foam-like silica within their mantle.
- Over time, this consolidates into translucent armor, threaded with organic fibers.

The etched hollows they occupy are as much their doing as the builders’. Some ledges are scalloped all along their length, each sinus home to one individual, like a row of empty sockets now filled by these mineral-teeth.

Inside their shells, their tongues are radular ribbons studded with siliceous teeth. They sweep continuously across the crystalline biofilms and the uppermost grains of sediment, grinding, ingesting, and passing the slurry through digestive loops rich in symbiotic bacteria.

Their waste—those tiny hard pellets drifting down—is more significant than it appears. Each pellet is a **capsule of processed stone**, its surface etched with microbial cues that make it ideal seed material for new films on the floor. Over epochs, the pavements below limpets become not just dust catchers but fertile plains of biogeochemical exchange.

The limpets are the primary **vertical conveyors**: turning vertical surfaces into floor resources. Without them, the hall’s energy and nutrient cycles would be even more impoverished.

Their shells are also history-books. Layer by layer, incremental bands record subtle fluctuations in chamber chemistry: the occasional arrival of fresh material from a crack above, a century of slightly higher warmth, the aftermath of an unusual tremor. If you could read those layers, you would see a bar-coded chronology of events in a world that otherwise refuses to mark time.

### The great, gentle harvesters

Gliding in infrequent procession, the **cylindrical grazers**—those six-to-ten-meter-long bodies banded with stubby fins—occupy the highest rung that still exists in this food web.

Their skins are thick and rubbery, armored internally with flexible rods of mineralized protein. Externally, they are mottled with patches of dull biofilm that matches the hall’s own, masking their presence from anything that can see at all.

Their broad disc-heads are less mouths than sensor arrays. The ring of pits on their surface is filled with gel rich in receptors:

- chemoreceptors attuned to the metabolic exhalations of wall-colonies,
- pressure sensors that can feel the echo of a tremor transmitted via stone,
- magneto- or electro-receptors that trace the faint fields produced by the bell-creatures’ pulses.

They have no eyes; their world is a topological map of scents, flows, and slow electrical flickers.

Feeding is meticulous:

- They drift to a wall-section dense with plate-bodies or microbial film.
- They open the ventral slit and deploy the **feeding curtain**: a fan of mucous nets, each thread laced with cilia that push captured food inward.
- They press this curtain against life, hold just long enough for unlucky individuals and loose particles to adhere, then withdraw.

They do not rake or gouge. Their neurology has been selected for an almost compulsive restraint. Those lineages that once overfed and stripped their grazing grounds left behind sterile stone and starved. The survivors have an instinctive avoidance of doing irreversible damage; their satiety circuits ignite long before overharvesting is possible.

Their role is to **prune, not to clear**. By preferentially taking the weakest, loosest, or most exposed individuals from colonies, they promote local diversity and prevent any single strategy from dominating too completely. They are the hall’s mild predators and its gardeners.

Reproduction, for them, is an event on the scale of centuries. They rarely meet, and when they do, the encounter is protracted:

- For days, they circle one another, aligning their chemosensory maps, synchronizing their internal cycles via hormone-like exchanges through the water.
- Only when their bodies’ slow clocks agree do they briefly intertwine, exchanging packets of gametes within buffering mucus sheaths.

Development of their young is extremely slow. Embryos may drift as neutrally buoyant, gelatinous threads for decades, feeding by osmosis from the chamber’s thin soup, before growing enough musculature to paddle deliberately. Mortality is low not because they are safe, but because they invest so heavily in each offspring that only a few can ever exist at once.

In their longevity and careful habits, they are the visible expression of the hall’s governing rule: *do nothing fast that must last long*.

### The niche-purists

In shadowed corridors branching from the main hall, the rows of **clean recesses** host perhaps the strangest inhabitants: the **scrapers**.

They are seldom seen because they do not glow. Their bodies are low, flattened ovals, each about the size and thickness of a human tongue. Their upper surfaces are armored with tough, keratin-like scales; their undersides are patches of muscular suction and extremely fine, replaceable teeth.

Their imperative is simple and absolute: keep certain spaces empty.

No one remembers why those particular recesses matter. The builders once stored something there, or worshipped there, or simply preferred the aesthetic of uncolonized niches. The scrapers’ distant ancestors might have been opportunists exploiting the fact that those cleaned spaces received periodic disturbances which dislodged biofilm, making feeding easy.

Over time, a feedback loop formed:

- The more faithfully a lineage cleared a recess, the more predictable its food supply became.
- The more predictable the food, the more its behavior ossified into obligation.

Now, generations removed from any origin, the scrapers operate like immune cells. They patrol the corridors in slow, patient sweeps. When they find colonization in a “forbidden” niche—any biofilm thicker than a faint haze, any settling plate-creature, any knot—they rasp it away. If needed, they rest there, sealing the space with their own bodies until the urge to move on returns.

They have no concept of purpose, but in them the architecture has successfully outsourced a fragment of itself: they are **stone’s will to remain as it was** made.

Interestingly, nothing in the hall contests them. The limpets, the plates, the films do not attempt to defend those niches. On evolutionary timescales, the energy-cost of fighting over them was not worth the return. Eventually, everything else ceded that tiny fraction of available surface to the scrapers’ compulsion.

Architecture thus retains a few clean sentences amidst an otherwise palimpsest text.

### The chamber itself, as inhabitant

Most of what “lives” here isn’t discrete at all.

The column of flooded halls behaves like a single, enormous organism whose tissues are:

- stone blocks and glassy ribs,
- microbial and fungal matrices,
- films, shells, knots, bells, grazers.

Information moves slowly but steadily through it:

- Chemical changes in one region diffuse along seepage tracks maintained by disk-keepers.
- Structural strain in a column is buffered by fused plate-bodies and velvet films, then subtly transmitted as pattern shifts in bell-positions.
- Population changes in wall-colonies alter the diet composition for limpets and thread-swimmers, which in turn affects pellet chemistry and floor-film composition.

There is no nervous system in the strict sense, yet responses are coherent.

A small collapse in a lower chamber might:

1. Release a pulse of mineral-rich dust and a spike in pressure.
2. Fungal plugs in connected cracks swell, limiting abrupt flow; discs etch new channels to distribute the pressure gently upward.
3. Bells anchored near the strained regions detect increased microvibrations; over years, they detach and resettle farther from danger.
4. Plate-body colonies migrate slightly, following the faintly warmer stone exposed by new fractures.

Nothing coordinates this from above. But from a removed perspective, the **hall survives by behaving like an extremely slow, distributed intelligence**—a mind whose thoughts are measured in centuries, whose reflexes take generations to complete.

Within that mind, each inhabitant is both itself and an organ:

- The velvet films are skin.
- The plugs are scar tissue.
- The plate-mosaics are tactile sensors and braces.
- The disks are peripheral nerves for fluid information.
- The bells are kidneys and lungs, refining the medium.
- The limpets are teeth and digestive ceca for the structure.
- The grazers are pruning hands.
- The scrapers are memory of an original form.

### Origins and endings

What came before these forms is lost, but their shapes hint at a history.

Some likely descended from shallow-water or even terrestrial life that retreated into the builders’ drowned infrastructure as planetary conditions worsened. Others may have originated in deep subsurface aquifers, migrating upward after collapse connected voids. Symbioses that were once incidental—bacteria in guts, algae on shells—became the core around which entire bodies reorganized.

In the absence of plants and sunlight, everything converged on a small menu of energies:

- geological warmth,
- slow oxidation of reduced minerals,
- the chemical ghosts of long-dead organic matter baked into the stone and ceramics,
- the occasional fresh influx from an unseen, deeper engine.

Because energy is scarce and intermittent, selection has all but extinguished lineages that gamble on growth or speed. What survives is what can **pause almost forever**, resuming function at the gentlest nudge. Even “death” is ambiguous. Many organisms in the films and knots can dry into a mineral-impregnated torpor and wake centuries later when moisture and gradient return. Some bells, when mortally damaged, precipitate their internal organs into porous stone-like masses that become new anchoring sites for films and disks, while a few surviving cells seed the next generation.

In such a system, extinction is less a sharp cut than a *fading of roles*. A function may dwindle if a new structural configuration makes it less necessary; its specialist species thin out, merge with related lines, or become vestigial. Conversely, when the hall’s geometry shifts—say, when a column finally gives way and creates a new cavity—niches bloom slowly into being, filled not by explosive radiation but by **gradual re-sculpting of existing bodies**.

### How they experience their world

To us, this place would feel suffocatingly quiet. To its inhabitants, it is rich with continuous, low-density information.

- The isopods feel life as pressure gradients across their plates, faint changes in warmth, minute chemical flavors seeping from stone.
- The disks live in the tug-of-war between acidity and neutrality at seams.
- The thread-swimmers navigate by the filament-fine eddies of water they themselves create, like worms of awareness tunneling through a nearly solid darkness.
- The bells sense time as the slow accumulation of filtered metabolites in their symbionts and the sagging of their anchors.
- The grazers perceive their environment almost entirely as a layered, shifting “taste-map” of wall and water.

None of them knows the hall as a whole, just as you will never feel every cell in your body. Yet their combined activities sustain a coherent environment with recognizable patterns, predictable enough that lineages can specialize down to the micrometer.

Even their reactions to “disturbances” are tuned to the chamber’s scale. A tremor that would terrify us is to them an unusual but not incomprehensible storm:

- Plate-bodies clamp down and dim.
- Films stiffen, contracting slightly.
- Plugs expand to counteract new flows.
- Bells flash in synchronized ripples, recognizing that the stone’s song has shifted.
- Grazers drift toward more stable central regions.

Minutes or hours later in our time, days or years in theirs, equilibrium reasserts itself. Dust has settled, cracks have found new balances, niches have bent around the new geometry.

---

### What it means to live here

To live in this chamber is to inhabit a world that has relinquished drama.

Birth, feeding, movement, even death happen, but they are woven so finely through time that they hardly disturb the water. Every inhabitant is constrained by, and complicit in, the same long peace:

- Energies are low, so no one can afford to be wasteful or violent.
- Volume is finite, so no one can flee far from consequences.
- Architecture is fixed, so adaptation must curve around it instead of remaking it.

The result is not stagnation, but an **ultra-slow metabolism of change**. Over millions of years, the balance will still shift: bell-creatures may dwindle if heat flux lessens; scrapers might relax their vigilance if recesses lose some subtle property they once cued on; a new line of knot-creatures might learn to tap into a slightly deeper chemical layer of the stone.

From inside a single lifespan, though, everything appears finished. The hall’s inhabitants are born into a script already written, act out their lines with great fidelity, and fade without perceiving the revisions that accumulate from age to age.

Who lives here, then?

- Not just worms and plates and bells and grazers,
- but **a place that has turned its last technologies—the craft of stone and the patience of life—into each other**.

The residents are the hall’s second architecture: a living veneer that has settled into its final design, no longer striving for more, only ensuring that what is already shaped does not collapse too soon.

They live as if eternity were real, and as if their task were simple: to keep breathing, quietly, in the dark, so long as the warmth in the stone persists.
