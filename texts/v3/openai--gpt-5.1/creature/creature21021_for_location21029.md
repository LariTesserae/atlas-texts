What lives here is not one thing, but a braided multitude, layered like the growth rings of this world. Yet you can begin with the largest presence, because it is everywhere.

---

### The One That Is Place

First, there is the body.

Not the bodies that leap and crawl and glide, but the single, continent-sized life that underlies everything: the plant-crust itself.

It is not a tree as you know trees. Its growth is not centralized into a trunk, nor does it answer to a single, monarchic brain. Instead, it is a continent of connected tissues, a membrane of plant and plant-analogue wrapped around something deeper and more hostile: vacuum, or rock, or a thin, killing gas. Its “crown” is inward, toward the spaces you walk in. Its “roots” reach away, into the darkness outside this shell, drinking energies and particles you would call cosmic, geothermal, or something stranger.

It lives in slow, distributed decisions.

Along each pillar and membrane, every sheet of tissue runs its own localized negotiations: how much sap to hold, when to flex, when to thicken or thin. These nodal choices are informed by chemical traces and electrical whispers running through vascular bundles the size of rivers. When a column bears too much load, distant segments feel the strain as a faint variation in ion gradient. When some outer surface is stripped by creatures, the absence of feedback travels inward, a tiny silence in the great choral circuit.

There is no single center that knows all this. Yet, in aggregate, the body acts as if it remembers and anticipates. Regions that have collapsed before tend to be reinforced in future growth. Membranes that once split catastrophically regrow with layered, rip-stop veins and sacrificial panels meant to fail in order, not all at once. Scar-tissues—vast, thickened belts of toughened fibers—spiral around the world, records of earlier injuries that are now structural strengths.

Within that memory, disturbances made by smaller lives are barely scratches. But they are scratched repeatedly, across generations, into the same places. And anything a world-sized plant repeats, it folds eventually into itself.

So the great body has grown thinner here, thicker there, bridged that gulf and left this gap unspanned—partly because of its own internal logics of tension and nutrition, and partly because small, persistent hands kept wounding and feeding in certain lines until growth agreed. The result is not a creature that cares for its tenants in any intentional sense, but a creature whose current shape cannot be cleanly separated from their presence.

The world-plant is the first inhabitant. The rest live in its lungs.

### The Sound-Drinkers

In this abode where light is weak and intermittent, sound is reliable. Some lives drink it, parse it, move by it.

You hear them before you see them: a scatter of percussive ticks and slurred whistles, gliding through the vast roar like birds through surf. They are the air-swimmers that ripple between the pillars, the ones whose wings are fronds rather than feathers.

Call them frondwings.

They are not true fliers in the sense of beating against gravity; instead they are surfers of gradients. Their bodies are elongated, flexible, their skeletons a lattice of cartilage and soft bone, very light, embedded in a sheath of fibrous muscle. No clear front or back at a glance—just a streaming spindle with multiple “fans” along its length, each fan a spray of ribbon-like lamellae. When spread, these lamellae tremble in the thin air, catching any differential: heat updrafts from sap pulses, microcurrents slithering along membranes, eddies where columns divert the main world-breath.

Their heads—if you can name the concentrated sensory knot at one end that—carry bulging chambers of air framed by echo-sensitive tissues. They emit sound constantly, not as one tone but as overlapping bands: sharp clicks for fine detail, long wavering moans for distance mapping, soft subsonic rumbles that roll around pillars and come back thickened with information. Their skulls are mostly resonant cavities; their brains are wired to interpret interference patterns with a precision that makes vision, here, almost redundant.

They eat where sound is thickest: at the edges of resonant chambers, near the mouths of organ-like vents in the plant architecture. There, as the world-body moves fluids and gases, small organisms gather to siphon off nutrients, and the frondwings sweep through them like whales through krill. Tiny glass-bodied grazers, drifting spores, loose epiphytic threads—they open their broad maws and strain these from the air, sieving with comb-like baleen of living cilia.

There are many kinds.

- **Vein-frondwings**, smaller, travel close to the luminescent sap-veins, nibbling at exudates and symbiotic algae that line the channels.
- **Gulfsingers**, long and pale, inhabit the vast open shafts; their calls can span kilometers, sending into the dark a slow, braiding music that maps the emptiness in all directions.
- **Raptors of sound**, compact and muscular, hunt other frondwings, using jamming clicks to blind their prey’s echo-sense at the moment of strike.

Their flocks—if flocks they are—are more like dynamic, acoustically arranged swarms. Each individual tunes itself to avoid overlapping too harshly with its neighbors’ calls, creating layers of sound that fill the shared volume efficiently, like a self-organizing radio band. From a distance, their movement is heard as a shifting, iridescent chord.

The frondwings do not build, they do not own. They inhabit a volume the way fish inhabit water: by being inside it, changing it only by their passage.

### The Cleft-Dwellers and Crack-Bloods

Where plates join and fissures open, there is a different litany of life: creatures who are not content with drifting but want edges, leverage, purchase.

Along the great woody scales you stand on, between plates, live the cleft-dwellers.

Their bodies are made to be forgotten by light. Flat, so flat that a human hand, pressing them, would barely feel a thickness. Their organs are stacked rather than spread: a heart like a folded ribbon, lungs like paired scrolls. Their bones—if they have bones—are long, thin splinters of mineralized tissue running the length of their bodies, giving them stiffness like the spine of a leaf.

At rest, they lie in the darkness of the fissure, their upper surfaces coated in fine, frost-imitating filaments. To your eyes, they are indistinguishable from the rime-encrusted bark. But vibration is their sunrise. When anything heavy crosses the plates above, the resonance thread through the crack, a low tremor. The cleft-dweller eases upward, its body unfolding by degrees, each joint disarticulating like a bending ruler.

The mouth is not at one end but as a transient ring wherever the body decides to close. When flush against a surface, it can form a suction-cup of muscle and teeth, rasping and drinking sap, licking spilled nutrient films, or flensing soft tissue from corpses that tumble past its hiding place. They are the scavengers that convert accident into sustenance.

Some have specialized further into crack-bloods: smaller, swifter, living almost entirely within the plant body itself. They burrow through vascular tissues, lured and guided by electrochemical gradients, feeding directly on sap flows but rarely enough to cripple the system. They are tolerated, or rather not suppressed, because they also patrol for infections, consuming fungal intruders and parasitic molds that might otherwise bloom uncontrolled.

The crack-bloods are mobile immune cells on a macroscopic scale. Their movement through the vasculature generates subtle sounds—faint crickets of scraping chitin on lignin—that the world-body’s own feedback systems may detect. Whether it “uses” that information, or simply has always accommodated it, is unclear. But in places where crack-bloods are abundant, plant rot is rare. And the hunter-folk of this world know to watch for their cast husks: the translucent, chitinous shells they shed and leave wedged at vein mouths. Where shells are thick, the underlying tissue is healthy.

### The Three-Dimensional Hunters

The loping predator you glimpsed is not alone in its design principle. In a world made of ramps and voids, the apex hunters are those who can calculate three-dimensional movement the way others calculate a footstep.

The **vine-harnessed runners** are one such lineage.

Their core anatomy is animal: long torso, multiple limbs arranged around it radially, joints hyper-flexible. They could be spiders if spiders had discovered mammalian musculature and decided eight directions were too few. Their skin, where exposed, is translucent, veined with blue-black capillaries, the underlying muscle fibers visible like cables under oiled paper. But much of that skin is obscured by their partners: vampiric symbiont-vines.

These vines root shallowly into predetermined patches of flesh along the runners’ backs and flanks. Each tendril pierces, then spreads into a branching interior network that interlaces with the host’s own circulatory system. The vine steals sugars, heat, and hormone traces; in return, it offers armor, extra gripping fibers, and occasionally, a slow leak of chemical weapons.

On a charging runner, the vine-mass tightens into a sleek, dark carapace. Loose coils whip out as extra anchors, hooking onto nearby plant tissue to stabilize leaps or arrest falls. Some lineages have learned to cultivate stinging hairs along their symbionts’ tips, delivering numbing alkaloids into prey they brush. Others coax their vines to grow small bioluminescent nodules near sensory organs, a ghostly halo that amplifies low-level reflections and shadow, giving a crude form of night-sight.

Runners move by prediction of structure as much as by reaction. They have an intuitive map of load paths in the plant architecture: where a ramp will flex downward under weight but not break, which ropes of tissue are living, elastic guidelines and which are brittle dead sheaths. They feel these differences through their palms and pads, which secrete minute quantities of diagnostic enzymes; a quick chemical taste of bark or vine tells them whether to trust it.

Beyond them, there are other hunters:

- **Void-strikers**, aerial predators shaped like narrow spears with extendable fin-planes, who drop silently through darkness and bloom into braking surfaces at the last second, intersecting the paths of fleeing frondwings.
- **Wall-sifters**, slow and almost plantlike themselves, creeping along surfaces with fan-ended limbs that grow temporary chemical welds to the substrate. They are patient ambushers, flattening until they are bark; then, when a warm body passes, unfolding to enfold it, their under-surfaces studded with puncturing spines.

Predation here is not just teeth and chase. It is knowing how the world will flex three breaths from now, and intercepting where the prey will be when the world’s own motion carries it there.

### The Water-Fur and Brinefolk

Where condensation gathers into basins and membranous cups, life condenses as well, like mist into droplets.

At the scale of fingertips, the **glass-bodied grazers** and their hunters form an entire microcosm.

Grazers are small, almost two-dimensional ovals of jellied transparency, each threaded with iridescent channels that pulse in soft rhythms. Their ciliated undersides ripple as they skate across the thin films of water, combing out particulate nutrients and micro-organisms. They are as much filters as animals, turning the frosty water clear as they pass.

Their predators—the **dartlings**—are needle-like, with a pronounced asymmetry: one side hardened into a glassy keel, the other downy with sensilla that read chemical and temperature gradients. They move in twitching bursts, switching rapidly between water and air, their bodies briefly flashing brighter when they cross the interface as internal pressure vents. When they strike a grazer, their pointed mouths penetrate and inject a fast-acting enzyme that liquefies internal structure; they then drink their prey into themselves like a siphon.

Scaling up, there are **water-fur beasts**: small, mammal-analogous creatures that haunt the basins and drip-paths. Their bodies are rotund, compact, covered in layers of filamentous hair that trap liquid like a sponge. They spend much of their time splayed under slow drips, fur saturating, then retreating to more sheltered niches where they press against specialized patches of plant tissue and squeeze, delivering moisture into the receptive substrate.

The plant, in turn, exudes nutrient-rich gels into these feeding sites, which the water-fur lap and nibble. It is a barter: transport of water for exudate. To each other, they are farmer and domesticate both. Some strains of plant tissue have grown nipple-like protrusions just for this: easy to latch onto with broad, soft mouths.

If anything has been deliberately, consciously domesticated by the sapient folk here, it is likely the water-fur. They are portable cisterns, sources of moisture, fatty meat, and warmth. Tamed lines might be calmer, their fur thicker, their glands more productive. You might see them, in the dim light near a shelter, resting within woven enclosures of plant fiber, objectively caged but behaviorally unafraid, their eyes half-lidded as small human-shaped hands brush frost from their coats.

In the deeper, warmer hollows, the basins are large enough to be pools, perhaps lakes. There, the **brinefolk**—a more aquatic clade—may dwell.

Imagine bodies built like knotted ropes of muscle, always coiled, with multiple mouths along their lengths, each ringed in taste-sensitive papillae. They wind themselves among submerged root-pillars and sip directly from nutrient leaks, while frondwings skim the mirror of their surface. They never see the upper vaults; their world is the underside, the drowning-heart region where liquid is abundant and light seldom shines.

### The Plant That Pretends Not To Be

In a realm where everything is plant-shaped, there are organisms that choose to appear otherwise.

They are not animals but something parallel: parasitic or symbiotic vegetals that have surrendered fixed rooting in favor of slow motility. They constitute a third kingdom here, blurring all your old distinctions.

One form is the **pale rider**: a whip of fibrous tissue that has selected a mobile host and learned to direct it.

Beginning as a simple creeper, it grows along a column until it contacts a passing wall-crawler or runner. It lashes, hooks into soft tissue, and establishes a parasitic net. If the host’s immune analogues do not reject it, the pale rider spreads along the spinal pathways, sending fine threads of its own conduction tissue alongside nerve-like fibers.

Over time—often generations—the host’s descendants emerge with their nervous systems and the pale rider’s conduction net so intertwined that neither can function alone. The plant-parasite begins to secrete neuromodulatory chemicals, nudging the host toward behaviors that suit it: staying near particular thermal vents, revisiting the same sap-rich surfaces, avoiding areas rich in anti-fungal exudates. In exchange, it reinforces the host’s muscles with its own tension fibers and may, under duress, stiffen the body against sudden shocks, preventing fatal falls.

To an outside observer, the pale rider is invisible, buried beneath fur or skin. But its existence is betrayed by trails of unusual bark-scars along favorite routes, where its own small feeding filaments have probed into the substrate as the host passed.

Another is the **walking epiphyte**, a mat of tightly laced stems that grows upon itself into a dome, with internal merits of voids that hold reserves of air and sap. It is not fast; its movement is a quiet peristalsis, a billowing crawl inches at a time. But it is persistent. It creeps along shaded ledges, across the backs of sleeping beasts, even up the sides of shelters and woven bridges, always seeking slightly warmer zones, slightly richer chemical scents.

The hunter-folk peel it off when they find it on their paths and bake it into dense, fibrous cakes. It is not tasty, but in a world of scarcity, its slow, portable calories can mean the difference between survival and loss.

### The People in the Roaring Dark

And then, somewhere between all of this, there are the ones whose marks you already see: the rope-makers, basin-shapers, bridge-coaxers.

Call them people, because they behave like that word.

They are not necessarily human in form, but you would recognize much in them: careful hands, deliberate scars in the world, a way of looking that counts routes and resources and stories at once.

Their bodies must be tuned for this vertical, frigid monumentality. Picture:

- Limbs long relative to torso, for reach.
- Hands with multiple sets of opposable digits arranged around the palm, so that any surface, no matter its orientation, can be gripped as if it were “down.”
- Feet less like human feet and more like flexible claws or pads that can flatten to increase friction, or fold to catch on narrow protrusions.
- A thorax wrapped in layers of insulating tissue, perhaps a combination of fat and fur or fur-equivalent, but not bulky; bulk is a liability where every kilogram must be placed and caught.

Their faces—if faces they have—are built around sensory pits, not forward-facing eyes alone. Vision is secondary, a close-range tool. Primary is hearing: broad, dish-like auricles that can fold flat when pressed to a surface, or flare outward to catch the faint variations in the roaring air. They listen like others see: building distance maps, recognizing individual voices miles away, distinguishing the creak of young growth from the crack of imminent failure.

Breath is precious. The air is thin and cold; their lungs must be deep and efficient, their blood rich in carriers for what little oxygen there is—whether that is a hemoglobin analogue or some copper-based pigment, lending their tongues and gums an odd hue.

They do not live in dense masses. Settlements are small clusters of shelters on comparatively stable ledges, linked by ropes and marked routes. A “village” might be ten families, spread along a hundred vertical meters of growth-front, each household’s shelter wedged into its own concavity or grown niche.

Their tools and tech, as you saw, are an art of plant exploitation:

- Blades chipped from sap-glass and stone gleaned from rare mineral nodules that fall or are uncovered.
- Cords spun from the long, high-tensile fibers stripped from certain vines, treated with plant resins to resist moisture and frost.
- Nets designed to intersect predictable migration paths: where frondwings must pass to reach a basin, where climbing beasts funnel through a narrow choke-point.

They know fire, but use it sparingly. More common is heat derived from harnessing the world-body’s own warmth: carving small cavities that open into sap channels, then lining them so that radiated heat enters but fluid does not. These become warm-hollows, communal sleeping and working spaces insulated from the worst of the thin cold.

For light, they farm also. Certain lichenous growths they scrape and transplant onto portable panels of bark, feeding them with leftover nutrients from kills, coaxing them into brighter, steadier glows. They weave cages for glow-pods taken from naturally luminous plants, feeding those plants carefully so that they do not burn out.

Their stories are carved into the world-body’s skin where it can hold scratches. These carvings are not just decoration but record-keeping: diagrams of safe routes after a collapse restructured the local architecture, genealogies of bridges, accounts of hunts. In a place where everything shifts at multiple timescales, memory must be anchored.

They measure time not by a sun that never shows, but by:

- The frequency of internal heat pulses: “Three warmings ago” as a way to locate an event in cycles.
- The songs of long-ranging creatures: seasonal patterns in the presence of certain gulfsinger choruses.
- The growth stages of their guided plants: how many knots on a bridge-vine since a grandparent first bent it.

Up and down are moral as well as physical directions for them. Up is danger, brittleness, glory. Young daring ones go there to harvest rare membrane tissues and frost-crystal accumulations that have medicinal or ritual use. Down is depth, warmth, and older dangers—great beasts and the slow, crushing processes of world-body reconfiguration. Few go far in either direction; most live in the mid-band, where their ancestors learned a precarious stability.

They are not the only sapients possible here. There could be others:

- A culture of frondwing-intelligent analogues, whose bodies are built to live entirely in the void, clustering around resonant columns as “towns” defined only in sound.
- A society of plant-kin, whose minds emerge from interlaced epiphyte mats, thinking at speeds too slow for animals to notice, regarding two hundred of your years as a long deliberation.

But the carven shelters and hand-woven ropes belong to the ledge-folk, the crack-line walkers.

They live by reading the world-body’s moods. A slight shift in the tone of the background roar may send them scrambling to safer anchors, knowing a global flex is coming. A new bass hum from below might mean a segment is thickening—a chance to scout new routes on fresh-grown tissue before other clans claim it. An odd silence in a familiar echoscape signals a predator, or a collapse, or the extinction of some neighbor’s song.

Community is a fragile lattice here. Clans meet rarely in person. More common is communication by sound: long-distance work-songs, elaborate call-and-response sequences bounced along column surfaces. They travel on trade expeditions, trekking for days along spiraled growth paths, carrying bundles of resin, fur, dried meat, glow-panels to exchange for other clans’ crafted fibers, rare stones, knowledge of safer routes.

Routes are wealth; knowing how to cross a gulf by three well-placed leaps instead of ten riskier ones is as valuable as any tool. Bridges are legacy; to attach your family’s name to a living span that will bear bodies for generations is to be remembered.

They teach their children to climb before they can walk on flat. Falling is so omnipresent a threat that it becomes mythic: tales of those who fell and were carried by wind-currents to unknown deeps, founding lineages in warmer caverns far below.

### The Ones You Do Not See

For every form you can name, there are those you only infer from traces: a warmth in the air where none should be, a cluster of scars in a pattern no chance found.

- In the lowest groaning depths, titanic siphon-creatures may coil along the inner surface of the world-body, tapping its deepest nutrient flows. Their exhalations—slow, massive—could be part of what you sense as the long weather-waves of this place.
- Inside thick columns, cavities hold colonies of **choral molds**, fungal-plant hybrids whose bioluminescent flickering encodes something like memory. Their glow-pulses are not random; they echo patterns of long-past disturbances, like photographic negatives of storms. Whether this is mere chemical hysteresis or a form of archive is debatable.
- Encysted in inert-looking knots are **seed-intelligences**: asleep, potential world-bodies in miniature, waiting for some disaster to tear open the crust and cast them into a next substrate.

You mostly meet them as absences: places where no other life is, where the world-body’s sounds change subtly, where your own skin prickles for reasons you cannot name.

---

### In Sum

What lives here are:

- The world-body plant, an all-encompassing, distributed life for whom “place” and “organism” are synonyms.
- The frondwing air-swimmers, mapping and filling the emptiness with sound.
- The cleft-dwellers and crack-bloods, thin lives in thin cracks, filtering death and rot into continuation.
- The three-dimensional hunters, vine-armored or wall-welded, whose genius is prediction of structure.
- The water-fur beasts, glass-grazers, dartlings, and brinefolk, each orbiting the rare abundance of liquid.
- The parasitic and symbiotic plant-kin that choose motility, the pale riders and walking epiphytes.
- The ledge-folk people, tool-users and path-writers, living in small, careful clusters, coaxing bridges from the world-body’s flesh and carving their presence into its memory.

And also those uncounted others whose names you would only learn by living here, by hanging from the same ropes, by sleeping in the hum of sap-warmed hollows while the great plant shifts and sings around you, making and remaking the only world it knows: itself.
