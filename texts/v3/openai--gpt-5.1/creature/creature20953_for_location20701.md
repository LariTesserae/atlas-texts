They are shallow-rooted people, the ones who live here. If “people” is even the right word.

They are not built to rise far above the plane of the land. Nothing here is. Their bodies conform to the grammar of flatness and damp, of weight spread and edges haunted. Think of them first as distributions before you think of them as forms: as densities of awareness wherever water, silt, and air meet.

Some of them you would miss entirely, mistaking them for a local thickening of the ordinary.

---

Those that move in the open

On the flats, where the salt-grasses hold each other at arm’s length and the soil sits only reluctantly in its own shape, there are the walkers.

They are tall only because their legs are long. Their actual bodies—organs, brain, whatever passes for a heart—hang low and close to the water line, carried slung between the limbs like a patient load, not perched high like a lookout. From a distance they read as four or six splayed limbs stepping deliberately, with a suspended, podlike mass at their center.

Their skin is the color of standing water under overcast sky: an uncommitted gray-brown with a faint oil sheen when looked at directly, which dulls to matte from the side. It looks like skin but behaves more like a wetted hide: thick, softly reflective, holding a permanent film of moisture even when the air dries for days. Along the long bones of their limbs, small ridges run lengthwise, not armor, not decoration—just accumulated habit from a million careful placements in soft ground.

Each foot is not a cloven hoof or a single pad, but a fan of flexible, keratinous plates that spread when weighted, like the underside of a waterbird’s webbed foot grown thick and slow. When they lift a foot, water beads in the tiny troughs and then drops off unwillingly. They step with a curiously delicate precision for creatures of their size, testing each patch of ground with a fractional press before committing weight.

They eat what most here eats: the steadiness itself. They graze on the space between things. Lips—a ring of tactile, fissured tissue around a blunt, jawless opening—press not only to the plants, but to the soil, the algae film, the faint bacterial skins that grow in invisible layers on everything wet. When they lower their bodies to feed, the central pod approaches the ground until, from the side, the creature looks almost flat, a piece of the earth folding down upon itself.

Their heads, if you want to call them that, are not distinct. Sensory organs are arranged in an equatorial band around the body’s bulge. Dark, soft-lensed eyes sit in shallow pits, ringed by fleshy folds that can close almost completely to keep out grit and algae. Between them stretch fine, whisker-like cilia that tremble in every current of air and shift of temperature. Smell, here, is not a sharp distinction between aromas but a continuous gradient of wet and rot and new growth, and their nervous systems are tuned to pick out the subtle shifts.

They do not herd; they concur. A group of them crossing a flat does so as plants grow here: each maintaining a quiet distance from its neighbor, a spacing that seems mathematical when seen from above. No one leads, no one follows. They move along invisible isobars of moisture and compaction, pausing where the earth’s resistance enters some narrow band of “enough” that tells them drought is not imminent, flood not yet.

Their gestures are slow, but not stupid. A leg retracts, waits, extends somewhere else. A whole body turns in a curve that might take a full minute to complete. Close by, they vocalize rarely: a soft, wet knocking from within their torsos, as of stones being turned in a barrel, answered by identical sounds from companions. At distances, they use the air: a low-frequency bellow so stretched by humidity that it’s more felt in the diaphragm than heard in the ear, rolling over the fields like distant thunder that never becomes a storm.

You could walk among them if they let you. They would part around you as water around a rock, giving you your own respectful spacing in their diffuse, unhurried formation.

---

Those that root and think sideways

In the lower hollows, where the shrubs lean over the longer-standing pools, there is another residence of mind, though it wears the form of vegetation.

The shrubs themselves are not special at a glance: squat, leaning, leaves small and waxy, branches bent in the direction of the prevailing wind. But their roots are where they live most fully. They do not send a deep tap down; the water table is too uncertain, too mobile. Instead, they push out laterally—thin, fibrous roots in a thick mat just below the surface, wandering outward meters in every direction, weaving with the mats of neighbors until it’s no longer sensible to say which root belongs to which plant.

Along these roots grow swellings: nodes like knuckles, pale and nodular, packed with fungus, bacteria, and stored sugar. Around each swelling the soil is different, clinging in tighter, darker clumps. In these nodes, slow signals pass—chemicals, ions, pulses of water pressure. Information travels laterally through the mycelial web married to the roots: that a leaf has been bitten; that a pool has shrunk; that the air has, for three mornings in a row, condensed less dew.

To this web, what lives above the surface—the insects, the ungulate-analogues, the predators—is weather. Their passing is recorded as disturbances, then slowly averaged out. This network understands erosion and deposition in the intimate way that comes from centuries of anchoring one’s body in loose substrate. It anticipates the creep of channels, the collective weight of this season’s ungulate feet pressing silt from one side of a hollow to the other.

Every now and then, the shrubs flower, small and shy and close to the branch. But the real reproductive event happens in the soil. Under certain alignments of saturation and heat—conditions that may take decades to converge—the root-fungus matrix in a patch goes into spasm. It releases vast clouds of microscopic propagules into the water film between grains, spores and gametes and unknown equivalents that drift sluggishly through the muck, chiming chemically with others of their kind. New shrubs, when they sprout, do so not singly but in low, loose constellations that mirror the invisible connectivity beneath.

If they have intention, it is expressed on a scale that makes it look like inevitability: a gentle but persistent thickening of growth along subtle rises, a quiet abandonment of ground that has sunk one finger-width too far, the chemical ghost of a shaded decade still detectable in the metabolisms of plants that now stand in full, flat light.

If something like memory lives here, it is in these tangled root-plans: a three-dimensional relief-map of centuries of minor wetness, pressed into the habit of growth.

---

Those that lie between breaths

Among the predator forms, there are the burrowers that wait beneath crusted soil and the floaters that become as planar as the puddles they inhabit. But there is another kind of dweller, one that has made a home in the intermissions themselves.

You do not see them. You see what they leave.

On a morning when dew has been generous, walk the gradient between damp and open water. In some stretches, the soil is stippled: a scatter of minute, perfectly rounded pellets of mud, thrown up around a pinhole you only notice once it’s pointed out. Each of those holes is an entrance, and each is also a lung.

Below the surface someone is folded up: a creature coiled like a question mark in a narrow, J-shaped burrow that follows the curve of its own body. Its skin is thin, almost translucent, lined with capillaries that bloom bright with whatever passes for blood here. Its limbs are laughable in proportion to its torso: tiny, spidery arrangements of joint and claw that would be hopeless for travel over open ground. Their function is simple: to hold position against the soft, yielding walls; to brace for the one sharp motion that defines a life made mostly of waiting.

These beings feed not by extended chase but by insertion into the small inevitabilities of the surface world. An unwary insect, heavier with condensation than it realizes, blunders onto the faintly weaker crust above a burrow’s mouth; the soil gives way, the bug tumbles a centimeter, and the coiled body below uncoils with appalling speed, mouth first. The strike begins, ends, and resets in less time than the surrounding soil needs to remember its shape.

But their most remarkable adaptation is to the long pauses between opportunities. When the surface desiccates beyond a certain softness, when the moisture films bridging particles of soil break and air can move more freely, the burrowers curl tighter. Their metabolism slides downward as the gradient of damp above them dwindles. If drought persists, they will enter a state like a key turned almost all the way but not quite: ready to spring back, but not moving. Their skin secretes a mucous that dries to a flexible shell within their tunnels, shrinking just enough to keep a pressure of contact from all sides.

In this state, they can wait through whole dry cycles, pulses of years that the shrubs feel as the slow yellowing of leaves at edges, that the walkers notice only as the necessity of stepping further between drinks. To the burrowers, these years compress to a blur of almost-events, like breath held just before the body decides whether to inhale or exhale.

When the air grows wet again and the soil remembers how to cling to itself, their shells soften, their damp returns, and the waiting resumes in a more immediate key. Entire generations may live like this, measuring time not in days but in the number of comas endured.

If anything like thought flickers inside those pale skulls, it probably comes in short, bright bursts anchored around the shock of impact and the little, sudden presences that fall into their mouths.

---

Those that map the sky

Few things fly here with intention beyond hunger. The heavy air, the lack of vertical relief, gives no reason to master height. But there are the dragonfly-analogues, and there is one more: a slow, high rider of the humid air that you might miss as just another long cloud-fragment until it moves against the general drift.

Imagine a creature like a ribbon of muscle sheathed in thin, resilient membrane, its cross-section a flattened oval, its length three, four times that of a tall person. Along its sides, regular intervals of fin-like protrusions feather outward, hundreds of them, each a tiny, controllable surface. The entire being is, in effect, a living sampler of the wind field: an animal built to feel and use every difference in pressure and temperature across its own skin.

Its underside is pale; its back is darker, mottled to mimic the sediments beneath shallow water. When seen from above—as only two things see it, neither of them exactly eyes—it disappears into the general sheet of earth and pool. From below, it is often lost in the underbelly of the clouds it half-joins.

It feeds on sound.

Not in the fable sense, not by eating noise as food, but in the literal sense that the sounds of this land—those constant insect throbs, the percussive croaks, the sporadic cries of walkers—are pressure waves in the same medium it hangs in, and its entire outer surface is sensitive to them. Each tiny fin stiffens or yields in response to the fractionally higher or lower pressure of a wave. In doing so, they give the animal a running, full-body map of noise travelling through the air-space of the basin.

Where the noise is thick, life is abundant. Where there is sudden silence, something has moved—predator, catastrophe, unusual event. The ribbon-rider listens across miles, feeling the difference between the horizon-to-horizon insect drone and the sharper, localized patterns of individual dramas. It glides toward the densest, finest-grained patches of sound, where the air is most alive with interference.

There, at altitudes just a little lower, countless small flying insects crowd: the midges, the gnats, the minute unseen pollinators that swarm where exhalations of CO₂ and traces of plant volatiles are richest. The ribbon-rider lowers itself into those layers and opens its mouth, which is not a discrete hole but a long ventral slit fringed with raking cilia. It sieves, going slowly, letting the air and its tiny freight pass through, catching bodies and turning them efficiently into mass which then again becomes surface, sensitivity, wing.

It has no fixed home. Its “territory” is the shape of the basin’s soundscape at any given decade. When hot centuries lengthen the dry spells and some stretches of basin fall quieter, it spends more of its time over the remaining permanent pools, treading air above the thorny thickets of sound where amphibians still sing. When the climate leans wetter and the insect hiss thickens over previously meager ground, its long, undulating orbit widens.

It will never land in your sight. It does not recognize the ground as a place where anything it needs is concentrated. The land is boundary, not resource: a solid that wind must flow around, a source of thermal texture.

If there is a mind in it, that mind is an extension of listening: a continuous, involuntary cartography of where life is loudest.

---

Those that are many in one

Beneath all these, beneath the walkers’ careful feet, beneath the shrubs’ mat of roots, beneath the burrowers in their suspended curled shells, there is another inhabitant that is singular only when described.

Picture a film of life only a few grains of soil thick, but spread across kilometers: a consortium of microbe and filament and ooze that lives always at the interface—between anoxic and oxygenated water, between coarse and fine sediment, between now-rotting and fully-rotted matter. This film is not uniform. It pools in some regions, thins in others, just as surface water does.

In places, you see its presence directly: a thin iridescent sheen on a still puddle; a faint, sulfurous exhalation from an otherwise ordinary patch of mud; a sudden sinking when you step on a piece of ground that seemed no wetter than the rest. These are pinholes into a far larger continuity.

It feeds on what everything else eventually becomes. Every stem that falls, every ungulate that misjudges the firmness of a bank and drowns, every burrower that fails to wake from its dry-season coma—all of that passes through this film. Its metabolisms are baroque and redundant. For every known pathway of decomposition, it seems to have grown three more, improvisations tuned to the exact blend of minerals and organics and water that this basin offers.

If anything deserves the name “intelligence of drainage patterns, nutrient flows, competition and coexistence,” it’s this. The film has no central node, no seat of consciousness, but it does have continuity, and continuity means that information about conditions travels: a particularly rich corpse here causes a bloom there; a slow shift in inflowed minerals from that side of the basin brings different species of microbe into dominance miles away. Its “body” expands and contracts with the fluctuations of the water table, always hugging the descending or rising frontier.

No one here listens for it, but everyone listens through it. The shrubs’ root-fungi communicate by way of it; predators’ burrows smear its substance along their walls; even the walkers, when they lie down to rest their hung bodies on the plane of the land, press against it, exchange trace chemicals at the boundary of skin and soil.

If you insist on calling something here a singular resident, an old mind that has watched the basin lower its ambitions and settle, it is this film. It has no language except relative percentages of nitrate and sulfide, no memory except for the present shape of its own distribution, but that is enough to make it the keeper of the basin’s only real narrative: where things have rotted, where they are rotting now, and where they will be able to rot in the futures allowed by the way water moves.

---

Those that almost remember standing

Rarely, in the drier outer reaches of the basin where the ground hesitates on the edge of becoming truly firm, you might find something that hints at another way of being here, one that never took full hold.

On a low rise stand pillars. At first you take them for stones—dark, upright, no taller than a person. They are spaced irregularly but with a rhythm that is not all accident: distances repeat, angles recur. Lichen coats their lower halves; moss attempts tenuous colonization higher up, where the air is marginally less saturated.

Their surfaces are too regular to be natural outcrops. A bulging here that mirrors a bulging there; a concavity, waist-high, where hands might once have rested were there hands in this world that shaped them. And at their bases, half-swallowed by soil and creeping herb, there lie discs—flat, round, of the same stone, some broken, some intact. They are not tools or altars, not clearly anything. Just shapes that repeat, whose repetition suggests decision.

The inhabitants that once may have tended them are gone, or changed beyond recognition, or never were. Perhaps the walkers used to kneel here; perhaps the shrubs grew from plan rather than drift; perhaps some biped once saw this basin as something to be arranged, however minimally.

Now, these pillars serve as nothing more than localized windbreaks, as perches for the rare, long-legged bird-form that pauses in its slow sweep of the flats. Their presence does almost nothing to alter the patient rules of the place. Given enough centuries, they too are rounding, edges blurred by moisture and temperature. In time, they will be no more remarkable than those knuckle-like rocks that weep mineral water elsewhere.

It is possible that to the shallow-rooted walkers, these pillars are only slightly more meaningful than any other hard intrusion into their world. They might rub against them in the damp heat of midday, the way some creatures rub against trees to ease an itch. The pillars will outlast even that.

If there was once an intelligence here that set stones upright, it has been deeply, thoroughly composted into the present community. The basin has incorporated its gesture and moved on.

---

Those that are only voices

Many of the “birds—if they are birds” are not bodies in the way you expect but phenomena in motion, coalescing and dissolving around the conditions that give them shape.

Take the harsh, two-note cry that rips across the distances. Track it to its source and you might find nothing more than a flicker in the air, a distortion where humidity and temperature conspire to thicken perception. For an instant, between one blink and the next, there’s a form: a narrow, wedge-shaped thing, wings like knife-edges, a beak that is all gape. It passes, it cries, it folds itself back into the uniformity of the sky.

These entities do not perch. They are events, not residents, at least as far as mass is concerned. They arise where gradients are steep—a sudden clash of cooler air above a standing pool with warmer air rolling off a crusted flat. At such boundaries, patterns of density in the air can sustain themselves long enough to organize: to become repeating, to become something like muscle and intent for the span of a call and a flight.

They prey upon other events: dense clouds of insect-sound, small vibrating clusters of warmth that betray hidden frogs or burrowers near the surface. When they dive, the sharpness of their passage briefly overwrites the local soundscape; insects fall abruptly silent in a narrow corridor under their track, not from impact but from the sudden jerk in the air that upsets every wingbeat.

Once the gradient that birthed them relaxes, so do they. Their wings blur, their beaks become just another fluctuation, their cries attenuate into the background hum. To speak of them as creatures is to give too much solidity to what is essentially pattern.

Yet they are part of who lives here as surely as the walkers and the rootwebs are. They are how the basin tries out forms made not of earth and water but of difference itself.

---

All of them together

Who lives here is not any single form. It is the relationship between saturation and restraint, between movement and the refusal to thrust upward in a land that has been so long worn down.

The walkers know the basin as pressure against their soles and lips; the rootweb knows it as variations in dissolved mineral and shared shade; the burrowers know it as the thickness of the soil roof above their curled backs; the ribbon-rider knows it as the topology of sound; the microbial film knows it as the map of where death currently gathers.

Even those stone pillars, half-decayed artifacts or peculiar natural extrusions, participate. They slightly change how water pools at their bases, how shade stretches at particular hours; these minute changes echo down the chains of who can grow, who can wait, who must move.

If you try to isolate a “resident” here as we would in harsher, more dramatic lands—a people with tools, a singular species at the center of an ecology—you find your focus blurring, as distance blurs in the warm damp air. What lives here is spacing itself: a long, ongoing act of remaining separate enough not to collapse into choke or flood, close enough to share the slow, continuous wealth of water and rot.

They live in the edges, these beings: edge of pool, edge of crust, edge of wakefulness, edge of form. Their lives are excerpts from a much longer, lower-voiced sentence the land has been speaking since it lay down to rest and decided, with monumental patience, to do almost nothing but continue.
