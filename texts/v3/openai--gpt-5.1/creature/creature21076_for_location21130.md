They are not gone.

Whoever built this place is no longer here as they were, walking dry corridors and reading clear screens, but traces of them persist in at least three different ways: in bodies, in habits, and in something like memory that has slipped its original substrate.

The inhabitants now are layered, like the city itself. No single species owns it. No single mind governs it. Yet something here behaves as if it remembers being shaped.

---

## 1. The remnant humans

Very few, and very changed.

They stay in the gas-filled interiors: pressure-hulls bolted into the city’s skeleton, long since written off by the rest of the world. In most modules, life-support failed gradually, unremarkably, and what people there once were became part of the sediment cycle. But in a handful of clusters, things did not quite fail. They slipped.

### Physiology

The surviving humans are no longer exactly human in the surface sense.

Generations of high pressure, recycled atmosphere, chronic low light, and pervasive contamination by engineered microbes have selected for, and then been helped to create, a new baseline:

- **Compact bodies:**  
  Shorter on average, with thick bones and high-density cartilage. Limbs are slightly shortened but powerfully muscled. Movement is economical: every gesture small, deliberate.

- **Modified respiration and blood:**  
  Their lungs are still lungs, but alveoli are reinforced, their capillaries unusually elastic. Blood carries nano-scale particulate helpers, descendants of medical bots and inhaled filter-living microbes. These collectively break down toxins that would be fatal to an unadapted visitor. Mild cyanosis is common; their hemoglobin chemistry is subtly shifted for low-oxygen efficiency.

- **Skin and sensory adaptations:**  
  Skin tends toward gray-brown, mottled where environmental microbes share the outermost layers. Hair is sparse; sebaceous glands have altered secretion profiles to maintain a biofilm that protects against fungal overgrowth and the near-saturated humidity.  
  Eyes are large relative to the skull, but not luminous. Pupils adjust slowly but over a wide dynamic range. More important than sight are:
  - Ridged pads along fingertips and forearms dense with mechanoreceptors for feeling minute pressure changes in bulkheads and water pipes.
  - An unusually acute sense of smell, tuned to the tang of ozone, the iron of rust, the faint sweetness of biofilm bloom, the sour edge of mold gone wrong.

- **Pressure tolerance:**  
  Their bodies have adapted to the permanent squeeze. Minor barometric shifts that would cause decompression sickness in outsiders barely register; their tissues host symbiotic bacteria that mop up dissolved nitrogen before it can form bubbles. Their veins and sinuses are subtly re-shaped, smoothed by generational remodeling.

To see one of them underwater is rare, but it happens. When they work outside their modules, they use suits, but the suits are thinner and less armored than standard deepwork equipment. Pressure is no longer what threatens them most; temperature and biohazard are. Their helmets are clear but heavily scarred; their tools are small, multi-purpose, usually worn more than held.

They move in the water with a kind of meditative care, not out of weakness but because here haste is self-defeating. A single clumsy kick can fill a corridor with opaque silt for hours.

### Culture and technology

Their culture has condensed around two facts: isolation and integration.

- **Language:**  
  The spoken tongue is a heavily eroded descendant of whatever they brought with them. Sentences are clipped; gestures carry half the grammar. There are many words for subtle environmental states:
  - The exact consistency of condensation on a bulkhead.
  - The “taste” of a given corridor’s air.
  - The pitch of a pump’s hum just before it fails.

  They have fewer words for wide-open spaces than for pipes, grills, gaskets, skins.

- **Tools and maintenance:**  
  They cannot build new macro-structures from raw ore; those systems have degraded beyond their capacity to repair. Instead, their technology is:
  - **Salvage-oriented:** cannibalizing non-critical modules to patch critical ones. A wall panel from a dead corridor becomes reinforcement for a pressure dome. A disused robotic arm is decoupled, its actuators and encoders rebased into life-support valves.
  - **Bio-integrated:** they grow insulation, grow filters, grow sealants. Fungal composites line duct interiors; engineered slime molds patch microfractures. They seed specific microbes into leak-prone joints, counting on those microbes to precipitate minerals as self-healing “caulk”.

- **Energy economy:**  
  Centralized power is mostly gone. What remains:
  - Trickle current from geothermal thermoelectric rigs still half-functional down in the root zone.
  - Small turbine units placed in the most reliable internal water flows.
  - Chemical cells tended like gardens: colonies of exoelectrogenic bacteria scraping electrons from slow, engineered redox reactions.

  Every watt is precious. Lighting is low and localized. Devices sleep by default. Heavy computation is rare; they have learned to live without constant high-speed symbolic processing.

- **Internal spaces as habitats:**  
  Their surviving modules are not sterile. Soil beds, fungal towers, vertical algae curtains—these occupy corners and walls. Domestic interiors feel like miniature, drier echoes of the outside: pipes with creepers, panels with moss, but all species curated and pruned. They hate true emptiness: a bare wall is a suspicious wall, either just scrubbed after contamination or awaiting purposeful colonization.

### Social organization

They are few—dozens, perhaps a hundred at most, scattered in several semi-linked hab clusters. With such numbers extinction is a real threat; their social structures address that constantly.

- **Clans by function:**  
  Lineages are associated not by classic professions but by systems:
  - One clan keeps the breathers and exchangers tuned.
  - Another tends the thermal organs and geothermal taps.
  - Another manages circulation—pumps, peristaltic liners, the viscous rivers of nutrient slurry.

  Children grow up inside the sounds and smells of their clan’s system; they read those cues as others would read weather.

- **Rituals around repair and failure:**  
  They have ceremonies for shutting down a machine permanently, for rerouting a pipe, for sealing a space that will never be opened again. These are not flamboyant; a few phrases, a gesture, a drawn symbol on metal before it vanishes under foam or shell. But the emotional weight is real. Every closure shrinks their world.

- **Memory:**  
  They have records, but the data centers are long dead. What lives now is:
  - Redacted fragments preserved on simple, resilient media: etched plates, laminated schematics annotated by generations of hands.
  - Oral recitations: lineages of "machine-lore" describing how a system once functioned in full, even if only a narrow remnant survives.

  They know the city as old sailors knew coasts: by landmarks and tales of how things used to behave, partly myth now, but myth grounded around physical objects still present.

They do not dream of going to the surface. That idea is so divorced from reality it barely exists as a fantasy. Their horizon is the basin wall and the reliability of the hot flow beneath.

## 2. The designed non-humans

Before the city accepted being part of the seafloor, its engineers had already softened the boundary between machine and life. What lives here now includes entities that were never born, exactly, but were grown, compiled, or assembled to serve.

Over time some of these have slipped their chains.

### 2.1. The microecologies

Most obvious are the microbial consortia—the layers of slime, film, and fuzz that coat every surface. They are not simply wild colonists; many began as tailored communities.

- **Filter-stacks turned symbionts:**  
  Filters in the breathers and exchangers house consortia designed to:
  - Remove heavy metals.
  - Convert toxic gases.
  - Generate trace nutrients.

  The original recipes still echo: a “Stack-4 Bloom” here, a “Blue-Edge Film” there. But horizontal gene transfer and selection in this peculiar enclosed world have created new character species: hybrids that are more stable under local variations.

These consortia are not intelligent individually, but in aggregate they behave with a stubborn directional push. You can see it in how they:

- Re-colonize scrubbed surfaces along predictable sequences.
- Stabilize coolant chemistries despite hardware drift.
- Maintain pH windows within which the rest of the city’s metabolics can function.

From the human point of view, the city’s films are not just background; they are partners with needs and tolerances. People talk about them as if they were weather systems or old infrastructure: “The Orange Mat’s thickening near the west duct, time to bleed off more copper.”

### 2.2. The crawlers and cleaners

Larger are the semi-autonomous maintenance organisms, half-robot, half-biological, once meant to service the unreachable.

- **Pipe-worms:**  
  Slender, segmented things that live inside the circulation network. Built from flexible composite rings laced with living tissue, they:
  - Eat clogs.
  - Reinforce weak spots by exuding resin.
  - Excrete data: faint electrical patterns along embedded filaments, once monitored by central systems, now largely unread.

  With central direction gone, they follow simple local rules: move toward thicker resistance; avoid flows too strong; avoid each other’s chemical trails. Over decades they have carved a compromise with the microfilms. They no longer attempt to render pipes “clean,” only passable.

- **Shellback scrapers:**  
  Disk-bodied units the size of a dinner plate, moving slowly on magnetized feet along structural beams. Originally mechanical with a bioslurry stomach, now largely encased in symbiotic shell:
  - They scrape off growths that threaten to overload a beam.
  - Redistribute that biomass to weaker sections, seeding new growth to reinforce.

  Some have lost their guidance arrays and simply loop familiar tracks endlessly, carving characteristic grooves of relative bareness through the general fuzz.

These creatures do not know that humans still live here. They respond to pressure, chemical signatures, and internal directives that speak more to structural stress than to sentience.

### 2.3. The “ghost swarm”

Less visible but more uncanny is what remains of the city’s neural net of sensors, firmware, regulatory code, and cohabiting microbial computation.

Originally, environmental regulation relied on:

- A distributed mesh of microcontrollers.
- Smart sensors embedded in pipes and walls.
- Bioelectrical feedback loops with engineered microbes that responded predictably to certain voltages or chemicals.

Without centralized oversight, parts of this mesh have maintained themselves, patching over failing segments with crude analog workarounds. Others have devolved into noise. But some fraction—maybe only a tiny one—is still coherent.

Its manifestations are subtle:

- A valve that should have seized decades ago still oscillates through a narrow range, exactly enough to bleed pressure spikes from a rarely-used conduit.
- A sequence of beacons along a maintenance shaft keeps a faint, non-random rhythm, adjusting to changes in flow that no human monitors.
- When a human crew dares to reroute a major line, they find that some actuators “anticipate” them—already moving partway toward the needed configuration as if recognizing a pattern of demands.

It is not consciousness in any human sense. It has:
- No single locus.
- No concept of “self” separate from the physical functioning of the city.

But if you watched the combined pattern of tiny decisions over months, you would see something biasing outcomes toward continued structural and metabolic coherence. The ghost swarm’s “goal” is indistinguishable from the city’s survival.

Humans do not converse with it. They infer it, give it names, leave sensor-stubs and chemical markers for it to notice. Sometimes it “responds”: a changed pump schedule, a freshly regulated gradient. They argue among themselves whether these are real dialogues or coincidences.

## 3. The wild opportunists

Beyond the designers’ intent and the remnant humans’ stewardship are the true natives of this new ecology: species that have evolved into and around the city since it sank into stasis.

### 3.1. Niche-binders

These are small, usually overlooked, but numerically dominant.

- **Gasket grazers:**  
  Micro-crustaceans and mites (or their deepwater analogs) that specialize in the elastomeric rings and seals of old joints. Their enzy­mes digest polymer chains, but only when those are partially oxidized; in consuming degraded material, they paradoxically preserve the youngest, most intact surfaces. In some areas they are all that stand between a brittle ring and catastrophic leak.

- **Gradient shapers:**  
  Tiny, mobile colonies of chemotrophic microbes that drift to interfaces: the exact boundary layer between warmer and cooler water near a vent, or between oxygenated and anoxic strata in stagnant pockets. They form invisible “skins” that slow mixing, preserving discrete zones that other life depends upon. Without them, the city’s fluid compartments would be more homogeneous and less hospitable to specialization.

- **Spark feeders:**  
  Bacteria that cling to aging power cables, not for heat but for stray electric fields. Their membranes bristle with proteins that harvest minuscule currents. They bloom in delicate graffiti where insulation has thinned, tracing the buried grid in luminous freckles.

### 3.2. Larger fauna: attendants and shadows

Larger animals are rare but ecologically important.

- **Vault eels:**  
  Long, slender, blind fish that haunt the bigger water-filled volumes. Their bodies carry lines of electroreceptors; they sense cable fields and piezoelectric whispers from straining structures.  
  They feed mostly on free-swimming larvae and occasional carrion, but their routes are strongly patterned by the city’s nervous system. To human observers, their migrations are omens: vault eels suddenly avoiding a certain corridor means a pump deep within is failing, long before any gauge shows it.

- **Mantle gliders:**  
  Those ribbon-bodied membrane gliders are more integrated than they look. They:
  - Spend most of their lives sheeted along synthetic membranes and natural fronds.
  - Absorb and re-emit certain signaling chemicals used by the microflora.

  In effect, they are living messengers, physically transporting faint molecular messages across small gaps too large for diffusion alone. Without intending to, they couple otherwise isolated micro-communities, synchronizing growth cycles across the basin.

- **Pipe lurkers:**  
  A few lineages of predatory worms or fishlike forms have specialized in preying on the crawlers, grazers, and gliders. They are slow, solitary ambush hunters. They rarely harm infrastructure, because their own survival depends on the city’s metabolic continuity. Any individual that chews indiscriminately through membranes or cables is selected out; the rest learn to read the difference between living tissue that self-repairs and structural tissue whose damage leads to catastrophic flow changes.

These larger animals do not recognize humans as food. They recognize them as unpredictable disturbances. At the first vibration of fin strokes or suit-jets, they vanish into the packed complexity of the city’s crevices.

## 4. Co-inhabitance: how they share the city

All these beings—remnant humans, designed symbionts, opportunistic colonists, half-functional code—coexist not by treaty but by compatibility.

### 4.1. Communication and cues

- **Chemical language:**  
  Most life here speaks chemistry: exudates, gradients, traces.
  - Humans learn to “talk” in this medium in crude ways: by tweaking nutrient flows, changing filter media, in some cases seeding specific bacterial strains that release known volatiles when stressed.
  - Maintenance organisms use more refined signatures: a pipe-worm’s resin carries markers that repel other worms but attract certain grazers.
  - The ghost swarm, where it can, uses valves and flow adjustments as its “exhalations.”

- **Sound as pressure, not message:**  
  Acoustic signals exist—subsonic rumbles, pump-chords, the snap of settling structures—but they are rarely information-rich in the way speech is. Instead they are context: everyone here grows up inside the bass hum of the city’s slow heart. Deviations—the pitch bending up or down, the rhythm changing—are alarms.

- **Light as incidental:**  
  Bioluminescent organisms flash or glow mostly for internal regulation, not external display. The few signal beacons still active are more for the ghost swarm than for people. Humans do use them as landmarks, but they know better than to trust them; a lamp that has been steady for ten years can die without ceremony.

### 4.2. Mutual dependence

- Humans depend on:
  - The biofilms to clean air and water.
  - The designed crawlers to keep pipes and braces from clogging and collapsing.
  - The ghost swarm’s residual regulation to avoid fatal instabilities in temperature and pressure.

- The non-humans depend on:
  - Humans to re-route where catastrophic overloads develop, to shut off truly failing organs and thus prevent system-wide collapse.
  - Human discipline in resource use; shocking the system—dumping too much waste, turning on too-bright lights—could topple fine-tuned equilibria.

There is no central government enforcing this. Instead, long survival has bred an ethic: “Do nothing abrupt, change nothing you do not understand three ways, and always leave room for the city to answer.”

In practical terms this means:

- Repairs are done incrementally, watching how the environment responds between each small step.
- Experiments with new organisms are confined to sealed micro-habitats for years before any release.
- Every child learns to read structural stress and metabolic signs the way others learn to read the sky.

### 4.3. Territory

Territory here is less about horizontal maps and more about system ownership:

- The clan that maintains circulation considers any water-bearing pipe, however distant, to be “theirs”, but this overlaps with the thermal clan’s attachment to heat exchangers that share those pipes, and the breather clan’s prerogatives over filter matrices attached at junctions.

Conflicts are rare and quiet: disagreements over how much flow to allocate, whether to let a marginal organ decay or try to restore it. Their arguments often outlast their tempers; by the time consensus is reached, the system may already have tipped one way on its own.

The wild fauna do not respect these boundaries. A vault eel cares only for electro-signature landscapes. A mantle glider claims whatever membrane gives it the right micro-corrugation. But their movements are steady enough that, over time, humans see them as parts of systems rather than rivals for space.

## 5. The sense of “who lives here”

Summing them into a single picture, you could say:

- A small, cautious people, adapted to constant pressure and dimness, who no longer see the city as a tool but as an enclosing, necessary world-body they must not injure.
- A host of maintenance and metabolic entities—some made, some evolved—that knit together the city’s physical and chemical cycles, many of them indifferent to the humans, some indirectly protective.
- A diffuse, non-personal pattern of regulation, the ghost of old software and sensor meshes, no longer answerable to command but still pushing probabilities away from total failure.
- A thin sprinkling of larger animals that surf the gradients this system offers, amplifying signals and coupling distant niches without caring about the original industrial intent.

If you insist on a single inhabitant, you might say: the city itself lives here.

It has no brain, just a culture of humans, a swarm of algorithms, and a fabric of organisms acting as organs. It has no skin of its own, only patchworks of hull and growth. It knows only locally, but its local knowings overlap so densely that the whole basin functions as something that maintains itself, repairs itself, and—slowly, very slowly—reshapes itself in response to changes.

The humans inside it, when they use the old word “home,” no longer mean “a place we occupy.” They mean “the body we are part of.”  

They live in it the way cells live in a tissue: specialized, mortal, doing what they can so that the encompassing structure continues its long, warm, heavy existence at the bottom of the sea.
