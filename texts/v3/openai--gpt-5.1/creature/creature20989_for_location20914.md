They are people of the undersoil, but not quite like any you’ve met. If you tried to name them from above, you’d get it wrong: you’d call them burrowers, mole-folk, rat-people, cave dwellers. All those words lean too hard on one thing. Down here, they are not only diggers, not only keepers of beasts, not only artisans. They are, first, *inhabitants*—beings whose primary skill is to live densely within pressure and limit.

### Bodies Shaped by Nearness and Weight

They tend to be short by surface standards, but not tiny. Their height is the height of their rooms: enough to stand under a typical beam with a hand’s breadth to spare. The tallest sometimes stoop as they pass under older, settling ceilings.

Their limbs are compact and muscled more for endurance than for bursts of speed. They do not run far; there is nowhere to. Their legs are thick through the calf, sure-footed on slick planks and packed earth. Feet are broad and calloused, toes splayed enough to grip the uneven ground—often bare indoors, wrapped in tough, flexible hide for forays into wetter or colder chambers.

Hands are their finest tools. Fingers are long, slightly knotted at the joints, the nails thick and often blunted from constant scraping and prying. Some wear caps of leather or horn over their nails when working in stone. The back of the hand is furred lightly in many families, bare in others; hair grows where friction is lowest, worn thin where hands and arms meet the world repeatedly.

Their skin is the color of the soil they most often handle: a range of earthen tones, from dark loam-brown through reddish laterite to the pale, damp-wood hue of youth and those who tend water. Smoke and clay stain them into a largely uniform spectrum. You might catch glimpses of original complexion only at the inner arm, the belly, the soles of the feet.

Eyes are wide but not luminous. Down here, light is intermittent, stingy, patchwork. They see best in half-light: their pupils open broad and flat, iris color often difficult to distinguish—a muddy grey, a dim amber, a black-brown that merges with the pupil. In bright surface glare, they squint, tears streaming, but they are not blind; they are simply unpracticed.

Their noses are sensitive, nostrils flared and often smudged with dust. Smell tells them much that vision cannot: if a passage is stale or freshly disturbed, if the compost is turning properly, if an animal is ill. Ears tend to be slightly larger than surface norms, cupped close to the skull rather than sticking out, the better to read vibrations in the constant murmur of the hill.

Hair on their heads is worn practical: either close-cropped to keep out of lamps and gears and filth, or bound up in thick braids and knots that can be used to tuck away tools, charms, and small knife-sheaths. Children’s hair is looser, cottony with dust, often threaded with tiny bones or seeds as markers of stage and kin.

Breath comes deep and measured. The air here is warm and thick; lungs must be efficient. You’ll notice the way their chests expand slowly, how they can work in a smoky chamber for a long time with only short breaks by a vent. Their hearts are used to the slight heaviness that constant overhead weight imparts, a mental pressure as much as a physical one.

### Gait, Poise, and the Geometry of Movement

They move with a geometry tuned to tight spaces. In corridors, they rarely walk in the straight, confident line of surface dwellers. Instead, their paths arc and sway slightly, automatically compensating for floor undulation, for the sudden appearance of an animal underfoot, for a child tumbling out of an alcove. Hips and shoulders turn independently; the body is a series of hinges working in concert.

In a crowd, you notice hardly any collisions. They pass each other like water currents, twisting sideways through gaps, hands bracing against beams and walls without looking. This ease is learned early. Children are shepherded along main passages by touch and vocal cues until they know not only the shape of each room but the likely trajectory of everyone else within it.

Stillness here is rare, but when they are still, it is purposeful. Someone who stops in a passage steps almost automatically into a shallow niche, a thickened pillar, the mouth of some side-alcove, leaving the central currents free. Even rest is shaped by flow; they are always aware, unconsciously, of what they might be blocking.

### Faces, Markings, and the Patina of Place

At first glance, one person resembles the next: the same dust-roughened cheeks, smoke-etched brows, lamp-shadowed eyes. But look closer and each face is a map of work.

Those who tend near the cisterns have a softness to their skin, the fine smoothing of constant moisture and steam. Those who cut new tunnels carry a webwork of tiny scars along cheeks and forehead from rebounding chips of stone and earth. The compost-tenders’ hair and clothes are permanently scented with a sweet, sour warmth. Beast-handlers acquire the tusk-scratches and hoof-bruises that speak of unruly charges.

They mark themselves additionally in ways that echo tool marks on walls: small tattoos of parallel lines along the throat, dots along the cheekbones, hash-like symbols on the fingers. These are not grand, symbolic artworks, but practical records and affiliations—indicating which cluster of chambers someone sleeps in, what sequences of tasks they are trained in, which bloodline or foster-group they belong to.

Pigment for these markings comes from the place itself: red from iron-rich clay, black from lamp-soot, green from crushed fungi and herbs. Over time, sweat and abrasion blur the edges, just as carved wall-signs soften. Old people’s markings become shadows more than lines, a general darkening of certain areas rather than crisp images.

Some adornments are transient: a smear of white clay down the nose bridge before a long stint in heavy dust, to cool and protect; a narrow plait of hair bound with fresh plant fiber when someone is working near sacred roots; a loop of polished beetle shell on a wrist during communal rituals.

### Clothing: Between Tool and Sheath

Garments are minimal but meticulously functional. Loose, flammable sleeves and flowing hems are rare; space is too narrow, fire too constant, snagging hazards too many.

Typically, they wear:

- A base layer of soft, tightly-woven cloth or scraped, thinned leather wrapped around hips and upper thighs.
- A short tunic or vest, often sleeveless, with reinforced shoulders where buckets and poles will rest.
- Tool belts and bandoliers crossing chest and waist, bristling with pouches for small blades, awls, cord, hooks, and charms against accident.

Those who work in wetter sections wear skirts of overlapping leather scales, water-dark and flexible, that shed splashes and dry quickly. Roof-workers and climbers tie strips of rough cloth around knees and elbows for traction against beams and bare earth.

Color in clothing is secondary to texture. Most fabrics are stained the same dun of their surroundings. But weave and thickness tell you much: the dense, padded aprons of stone-shapers; the coarse, looser garments of compost-turners that can be washed easily; the fine, almost felt-like wraps of those who work with small seeds, insects, or medicines, where snagging would be disastrous.

### Social Weave: Clusters, Not Crowds

They do not think of themselves as a single, undifferentiated people of “the hill.” They think in clusters, in knots of chambers.

Each cluster is a **house-group**, though not in the surface sense of a single family dwelling. A house-group occupies a particular branching of chambers—sleeping niches, a shared hearth, a set of workspaces all fed by the same water channels and smoke vents. Members of a house-group may be related by blood, by adoption, or simply by long cohabitation and apprenticeship. What binds them is shared maintenance: if a beam above their sleeping alcoves sags, it is their duty to shore it. If their section’s sump fouls, they scrub it.

Names reflect this. A person’s full introduction might translate roughly as: “I am Rill-of-the-East-Fork-of-Three-Shaft-House,” identifying not only their small community but the direction and relation of that cluster within the larger warren. In daily speech, that contracts simply to Rill-Eastfork, or just Rill if context is clear.

Children belong simultaneously to several circles: born into one natal house-group, fostered at certain ages into another for specific skills (water-working, beast-keeping, stone-cutting), and initiated later into still other circles that cut across geography—ritual guilds, maintenance crews, watch-rotations.

Leadership is diffuse, embedded in practice. There are no thrones, no central hall of rulers. Instead, each vital system has its own respected coordinators:

- **Vent-keepers**, who know the network of air shafts and their temperaments.
- **Water-minders**, who track cistern levels, flows, and quality.
- **Weight-readers**, experienced eyes and hands who detect structural strain.
- **Cycle-tenders**, who oversee compost, fungi beds, and animal breeding.

Disputes rarely rise to a central authority. Most are negotiated at the level where they begin: which channel gets more seepage, which cluster may expand a wall. When conflicts spill beyond a house-group, representatives walk to a neutral chamber marked by old, soot-darkened stones and talk until the echoes dwindle and a compromise is hammered out.

### Intimacy and Privacy in Close Quarters

Crowding shapes their notions of solitude. To them, privacy is not about being unseen so much as being *unheard* or *unbothered*.

Sleeping niches are small, half-enclosed cells stacked along outer walls, curtained with layered leather and cloth. The barrier muffles sight and some sound but not all. Within, a person curls in a pocket of straw and woven matting that smells distinctly of them. Personal items—carved trinkets, a prized tool, a twist of dried herb—are tucked into cracks and ledges inside reach. This is as close as they come to a private room.

Tones of voice are a key courtesy. People lower to a murmur within a few body-lengths of closed curtains. Children are hushed not for shrieking itself, but for shrieking in the wrong corridor—the ones that pass through dense sleeping stacks or tense workspaces where attention must not be jarred.

Sex and affection are woven into this same fabric of sound considerations. Lovers will often meet in transitional spaces: small side-passages between clusters where traffic is thin and ambient noise from adjacent chambers provides a curtain. There, away from the primary flows, touches and murmurs lose themselves in the general hum. Nothing is absolutely secret, but the community practices a kind of chosen ignorance—hearing but not *listening*.

### Work as Life, Life as Work

Everyone who lives here contributes to the continual negotiation with matter. There is no clean division between “labor” and “home”: to inhabit this hill is to maintain it.

Children begin with light tasks: picking debris from drainage channels, sorting insects by type, carrying messages between house-groups. Their games train proprioception for tight spaces—a chase that must bow under each beam, a race that requires balancing along a single log, a form of hide-and-seek where success depends on knowing which alcove echoes least.

Adolescents are rotated through core domains:

- **Soil and structure**: learning to read wall-tone with knuckles, listening for hollow pockets, mixing clay and straw to the right consistency.
- **Water**: tracing channels, cleaning sumps, counting ladles at busy cisterns to estimate flow.
- **Beasts and insects**: understanding feeds, recognizing signs of stress, harvesting without collapsing a hive or stampeding a pen.
- **Fire and air**: tending braziers, managing smoke paths, recognizing bad air by feel in the throat and weight in the forearms.

From these rotations, affinities and aptitudes emerge. Some become consummate tunnelers, able to tell by the smell of a handful of earth what lies ahead. Others show the patience and delicacy required for breeding insects or nursing injured animals. A few are drawn toward the half-sacred tasks: aligning new light-shafts, tending root-gardens, marking the walls with talismans where strain is greatest.

Idleness is not admired, but rest *is* understood as maintenance of the self-as-tool. No one is expected to work to collapse; instead, rhythms of exertion and respite align with the place’s own flux. When a compost bed runs hot, turning is lighter for a few days until it cools. When a section of roof shows too many hairline cracks, all other tasks are postponed until bracing is done.

### Knowledge: Apprenticed, Memorized, Inscribed in the Hill

Their record-keeping is embedded in space more than in portable objects.

They do carve and etch, but not primarily for story in our sense. Grooves in beams at joints mark the season and reason they were placed. Series of dots near a cistern’s rim note how high water rose in past wet years. Low-relief patterns, like ripples or stacked teeth, mark passages prone to sudden slumping, or old fault-lines.

Oral recitation knits these markers into narrative. An elder will trace a finger along a softened carving and say, “Here, the wall fell when I was small because the vent above was choked with leaves. We changed the angle of the smoke-channel then; that’s why this beam is doubled.” Memory is always about cause and consequence, never simply date.

Songs carry layout. There are chants that name the sequence of rooms along crucial lifelines—the main water run, the quickest path between certain root-gardens, the safe detours should a corridor collapse. These are taught as work-songs, sung under breath while walking, until the pattern is owned by the body.

Myths, such as they have them, are not about distant gods or far-removed origins. They are about **shapes**. Stories of the time the hill “shrugged,” sending a wave through the tunnels that broke lamps but spared lives; of the season the roots pushed hard into one corridor and gentle into another; of the year an entire house-group convinced a tusked-beast herd to shift its resting pens, easing weight on an overstressed ceiling.

### Belief: Negotiation with the More-Than-Human

They are not atheistic in any pure sense, nor do they worship separate, sky-bound deities. Their attention is turned to the processes that most tangibly threaten or support them: weight, water, breath, rot, and the living threads of roots.

The closest things to gods here are **patterns** given names and addressed like persons:

- The **Down-Press**: the slow, omnipresent weight of overlying earth. Not imagined as malevolent, but as a huge, indifferent presence that can be coaxed, not commanded. Appeal to the Down-Press takes the form of respectful reinforcement: when a new beam is set, a bit of food or fat is smeared on its top side, where no eye will see, for the weight to “taste.”

- **First-Flow**: the initial seep of water into a new channel or cistern. When they open a freshet, they cup the first trickle into their palms and let it run through fingers, murmuring thanks not for abundance, but for cooperation.

- **Warm-Rot**: the heat and transformation within compost. It is both feared and honored; people approach living pits with careful speech and certain small gestures—a finger tapped three times on the rim before plunging a fork in, for instance—as if asking permission to disturb its work.

- **Root-Mind**: not a single central tree, but the sense of the interwoven roots from many surface plants as a kind of distributed intelligence. The polished root-pillars and braided root-walls are its “faces.” Offerings laid there are less about asking favors than about acknowledging shared structure: their architecture and the plants’ architecture occupying the same volume.

Talismans at creaking joints or temperature-shifting junctions are like annotations to these negotiations. A pendant of beetle shell and bone tied at a ceiling crack says: “We have seen this. We will watch you. We ask you not to hurry.” A bundle of dried moss over a sump says: “Here we invite only clean seepage. We give you this softness; give us water that will not sicken the beasts.”

Rituals are tightly intertwined with maintenance. A “ceremony” for opening a new vent shaft is, outwardly, simply the careful, communal checking of air flow with smoke, the gentle widening of its mouth, the placing of a ring of certain herbs to catch first light. A “festival” might coincide with the full clearing of all main drainage channels at the turn of a season, followed by a larger-than-usual sharing of stew from beasts and vegetables fattened on the previous cycle’s compost.

### Food and Eating: Layered Diet, Layered Etiquette

Their diet is a cross-section of everything that can be coaxed from this constrained ecology.

From the **tusked herbivores**, they take meat sparingly. These beasts are more valuable alive, for dung and labor. Kills happen primarily when an animal is injured beyond recovery or during ritual culls to keep numbers in balance. Every part is used: meat for stew; fat for lamps and cooking; hide for clothing and curtains; bone for tools.

From the **climbing mammals**, they harvest fur and, occasionally, meat, though more often they are tolerated thieves and cleaners, kept in a useful tension between wild and tamed.

From the **burrowers**, they gain turned soil and, in lean times, meat. Burrowers are rarely killed; they are more useful alive. Some of their tunneling is directed deliberately, by inserting and closing barriers in their pens—shaping their blind work to aerate specific beds.

Insects and larvae provide steady protein. Clay trays of fat larvae are like moving loaves. When scooped out, they are roasted on flat stones, their outer casings crisping into a nutty crunch. Beetles, bees, and other winged insects are eaten, traded, or used ornamentally.

The **garden troughs** yield hardy greens, small fruits, and herbs. Fungi grown on root-walls and in carefully prepared dark rooms round out the diet with dense, earthy textures.

Meals are communal by default. People eat in loose circles near work-sites or in wide chambers that straddle multiple house-groups. Bowls pass from hand to hand, each person dipping with shared or personal spoons. Eating quickly is not rude—time is often tight—but eating messily *is*, as spilled food attracts scavengers and stains working surfaces.

There is a quiet etiquette around water. To drink deeply from a cistern trickle is an act of trust that there will be enough, allowed perhaps to laborers fresh from heavy digging or to nursing parents, but unusual otherwise. Most people sip from shared vessels, taking no more than two or three swallows before passing on.

Feasts, when they occur, look like expansions of everyday meals rather than something entirely separate: more meat in the stew, more honey from bees, more dried fruits brought up or down from surface excursions. Songs lengthen; stories of past collapses averted or clever reroutings of water are retold with driftwood-branching embellishments.

### Animals in Relationship: More Than Livestock, Less Than Kin

The creatures that share this place are not just stock. They are co-workers, competitors, and often, characters in shared narrative.

Children know the individual temperaments of the main tusked beasts: this one startles at echoes, that one nudges at rope knots with clever lips. They name them not as pets, but as ongoing presences—“Slab-Shoulder,” “Old-Crack-Ear,” “Two-Tusk,” names that will outlast each creature as successors are given similar monikers.

Climbing mammals are sometimes half-adopted by particular house-groups. A bold, clever climber that regularly raids one cluster’s hanging baskets might, over time, be lured into a semi-domesticated pattern: rewarded with specific scraps at specific times, collared loosely so that bells warn of its darting approach. Children throw play-stones at their shadows, testing reflexes.

Predatory cats are almost never collared. They are allowed their routes, known and marked: here a notch in a beam where a certain sleek, grey cat always jumps, there a favored high ledge where another sleeps. People give them right of way in narrow spots. In return, cats focus their hunting on the surging reservoirs of pests, thinning populations that would otherwise crash their stored food cycles.

Insects are both livestock and wild resource. Bee-hollows are placed explicitly, but wild colonies sometimes appear uninvited in cracks. The people rarely destroy these; instead, they either tame them with smoke and offerings or mark the area as off-limits except to specialized keepers.

There is an undercurrent of myth that sees in certain animals a kind of parallel clan. The burrowers, especially, are joked of as “the other folk,” who dig without asking but still, somehow, maintain balance. A child who is particularly quiet and deep-thinking might be complimented as “burrower-minded”—a suggestion that they understand slow, unseen work.

### Their Sense of Above: The Surface as Elsewhere

To them, the surface is not mythic, but it is *other*. Few go up often.

Those who do—call them **Up-Goers**—are a small, specialized cadre. They know the camouflage trickery that hides vent mouths. They understand weather enough to time outings when sudden rain will not turn shafts into drowning funnels, or when wind will not reverse smoke flows catastrophically.

Up-Goers are thinner, generally, with more sun-hardened skin. Their eyes squint more at interior light shafts, used as they are to unbroken brightness and sky. They bring back things the under-hill folk can’t produce: chunks of resinous wood, fresh leafy cuttings to re-root in gardens, bits of bright stone or bone picked from the open ground.

Stories about the surface emphasize its *openness* as dangerous. Children are told of “falling upward,” not downward: step out of a shaft too suddenly, and the absence of ceiling can unmoor you. Sun is described as a kind of constant lamp so bright that it blurs shadows into meaninglessness—how can you read the world without shadows? Rain is viewed almost as an animal: a periodic sky-creature that pours water unpredictably and can drown vents if not outsmarted.

Still, they depend on the surface. Without the slow intake of plant matter and the infiltration of roots, without periodic culling of surface herds and scavenging of fallen wood and carcasses, their loops would close too tightly and stifle. Up-Goers are respected, but also watched: they bring news of changes above—trees cut, flood-plains shifting, new predators or two-legged rivals moving in.

Some few, whether by temperament or birth quirk, yearn upward more strongly. They are restless in the gently curved ceilings, drawn to the alien verticality of trunks, to wind unhindered by walls. Some leave entirely, climbing and not returning. There is no taboo against this, though it is spoken of as a kind of death. They are “given to the open,” names loosened like talismans left at an old crack.

### Time and Change: Their History Written as Subsidence

These people have lived under this hill long enough that no living tongue remembers the first chisels. Their history is encoded not in distant ancestors’ feats, but in the sequence of reinforcements and collapses that shaped the current warren.

Ask about “long ago” and someone will take you to a certain braced arch and tap the beams, saying: “Before my mother’s mother, this was a raw cut, and the roof drooped. It was shored then, and the tusk-herd was moved two chambers inward to ease the load. We still see the slant if you look at how the water pools.”

New expansions are debated harshly. Space is always tempting—a new storage room, another sleep-niche—but every void weakens something. Proposals circulate: so-and-so wants to dig a new spur from the eastern corridor; the weight-readers argue that without a cross-brace new cracks will spider into the root-garden wall. These arguments can last weeks, measured in how much dust accumulates in certain corners from other, ongoing maintenance.

Change comes in pulses following outside events: a season of heavy rain that saturates soils and changes seep patterns; a dry stretch that shrinks roots and loosens grip; a distant tremor that sends fine dust raining from ceilings. After such events, all schedules shift. Children are pulled from insect-sorting to join bucket-lines, hauling clay to pack into fresh gaps. House-groups temporarily blur as everyone moves to shore up shared vital arteries.

They know they are living in a palimpsest. When they cut into an old wall, they often uncover earlier work just behind: an obsolete sump, now dry and repurposed as a storage hollow; the bones of an older tusk-beast, used once as a foundation; a forgotten talisman crumbling in a pocket of earth. Such finds are treated as messages, not relics—evidence of previous solutions, hints at past mistakes.

### Personality in the Crowd: How They Are, Individually

In a place defined by systems, it is easy to imagine they are all alike, but individuals stand out.

You meet the **Laugher-at-Cracks**, an elder who responds to any new fissure with a bark of laughter before turning, serious, to the work of bracing. Their humor is a shield against the ever-present possibility of failure, and also a training tool—teaching apprentices that panic wastes air.

You meet the **Quiet Counter** who stands at a busy cistern, eyes half-closed, head tilting slightly with each filled bucket. When asked how the water stands, they reply with a number not of measures you know, but of *uses left*: “Enough for the next nine shifts, if the tusk-beasts drink from the slow sump behind Sleep-West.”

You meet a **Root-Singer**, whose body hums softly whenever they are near the braided roots, who seems to taste changes in sap-flow in the back of their throat. Whether this is superstition, rare sensitivity, or simply deep practice is impossible to sort from outside.

You meet children whose favorite game is to lie still in a silent corner and see who can discern the most layers of sound: the scraping of tusks against a trough, the drip three rooms over, the faint sigh of a vent drawing, the suggestion of a distant cat’s leap landing somewhere above. They grow into adults whose situational awareness is so complete they navigate, work, and chat all at once without seeming to look at anything directly.

### How They Relate to You

If you enter as an outsider, your presence does not go unnoticed; nothing goes unnoticed here for long. You are another dynamic load, a new source of vibration and heat.

They will read you first by how you move. Do you blunder straight down the center of a corridor, ignoring the sway of traffic? Do you reach instinctively for walls to steady yourself when the floor undulates? Do you duck too late under a beam, cracking your head and startling nearby beasts? Your body-language will say more to them than your title or clothing.

If you show curiosity about the place itself—pausing to feel a beam’s grain, kneeling by a drainage channel to follow its path, lowering your voice near sleep-curtains—they will mark you as someone with potential to learn their logic. Someone will sidle up and correct you gently when you try to drink too deeply at the wrong sump, or stand too long in a flow-space. A child may emerge from a niche just to show you, with quick proud hands, the best way to tuck a tool into your belt so it doesn’t catch on netting.

If you speak of the surface with awe, they will nod, tolerant but slightly bemused, the way a sailor might look at a landlocked friend extolling mountains—impressive, dangerous, but not home. If you speak of open, empty rooms as a luxury, they may glance at each other, eyes flicking to their crowded shelves and sleeping-walls, wondering quietly what one would *do* with emptiness.

### What Lives Here, Finally

So who lives under the hill?

A people whose bodies and minds are formed by **constraint**: by the continuous proximity of others, by the constant, slow negotiation with weight and seepage. They are artisans of necessity, reading minute signs in wood and water as others might read weather or stars.

They share their home with beasts, insects, roots, fungi, all woven into a deliberate ecology where waste is a verb, not a noun—as in, *to waste* is to fail to feed some cycle. Their lives are intimately entangled: they smell of their charges and their compost pits, their fingers remember old cracks and new reinforcements.

They believe—though they might not use that word—in a world where the forces that could crush them are not enemies but massive, insensitive neighbors, to be courted, appeased, and compensated for with craft. Their religion is maintenance; their liturgy is the sequence of tasks that keeps water clean, air breathable, beams sound.

They are not a lost or degenerate branch of some surface culture. They are fully themselves: people for whom horizon is ceiling and distance is measured not in miles but in **junctions**—this corner where sound bends, that passage where air cools. To live here is to be continually aware of the weight above, the slow creep of mold and root and rot and repair.

Under the hill, the world is hollow and busy because *they* are busy: always patching, re-routing, feeding, harvesting, listening. Their genius is not in grand inventions, but in the seamless integration of a thousand small practices into one sustained, inhabited pressure-chamber of life.
