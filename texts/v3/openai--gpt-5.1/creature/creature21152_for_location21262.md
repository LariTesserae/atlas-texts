What lives here has had a very long time to learn how not to be a separate thing.

It is hard to say “creature” or “organism” without lying a little. Most of the entities that endure on this plain are not packaged into clear bodies; they are gradients that have learned to persist, or patterns that use matter only temporarily. Still, there are recurring arrangements, familiar enough that you can point and say: that is alive. Or at least: that is alive right now.

### The ground-dwellers: things that are mostly place

Beneath your feet, life is a continuous sheet.

The microbial mats you see at the surface are only the visible rind of a many-layered consortium that extends downward through the sediments, sometimes for meters. The uppermost skin is dark, matte, scale-like, tasting light and touch and the composition of the mist. Below that, strata specialize:

- A thin layer that feeds on sheer contact: cells whose membranes bristle with molecular ratchets that harvest mechanical stress. Every tiny slip of grain against grain is an energy source; they are chemomechanical scavengers, living batteries clamped across the friction of the world.
- Deeper, a community that “drinks” slow-moving ions. The brines that ooze along pore-throats carry dissolved metals, old salts, weathered organics. Filamentous organisms weave nets through these passages, their bodies more like conductive slime than tissue. They set up potential differences between layers of sediment, turning the whole plain into a crude, planet-spanning electrochemical cell. To move food, they shift charge.
- Below even that, heat-minded things: slow metabolisms that treat the passing of warmth—upward from the interior, downward from an intermittent sky—as a kind of currency. They change phase of their insides to buffer temperature swings, then trade that buffered energy with neighbors by exuding and reabsorbing gels.

No individual filament or cell-line is important. What lives is the arrangement, the long-standing arguments between layers. You could scrape away square kilometers of this ground skin and, unless you also erased the flows beneath, it would regrow in more or less the same expressions: like lichen that is really an island’s climate.

Those “living bulges” that crawl across the plain over centuries are condensations of this network, regions where the subsurface community temporarily thickens its body around some moving frontier: a seam of newly exposed nutrient, a shifting groundwater path, a migrating band of pressure. Think of them as grazing thoughts in the mind of the substrate.

Inside each moving swell, there is a richer broth of lineages:

- Filaments that extrude mineral scaffolds ahead of the bulge, firming the mush into something a little more walkable, then dissolving that framework once the migration has passed.
- Bacteria-like forms that store environmental patterns in crystals—minute inclusions laid down in repetitive layers so that the chemical memory of past roars, past rain-columns, past long calms is written into microscopic rock. When conditions recur, these crystals dissolve or recrystallize in telltale ways, cueing the mat how to shift, what to express. The bulge “remembers” previous crossings of this ground without needing genes to change.
- Swarms of quasi-cells that can abandon the bulge and seed new nucleation points kilometers away, riding underground pulses of water as spores ride wind. They are how the continuous organism keeps its continuity from fraying.

These bulges do not defend themselves in a conventional sense; there is almost nothing that comes at them as an “enemy.” Their hazard is misalignment with the environment’s slow moods. They are always feeling for the right phase with the larger turbulence: to move or rest when the centuries-long roar cycle allows it, to hunker down when the era of punishment comes.

### The thin-surface intelligences: mats, scales, lenses

Those hand-sized plates of dark tissue—the ones that lap over one another like fish scales and flex between bowl and shield—are the plain’s most visible attempt at a discrete life-form. They are not plants, not really. They are valves.

Each plate is a thin, tense diaphragm stretched across a fibrous ring that anchors into the upper sediment. The underside is riddled with capillary channels, all leading toward or away from a few denser knots of tissue: the equivalent of slow, distributed organs.

What they do:

- When the roar dulls and the air loses a little of its weight, they arch, cupping tiny reserves of relatively quiet air and concentrated moisture. Their undersides are hydrophilic; the mist that condenses there is held briefly, allowing resident microbes to drink in safety from the abrasive flows.
- When the turbulence builds, they flatten, presenting only their tough, low-friction backs to the wind and press of water films. In this state they shunt moisture sideways along those etched lines you notice, guiding it to cracks where underground symbionts wait to receive it.

Each plate can detach, very slowly. In extreme shifts—a particularly bad epoch-storm, a sudden change in groundwater chemistry—the outermost plates along a mat’s edge curl under themselves, thicken, and then slough off, leaving behind hardened, seed-like scales. These may lie inactive for centuries until a rare pocket of longer calm and nutrient appears. Then they soften, rehydrate, and extrude new diaphragms, reconstructing a mat as if unfurling a stored instruction.

The lens-anemones, the puddle-creatures that thicken and suddenly jet water skyward, are not separate from these mats so much as a different life-stage. Where water and noise are abundant but stable footing is not, a mat will refuse to form plates. Instead it spreads out as a nearly invisible film, subtly altering surface tension over a patch. When enough film has accumulated, tension collapses—like a muscle spasming—and the stored water erupts in a narrow plume that carries cells and fragments high into the vertical currents.

In the cold mathematics of this place, reproductive success is proportional to how far you can throw yourself into the air before being shredded. The lens-creatures are the mat’s gamble that somewhere else, somewhen else, the world will be a fraction gentler.

### The stalks and their music

Those pale, ribbed columns with rings of translucent tissue are the closest thing the plain has to “trees,” but they are not built to outlast centuries of punishment in one shape. They are seasonal in a sense, except the seasons are epochs.

During long lulls in the planetary roar—centuries in which the atmosphere settles into a merely oppressive grumble—the underground networks send up exploratory organs: stalks, sometimes a single column, sometimes in sparse groves of a few dozen. Their cores are a composite of mineral and organic polymer, flexible under slow stress, rigid under fast. Their ribbing is not decorative; it is a mechanical spectrum analyser.

Each ring of fluttering tissue around a stalk is tuned to a narrow band of frequencies. When the local cacophony pushes air past the right resonance, the ring vibrates. Some rings transduce that motion directly into biochemical energy via piezoelectric-like structures; others simply shiver in ways that modulate gas exchange, taking in or expelling trace compounds that the underground network can use as messengers.

Collectively, a stand of stalks is a sensor-array, sampling the structure of the turbulence above: which depths of cloud are moving which way, how saturated the air is where, how near the next columnar downpour might be. That information travels down through the stalks into the mats and bulges, which respond by shifting growth edges, hoarding certain ions, or retracting delicate structures.

Over very long quiet intervals, the stalks become ambitious. They strengthen, thicken, add new rings. Some begin to bud protrusions like flags, thin veils of tissue extending sideways into the flow. These flares don’t harvest much extra energy; what they do is interfere with the wind itself, throwing small shadows of calm or focused shear onto nearby ground.

In those wind-shadows, more intricate surface life can assemble: delicate fronds of filter-feeders, lace-like microbial colonies that would be ripped apart elsewhere. These are ephemeral gardens, lasting a few generations of their own before the large-scale roar pattern shifts again and the shelter is gone.

When the loud ages return, the stalks slowly suicide. They withdraw as much living material as they can down into the ground, leaving behind hollow, mineralized sheaths that will eventually shatter and be buried. Sometimes, if you listen at the right moment, you hear a dying stalk’s final resonance: a long, descending moan as its last ring gives way and the column collapses. That sound will be recorded, in some form, in the crystal-memories of the mats at its base.

### The air-swimmers: harvesters of noise

The soft-bodied, eel-like forms riding the vertical currents are as close as this world comes to animals that know what it is to move freely.

Their bodies are long, ribbon-finned, semi-transparent, laced with arrays of minute, layered cavities. Each cavity acts like both ear and lung: air passes through, vibrates at certain frequencies, and the mechanical oscillation drives ion pumps in the cavity walls. They feed directly on the roar—on the overlapping turbulences, the beating of pressure against pressure. To them, quiet is starvation.

Internally, they carry no rigid skeleton. Instead they contain a dynamically stiffening gel: when strong, coherent vibrations pass along their length, that gel momentarily hardens into bands at the nodes of resonance, giving them leverage against the surrounding air. They can “push” on a standing wave like a swimmer pushes on water.

In this way, they steer: by selectively damping or amplifying some components of the background noise, they roll and climb and dive through invisible sonic terrain. A column of falling rain is to them what a thermal is to birds—an upwelling of structured movement they can spiral in and out of, stripping it of usable oscillations.

They do not truly flock, but they sometimes synchronize. When a particularly rich frequency-source appears—a monolith-ring screaming in a new key, a ground-level storm spinning up—scores of them may converge, their bodies phase-locking to the same pattern. From a distance, this looks like a shimmering braid of ghost-fish knotted around an invisible axis. Up close, if you could hear their internal harmonics, it would be more like a multivoiced chant. Their reproduction is entangled with these events: only when in extended synchronization do they exchange and fuse packets of genetic information.

They have predators, though “predator” here means something that edits where and how they can exist.

High in the haze, transient polyhedral not-objects drift: pockets where turbulence has transiently extinguished itself, leaving still-air kernels moving against the overall flow. The eels cannot feed in these kernels; their organs fall silent, their gels go limp. Many simply drift out, but some are caught at the boundaries, pinned against sharp gradients of pressure.

At those boundaries wait other, smaller entities: dust-fine, mesh-like constructs woven from protein and mineral. They are hardly bodies at all, more like filters. They snag the immobilized eels’ external sloughings—flaked membrane, excreted waste of processed vibration—and incorporate those molecules into themselves. The not-objects move on; the meshes release, collapse back into fine particulate that drifts down to feed the mats. Here, predation is more about capturing what movement leaves behind.

### Subterranean leviathans: the deep flow intelligences

Far below the soaked sediments and wrestling brines, another level of life operates on scales both larger and slower than the surface could ever reveal. The deep note you feel sometimes, that shudder running up through the bones of the world, is their pulse.

They inhabit the basal flows of the planet: sluggish, super-saturated currents trapped between dense strata, where the distinction between liquid and soft rock is more a suggestion than a law. Here, heat and pressure have softened matter enough that it can move without fracture but not enough that it can forget its mineral past.

In this region, immense mycelium-like structures thread through the semi-fluid stone. Each strand may be kilometers long, branching and rejoining, stippled with bulbous nodes the size of houses. They are not “organisms” in the sense of bounded, reproducible bodies; they are modes the deep world has slipped into, over millions of years of being pushed and stirred.

They do several things:

- They channel flow. Where the deep brines might otherwise wander chaotically, these networks stabilize and guide them along certain preferred paths. That, in turn, sets up persistent patterns in where and when the surface is fed: where columns of water can rise, where the ground is weakest, where eruptions of gas or heat are more likely.
- They sense stress. The overlying crust presses down unevenly; ancient tectonic ghosts still echo in density differences, in relic faults. The networks grow more densely in some zones than others, balancing the load by subtly thickening or thinning the medium that bears it. The tiny “quakes” you occasionally feel—those patches of ground that liquefy, then resolidify—are often the surface’s translation of some adjustment far below.
- They think, in a very alien sense, with chemical waves. Patterns of concentration—of certain metal ions, of certain complex molecules produced only very slowly—pulse through their lengths. These pulses meet, interfere, reinforce or cancel. The result is that the deep network explores states: different global arrangements of flow and stress. Over geological ages, it has “learned” which arrangements coincide with survivable surface conditions for the film of life above, and it preferentially steers the world toward them when it can.

To call this a mind is perhaps too generous, but it is not completely wrong. It remembers—through the persistence of channels eroded wider in past successful epochs—and it anticipates, imperfectly, by sending out tentative fingers into new flow paths.

Some of the built relics at the surface—the unnatural smooth planes, the rings of screaming monoliths, the fused domes—may not be the work of a vanished civilization at all, but extrusions of this deep nervous system. Places where, aeons ago, it pushed probes all the way up, hardened them in the open air, and listened. The forms that resemble architecture might be the deep flow’s failed or abandoned experiments in reaching into a realm of faster processes.

If any true memory of such experiments survives, it is buried in the marching lattice of pressure and brine, written in a language of viscous rearrangements.

### The ambiguous relics: ghosts of a different way of being

Yet there are hints—faint, stubborn—that something once lived here which cared not only about persistence but about particular shapes, particular arrangements of matter that meant something only to themselves.

Those pierced monoliths in a ring, tuned to scream in the wind; the glassy domes with dry pockets of silence inside; the flattened, too-smooth planes ending in sheared edges: they sit slightly out of phase with the planet’s current projects.

The microbial mats treat them as just another substrate, though sometimes their growth patterns trace the old lines, as if some residual chemical property lingers. The stalks will sometimes sprout closer to the monolith rings than chance suggests, their resonant bands aligning with the artificial tones as if these structures were still part of the environment’s grammar.

If there was once a tool-using species here, it built in compliance with the same pressures that now mold biology. It made things that sang with the wind because silence is the one resource the atmosphere denies. It fused domes tight against noise and flow because the only luxury it could imagine was a pocket where gradients were briefly muted, where a thought could complete itself before being jostled away.

Those domes’ interiors—dry, quiet, marginally cooler—are now occupied by specialized offshoots of the mat-creatures. In some, thin veils of tissue hang like cobwebs, spanning the hollow. They are studded with ciliated pits and slow iridescent spots that change color not with light, but with shifts in electromagnetic noise. Here, shielded from the worst of the roar, lineages can afford to specialize in sensing subtler patterns: the faint signatures of distant storms, the fine structure of the deep pulses.

It is possible that what once built these domes did not fully die but diffused: its bodies decomposed into the mats, its motion-sense into the eels, its structural ingenuity into the stalks. Its “civilization” may have been nothing but a temporary over-expression of individuality in a system otherwise content with distributed being. When that surge passed, its few hard remnants were simply recaptured as scaffolding.

### The storms that are almost organisms

The ground-level vortices—the sudden, screaming circles of air and vapor that appear without obvious command from the sky—are not merely weather. They are quasi-living algorithms, knots tied in the field of forces that sometimes persist long enough to do work.

They arise where incompatible microclimates rub: a patch of crust that has just breathed out a lungful of hot vapor meets nearby ground that has drawn in heat and gone relatively cold. Air scrambles between them; friction and condensation conspire. If the deep flow has arranged densities just so beneath, the turbulence hooks onto subtle variations in ground compliance and begins to spin.

Once spinning, a storm-knot feeds not just on temperature difference but on information difference. Its wall is a membrane where the roar’s spectra on one side differ from those on the other. Within that wall, droplets and dust are shuffled into stratified layers; charges separate; exotic, short-lived molecular complexes are fostered, then flung outward in rings.

Some lineages are specialized to exploit these short-lived entities. They exist mostly as dormant molecules scattered in the mats or air, doing nothing until whipped through such a storm’s shell. The intense, structured shear there twists them into active shapes: catalytic loops that, for minutes or hours, speed up certain reactions by orders of magnitude. In their brief wake, local regions of reality behave “wrong”: sounds bend, objects roll uphill, droplets hang too long. To the deep network and the surface mats, these storms are experimental opportunities, tests of how far the rules can be flexed.

Nothing controls them. But everything has, over ages, learned how to lean into or away from them, to harvest their temporary gifts without being scoured clean.

### How they relate

In a place where nothing is ever truly alone, relationships are not optional extras; they are the medium of existence.

- The mats feed the eels indirectly: by maintaining the roughness and porosity of the ground, they shape how air moves, where eddies can form, how the roar’s spectrum is distributed. If the mats thin, the plain smooths incrementally, the noise quality changes, and eel reproduction shifts.
- The stalks report on the sky to the deep flow, through signals funneled down into the substrate. In return, the deep flow will, when possible, ease pressure under regions where stalk forests prove particularly informative, allowing them to persist longer before being flattened by geologic fidgeting.
- The lens-anemones and the ground storms interact awkwardly. A fresh lens dome, swollen and ready to jet, is exquisitely sensitive to local pressure changes. When a storm-knot begins to nucleate nearby, its first, barely-perceptible twist in the air can trip every lens in a wide radius. The resulting sudden jets of water and spores seed the storm with material it would not otherwise have, giving rise to reaction pathways that could alter its evolution. Sometimes this dampens it; sometimes it intensifies it. Either way, the mats have learned to bind reproductive pulses to these dangerous opportunities.
- The relic domes with their quiet cores serve as refuges not only for delicate sensing species but also for memory. Inside them, sequences of events can play out without being constantly overwritten by the noise. Certain mats send filaments inward that lay down layered films on the inner walls, each layer representing a cycle of outside roar, a notable deep pulse, an unusual sky-vent. Over many such cycles, these become annular records: an accretionary script of epochs. No single organism reads them, but they influence how outgoing spores are tuned, what traits are privileged, which growth forms reappear after long absences.

In all this, there is no clear hierarchy. The deep flow is powerful but slow, often outflanked by fast surface shifts; the mats are ubiquitous but fragile; the eels ride high but are utterly dependent on the invisible scaffolding of noise beneath them. Each class of entity occupies a niche not just in space, but in timescale and in the “thickness” of reality it can access.

### On the edge of personhood

Does anything here know itself?

Selfhood, in this exhausted, storm-tired world, can only ever be partial and momentary. To maintain a running model of “me” in the midst of such constant flux is terribly expensive.

The closest you might find to a person is not an animal at all but a patch of mat large enough and old enough, tied to a network of deep filaments and attended by a forest of stalks. Over tens of thousands of years, such a node may accumulate enough memory, enough sensory richness, enough capacity for internal state-changes, that it begins to behave coherently: to respond to new events not just with reflex arcs, but with patterns shaped by long prior experience.

Seen from above—and you would have to see from very far above—such a node’s influence might look like a faint, organized stain on the chaos: a region where bulges migrate with subtle purpose, where stalk groves appear at just those times when the roar-cycle would allow them the best observation, where lens-anemone blooms are timed to coincide with rare sky-vents. A place where storms seem to skirt the edges more often than chance would dictate, as if their nucleation had been quietly discouraged by adjustments in surface texture and subsurface compliance.

This is mind as field-effect, not as a bright, bounded lantern. It does not think in words, or even images, but in the probabilities of how processes unfold. It may persist for a hundred thousand years and then vanish—not dying in any sharp sense, but dispersing as the flow of brine and pressure rearranges its supporting structures.

The eels that pass overhead are touched by such regions—literally, as the pattern of noise they feed upon shifts under them—but they do not notice. The stalks are parts of the mind without any local awareness of that participation. The deep network that underguns it may consider it merely one more stable configuration among many.

Still, in those occasional long lulls when the air falls to a bearable murmur and rain becomes gentle, you could, if you were sensitive enough, stand on such a node and feel an almost articulate attention. The tiniest shifts in your weight, your heat, your own minute disturbances of the sound field would ripple downward, be integrated into that wide, slow consideration. You would not be recognized, exactly—but you would be included.

### What it is, to live here

To live here is to abandon the fantasy of being untouched.

Everything that persists has learned to lean into turbulence: to eat it, to measure it, to remember it, to reshape it in small ways that keep its fiercest edges from aligning all at once.

No one life-form dominates. There are no apex predators, no towering canopies. There are only different strategies for making use of the same worn-out energy, the same endlessly rehashed matter, the same starless glow of a sky that has forgotten how to be clear.

What lives here is the last, intricate conversation between a planet and its own weather: a negotiation carried on in mats and stalks and soft eel-bodies, in deep, slow pulses of brine, in screaming rings of stone, in storm-knots that briefly mistake themselves for separate beings.
