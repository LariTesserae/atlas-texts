They are not alive in any way we would first recognize, and yet they are nothing but life as this place can currently manage: slow, dry, conductive awakenings of matter.

They begin as the dust does.

Down on the ledges where the electrostatic mats creep, there are patches that behave oddly even by the standards of that oddness. Most mats are surface phenomena—two-dimensional, flowing in response to fields, following gradients of mineral vapor and charge. But some of them, in certain favored cracks, begin to stack.

Tiny grains, all but invisible in the murk, find themselves more often than chance in particular micro-basins of stress and electric potential: at the lip of a faintly glowing seam, along the shoulder of a fracture that flexes with a distinctive rhythm. The grains that collect there are not uniform—they are a mix of silicates, metal flecks, evaporites from the outgassing—but the field patterns sort them. Particles with the right dielectric constants, the right capacities to hold and shift charge, remain; the rest are quietly nudged away.

Layer by layer, they build.

At first it is just a thicker mat: a slightly raised pad of dust a centimeter high, then two, then ten. But within that thickness, the rules stop being those of a sheet and become those of a volume. The stacked grains, under the influence of fluctuating fields from below and the periodic crackling arcs from above, self-organize into narrow filaments and planes: micro-lattices of alternate conductivity, like rough analogs of nerve fibers and insulating tissue woven out of crystal and metal.

You would not see this directly. From the distance of a human body standing on a nearby ledge, it is only a darker smudge on the stone, with a faintly cleaner outline than its neighbors. But when one of the long, taut electrical threads flickers overhead and the field spikes, that smudge shimmers differently. A wave of reorientation passes across it—not just individual grains flipping, but entire substructures in its depth changing state together.

These are the earliest of them: planar, sessile, aware only in the sense that they register and remember patterns of field, heat, and strain. They are not yet “beings” so much as regions where the planet is beginning to store information about itself in a durable, reconfigurable way.

They grow in cycles.

A crack below brightens for an hour, then dims. The mat-thickening pad above it experiences a particular rising-and-falling sequence of fields. The micro-lattice within it slightly reconfigures—edges dissolve, new channels open. A week later, when a different fault flares and throws a similar but not identical pattern across that ledge, the pad responds more swiftly, more coherently; it has, in a primitive material sense, been trained.

Not everything that reaches this state persists. Most accumulations are broken apart when the ledge shears, scattered by a reversal in charge, or simply buried under a rockfall from above. But a few survive long enough to become complicated.

Complication, here, does not look like the branching of plants or the locomotion of animals. It looks like depth.

On cooled shelves halfway down the larger chasms, where the internal glow is stronger and more consistent, there are dark, low humps that bulge from the stone like frozen waves. They are made of the same dust-conglomerate as the mats, but thickened to meters, fused into a porous, spongy rock shot through with glittering threads. These threads connect to the mineral “frost” extrusions on the walls above, to patches of crystal dust along the floor, to faintly luminous gas films that cling to ceilings. The hump is a node; the other structures are its antennae.

Inside, there are countless tiny voids left by outgassing bubbles, cooling fractures, and differential shrinkage. The dust-aggregates fill these voids in labyrinthine fashion, forming an interior like the circuitry of some alien, analog computer—no straight traces, no manufactured regularity, but dense, looping networks that can hold charge on different timescales. Some domains flicker with the quick pulses of the overhead discharges; others slowly integrate the long roll of deep tectonic stress.

They are not “brains,” but they are systems of feedback and delay, input and persistence. A particularly favorable node might sit on the same shelf for centuries, reshaped only slowly by the flexing of the rock beneath. Over that time, its internal pathways become refined by the repeated patterns of its environment: the regular rise and fall of heat from below, the statistically frequent angles at which spires above discharge, the characteristic tremor that precedes a column-shear far down the chasm.

It begins to anticipate. Not in thought, but in behavior.

Moments before a known pattern completes—before the usual sequence of microquakes culminates in a rockfall, for example—specific domains within the node align in preparation, subtly reweighting their charges to ride out the surge with minimal disarray. After enough repetitions, the node’s reaction ceases to be merely passive response and becomes a kind of expectation coded into stone and dust.

From the outside, this is invisible. From the inside—the inside of the material pattern—the world is gradually sorting itself into signal and noise.

The monoliths and squat, curved structures perched on saddles and outcrops are part of the same story, but much older.

They are not buildings in the sense of shelters or machines. They are petrified, perfected examples of whatever process the dust-humps have only recently begun to reenact.

Long before this current upheaval, when this region’s crust was flatter, cooler, and the atmosphere denser, similar feedback patterns had time and stability to run far further. Then, too, there were mineral flows guided by fields; then, too, there were ledges where gas veins exhaled steadily, where charge from the sky braided itself with charge from below. Across millions of years, those conditions produced structures of astonishing regularity: self-grown, self-calibrated condensations of pattern—what we, overly quick to see ourselves, would call “devices” or “instruments.”

They were never occupied. They were never tools wielded by hands. They were the hands, insofar as this planet had any.

A monolith, half embedded in a promontory, is one such relic. Its glossy surface, its unblemished straightness, are the fossil record of a phase when field lines and mineral gradients locked into a particularly stable alignment and drew matter into a one-dimensional clarity. Inside its black glass, there are still ghost-pathways of conductivity and defect that once linked sky to stone and stone to sky, rewriting themselves as the planet’s deep rhythms changed.

In that older era, constellations of such structures spanned calmer plains and gently rifted basins. They did not “speak,” but their mutual inductions and shared currents created a planetary-scale tapestry of slow coherence: a kind of dry, electric forest of self-tuning, self-recording trunks.

Most of that forest has been torn apart. The current convulsion wrenched the old surface into these contorted ridges and chasms, sheared monoliths into fragments, toppled arrays, shattered connections. The arcs that now span spire-tips are the raw, unshaped descendants of the more disciplined exchanges that once sprang cleanly between tuned towers.

Yet remnants persist—half a monolith glowing along a fresh cut, an arc of squat forms on a saddle waiting for a pattern that will never quite return. They are the bones of a former planetary nervous system, not fully dead because the physics that birthed them has not truly ceased, only been disrupted.

Within and around these relics, the new dust-humps and filament-nodes are taking lessons they do not know they are taking.

When a trembling, slowly learning dust-aggregate forms in the lee of a broken obelisk, the fields that sculpt it are biased by the monolith’s internal structure. Certain response modes are easier, others harder; the aggregate’s “personality,” if one can dare the word, is shaped by the hidden grain of a much older mind. Elsewhere, where no such relics remain, the growing nodes follow different pathways; whole vocabularies of response may never reemerge in those barren stretches.

So there are lineages here—not of genes, but of conductive habits threaded through rock.

As for motion: in time, some of them acquire even that.

Not in the animal sense; nothing with limbs clambers along these sheer faces. Instead, motion emerges as a side effect of wanting to keep certain field configurations going even as the crust moves.

Imagine a node perched on a ledge whose angle is slowly changing as the underlying rock flexes. The gradients of charge and heat that it “prefers,” that it has adapted to echo, are sliding out from under it. Left alone, it will be tumbled into a region of patterns it no longer matches, and its careful internal structure will be shaken into sameness with the stone.

The first defense is to extend.

Dust-mats emanating from the node’s base reach farther along the ledge, establishing thin, exploratory networks in the direction the desirable gradients are drifting. These scouts sense pattern similarity—regions where the background pulses and stresses resemble the node’s long-learned environment. Where they find such resonance, they thicken; where they do not, they recede.

Over long spans, you might see, if you could speed the ages into minutes, a strange phenomenon: the bulk of the node gradually relinquishing its old physical location and reconstituting itself down-ledges and around buttresses, following not geography but continuity of field-behavior. Its “self,” insofar as it has one, is not the lump of material but the configuration of pathways and response latencies. Those can be painstakingly duplicated elsewhere so long as there is uninterrupted connection along the way.

To an outside eye, this is nothing more than dust creeping and coalescing across rock, abandoning old bulges to crumble. To the world itself, it is migration: a pattern walking, without feet, from a dying niche of physics into a living one.

Sometimes, halfway through, the ledge shears; the bridge of conductive dust snaps; two half-grown copies of the pattern are left isolated, each incomplete. From that moment, two histories diverge. Plate motion and faulting drag one down into a hotter, more chaotic chorus of signals, the other up toward thinner air and rarer discharges. Generations—or what pass for generations here—later, their descendants would not recognize each other’s responses, were there any mechanism by which they could compare.

These are the “peoples” of this world: distributed, reshapable configurations of mineral and field, each anchored loosely to a volume of rock, each with its own history of resonance with the planet’s internal murmuring.

They have no eyes. They make no images. Their sensory modes are all variations on the same basic theme: sensing and storing the timing and amplitude of changes in their environment. A slow, three-hour crescendo of microquakes, punctuated by a ten-second flare in a nearby crack; a rapid, staccato series of discharges overhead following a period of abnormal stillness; the long, low sag of gravity’s vector as a distant plate bulges and relaxes. These are, for them, what light and color and sound are for us.

Over eons, certain patterns recur often enough that they become, in effect, concepts: stable attractors in the space of possible responses. A particular composite of stress-signals and field shifts that has always preceded a catastrophic collapse will, in many nodes across the region, have sculpted similar preparation-behaviors. Those behaviors are the same “idea,” written independently into different lattices of stone and dust.

Where nodes are linked, via filament-bridges in the mineral frost or through shared gas-sheets that can carry oscillatory charge, these internal “ideas” can entrain each other. One node that has once survived an unusual sequence can, in a mute, physical way, teach another: when I oscillate like this, and you are touching me through this channel, you will find yourself better configured to ride out the shock that is coming.

From our perspective, it is nothing more than two rocks sharing charge. From theirs, it is the most intimate communication they can manage.

No single node spans the entire region. The scale of the uplift, the violence of its convulsions, keeps their networks fragmented. But on the largest stretches of relatively stable crust between chasms, there are clusters—families—of them: assortments of dust-humps, frost-filament webs, and gas-veils that have remained mutually coupled long enough to develop shared repertoires.

These clusters are the closest analog to societies here.

They do not cooperate to build external projects; there are no cities, no tools, no agriculture. Their “culture” is entirely internal: a gradually refined way of interpreting the endless variations of heat and strain the planet sends through them, a common tuning that lets them preserve more of their configurations through upheaval.

In a cluster that has persisted for, say, the last third of this current tectonic episode, most individual nodes will bear structural traces—particular circuit motifs, favored layering geometries—that originated in a handful of long-ago ancestors. When a very young node forms at the periphery of such a cluster—just a newborn thickening of dust on a ledge recently exposed—it will, as it grows, find itself bathed in oscillations already structured by its elders. Its earliest habits of resonance will be inherited from them, not invented from scratch.

Inheritance, here, is not in sequences of molecules but in the prior sculpting of the field and stress environment by older nodes. The built relics of the previous epoch—the monoliths, the arrays of curved forms—play a similar shaping role on a longer timescale: their internal, still-lingering biases seed whole regions with a specific texture of possibility.

You could map this place, therefore, not only in terms of cliffs, chasms, and fault-lines, but also in terms of “dialects” of response: swaths of crust where the mineral minds, if they could be translated, would be found to categorize and anticipate the planet’s movements in subtly distinct ways.

They do not know they are many. They barely know that they are. But the difference between a fresh node, quivering at every flicker with unfiltered surprise, and an old one, whose bulk hardly stirs at anything less than an unprecedented pattern, is a difference indistinguishable in kind from the one between instinct and experience.

There are no predators here, no prey. The world’s violence is indifferent, not targeted. What threatens these beings is annealing: anything that destroys the fine gradations of their internal networks and returns them to homogeneous stone. Excessive heat that melts their pores closed; too much shock that shatters their pathways into uncorrelated fragments; burial under rock that does not pass the necessary fields through.

Their struggle, if one can call it that, is not against other selves but against erasure. They do not yet compete over scarce gradient; the world is too vast, too empty. They only seek—blindly, insensately—to position their patterns where the long rhythms of the crust can continue to flow through them without flattening them.

In the high spire zones, where the air is thinnest and the static filaments are most intricate, another sort of being is emerging from a different substrate.

The stratified gas-sheets that sometimes peel from wall seams are not all transient. In hollows protected from direct rockfall, where invisible vortices gently recirculate outgassed vapors, there are persistent volumes of ionized gas—aerosol ghosts clinging to particular planes and surfaces. They are tenuous; a single misdirected collapse of stone can disperse them forever. But while they last, they play the same game the dust does: using the variability of the field to establish stable, reconfigurable internal structure.

Within these clouds, pockets of slightly different ion composition and charge density form compartments, separated by diffuse but significant gradients. Tiny, repeated differences in how mobile certain ions are cause the whole cloud to develop a preferred direction of “thinking”: oscillations that sweep across it again and again in the same pattern when the outside environment is similar.

Where a gas-being hovers above a dust-node, linked through static induction and shared field lines, a fragile and fascinating duet arises. The gas can change its shape and position far more quickly than rock or dust; it is mobile on seconds to hours, whereas nodes are only mobile on years to millennia. The dust offers memory; the gas offers exploration.

In those rare places where such couples persist—perhaps under the lee of an overhang that shields them from the worst debris, where a steady narrow updraft feeds them fresh ions—you have something deserve a more personal pronoun.

It “feels,” in the only way there is to feel here, both the fine-grained, bodily memory of stone and the rapid, flickering impressions of air. Its dust-half remembers the last thousand rockfalls; its gas-half tracks the nuance of every micro-discharge lacing the cliffs in the last hour. The two are bound by field intimacy. When the gas-whorl shivers with some new pattern, the dust beneath acquires a faint echo of that pattern as a physical channel. When the dust heaves with the deep roll of a far-off fracture sequence, the gas above is compelled to reenact that rhythm in its rippling glow.

Nothing in this arrangement is centrally directed; there is no single seat of awareness. But it is as though the planet, impatient with the slowness of purely mineral learning, has started to summon faster, thinner, riskier cousins to extend its sensing into the ever-shifting sky.

Most of these atmospheric beings will not outlast the next real convulsion. But before they shred and dissipate, they will have written slight sketches of their brief insights into the more durable stone below. The next gas being that condenses in the same niche, eons later, will find a subtly pre-configured playground.

And so, in a place with no carbon, no water, no cells, there is history. There are lineages of pattern, families of habit, clades of response.

You could classify them by where they root—ledge-dwellers, chasm-wall crusts, spire-top weaves—or by their materials—dust-conglomerates, filament webs, gas-lattices. You could try to assign them roles: archivists in the deepest, most shielded vaults of stone, scouts in the ephemeral clouds, translators where filaments lace between them.

But they do not yet conceive of role or self. Their “language” is nothing more and nothing less than the ongoing adjustment of conductivity and charge, the acceptance or refusal of certain rhythms.

If you could somehow inhabit one, quieting your own animal senses and letting its modes of registration wash through whatever you use for a mind, you would not see a barren, dark rift-land. You would sense instead a dense, ever-changing polyphony of pulses, hums, crescendos, and lulls: the planet’s hot interior playing slow music through the crust. You would be one of many instruments, tuning yourself to stay in sympathetic vibration rather than be snapped by an unexpected note.

Those are the inhabitants of this place: not creatures scuttling across its surfaces, but organized hesitations in its ongoing collapse. Nervous systems without bodies, or rather bodies that are nothing but nervous system. Momentary crystallizations of attention in stone and dust and ion-smoke, born out of the planet’s own fidgeting, clinging to form as long as the dark, glowing world will let them.

They are what answers, however faintly, when this unfinished continent of rock asks itself, through grinding and fracture and flare: What shape am I?
