They are a cliff people, these inhabitants, but not in the simple sense of living “on” a cliff. They live in cross-section: in the cut of the mountain, in the negative space where the rock failed and the world slid open. Their bodies, their habits, their tools are all answers to that specific subtraction.

They are not many. This is the first thing to understand. Their settlement, spread across that vertical corridor, would not fill even a modest plain-village outside. A full census might name a few hundred individuals, perhaps fewer, threaded thinly up and down the stone like beads on a long, frayed string. Density would kill them here: too much noise, too much waste, too much demand on the slow pulse of water and vegetal growth. So they keep themselves small in number, or the place keeps them small; the distinction may be more philosophical than real.

Their bodies are built by the rift in obvious ways.

At a glance, you’d call them humanoid: upright, two-armed, two-legged, skull balanced over spine. They have not abandoned bipedality, but they have made an uneasy truce with it. Their legs are long and tendon-lean, with a reversed emphasis: enormous strength in calves and thighs, but ankles and knees layered with compact, ropy muscles suited for micro-adjustments rather than long strides. The knee joint bends a little more freely backward than you might expect, allowing a partial crouched stance that keeps their center of gravity low when moving along narrow ledges.

Feet are wide, prehensile things, more like articulate pads than rigid platforms. Each foot has four primary toes and a fifth, thumb-like nub on the outer edge. The underside skin is toughened and corrugated—but not calloused into insensitivity. They must feel the stone: the grain, the faint damp, the minute give that signals instability. You can watch a person here halt mid-step because of some barely perceptible shift in rock texture, then choose another foothold without consciously noticing what they’ve perceived.

Hands mirror the feet’s adaptation. Fingers are elongated, knuckle-jointed, with a noticeable spread between index and middle finger. Webs of skin stretch faintly between fingers and thumb when fully splayed, giving them a gecko-like capacity to brace against surfaces, distributing pressure and friction. The nails are more like blunt claws—thick, slightly curved, honey-keratin colored—with a surprising sensitivity beneath. Their tools are often designed not to replace the nail but to extend it.

They are not tall. Full-grown adults cluster around a compact height, their bodies proportioned for clearance through tight passages, for turning in cramped shafts, for curling in suspended nests. A tall outlier might reach a head and shoulders over their peers and is subtly disadvantaged: more likely to strike forehead on overhangs, to require extra contortions in crawling fissures. Stature is not admired here; poise is.

Their torsos are flexible rather than massive. The spine has an unusual fluidity; many vertebrae are slightly more mobile than in the bodies you might know, so that a person can twist from the waist and fold around protrusions with snake-like grace. Shoulder joints rotate broadly, arms swinging not only forward and back but wide to the sides, capable of both ape-style reach overhead and crab-like bracing along walls.

Their faces carry the most visible concessions to life in dim light. Eyes are large relative to skull size, irises wide, whites only a thin crescent at the edges. In this light, color is mostly swallowed, but where it can be seen, their irises tend toward deep greens, grays, and amber-browns—shades that disappear into pupil dilation so fully that in gloom you see only glossy dark circles reflecting the faintest sparks of fungus-glow and sky-fragments.

Brows are prominent ridges of bone and thick skin, serving like natural visors to deflect trickling water and minor falling grit. The bridge of the nose is low and broad, nostrils flared more sideways than downward—shaping breath along the face, warming air against skin, better catching subtle shifts in humidity and smell.

Ears sit close to the skull, small and layered with ridged cartilage. They excel not at volume but directionality; the inhabitants live in a place where you must interpret echoes as much as original sounds. They can hear the difference between a drip hitting still water and one hitting disturbed water, between a footfall cushioned in moss and the same footfall on naked stone. Children learn, before language fully settles, to turn toward the phase of a sound, not simply its apparent origin.

Their skin, where uncovered, tends toward deep earth tones modulated by climate and ancestry: umbers, dark olives, slate browns, copper-golds, all muted by the diffuse light. Whatever the native pigment, an overlay of habitat patina quickly asserts itself: soot from hearths, fine stone dust, greenish smears from constant plant contact. There is a faint, almost luminous undertone to their skin in the deepest dark when the eye is fully adapted—not bioluminescence, but the human-equivalent of a cliff-lizard’s way of not fully disappearing.

Body hair is sparse and functional. On the head, it is usually worn close or bound: braids threaded with fine cords and beads, tight knots that don’t catch on roots or rock edges. Loose hair is dangerous here. On limbs, fine down traps a micro-layer of air for warmth without becoming soaked; in cold seasons, this thickens subtly and then recedes again, a quiet seasonal molt.

Clothing is not about modesty so much as traction, protection, and pocket. They wear layered, wraparound garments—short tunics or vests and long strips wound around legs and arms, all secured with knotted cords. Materials come from above and within: woven fibers from roots and shrubs, felted mats from hair and plant, treated hide from the rare larger animals they hunt or scavenge. Colors are mostly the unforced tones of their origin—browns, desaturated greens, gray-beiges—but many garments are patched with subtly brighter scraps: the inner bark of a plant that dries to soft gold, fungus-dyed panels in iron red or dull teal. These flashes are rarely large; they appear in cuffs, neckbands, belts, where they can be seen up close without becoming targets in the half-light.

Their clothing is full of small loops, attachment points, and hidden slits. A person can secure a tool to their hip but at a moment’s notice slide it through a slit so it lies flush along thigh or back, nothing protruding to snag as they shimmy through a gap. Children’s garments sport extra handles at the shoulders and back, so adults can catch and stabilize them quickly.

They walk the rift with a gait that looks cautious from the outside—but it is not hesitation, it is calibration. Each step is an argument solved in real time between stone, muscle, balance, memory. When they travel on the rare broad surfaces, they do not immediately lengthen their stride into our familiar confident walk; their instinct is still to keep the weight ready to rock backward or sideways, to break a fall that might come from any angle.

Their tools are a continuation of their anatomy.

Stone blades are not straight, swordlike things. They are curved, asymmetrical, knapped to fit in the palm as if grown there. Many have secondary grips along the back or side so the tool can be flipped and used differently depending on whether you are scraping bark off a root directly in front of you or slicing a vine while hanging below it.

Hooks are everywhere: bone hooks, wooden hooks, horn hooks, gently barbed. They use them less as weapons and more as ways of making the world hold them. A climber does not so much pierce the rock as jam a clever shape into an existing defect, hang weight for a moment, then move on. Long poles tipped with fanlike bone claws are used to test unreachable ledges for stability, to dislodge suspect stones before committing to them.

Their cords and nets are more advanced than any other technology they possess. Fiber-craft is their mathematics, their writing, their architecture. You see knots whose logic is immediately opaque but unmistakably purposeful: knots that distribute load so a broken strand doesn’t mean collapse, braids that store stretch in particular segments for controlled give, woven patterns that turn a flat net into a cupped cradle with a subtle twist of one corner.

Suspended platforms are not improvised; they are engineered over generations. Every attachment point is redundant: three cords from three differently oriented anchors, so that if a block spalls off a wall the platform will tilt, not drop. Children here grow up reading tension and slack the way others read faces.

They maintain fire as a carefully rationed privilege. Open flame is too risky in a place of wood-scarcity and complex air currents. Hearths are small, often in hooded niches with smoke-guide vents skillfully carved to lead exhaust toward fissures that open above. Charcoal is more prized than bright flame; it permits slow, controlled cooking, drying, and warmth without filling chambers with soot. Ignition is a communal function: special hearths, never allowed to go out, from which others borrow coals. A lost fire is a minor shame, not because of any superstition but because it implies inattention in a life where attention is survival.

Their metal is story rather than infrastructure. They do not mine veins deeply—it would destabilize the already precarious rock—and ore-bearing stones are rare, scavenged from rockfalls or washed into accessible cracks by time. What little metal they hammer into existence is mostly copper and soft iron, shaped cold or with modest heat into thin plates, edges, and inlays. These pieces are kept bright, oiled, polished. A metal ring around a stone knife’s handle is as much a mark of continuity—the memory of the ancestor who found or forged it—as it is a practical reinforcement.

They inscribe little on flat surfaces; stone walls are precious circulation space, not canvases. When they mark, they carve shallowly along horizontal seams or on the undersides of overhangs: lines of tiny chisel-dots, notches, slim wave patterns. Much of this is code: indications of past rockfalls, notes about water seepage, warnings about air-tension zones. Some of it is decorative, but even decoration doubles as mnemonic—songs hung as patterns where voice cannot be conveniently used.

Sound is their second skeleton. They are wary of loudness, but they are masters of nuance.

Speech is low and close. Their spoken language favors consonant clusters that can be mouthed without much breath: clicks, soft affricates, liquid consonants, hums shaped in the nasal cavity. Vowels are short and often lowered, blending into the hum of the place. Their voices sit naturally in a narrow range of pitch—neither shrill nor booming—chosen less for expressive drama than for how it sits within the echo-prone geometry.

Beyond ordinary talk, they use three more formal modes of communication:

1. **Tap-code:** Sequences of taps on stone or wood, differentiated by spacing and location rather than volume. A rapid double-tap and a slow single on a particular support can mean “person approaching from below.” Complex patterns announce births, deaths, rock shifts, water surges. Children play patterns on bridge-rails that carry up and down the rift for fun, transmuting information into game and back.

2. **Clack-stones:** Small, portable stones of specific shapes and densities that, when struck, produce distinct timbres. A set of clack-stones is like a limited instrument and signaling kit in one. They can be played in rhythms that mark time for collective tasks (like hauling a rope) or as beacons across wider spans of the chasm. Animals have learned to associate certain rhythms with food or danger, weaving themselves into the signaling web.

3. **Light-flicker:** In the brighter upper sections, polished reflective fragments—smoothed metal, shiny mineral—are used to flash small patterns of light to opposite walls. These are not elaborate heliographic alphabets, but more like shorthand: “come,” “stay back,” “water changed,” “watch the sky.” On days when cloud thins and the narrow sky brightens, a whole gossip of flickers may ripple across the rift face.

They carry music, but it is mostly intimate. Simple flutes fashioned from hollow roots or bones, low-pitched and breath-hungry, are played near sleeping spaces or at ceremonies by pools. Rhythms are tapped more than sung; song, when it rises, often braids with dripping and trickling, imitating natural cadences. Harmony is difficult in such odd acoustics; they sing mostly in unison or with one high call against a cluster of murmured responses.

Their relationship with the animals is not domestication in the plow-and-pasture sense. It is cohabitation, treaty, and slow, careful shaping.

They maintain symbiosis with the small many-limbed “frog-spiders” of the damp walls. These creatures keep films of algae and bacteria in check, preventing slippery overgrowth on crucial handholds and walkways. In exchange, the people cultivate lines of fungus and tender plant on certain “feeding walls,” never scraping these clean, even when they need the wall-space. You can see the difference: a working wall kept nearly bare, with only a few tolerated moss streaks, and a feeding wall, velvety and glaringly rich next to the starkness of its neighbors.

The eel-bodied climbers—sinuous mammals with whiskered snouts—are encouraged but not owned. The people leave bits of cleaned-off marrow-bone or fruit mash in specific cavities to attract them to remain in the vicinity; their constant exploratory movement flushes out pests, and their keen senses detect subtle tremors before humans notice. Elders say you should watch where the eels sleep; they avoid certain roots and overhangs whose micro-movements portend collapse.

The long-bodied gliders that knife through the slit of sky are treated with wary reverence. Their occasional plunges into the rift to seize small prey, their silent, glassy eyes, and their habit of hanging motionless suggest to the people a partial kinship with the air-tension zones. They do not hunt these creatures systematically. Rarely, if one dies in the rift—caught in a rockfall, overcome by sudden storm—they will harvest skin and bone with a solemnity reserved for important dead. Membranes become precious coverings for small windows; bones are shaped into thin instruments that make eerie, wavering tones when spun.

The heavy, pale-furred quadrupeds that browse on hanging foliage are the nearest analogue to large domestic animals. These beasts can be persuaded, in some seasons, to bear simple packs along the more generous ledge-paths above. But “persuasion” is the right word: punishment and tight control do not work here; a panicked large animal is a disaster in constrained vertical corridors. The people have developed a suite of soft, repetitive sounds and tactile signals—brushes, taps, rest-hand on shoulder—to guide them. They shear their fur sparingly for insulation pads and weaving fiber, always leaving enough so the animal can function in cold and heat. Meat, when taken from them, is usually from the fallen, the infirm, the very old; ritual thanks is paid not from sentimentality but from a clear understanding of how narrow the margin of coexistence is.

The boulder-like pool creatures are something else entirely: not pet, not livestock, not deity exactly, but axis. No one remembers settling the rift without them already there. They are the oldest neighbors, big enough and slow enough that their mere presence shapes architecture. Children are taught their moods the way they are taught stone’s moods. A flick of one filamented limb toward a certain wall can send a quiet word up the rift: “There is movement under us; tell the ones who live above.” When pulses of subterranean water shift, these beings reorient, rise or sink, years before the people have the means to read any consequence. Whether this is cognition or blind reaction is irrelevant; the inhabitants treat it as knowledge, and knowledge is honored.

Not everything is mutualistic. Predators do exist that will take a small child or unwary adult. There are sinuous root-dwellers whose bite carries a slow, suffocating venom; plummeting fliers with needlelike beaks; blind, pale insect swarms that can skeletonize small carcasses in hours under the right conditions.

But even these dangerous species are folded into a moral map of the rift. Ledges where root-dwellers are known to coil gain names that translate roughly as “Edge that Eats” or “Sleeping Teeth.” Children who reach a certain age are taken on supervised circuits past such places, to be shown: here, we do not linger; here, the sound of water is wrong; here, your skin should prickle, and if it does not, you are not listening well enough.

They do not romanticize nature. They simply live too close to it to lie about it. A predator that has taken a child may be hunted in vengeance, yes—but even then, care is taken not to exterminate it entirely from that micro-habitat, lest some worse imbalance follow. Their stories remember how removing one shape from the living lattice can let another bloom too widely, choking off water, or driving prey species into new, more dangerous behaviors.

Their social units are small and nested, mimicking the architectural pattern of the rift.

The basic group is the ledge-group: five to twelve individuals sharing a cluster of adjacent living niches, platforms, and small workspaces. A ledge-group is often composed of kin, but “kin” here is as much practice as blood. A child whose parents are killed in a rockfall or sickness will be folded into another group almost immediately; the ledge is what matters.

Each ledge-group tends certain plants on its immediate walls and shelves, maintains specific sections of walkway and bridge, and is responsible for the care of designated structural roots and anchors. They become experts in their microclimate: they know exactly how condensation beads on their own roots for each type of sky, exactly how their section of wall sounds when tapped after a storm, exactly how much their suspended platform should sway when three bodies cross.

Above the ledge-group is the rung: a collection of groups stacked along a vertical swath of the rift, usually spanning a particular gradient of light and damp. One rung might occupy the deeply shaded, moss-thick mid-lower section; another, the brighter, wind-touched upper chambers. Rungs coordinate resource movement up and down: fungus grown in low damp travels upward in exchange for resin from sun-hardened shrubs; cold caches below hold certain foods for those above, while vantage points above watch for incoming weather that will affect everyone.

Leadership is not centralized. There is no single chieftain spanning the whole rift. Instead, each rung has one or more “listeners”—older individuals chosen less for charisma than for steadiness and depth of attention. Listeners tend to move more between ledges, gather information, mediate conflicts, and maintain memory of long-term patterns: when the last great rockshear happened, which root-lines failed, which routes were abandoned and why.

Their rituals center on three axes: stone, water, and line.

Stone is the first teacher. They treat the rock not as a personified spirit but as a living condition that must be read and answered. Before a new niche is carved, a quiet, almost technical ceremony is performed: days of tapping and listening, smearing damp soil into hairline cracks, hanging weighted cords to watch for minute shifts. Children participate, learning the difference between an “empty” ring of solid basalt and the hollow traveler’s sound of a hidden fracture. At the conclusion, if the stone is judged cooperative, a small offering is made: usually a tuft of moss or a ragged plant pulled from a place where it struggled and placed gently nearby, with a murmur of “Grow better here.” The gesture is more than symbolic; transplanting hardy pioneer species is part of stabilizing new surfaces.

Water is the most openly sacred substance. Not because it is rare here—it is more present than outside—but because it is the tether between rift and the old outer world. It descends from a sky most of them seldom enter. They read in its taste the trace of those altitudes.

There are named pools, with histories. The shallow central pool of the mid-chamber—the one arced around by walkways, shared with the boulder-creatures—serves as a communal mirror. When important decisions must be made that affect many rungs—whether to abandon a particular corridor in anticipation of deepening fracture, whether to guide a new cluster of young couples into forming a ledge-group in a risky but resource-rich section—they gather on the stone fringes of that pool.

Here, they will not speak loudly. Instead, the debate weaves over hours or days, marked by pauses in which attention turns to the water’s surface: to its unruffled reflection of broken sky, or to how the slow stir of the ancient creatures beneath changes it. If a water-tension anomaly—one of those curious zones of prickle and slow droplets—happens to drift closer or further, they treat that too as another thread of data in the weave.

Children learn to swim in smaller, shallower basins warmed by filtered light. Their first long, unassisted float is celebrated: a rite of “not-falling,” equal in status to a first climb along a narrow external ledge. Those who are best at both become the rift’s natural couriers.

Line—fiber, cord, continuity between points—is their most explicit metaphor for relationship and obligation. They conceive of social ties as literal strands: some taut and load-bearing, some loose but present, some broken and trailing. When two people formalize a bond—be it partnership, adoption, oath of mentorship—they will often sit side by side, each twisting a cord from fibers they have separately gathered, then splicing them together so the final rope bears a visible transition: one texture into the other, then both inextricable.

Significant events often involve the cutting or tying of lines. To mark a death, the cords that individual knotted into shared work—bridge-anchors, net edges, garment ties—are not removed. Instead, nearby, a small additional knot is added: a “stone-knot,” tight and compact, that says: this line has passed its weight but remains part of the lattice. Only if someone has committed a grave breach—risked others’ lives through carelessness in stone or water matters—will their knots be unpicked and retied entirely, their contribution dissolved back into anonymity. This is their deep punishment: not exile, for one cannot be easily exiled from a vertical community, but erasure from the map of the made world.

Their cosmology is pragmatic but not unfeelingly so. They know the outer ridges exist, though most rarely go there. They know there were people before them who did not live in rifts—elders speak of broad ground and relentless sky as if of a dream inherited from someone else’s sleep. Legends say that when the mountain cracked, some chose to go down into the wound and some stayed out; their own lineage claims the descent as an act of sense, not cowardice. Here, in the fracture, water could be held, wind softened, life stacked.

The luminous fungi and mosses in the deepest recesses carry a quiet spiritual weight. Their low radiance is not blinding spectacle but patient persistence in absolute dark. Tales are told of a person lost after a rockfall finding their way back by the slow pulsing of a certain familiar patch of glow, as if the place itself breathed with them. Another story names these organisms “the stars that fell and could not climb back,” fragments of the outer night caught deep below. Children press their palms to them, watching the soft brightening, and are told: “They answer when you touch. So do we. So does the water. The stone is slow to answer, but it does.”

The air-tension zones—the places where mist hangs and hairs rise, where echoes lag—are treated almost like weather but with personality. They do not worship these anomalies, but they none the less assign them attention and give them names: “The Standing Breath,” “The Thin Wall,” “The Split Note.” Talisman bundles of bone and seed are hung not to ward them off but to reveal them: when the slightest movement of air makes seeds whisper, they know the edge of such a zone has shifted.

Over generations, they have learned that these places correlate with certain events: with deep, distant thunder that never reaches as sound; with the slow progression of fractures; with strange, brief sky-lights beyond the rift’s rim that some have glimpsed at night. Whether there is physics here not fully articulated or something stranger, they craft behavior around observation: avoiding sleeping too near these tensions, storing no critical food or tools in their bounds, teaching children a simple rule—“If your skin sings and nothing moves, step away.”

Seasons for them are less about temperature swings and more about rhythms of water and rock.

There is a wetting season, when outside clouds drag lower, and the rift sweats. Condensation fattens roots, walls glisten, moss regreens. They work then at expansion: carving new niches in softened seams, laying new cords while the stone is pliant, transplanting seedlings to fresh cracks. It is a time of industry and careful daring.

There is a leaching season, when water recedes, and the rift dries to its basaltic bones. Paths crackle with fine grit, some pools dwindle to glass-bottomed dishes. They focus inward then: repairing, consolidating, culling plants that overreach, treating cords with oils and resins to survive the drier air. Sickness travels more readily in this time—dust in lungs, infections in minor cuts—so they emphasize rest and small, local tasks.

And there is a falling season, tied to freeze-thaw cycles in the upper mountains. Then, rock breaks. Sometimes it is only sand-rain and pebble-chatter. Sometimes whole slabs shear away in a grinding roar that shakes the vertical village like a gong. They have mapped likely fall-chutes over generations; shelters are carved in the lateral blind pockets out of the line of descent. In those days, they restrict movement along certain spans. They huddle and listen. Falling season births new ledges, kills old paths, reveals new small caves. Afterward is a time of cautious reconnaissance: young, agile climbers go with older, more experienced ones, tapping, testing, naming.

Birth and death are folded almost seamlessly into these cycles. A newborn is presented first not to a central temple but to the ledge: held in strong hands at the threshold, to feel the cool, sweet air of the fracture, to listen to the nearest drip and the nearest clack-stone echo. Only then are they brought deeper, to warm arms and hearth.

The dead, if they leave intact bodies, are not buried in deep soil—that does not exist here in quantity. They are placed in carefully chosen crevices or small side-chambers near root systems, wrapped in woven shrouds that will slowly fray, their bones left eventually for the rootlets and mosses to grow around. Specific areas are reserved for this, and they are not morbid spaces. Children visit them as they would visit older kin in a quieter district, learning to recognize the names and knotted cords associated with particular remains. “Here,” a grandmother might say, placing a hand where the stone slightly bulges, “is where your grandfather’s weight helps hold this wall.”

If someone dies by falling into inaccessible depths, there is no recovery. Instead, a stone or shaped piece of wood is dedicated in their name, placed in a joint or crevice where it adds stability. The logic is consistent: if we cannot retrieve the body to join our walls, we add another weight in its stead.

They know, as keenly as any ecological scientist, that their settlement is ephemeral on a long enough timescale. The rift is young, but it is still deteriorating; it will widen, deepen, reorder itself. They do not imagine a permanent city here, only a lineage of habitation that will move as stone and water dictate. Their history is recorded less on static surfaces and more in stories of routes: “Once there was a bridge that stretched from this beetled overhang to that crack we now cannot see; once water fell from here instead of there.”

Migration, when it happens, is not the heroic horizontal trek of many myths but the slow vertical drift of a climbing vine. A ledge-group whose shelves grow too unstable may prospect upward or downward for a season, carving temporary alcoves and testing microclimates. If they find a viable new spot, they shift: cords unknotted here, retied there, plants and even bits of root transplanted carefully. The old ledge is not erased; it becomes a remembered ruin, a caution or an encouragement for future generations when stone shifts again.

Strangers do not often find this place. The outer mountains are harsh, the rift’s throat narrow and visually obscure. Yet occasionally, a figure from the wide world appears at the upper edges—sun-browned, wind-chapped, wide-eyed in the dim. The inhabitants have protocols for such arrivals. They do not rush to greet; too much noise, too sudden movement might panic the outsider and produce tragedy.

Instead, they send one or two practiced emissaries up onto broader shelves, where they can be seen in stages, against recognizable stone, not emerging suddenly from concealed alcoves. Hands are kept visible, gestures slow. Water is offered first, then guidance along the safest visible path downward if the stranger wishes to continue. Many do not; the vertical depths unsettle them. They leave puzzled and perhaps with half-remembered images of lights inside the mountain.

Those who stay—rarely—must learn the rift from scratch. Children adapt best: their joints, expectations, and senses are still fluid. They develop new foot pads, new habits of listening, new reflexes of grip. Adults struggle more. Some never acclimate fully to the constant sense of proximity to long falls. They walk the ledges with permanent tension, their muscles never unwinding. In time, though, even they learn to recognize the particular sweetness of the air here, to sleep to the polyphony of drips, rustles, low animal breath.

So who lives here?

A thin people in number, but not brittle. Bodies made for the edge between floor and void, for constant three-dimensional negotiation. Minds braided into stone, water, line, capable of reading minute shifts in an environment that barely registers change to outsiders. They are quiet not from shyness but from respect—for echoes, for animals, for the fact that in such a place every action travels further than you intend.

They do not dominate their rift; they occupy it the way moss occupies a boulder: intimately, adaptively, always one crack away from being scraped off by the mountain’s next slow gesture. They know this. It is not tragedy for them. It is simply the rule of the place.

And in obeying that rule, in learning its nuances so deeply that their bodies and tools and stories could not exist anywhere else, they become as much a function of the rift as any root, any drip, any slow, stone-skinned creature in the pool: inhabitants, yes—but also, now, part of the organ that this high, young wound has grown into.
