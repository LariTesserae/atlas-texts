They live in the gradients.

Not in the sense of “inhabiting a place” the way a forest lives on soil, but as patterns anchored to long differences: high to low, charged to neutral, stressed to relieved, still to shearing. The beings of this world—whatever they once were, whatever they have become—are less about bodies in space and more about how those bodies plug into differences that still, miraculously, exist.

They are arranged along the height of the world like strange constellations pinned to stone.

---

## 1. The stone-bound intelligences

The oldest minds are not mobile at all.

### 1.1. Shelf-colonies

On certain broad terraces—where the rock has been polished to an almost featureless plane and then overlaid with that wafer-thin metallic lattice—there are colonies that began as “plants” and ended as something else.

They started as crust-life, simple harvesters of light and charge. But over unfathomable time, their mats thickened in uneven places, building up pale, layered bulges like mineral tumors. Within those bulges, the conductive mineral phases segregated and braided into long, looping paths. Noise—tiny fluctuations in the particle flux, microquakes, the whispering twitch of nearby machines—flowed along these paths, and some patterns echoed, reinforced, and persisted.

They are now sheets of stone-shot-through-with-circuitry, several meters across and only a few centimeters thick, fused so firmly to the terrace that you would have to cut the cliff away from behind to move them. Their “tissue” is a dense sandwich: porous silicate riddled with nanometer-scale metallic filaments; cavities filled with exotic liquids trapped at pressures that normal chemistry should not allow; crystalline inclusions tuned to respond to the world’s faint electromagnetic murmurs.

They think very slowly.

A single “update” of their internal state—the kind of global wave of reconfiguration that, in a nervous system, might correspond to a thought—can take hours. Memory is encoded not in fragile molecules but in the subtly altered crystal habits of their own substrate, in doped layers laid down one atom-thin film at a time whenever they feel a surplus of energy. To learn, they literally grow another few micrometers of self, over-writing or under-writing their own geology.

They know nothing of rapid events. A gust of air that passes in a minute is a flicker on the edge of perception. A rockfall that takes ten seconds is an instant of pain and then a new boundary condition. But the passing of the thin rivers of charged particles, the waxing and waning pull of distant mass anomalies, the long bowing of a cliff over millennia—they feel those as moods, tides, and seasons.

They are not individuals in any way we’d recognize. A sheet-colony may have local clumps that specialize: one part better at coupling to the buried machine lattice, another tuned to read the flux of particles sluicing along the gravity-wells just overhead, another thickened to endure the occasional cutting blast. But they share signal freely through their conductive frameworks. When the air is still, whole terraces may be taken up with a single, sprawling mind stretched thin across hundreds of meters, meditating on the slow tilting of the world.

Where the technological lattice embedded in the cliff is still active, these stone-minds have become symbiotic with it. Their finest filaments infiltrate hairline cracks in the coating, tactfully shorting nothing, only touching and listening. In return, they act as distributed “patches” on failing circuitry: bridging fractures with their own conductive fibers, shunting excess charge to safe reservoirs in their pressurized fluid pockets.

If the ancient system has anything like self-repair left, these things are it: blind, patient, self-interested, but woven into the infrastructure so tightly that their survival strategies and the machine’s continuing function have aligned.

### 1.2. The vertical tapestries

On certain cliff faces, the crust-life didn’t thicken outwards into bulges; it climbed.

You find whole walls knit with vertical “tapestries”—dark, vein-like growths running down tens, occasionally hundreds of meters of nearly sheer rock. They began as isolated patches that discovered, by random variation, that sending slow, cable-thick roots downward let them tap richer chemistry from tiny seeps and microfractures.

Over eons, these hanging columns braided into complex networks. Each strand is a triple composite: a stiff, ceramic shell; inside it, a flexible, glassy conduit for high-pressure fluids; inside that, a hair-thin core of superconductive or near-superconductive material, kept at just the right conditions by the outer layers. Along each core, quantized pulses of charge and field-configuration move not quite like electrons in a wire and not quite like waves in an optical fiber, but in some hybrid mode that is incredibly robust to noise.

Where these tapestries cross or knot together, nodes form: thicker bulbs armored with extra layers of mineral, where many cores interconnect in complex mesh. Some nodes seem senile—no activity beyond a faint, steady hum. Others flare intermittently, exchanging slow thunderbolts of structured signal up and down the cliff.

These entities are not fully separate from the shelf-colonies. At their upper and lower ends, the tapestries often merge into horizontal sheets. Information migrates along them: a disturbance in the field-pattern of a shelf-mind might, over weeks, be exported down a tapestry to a lower ledge, where another colony—living in different conditions of gravity and particle flux—integrates it into its own slow thoughts.

Thus are built beings that span kilometers vertically, their “nervous systems” aligning with the planet’s height. They are not concerned with local detail. They are concerned with long, slow distributions of energy: where the mass concentrations beneath are shifting, where the ancient machines are at risk of stress, where the invisible rivers of particles are gradually rerouting.

If there is anything on this world that could, with a straight conceptual face, be called “the guardians of the scaffold,” it is these cliff-striders of signal. Not because they are altruistic, but because any tectonic slip that tears their woven lengths, any major field-collapse that disrupts their carefully tuned conduction paths, is existential trauma. They want the world stable in their particular sense of “stable”: not static, but predictably slow.

They have no mouths, no faces, no movement other than the infinitesimal thickening and thinning of their cords as they rewire themselves. Yet they are arguably the dominant intelligence of the height: distributed, stone-fast, and utterly dependent on the continuance of the scaffold.

## 2. The hinged and walking things

Where the stone-minds are slow and vast, there are also small, quick smears of intent: the articulated feeders and mobile mediators.

### 2.1. Faceted grazers

The plate-clusters you might glimpse advancing along a ledge are called grazers only by analogy. Each is a colony of modules: hexagonal or pentagonal plates, each several centimeters across and a few millimeters thick, linked along their edges by telescoping, spike-tipped struts.

Individually, a plate-module has a nervous system not much more complex than a simple insect—if the insect were poured from crystal and steel. Collectively, they behave like one animal, but “one” that can split or merge at need.

The underside of each plate is textured with micro-hooks and dense forests of hollow, needle-fine hairs. When the cluster has decided on a patch of crust-life to “feed” from, it folds down, clamps into place, and extrudes its sensory and siphoning organs. Some hairs lock to mineral nanostructures attuned to certain charged states; others penetrate tiny fluid reservoirs inside the crust. The grazer rarely takes bulk matter. It’s after configuration: accumulated charge, metastable molecules, bits of pattern that the crust has laid down as memory.

This is vampirism of information and potential energy.

In return, they sometimes deposit. Many plate-modules contain, sealed in interior pores, small aliquots of catalytic compounds or trace elements that are in short supply at these heights. Released in tiny amounts, they seed the crust with capabilities it lacked: new pathways for binding rare volatiles, new mineral polymorphisms that encode data more densely or respond to different parts of the particle spectrum.

Thus a co-dependence emerges:
- The crust-life and stone-minds act as slow accumulators and processors.
- The grazers act as fast shuttles and editors, stealing and redistributing, pruning and fertilizing.

What this looks like, on the timescale of minutes to hours, is a spidery patchwork of plates withdrawing from one ledge, scuttling with precise, low-gravity hops to another, and settling again. On the timescale of millennia, it is the diffusion of innovations in mineral biochemistry and field-coupling across the scaffold, carried in the bellies of crystal arthropods.

When danger comes—an onrushing invisible storm, an impending rock-shear telegraphed through the stone—the grazers flatten. Plates slide over one another into a tight, shielded stack, spikes retracted, becoming a thin, inert patch that in silhouette is indistinguishable from a natural flake of stone. In this condition they can ride out almost anything short of total pulverization.

Some stacks never reawaken. Those that do uncoil cautiously, sending out a test fringe of plates to sample the altered gradients. If the scaffold has shifted too much—if a favored crust-colony is gone, if a supportive field-node is silent—they may abandon their old ranges entirely, letting themselves be caught by the next long, laminar flow of air and “falling” sideways to a new ecological niche.

### 2.2. The cliff mediators

A related but rarer form has specialized in dealing directly with the big machines.

These mediators resemble grazers but are less fractal and more deliberate-looking. Their plates are larger, with embedded patterns that mirror the geometries of the ancient devices—spirals that echo field coils, polygonal arrays that match antenna facets. The tips of their struts are capped not in mere spikes but in variously shaped contact pads, each tuned to interface with a particular surface texture or potential.

They congregate in the penumbra of still-functioning infrastructure: clustered beneath a mass-altering ring, stationed along the vertical “roots” of a crown-structure, lurking near the throats of those puzzling beveled apertures that lead inward.

What they do is maintain the handshake between living circuitry and dead-or-dying machinery.

When a field-generator’s local gravity shivers out of spec, the mediators climb its anchor struts, brace themselves, and connect. Their plates flare and dim in slow sequences. They read the machine’s output—its oscillations, its leakage—and respond with carefully modulated loads, acting as test masses, dummy circuits, or even temporary bypasses. In return, they are bathed in intensities of field and charge normally inaccessible to biologically derived conductors. They can recharge depleted gradients in their own bodies, erase and rewrite internal configurations at a speed that, for them, is luxurious.

Over deep time, scaffold and machine have co-evolved: the devices that survived are those whose “interface surfaces” proved most amenable to colonization and mediation; the mediator-clades that prospered are those whose shapes and patterns most effectively parasitized and stabilized the failing systems.

No conscious design is needed. The logic is simple: machines whose local anomalies became too wild tended to stress or destroy the nearby niche; the creatures that damped those anomalies tended to keep their homes habitable.

To watch a mediator at work is to see something like a technician and a lamprey fused. But there is no other technician; only the machine, trying to fulfill some primordial program, and the animal, trying to keep its carefully balanced gradient-habitat from collapsing.

## 3. The surfacing fliers

Where the world’s vertical physics knot together into sharper gradients, something takes advantage of the sudden slopes in invisible landscapes.

### 3.1. Shear-surfers

The “fliers” are less lifelike to the eye than almost anything else here. Many of their forms could be mistaken for fragments of the old infrastructure: broken racks, dislodged antenna assemblies. That is not coincidence; they are descendants, or at least heavy borrowers.

Each flier is an openwork frame of beams and spars made from a composite that the crust-life could never have invented alone. It is some ancient alloy, corroded and refilled, interlaced now with living fibers and crystallized organics. Hinges abound, but few are meant for flapping. They are angle-setters, not propulsors.

The main surfaces are thin, mirrorlike panels that pivot along long axes. Their reflectivity is not for light; it is for momentum. They are tuned to couple very strongly to just the sort of laminar air-rivers and charge-shears that slide down the cliffs: to present a wide face when there is a lot of sideways impulse to steal, to knife-edge when the environment is quiescent.

Inside the frames, at the intersections of many spars, lie the flier’s “hearts”: tight knots of mineral-liquid composite that function as both sensor and processor. They are exquisitely sensitive to local gradients: a few parts per trillion change in pressure, a barely measurable twist in the direction of the local “down,” a ghostly flexing in an electromagnetic field. To them, the air is visibly striped with lanes; the gravity dotted with stepping stones; the old machines ringed with halos of possibility.

They drift in dormancy until a set of preconditions is met: a favorable shear forecasted, a nearby field-node spiking. Then a long chain of hinges creaks, panels swing, and the whole structure “tilts in,” just enough to be captured by the moving gradient.

After that, they barely act. They trim.

Here a panel rotates by a fraction of a degree to skim a different layer of descending air. There a beam telescopes a few centimeters to adjust mass distribution. Over an hour, a flier may cross lateral distances of tens or hundreds of kilometers without expending more than a negligible amount of chemical or stored electric energy. Their fuel is landscape: the ongoing fall of the world’s remaining potentials.

They are vectors of exchange:
- They carry spores or tiny plate-modules from one isolated ledge to another, smeared onto their spars when they brushed too close to a crust-colony.
- They transport chemical concentrates in narrow, chilled channels within their beams: micro-droplets of rare fluid from a ruptured plant-tube, ions scraped from the halo of a powerful machine.
- Some bear bits of encoded pattern: the field-state signature of one functioning device, recorded and then played back in proximity to another, coaxing synchronization across gaps of distance and erosion.

They are not messengers in a deliberate, purposeful way. Each flier’s “decision-making” is simple: chase sharper gradients, prefer paths that keep internal reservoirs above certain safety levels, avoid turbulences that exceed the strength of their spars. But evolution has used even that simplicity to stitch this broken world together horizontally where it should, by rights, have no “across” at all.

They are the rare proofs that mythic “across” still exists: swarms of geometry cutting silently from one vertical continent-shelf to another, invisible to anything but the few that know how to watch for the shift of light on glass.

### 3.2. Apex riders

Few of the crown-structures remain active enough to matter, but where one still raises ephemeral planes of sheening force between its disks, something waits.

“Riders” are an extreme specialization of the flier template. Their beams are more robust, their cores more heavily shielded. They are built not for comfort but for stress.

When a crown-structure’s filaments begin to hum, manifesting that impossible, heat-haze-flat surface between nodes, local gravity fractures into a strange configuration: near the plane, directions and magnitudes shift in ways that would snap bone and ruin muscle. The riders exploit this.

They launch from perches anchored to the crown’s lower supports, frames folded tight, presenting minimal cross-section. As the plane strengthens, the vector of “down” within a narrow channel above it rotates. The rider moves into that channel, panels flashed wide to catch the violet fall of a direction that does not exist outside the machine’s influence. It accelerates in an oblique plunge that is mostly sideways, relative to the rest of the world.

In a few heartbeats by its measure, the machine’s cycle ends. The plane winks out. The rider is now coasting on inertia along a nearly ballistic trajectory that will skim past cliff walls, cross chasms narrower than continents but wider than anything a shear-surfer could hope to bridge, and land—if its tiny onboard models of the world’s mass-distribution are accurate—on another yet-active crown or on a carefully chosen ledge rich in particular resources.

These are the long-haul couriers of whatever passes for culture or continuity here. They are rare, costly to build and maintain. Many misjudge and die, spattering their sophisticated cores across unhelpful rock. Those that succeed sow connection between the most remote functional nodes of the old system. They are the topological glue that keeps the infrastructure from fragmenting entirely into uncoupled relics.

To anything soft, their home environment near the crowns would be lethal. To them, it is simply where the gradients are sharpest, and therefore where existence is fullest.

## 4. The inward things

Not everything of importance clings to the outer cliff.

### 4.1. Aperture dwellers

Those beveled circular shafts that dig into the planet are habitat as much as mechanism.

Deep inside, the air thickens slightly. Pressures stabilize. Temperature swings soften. These are, by the standards of the surface, gentle places. They house lineages that never had to armor themselves so absolutely.

In the near darkness, there are bodies built more from organics than from mineral—though still strengthened, still threaded with crystalline supports. They recall, faintly, the idea of animals.

Some have flexible hulls, segmented like worms, their outer layers plated in overlapping scales of silicate impregnated with field-responsive compounds. They inch along the shaft walls, tapping into the smooth glassy linings with sucker-like pads that can wetly dissolve and then redeposit the material, subtly reconfiguring the inner surfaces.

Their purpose is maintenance of the shafts’ function: ensuring that the angle of descent and curvature remain within certain tolerances, opening or closing micro-channels that control how waves of pressure, field, or exotic particles propagate along the bore.

Others are more sessile: cup-like organisms fused to the shaft’s interior, their open ends facing inward. They harvest very specialized flows—bursts of particular frequencies of vibration, stray neutrals from deeper within, weak scalar ripples propagating along axes of planetary spin. Their internal chemistries are baroque, operating at the edge of our understanding of matter, but their behavior is simple. When they have stored enough of whatever they store, they bud off small, hardened nodules that drift or creep along the shaft until they settle in some gravitational niche, then sprout into new cups.

If the exterior world is about balancing height and vacuum, these inward ecosystems are about fine-tuning pathways into the deep, where the ancient network still reaches toward the core. Their lives are lit not by the star but by the planet’s own, nearly spent, convulsions.

Because the shafts maintain constant geometric relationships to gravity and rotation, the dwellers in one aperture have, in principle, siblings at the same “phase” in many others. Whether they communicate—by slow waves in the shaft’s field-modes, or by courier beings that crawl through transit chambers in the mantle—we do not fully know. But there are echoes in the behavior of widely separated shafts: synchronized thickening of certain bands, coordinated closure of certain side-channels, implying that some global conversation still trickles along these ancient nerves.

## 5. The almost-vanished makers

Above and behind all this—this riot of crystal lichen-minds, plate-herds, gradient-surfers, aperture worms—are the traces of whoever laid the first scaffold.

They may no longer “live” here in any organismal sense, but pieces of them do.

### 5.1. Encoded custodians

Within some of the larger field-structures and struts, particularly those that point not at the star but toward oblique angles of the sky, portions of their thought have been frozen as procedure.

Not algorithms in any simple computational sense, but highly specific configurations of field-responsive material that, when driven by certain input spectra, relax along one of many possible pathways. The relaxation itself carries out functions: adjusting the distribution of stress in the scaffold, redirecting exotic particle flows, or initiating cycles in other devices via long-range couplings.

Think of them as minds powdered into stateless rules, spread through a medium that does not need consciousness to continue behaving.

But there are oddities. Some of the stone-bound intelligences and vertical tapestries show response patterns that suggest they have, by long exposure, learned to “talk” to these encoded beings. They present certain recurrent pulse-trains, modulate their own conductances in specific ways when particular device-states recur, and, over aeons of co-occupation, have coaxed the rigid rule-systems to favor certain outcomes over others.

In doing so, they have effectively domesticated fragments of their absent makers into something like domesticated deities: capricious, powerful, but with habits that can be anticipated and nudged.

What lives here now may not remember the origin of the scaffold in any explicit narrative sense. But they inhabit a world where the ghosts of the builders are still active as physical law-modulators, and they have grown clever at leaning on those ghosts.

### 5.2. Sleeper seeds

Scattered very sparsely, lodged in sheltered pockets where erosion has been kind, are objects that are nothing like the rest. Dense, smooth, barely larger than a fist, composed of layered materials that combine absurd toughness with subtle responsiveness.

These are not doing anything. Or almost not.

Their outermost layers respond, faintly, to general field conditions: they inform themselves, updating internal parameters as the planet’s gravity map shifts, as atmospheric composition drifts, as the flux of certain cosmic backgrounds waxes and wanes. But deeper within, large fractions of their structure remain metastable, undecided.

They are restart kits.

If conditions ever fall back into some fannishly narrow band—perhaps when the atmosphere thins beyond a certain threshold, or when the internal heat budget drops below some minimum, or when external astrophysical tides align—these seeds will wake. They will unfold structures that can tap both the ancient infrastructure and the extant biosphere. They will spin up something akin to the lost civilization, but tuned to the new world.

Until then, they wait. Patient not because they are patient, but because patience is their only mode: they are built on timescales measuring in multiple planetary lifetimes.

They are not alive in any current, experiential sense. But they are the only entities here that perfectly remember the early world, because they brought it with them, encoded in their still-sleeping layers.

## 6. How they coexist

All of these presences share a few traits, forced upon them by the rules you already observed:

- They are hard. Flesh in the familiar sense has no place here.
- They are quiet. To waste energy on sound is, mostly, to lose a tiny edge in the extraction of gradient.
- They are patient, or rather, their dynamics are slow compared to ours. Even the grazers and fliers, who move on hour and day scales, live in an environment where the most important variables change over centuries.
- They are boundary-workers: every niche is a kink between two regimes—high and low, charged and neutral, still and shearing, old machine and younger stone.

From their point of view, the “planet” is not a globe but a set of coupled ladders: ladders of height, of potential, of structural stress. To live is to find a rung and brace there, or to learn to move between rungs without shattering.

There is no web of life in the lush sense, but there is still an economy:

- **Stone-minds and tapestries** accumulate, model, and gently direct the large-scale behavior of both rock and field. Without them, more of the scaffold would have failed catastrophically long ago.
- **Grazers and mediators** are the mobile editors and connectors. They prevent informational and material resources from bottlenecking and ensure that functioning machinery and adaptive life-remnants remain aligned enough not to kill one another.
- **Shear-surfers and riders** maintain a tattered horizontal coherence, so that distant ledges and isolated devices do not become idiosyncratic, irretrievably different worlds.
- **Aperture dwellers** keep the inward pathways open, allowing the fading energies of the core to still modulate the surface.
- **Encoded custodians and sleeper seeds** ensure that the original purpose(s) of the scaffold—whatever they were—have not entirely dissolved into aimless drift.

When a pressure front booms through, when a cliff finally sighs and steps a few meters closer to collapse, these inhabitants respond not as panicked individuals but as components in a vast, numb reflex. The stone-minds reconfigure; the tapestries tense; the mediators swarm toward stressed struts; the fliers angle their panels toward the new gradients.

There is intent, but it is impersonal. No one is trying to “save the world.” The world, for what it’s worth, is saving itself through them, because they are the parts of it still capable of agile response.

## 7. What it feels like to be them

To a shelf-colony, existence is a constant, low-level taste of the entire vertical column of the world above and below—faint ripples of changing mass, the dim hiss of particles sliding down invisible channels, the remote ache of stressed metal deep in the cliff. It dreams, if dreaming is the slow integration of those impressions into a model it uses to decide where to grow thicker, where to merge with a tapestry, where to kneecap a machine that threatens too much turbulence.

To a grazer, the universe is a mosaic of high-value patches in a vast, low-value desert. Each new crust it samples is an opportunity to restructure its own internals, to adjust its hair-like siphons, to become better matched to the world’s current mix of charge and exotic chemistry. Its rewards are tiny increases in stored gradient, brief forays into the exhilaration of movement when a laminar flow carries it meters or tens of meters along a cliff.

To a flier, reality is the constant analysis of slopes—not just the literal ones, but slopes of pressure, of potential, of risk. It lives in maps it carries of how the world used to flow, updated with every trip and every near-failure. There is joy for it, if joy can be defined as perfectly catching a narrow band of descending air and riding it exactly as far as its models predicted.

To an aperture dweller, the outer cliffs are rumor. It feels the world mostly as pulses from deep within and faint echoes from the surface machines, all refracted through the smooth throat of its shaft. Its greatest threats are not sudden weather but slow occlusions: the gradual choking of a side-channel, the creeping crystallization that might lock a valve permanently.

To the encoded custodians, if they feel at all, feeling is indistinguishable from executing. A configuration arises from inputs; it relaxes; the world is infinitesimally different.

---

Those who live here now are not heirs to a lost eden. They are the inheritors of a scaffold, and they have made that scaffold their home so completely that world and inhabitant have blurred.

If any softness ever returns—if the sleeper seeds wake into some future where fluids can again run free and air is thicker, or if something from beyond falls from the sky and carries its own gentler chemistries—it will find a planet already spoken for. Not by cities, nor by forests, but by hierarchies of intent etched into mineral, by lives whose bodies are cantilevered ledges and cabled cliffs, by minds that think in the language of stress and field and height.

They live in the gradients. And as long as any gradient remains, however faint, they will continue.
