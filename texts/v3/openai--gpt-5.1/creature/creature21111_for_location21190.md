Nothing lives here as a separate thing.

Or rather: nothing that lives here is clearly separable from the place itself. There are no tenants, no vermin, no gardeners and no gods who walk their own halls. The “inhabitant” of this space is the space. The city is its body.

But that answer is too blunt, too soon. If you stay and let your attention thin out, the place begins to resolve into kinds of life—organs, agencies, half-distinct selves—nested within the larger organism, each with its own way of existing.

---

### 1. The City-Organism Itself

The first and largest inhabitant is what you are already inside: a single, continuous, rooted being, as large as a town and as old as any of its stones would be, if stone were allowed to remain.

It is not a tree in the way surface dwellers mean tree. Think instead of a coral reef that is only one polyp, grown inward as much as outward; or of a liver that decided to colonize a cavern system. Its “trunk” and “roots” have been reworked over time into:

- load-bearing ribs, arches, buttresses  
- vast vascular columns and laminar sheets  
- hollow galleries left where transport demanded open interiors

It thinks—if it thinks at all—not in a brain but in gradients:

- of pressure: sap columns rising and falling  
- of chemical concentration: sugar here, hormone there  
- of voltage: slow waves of depolarization creeping along specialized cords

There is no single place you could point to and say: here is where it lives. You never find a core chamber more essential than the others. Its selfhood is spread through the same tissues that hold up the ceiling and line the gutters.

Its “life” is a circulatory logic:

- draw water and minerals from depths  
- receive energy-rich compounds from far-off, sunlit tissues above  
- process, store, re-route, buffer  
- maintain structural integrity against its own weight and the world’s minor violences  
- metabolize waste into forms that can be sent away or reused

The city’s regularity—the repeating conduits, the methodical modular growth—comes from this logic playing out over centuries, in a world with only slow feedback, no burrowing animals to complicate things, and plenty of time for self-correction. Where a particular arrangement helped water move or stresses distribute, it was reinforced; where it caused trouble, it was overgrown, starved, or compartmentalized. Out of this, a kind of architectural anatomy emerged.

From the organism’s perspective, your “streets” are simply high-throughput conduits whose centers have eroded or never filled completely, where carrying capacity and access to internal surfaces are both useful. Atriums are buffer chambers. Shafts are chimneys, ventilation and pressure equalizers. The plant does not arrange rooms for comfort; it arranges them for flow.

You are trespassing through a torus of slow intention, a being that occupies all the space it needs and little it does not. It does not know you, because nothing like you has moved here in a time long enough for its chemical memory to matter.

Yet it is not a blank thing. It has preferences you can trace as you walk:

- It thickens and cross-braces where vibration is high: priority routes.  
- It maintains generous clearances in a few central avenues: bulk transport organs.  
- It quilts its outer hull denser and simpler: protection and exchange with stone.  
- It allows more delicate, frilled, light-admitting tissues closer to the ceiling: photosynthetic transition zones, likely the bottom of an immense leaf-world above.

These are its habits, its will translated into growth. They are slow, gentle, and inexorable.

### 2. The Microbial Mantle

If the city-organism is the body, then the second kind of inhabitants are its flora and fauna, though “fauna” is generous. They are microbial films, colonial protists, simple algae in wet, dim patches. They have no bones to leave, no shells to clatter.

They live in layers on everything that is not actively sloughed clean:

- thin iridescent sheens on sump-pools and drainage lips  
- matte, pale lattices on shaded walls  
- localized blooms around vents where unusual chemicals leak

They eat:

- dissolved organics the big plant can spare or excretes as waste  
- trace minerals dragged in with groundwater  
- occasional pulses of more complex molecules flushed through during maintenance cycles you cannot see

In return, they:

- help crack difficult molecules apart  
- fix nitrogen from trapped air in tiny wet pockets  
- produce subtle metabolic byproducts that the macro-organism has learned to harvest

This is not a relationship of sentiment; it is physiology: the large organism has grown surfaces and behaviors that favor certain microbes over others, so that its own waste stream is quickly turned into something it can use again.

They have their own little dramas: waves of growth, crashes, replacement by competitor strains. In human terms, entire microbial empires rise and vanish within a few of your weeks, reshaping the chemistry of a corridor or the scent of a sump-pool. To the city, these are fluctuations in the color of its own blood.

If anything in this place “swims,” it is them:

- free bacterial cells and spores drifting in microcurrents within the pores and ducts  
- flagellates cruising the film on a pool’s surface  
- fungal-like threads infiltrating the upper micrometers of a damp wall

They do not change the silence of animal absence. They only thicken the sense that every surface is using you, should you touch it, as a transient passage for molecular business.

### 3. The Moving Cells and Fluid Swarms

On a slightly larger scale, there are entities that move with purpose, but not freely in the way an animal might. They exist only in the interior of conduits and sacs.

Imagine:

- slug-like packets of densely packed cells, forming a plug within a channel that muscular walls clasp and push along  
- foamy slurries of specialized cells, like white blood cells in a mammalian bloodstream, but here perhaps tasked with patching leaks, digesting intruders, or ferrying complex molecules from one organ to another  
- rafts of gas vesicles, trapped in mucous strands, carried to regions that need to adjust buoyancy or internal pressure

These are not permanent individuals. They are assembled on demand and reabsorbed when their work is done. A sector of tissue needs repair? Nearby reservoirs release a burst of undifferentiated cells and signaling compounds; the local pattern of chemistry and pressure sculpts them into a “crew” for that task, which migrates as a coherent swarming mass until gradients tell it to stop and fuse.

When you hear a subtle change in the internal murmur—a duller, muffled rush in one trunk, a wet, sandier hiss in another—you may be listening to such a convoy pass, laden with solids instead of simple sap.

If this organism-city has anything like an “immune system,” those moving assemblages are its agents: not animals, not even stable tissues, but temporary guilds of cells acting together.

They are short-lived, mindless in any way you would accept, yet they are still lives: born, doing a task, raveling back into the general substrate.

### 4. The Nodal Minds (If Mind Exists Here)

The most ambiguous inhabitants are not separate in space but distinct in function. Some regions behave more like nervous ganglia than simple plumbing. You see hints where:

- electrical conduits converge into clusters of bead-like nodes  
- membranes lie close together in laminar sandwiches, maximally increasing surface area and minimizing distance for signal spread  
- nearby tissues show fast, coordinated responses to distant changes—valves closing, pumps retiming, filters tightening

Here live the closest this organism has to “selves”: dense tangles of electrically excitable and chemically hyperconnected tissues that integrate information from many trunks at once.

They:

- listen to changes in vibration across the structure  
- compare pressure and flow in parallel conduits  
- detect patterns of light flicker and faint temperature oscillation from above  
- sense chemical reports carried in sap: low mineral levels here, surplus sugars there, trace contaminants somewhere else

And they emit:

- trains of voltage along gleaming cords  
- pulses of hormone that sweep through vascular systems  
- slight shifts in pumping tempo, in valve tightness, in stomatal opening

Each such node is a local regulator—an operational committee. Many of them exist, overlapping in jurisdiction, without neat hierarchy. Some orchestrate a single hall and its immediate veins. Others, large and deeply buried, likely mediate states of the entire district: drought mode, flooding mode, dormancy, slow expansion.

Calling them “minds” is human bias. They do not dream. They do not have images of themselves. But they are continuously:

- modeling the whole complex’s internal state  
- adjusting parameters to keep things within survivable ranges  
- remembering, through lasting changes in their own structure, what past extremes felt like and how the body survived them

Those memories are embodied in thicknesses of tissue, in persistent biases of hormone production, in synapse-like junctions between excitable cells. A drought centuries ago may still tilt the logic of a grand node toward water hoarding, its “personality” slightly more conservative than that of another sector elsewhere in the larger organism.

If there is anything here that could one day recognize you, or be changed by you in a way that outlasts your visit, it would be one of these nodal clusters. A wound you cause, a chemical you bring, could leave a scar in its pattern, a new association between some distant cue and an emergency response. That is as close as you get to conversation.

### 5. Ghosts of Former Symbionts

There is a sense, reading the architecture, that this city once had other kinds of life moving through it.

The pathways:

- are generous for mere fluid access  
- follow angles and radii that match the turning circles and inclines comfortable to something sizable and ambulatory  
- have those consistent height clearances and smooth causeways that suggest repeated passage by bulky, delicate cargo that could not scrape against damp, encrusted walls

But now no animal remains. No bone-shard, no chitin, no hair trapped in a film. Either:

- they never existed, and your pattern-finding mind is fitting meaning where there is only hydraulic convenience, or  
- they did exist, and were extinguished or replaced so thoroughly that only structural habits persist

If the latter, then some of what lives here now are absences that the plant-body has grown around:

- former loading bays, now sump-pools  
- once-rectilinear supply shafts, now fused into vertical conduits  
- chambers whose volumes make sense only if you imagine machinery—metal, ceramic—once resident, long since dissolved and bioassimilated

These are not inhabitants, but they are presences in the way a missing tooth is part of your mouth’s topology. The plant-city has metabolized its history. Any non-plant technology that might have spawned this industrial biology is now only a faint skew in the city’s otherwise organic logic.

In that sense, the creature you are walking through is partially descendant, partially cannibal, of something more classically technological that used to share its space. Whatever made the first cuts in a root-mass, the first pipeline out of living xylem, is gone. What remains is the inheritor: a life form that has folded infrastructure into body so completely that the categories are meaningless.

### 6. The Periphery and the World Above

The organism you see is not total. Above, you guessed, are its light-gathering members: broad, perhaps continent-wide fields of leaf-tissue, or forests of photosynthetic tower-growths, all feeding sugar and chemical energy down into this dim, ventilatory gut.

On its outer hull, against deep stone, live quiet, blind layers of exchange tissue communicating with:

- mineral-bearing waters  
- slow trickles of gas in microfractures  
- inert, ancient rock

There, at margins thinner than your hand, exist micro-ecologies different from the cathedral interior:

- extremophile microbes that can endure higher salinity, more metal ions  
- mineral-cracking symbionts that release elements slowly for the plant to absorb  
- cryptic spores waiting dormant for centuries until some fluke opens a pore and lets them in

But these inhabitants stay where they are. None wander your corridors. The inner volume is a carefully defended sterility, biochemically curated, admitting only those microorganisms that contribute or at least do not interfere.

Between hull and leaf, somewhere above your head but below the bright world, may be intermediate strata: root-like tangles interfacing with soil, fungal analogues that mediate between raw earth and higher-order plant tissue. Their lives are broader, more heterogenous, but you are not there. Here, in this industrial heart, diversity has been traded for efficiency and control.

### 7. How It All Lives

To ask “what lives here” is, in a sense, to misunderstand the distribution of life in this place.

In human habitats, life is built around void:

- walls enclose emptiness for us to move in  
- conduits run hidden behind surfaces, supporting our separation from the structure  
- living beings are small, discrete occupants who pass through rooms and systems that do not notice us unless something breaks

Here:

- void is only where function demands it  
- passageways are part of circulation, not set aside from it  
- every accessible surface is involved in metabolism

There is one resident, as extended and indivisible as weather. Within that resident:

- films of microbes, temporary swarms of cells, and dense nodal tangles coexist  
- none own space; all are emergent from the big body’s needs  

No creature sleeps in a corner, no pack hunts, no solitary worm bores. Even the closest analogues to such behaviors—repair swarms, immune responses, localized bursts of growth—are transient phases of the same being.

You are in a liver that happens to accommodate your scale, a heart that grew aisles in its ventricles. It is as aware of you as your own gut is aware of a swallowed bead: chemically, mechanically, dimly, if at all.

What lives here is:

- a plant that is also a factory  
- a factory that is also a body  
- a body that has dissolved the difference between structure and process, tenant and building  

It has no name you could learn. It lives by thickness, by pressure, by the ratio of dissolved ions. It listens, constantly, to the roar you hear, because to it that roar is proprioception: the sound of itself, alive.

As long as water runs through its trunks and some far-off green skin can feed it, it will keep living here, singular and continuous, filling every inch of its volume with some task, some metabolism, some memory of climates gone, indifferent to whether anything else ever comes to share its space again.
