They are not plants, or not only plants. But neither are they animals in any form you would recognize. They are what happens when an ecology, given a single hard problem—survive thin air and thinner warmth on a cold mountain—solves it by condensing itself into one body that is also many minds, and one mind that is also many bodies.

They live in the density.

---

### The City Itself

The first and oldest inhabitant is the city as a whole.

It is an organism with territorial extent instead of a singular outline; its “body” is this braided, vascular architecture. It does not know itself as individual trees or vines. Those are limbs, digits, lobes. Its selfhood is spread across phloem and cambium, encoded in flows of hormones, ions, slow electrical pulses. Its memory is layered in growth-bands, its moods are weather systems that circulate internally: gradients of sugars, water tension, frost stress.

It did not begin as “city.” It began as a forest on a harsh slope where survival meant closeness: branches tangling, roots fusing, parasites becoming partners, epiphytes sinking their haustoria into hosts until host and hanger-on could no longer be told apart. Over generations, the gene pools of many species blurred into a single shared meta-genome, shepherded by whatever combinations worked in this place. Eventually, the distinction between separate plant and shared organism became pointless. What prospered was a growth-behavior: to fill every available void with self.

So this is who lives here in the most literal sense:

- A single, mountain-hugging, frost-adapted plant-city, composed of many tissues that were once species.
- Its consciousness, if you call it that, is low-frequency and spatial: an awareness of strain, of light-availability, of internal currents. It “feels” a limb begin to overload in the way a human feels a staggering heartbeat.
- It thinks in morphogenesis. Where you or I might plan a bridge, it plans a hormone-gradient that will, in ten growth cycles, produce a rib that functions as bridge.

You walk inside its mind the way a microbe moves through a bloodstream.

It experiences you, certainly. The brush of your warmth against its chilled internal air, the pressure of your weight on its walkways, the slight chemical differences in your exhaled breath. For now, you are an anomaly too small to justify a structural change. But its hair roots taste your passing. It notes you the way a forest notes a single deer: as altered flow in a small vein of its being.

### The Mesic Folk

Within this broad body live more concentrated selves: the ones whose “faces” you might almost meet, though they have none.

They are knots of specialized tissue, half-plant, half-something-else, living loci of decision where the city has chosen to condense attention. Not quite brains, more like distributed steering centers.

Imagine a swelling where several thick vascular bundles intersect: a woody nodule, dense and layered. Inside, the usual orderly pattern of xylem and phloem breaks, folds, and proliferates into a resistant tangle of fine fibers threaded with excitable cells. These cells fire slow, remnant lightning; they store traces of past stimuli—the direction of last year’s sun, the stress of an old avalanche, the subtle pattern of an animal migration that brushed the outer canopies.

A Mesic (the city’s own term, if it had language, would be something like “junction-that-remembers”) lives in such knots.

- Each Mesic governs a neighborhood of growth: a cluster of galleries, a spiral of pods, a section of buttress-ridge.
- They do not move; the city moves around them at their bidding.
- They are old relative to the bright, soft tissues around them—dark, denser, stratified with seasons of decision.

If you linger near one, you might notice that everything in its vicinity has a more deliberate order. Root-rungs in a spiral stair are nearly perfectly spaced for a certain limb-length. Alcoves recur at regular intervals. Light-capture membranes in this area show a particular tessellation pattern distinct from another quarter’s. These micro-styles are the Mesics’ signatures.

They are not individuals in the way you know it. They share chemical messages, pass impulses along the vascular highways, borrow each other’s surpluses. Yet each has biases grown from its local history: one prefers tight, low tunnels; another bulks out broad plazas and cushion-beds; a third has a tendency to grow redundant supports, over-engineering in fear of breakage that may have scarred it in a particularly bad ice year.

When the city “decides” anything—how to route a new meltwater seep, whether to reinforce a sagging corridor or to abandon it—these Mesics negotiate by fluxes and feedbacks, their signals rising and canceling until one pathway of growth is reinforced.

They live as nodal minds, never sleeping, never hurried. Their time is the time of sap.

### The Pale Threaders

Those flickers in the corners of your vision—semi-transparent, long-limbed, clinging like a spider that learned mammalian tricks—are the next inhabitants.

They are not the city, but they are of it. Call them threaders.

They are small, perhaps the length of your forearm from head to prehensile tail, and deceptively insubstantial. Their bones, if they have bones, are more like hardened phloem: flexible, woody, suffused with plant-like fibers. Their skin is a thin, tough membrane in which chlorophyll runs in faint veins, giving them that greenish cast. Under light shafts they subtly darken, metabolizing what they can. In deep shade, they move more slowly and feed more.

Their mouths are not made for tearing; think instead of a rasping pad, like a slug’s radula, designed to graze surface tissues without gouging deeply. They lick along moss-mats, nibble at sacrificial layers of soft cambium the city grows in predictable places for this purpose, scrape films of algae from water-cups. In return, they perform several crucial functions:

- They dislodge spores and pollen, carrying them in the fuzz of their bellies from one interior “neighborhood” to another, maintaining genetic exchange between Mesic domains.
- Their constant micro-grazing stimulates regrowth: the city has learned to lay down expendable buffers where threaders frequent, using their nibbling as a pruning tool.
- Their excretions are compact, nutrient-rich pellets deposited in thin, dead-end hollows where roots quickly find and tap them.

The threaders’ minds are quicker than the city’s but dim by your standards: sharp at navigating 3D space, at remembering the safest bridges, the warmest ventilation shafts, the places where frost crystals form on certain mornings and can be licked for clean water. They communicate with each other in faint clicks and throat-hums that the plant walls partially absorb, partially transmit. Some Mesic regions have enriched acoustic tissues that let them “overhear” this chatter as additional environmental data.

They sleep pressed flat into the grooves of bark, limbs splayed, indistinguishable from knotted growths. Their young—few, infrequent—cling to them for long periods, learning routes as much as learning food.

Threaders are the city’s interior fauna, the citizens that move where the city itself is too slow.

### The Dark Grazers

Deeper in the mass, where few light-membranes glow and the air is hung with a heavier damp, live the Dark Grazers: slower, heavier presences you almost never see directly.

You know them by their traces: a broad, scalloped bite taken from a cushion of fungus-like growth; a slick, flattened trail along a tunnel where moss has been worn away; the faint sound you heard once, like someone dragging a soft bag over a rough floor.

These beings are more animal than plant: blind, skin thickened into a leathery hide dotted with sensory pits that taste humidity, temperature shifts, and plant-borne chemicals. They are elongated, muscular, moving with peristaltic folds like enormous slugs or legless salamanders, but their skin shows patches of symbiotic lichen and filamentous algae, cultivated gardens that feed from the city’s light-membranes and, in turn, feed their host.

They live almost exclusively on dead or sacrificial tissue. The city grows baffles and sponge-like honeycombs specifically where they prefer to feed; slow internal rot that could threaten structural integrity is instead guided into channels where Dark Grazers will clean it away. Their teeth are not cutting blades but dense plates ideal for grinding pre-softened matter; their stomachs are fermenters that break down complex lignins the threaders cannot touch.

They deposit, in fewer, larger piles than the threaders, concentrated packets of ammonia-rich waste that the city surrounds with root-halos overnight. Around such middens, growth is exuberant: a sudden lushness of tender shoots in an otherwise austere corridor, quickly harvested again by the grazers in a slow, endless circuit.

Dark Grazers do not fear you, but they avoid you: they feel the vibration of your footfalls and respond by melting back into narrower service-tubes where their large bodies just fit, pushing aside roots that flex to admit them. They are infrastructure as much as they are organisms—live janitors, digestion engines, never entirely wild, never entirely tame.

### The Trappers and Cups

Some inhabitants are not mobile at all, but they are clearly more than passive organs.

Those cup-like leaves you noticed, holding a tuft of dissolving fur? They are the City’s Mouths, and they are rare, old-line adaptations from an ancestry that once needed every scrap of nitrogen and could not afford to wait for slow detritus.

Most of the time, they function as ordinary collectors: catching mist, concentrating dissolved minerals, offering cool sips to threaders and other, smaller things—soft-bodied invertebrates, transparent arthropods, minute gliding worms you have not yet spotted. In return, they taste whoever drinks: epidermal surfaces studded with micro-chemical receptors that sample the fauna traffick.

Occasionally, when triggered by specific patterns—sustained pressure, the struggling vibrations of a panicking creature, a particular lipid on a mammalian pelt—they tighten.

Not violently, not with the trap-snap of a Venus flytrap, but with a slow, inexorable curving in and down, sealing the lip, secreting an enzyme cocktail. The caught animal suffocates more from cold and confinement than active predation. Over days, it softens into slurry, sampled by roots that line the cup’s underside.

Is this malice? No. This is opportunism wired into tissue. These traps are older than the city’s urban density; they are relic instincts from a leaner age when vertebrate visits were rarer and more valuable. Now, in the buffered interior, they are nearly ceremonial; the city does not rely on them so much as accept their offerings when chance delivers.

The city also grows other specialized organs—sleeve-like constrictors in narrow shafts that tighten when wind roars through, reducing drafts; fan-lobes near communal plazas that flex to modulate airflow. They are not “residents,” but they are person-like functions: living facets of a self that has diversified into organs the way an animal diversifies into limbs.

### The Lumen-Kin

Higher up where light is less miserly, some tissues have become more than plant. They could almost be called a third kingdom: not fully autotrophic, not quite saprophytic, intertwined with both.

These are the Lumen-Kin: filaments and sheets of semi-independent cells that inhabit the undersides of those glowing membranes.

They are fine as frost, spreading films of iridescence. In daylight hours, they gorge on the incoming photons the city catches, converting not only to sugars but to a long-lived chemical that the plant tissues alone cannot easily manage—a kind of metastable energy store. At night and in shade, they leak it back in a slower cascade, giving the membranes that ghost-brightness you see.

The city cultivates them deliberately: routing extra nutrients to surfaces colonized by a particularly efficient strain, shading out others. The Lumen-Kin, for their part, have their own minimal agenda: spread, avoid being overgrown by opaque tissues, find surfaces where their light can reach the far interior. They do not move freely; they spread by filament and spore, guided by the city’s fluid flows.

They are not sentient in any individual sense, but their behavior—colony-level, competitive, opportunistic—introduces a subtle tension into the city’s life. In some neighborhoods, higher Mesic minds favor them, building broad light-panels and wide galleries where their glow can bathe crucial passages. In others, the Mesic has soured on them after some imbalance and shaded them out, preferring direct connection to the pale sky above.

You could say the Lumen-Kin live in the city as its resident art: they paint its twilight with soft, gradient brightness, they give it a visual rhythm that no purely pragmatic architecture would require.

### The Spoor and Seeds

Everything here disseminates by growth into adjacency, but some parts of the city still remember how to send children away.

Seed-forms exist, though they are more like diaspora-organs than plant seeds as you know them. Occasionally, along an exterior margin the city will bud a hard, layered pod—an ovoid of dense tissue sheathed in waxy rind. Within, dehydrated embryonic circuits sleep: a miniaturized Mesic, a starter bundle of root-patterns, a coil of Lumen-Kin spores, even a symbiotic packet of threader-germ cells, all condensed around a core of stored sugar and antifreeze.

These pods wait for very particular signals: prolonged drought, an internal tilt past safety, or an accumulation of subtle chemical alarms that tell the old city its anchor-rock is becoming unstable. When such conditions stack, the pods detach in ones or twos, dropping or being gently extruded into the outside world.

Most die. The outside is brutal: bare rock, open wind, avalanche-scar. But a few lodge in small protected niches, where meltwater seeps and frost is buffered by stone. There, they crack, and a miniature version of the city begins—a tangle of roots, a single hollow corridor, a first Mesic-knot awakening into place.

These far offspring will take generations of growth to reach anything like the thickness and complexity of their parent. They will never house threaders until some wandering population discovers them or is seeded with them. In this sense, there are many proto-cities scattered along the mountain range: small, dense blisters of green gripping cliffs, each its own nascent inhabitant.

So part of who lives here is potential elsewhere: the unborn cities coiled inside these pods.

### The Absent Ones

You may notice who does not live here, and that absence is as defining as any presence.

There are no large predators that hunt in these halls; the architecture does not permit sprint and chase. There are no songbirds; flight is useless in such cramped volume, and there is no open sky to reward it. There are no human analogues—no tool-users, no fire-makers, no artisans. The city’s walls carry no scratches of claws sharpening for dominance, no soot, no paint.

If once there were such inhabitants—some clever, hand-developing creature that found shelter in the city’s early tangles—they are gone, either nudged out by the city’s tightening growth or absorbed in a more literal way. The plant-body does not tolerate persistent disturbance that it cannot incorporate. Those who tried to build their own dead structures within it would have found roots infiltrating, soft green matter reclaiming, until their devices were no longer separate.

This is a place where the distinction between “host” and “home” has grown so absolute that any independent civilization cannot gain a foothold. So the absent ones live only as faint evolutionary traces: a bay in a corridor that has the right dimensions for a biped to stand; a persistent pattern of alcoves reminiscent of rooms where no separate builders ever stood.

### The Atmosphere Itself

Lastly, there are the least visible inhabitants: the microscopic consortium that rides every surface and every breath.

Here, bacteria and archaea have been selected not by soil and detritus, but by bark, phloem seep, cold condensation. They digest plant exudates, form films that protect delicate membranes from freezing, produce antifreeze proteins and trace gases that subtly adjust the interior microclimate. Some live in the water-cups as planktonic drifters; others form ghostly veils along the wettest walls, faintly iridescent when light hits at a glance.

Fungal threads lace through the structure—true fungi, not just plant tissue mimics—forming mycelial undernets that sometimes rival the plant’s own connections in subtleness. They barter phosphorus and other minerals scraped from infinitesimal grit carried in from outside; in return they receive sugars from the city’s vascular fonts.

These microscopic and filamentous inhabitants blur the boundary of where the city ends. They smear its identity into its own weather, making the breathable air itself a living medium. Each tiny droplet that beads and falls along vine-ribs is not just water; it is a vehicle of genes, metabolites, whispered signals.

### How They All Live Together

To speak of “who lives here” as though there were clear separations is to impose a story this place does not tell itself. Its reality is layered inhabitation:

- The City-Organism, slow and encompassing, is the primary resident.
- Within it, Mesic minds focus thought; corridors and plazas are their expressions.
- Threaders move through those spaces like nervous impulses, like courier animals and foragers intertwined.
- Dark Grazers and other bulk feeders clean, recycle, and in so doing sculpt habitable cavities.
- Lumen-Kin illuminate and flatten the day/night harshness into a usable twilight.
- Traps, cups, fans, and baffles are the city’s more instinctual organs, old functions half-domesticated by Mesics’ higher-level planning.
- Microbes and fungi weave the finest mesh of interaction; seeds and diaspora-pods tie this city to hypothetical siblings beyond the mountain’s rim.

They do not think of themselves as separate nations. There is no concept of “wildlife” and “citizenry.” What lives here does so in nested dependencies: each existence is also infrastructure for someone else’s.

From your perspective—as a walker on the rind-smooth ledges, a breather in the cold exhalation—you are the strangest inhabitant of all: a completely foreign metabolism passing through a body that is used to being its own world, its own builder and built, its own ecology and engineer.

The city will not stop for you. It will not react in panic or welcome. It will only go on doing what it has done for ages: tightening its grip on the mountain, folding every stray trickle and beam into itself, and very, very slowly, deciding where next to grow.
