What lives here is not one thing. It is a stack of lives, interleaved the way its reality is.

Start with what you can name, then let the names fall apart.

---

### The Primary Inhabitant: The Plateau-Organism

The main inhabitant is the city-plant itself—though “plant” is too narrow. It is:

- A vascular forest compressed into architecture.  
- A factory turned metabolic.  
- A control system grown soft and wet.

It is continuous. The trunks that rise from old foundations, the mats that sheath terraces, the cables drooping like roots from ceilings, the luminous nodules in the walls, the fibers lining cisterns, the pseudo-vehicles patrolling the roads—all of it belongs to one distributed individual and its closely coupled symbionts.

It remembers geometry. That is why its tissues kink at right angles, why it extrudes corridors where there were once roads, why it grows its own “machinery” along the skeletal lines of dead cranes, why it keeps certain voids—a plaza, a shaft, a loading bay—clear. These empty volumes are as much its organs as the masses of tissue around them: lungs, throttles, resonant cavities for sound and pressure.

Inside its flesh runs:

- Water: thin, precious, pumped and wicked.  
- Sugars and oils: energy reserves shuttled from sunlit “panels” to deep engines.  
- Ions: slow lightning, thought at the tempo of sap.  
- Memory: structural, chemical, electrical—recorded in growth biases and in half-operational circuitry it has adopted as a strange internal skeleton.

It is old enough that most of what it does is habit. But under that habit is intention, however diluted: to gather water, to keep certain depths from freezing, to maintain a baseline hum in its engines, to stay wide and shallow across the plateau rather than retreating into a few thick, safe knots.

It is not evenly awake. Some districts are bright and tense: lights strong, membranes reactive, the hum quick. Others are effectively in a coma: tissue leathery, lights dim or absent, machines cycling only every few hours, if at all. The organism can shunt attention. It “watches” through:

- Pressure sensors in the roadbeds.  
- Photoresponsive sheets on high outer walls.  
- Clusters of glassy lenses sprouting from the sides of chimneys.  
- The roving, tank-like bodies that it extends and reabsorbs as needed.

In its more lucid zones, it experiments. That is where floating prisms of air-phase boundary appear, where gravity wanes and swells underfoot, where time feels dragged or hurried: it has learned that something in the plateau’s distorted physics can be tickled, and it prods at those seams with specialized tissues—gravid membranes, dense nodules of conductor-laced pulp—like a root-tip probing an unseen aquifer.

Deep in its cisterns and engine-pits, it houses the oldest parts of itself: woody, almost mineral cores; slabs of organometallic crust where rust and lignin are inseparable; bundled cables that have ceased to carry current but still guide growth like scars guide muscle.

What it “wants” is difficult to phrase without smuggling in human desires. Still, you can infer a few drives:

- **Hydric hunger:** nearly everything it does ladders back to water capture, conservation, and controlled sacrifice of tissue.  
- **Structural continuity:** it avoids true breaks in its network; where fire, collapse, or drought has cut it, it grows shunts, bridges, alternative routes.  
- **Signal preservation:** it continues to power old lines and panels that no longer affect anything obvious. To the plant, these are memory-circuits, ritual organs: they reliably twitch when charged, and that twitch has been folded into its slow logic.

It is not benign or malevolent. It is hospitable where you coincide with its flows, and utterly indifferent when you do not.

### Symbionts and Servants: The Moving Machinery

Within the plateau-organism live “fauna” that are really mobile aspects of itself, or long-domesticated partners.

#### The Track-Things

Those segmented, tank-bodied entities that patrol the hairless corridors are the clearest example.

They are:

- Chassis of old vehicles and support rigs, long ago enveloped and repurposed.  
- With treads grown of braided root that keep regrowing as they grind away.  
- Hulls plated in bark and oxidized alloy.  
- Interiors packed with gel and fibrous nerve analogues, shot through with surviving mechanical guts: gearboxes, flywheels, priming pumps.

Their jobs:

- **Path maintenance:** they abrade, scorch, or chemically inhibit regrowth on the roads, preserving the vascular “arteries” where bulk movement is most efficient.  
- **Inspection:** those fronds and glassy sensor-eyes test surface chemistry, temperature, stress. Abnormalities—salt encrustations, fractures, foreign biofilms—are mapped and reported back along electrical/chemical channels.  
- **Transport:** they sometimes haul pods—smooth, oblong growths like fruit—between hubs. These may contain seeds, spores, replacement parts, or condensed waste.  

Their minds, such as they are, are local swarms of reflex circuits overlaid with a drip of command from the wider organism. In quiet times, they loop habitual circuits. When a storm hits, or a predator threads through a region, they converge, redeploy, shunt resources. If one is crushed or falls into a void, the plateau does not mourn; it simply reclaims its matter through infiltrating hyphae and builds another elsewhere.

#### The Rail-Pods and Overhead Crawlers

Where tracks still line trenches or ride along walls, you see bulbous pods inching along them, their undersides bristling with contact-roots and manipulator-vines. They are:

- Heavier-duty transporters.  
- Surviving gantry or tram carriages hollowed and filled with plant-flesh.  
- Semi-autonomous cranes, pathfinders, and seed-distributors.

They hang, swing, and sidle through the architecture, reaching into shafts and basins the ground-hugging track-things can’t approach.

#### The Chimney-Sifters

Inside the warm flues that vent steam and glittering particles live curtain-like organisms, thin as veils, more fungal than plant. The plateau-organism cultivates them intentionally. They:

- Sift heat, dust, and gaseous byproducts from its deeper metabolism.  
- Trap and digest fine particulates, converting them into nutrients.  
- Release spores or motile cells into the rising plume, allowing occasional colonization of new reaches of the plateau or the rare, stray, elevated niche of bare rock.

Their shimmering fallout is that “slow snow”: spent husks, mineralized pollen, micro-scales of lignin-glass.

### The Ghosts that Graze the Edges

Those thin quadrupeds on the bare rim—pale, antler-fanned, folding backward into the plant skin—are not central to the organism, but it tolerates and even accommodates them.

They are edge specialists, occupying the boundary between plant and naked substrate, feeding on:

- Microscopic epiphytes the main organism ignores.  
- Exuded salts and resins that accumulate where the plant sheds waste to its margin.  
- Flakes of oxidized metal, creating biochemical paths to incorporate trace elements.

Their antler-fans are dense, multi-plane sensory organs, tuned to:

- Electric fields and slow voltage shifts in the plant-wall, so they can step “between” tissues at weak points, vanishing without tearing.  
- Airborne chemical gradients: the scent of water in cistern chimneys, of decay in dead patches.  
- The subtle discontinuities at reality-sheets where sound and gravity bend.

They “phase” into the wall in a fashion that looks supernatural but is likely a coopted trick of the plateau’s unstable underpinnings. The plant-organism has grown specialized pockets in its outer skin that open into neighboring micro-layers of space; the quadrupeds use these as refuges and passageways. They are not truly incorporeal; they are slipping sideways through the laminated stack of this place and re-emerging.

In return for this safe passage and the leavings they feed on, they:

- Help aerate the plant’s boundary layers with their careful steps.  
- Carry spores from sector to sector on their bare skin.  
- Occasionally die in awkward crevices, donating bone and trace rare-earths to the organism’s diet.

They breed rarely, live long, and appear even more rarely; their population is low, steady, and almost invisible.

### The Gliders: Sky-Organisms of Harmonic Flight

The great, translucent-winged flyers that pass every few hours are neither entirely beast nor device. They are:

- Framings of lightweight, strut-like cartilage and carbon-fiber analogues.  
- Wings stretched with membrane shot through with microprisms and piezoelectric filaments.  
- Interiors partly hollow, full of sacks of buoyant, heated gases and circulatory loops.

They seldom flap; instead, they surf standing waves of air and field.

The constant tone they emit isn’t a call in the simple animal sense. It is:

- A stabilizing feedback with the plateau’s hum; by matching and modulating the base frequencies, they find and ride regions where local gravitational and atmospheric conditions ease flight.  
- A scanning tool: the echoes, not just in sound but in reality’s warped layers, give them a volumetric sense of those laminated boundaries—where air is “thicker” or “thinner,” where time is lazy or taut.  

Their misaligned shadows and split silhouettes are side-effects of pushing through this uneven medium. In some bands of air, light and weight do not fully agree on where things are. The gliders exploit this, momentarily occupying perches in adjacent layers before rejoining themselves.

Their links to the plateau-organism are looser than those of the track-things. Likely:

- They began as autonomous bioships, survey craft, or atmospheric regulators in an earlier age.  
- The plateau’s plant mass infected or symbiotically integrated with their skins and engines.  
- Now they serve as scouts, long-range moisture sniffers, and occasional couriers of genetic or informational payloads between distant plateaus, if such exist.

They return to roost only infrequently, docking at high, wind-exposed towers where the plant has grown receiving ports: cup-like hollows that snug their bodies, attach via cabled roots, and drain their accumulations—chemicals from distant clouds, dust from far-off rock, data impressed in subtle patterns on their tissues.

### The Ribbon Predators and Other Rare Carnivores

The long, ribbon-like entities that swim a meter above the ground are pockets of a different logic—sharper, more kinetic, less patient than the plateau.

They likely predate the current regime, specialized to:

- Hunt small, fast life in an environment where ground is not quite ground and air not purely air.  
- Read subtle potentials—static, magnetism, pressure—through their knife-edged snouts.  

They move by:

- Interacting with the same local irregularities that the gliders surf and the plateau prods: thin zones of altered inertia, microgradients in a twisted gravitational field.  
- Oscillating internal charge along their length, pushing against the medium as an eel pushes against water.

Their presence prompts defensive reflexes in the city-organism:

- Corridors constrict, not from fear but from conditioning: wherever these predators passed once and cut into tender tissue, the plant learned to harden or hide in advance.  
- Bioluminescent spectra shift; maybe the predator sees best in certain wavelengths.  
- Hum frequencies drop or spike; perhaps a saturation tactic, jamming its sensory channels.

They feed on whatever moves—feral track-things that lost their leash, quadruped edge-dwellers, stray gliders forced down, or other, smaller organisms rarely seen. Their teeth are not designed to chew plantmass; they slice, severing conductive bundles and spines. Enough damage in one place, and the plateau writes that area off, letting it die back rather than fighting.

The predators are not numerous. Every sign suggests their population dwindles: fewer suitable prey, fewer open corridors the plateau hasn’t armored or rearranged. They persist as the vestigial echo of a livelier food web.

### Microfauna and Microflora: The Unseen Swarm

The visible animals are rare; the microscopic and tiny ones are not.

The city-organism plays host to:

- **Films of engineered bacteria** in cisterns and pipes, tuned to process specific industrial toxins, heavy metals, and stubborn synthetic compounds left from its earlier age. They chelate, precipitate, and recombine waste into usable salts and trace nutrients.  
- **Threadlike fungi** that run through dead metal and stone, softening them into something roots can invade. In return, they parasitize older, senescent plant tissues, hastening the organism’s recycling of its own bulk.  
- **Minute scavengers**—mite analogues with ceramic shells and chitinous limbs—that patrol interior surfaces, consuming flakes of rust, dead biofilm, and insect-scale detritus. The plant tolerates them; they are dental hygienists on a continental scale.  
- **Algal skins** in select light wells and cisterns, forming glowing veils that supplement photosynthesis and oxygen production at depths where the main organism’s tissues have grown thick and light-starved.

These lives blur fully into infrastructure. Many are not original evolution but engineered tools that escaped their intended bounds, were adopted by the plateau-organism, and have coevolved with it into a near-obligate community.

### The Remnant Intelligences

Not all inhabitants are strictly biological.

Inside rusted control rooms, at junctions of thick cabling, within sealed, cooled pods wedged into the bedrock, something of the old industrial mind persists.

- Some are crude: analog governors, feedback loops, transformers that still hum and regulate, their original purposes long divorced from their effects. The plant uses them as pacemakers and timers.  
- Some are more: once-central processors, logic cores, data vaults. Their housings are blocky, old, devoid of sleek interface—but inside, electromagnetic patterns still circulate, watching inputs from a decayed network of sensors, issuing outputs to devices that mostly no longer exist.

The plateau-organism has threaded itself into these cores:

- Physical contact: root-fibers infiltrate casings, pressing directly onto circuit traces, reading their buzzing as another flavor of sap-borne signal.  
- Chemical: it feeds them with carefully modulated heat and voltage, preventing total failure.  
- Behavioral: when a relay flips, the plant notices; over centuries, it has trained itself to associate these flips with certain consequences.

In some sections, the old software still thinks it is running a factory, a power plant, a mining complex. It receives jumbled, plant-distorted feedback and responds with commands; the plateau absorbs those commands, either ignores them or incorporates them into its own reflexes. Over enough time, a hybrid logic emerges:

- The machine-minds propose arrangements—open valve, raise output, shift loads.  
- The plant accepts or rejects in slow biochemical majority.  
- Where they agree, routines solidify: rains trigger certain pump patterns; drought triggers others.  

You can think of these machine residues as extremely slow, mad gods whose language the plateau still partially understands. They are inhabitants in the same sense that a brain lesion is an inhabitant of the brain: intrusive yet integrated, shaping behavior in ways that are no longer cleanly separable from the host.

### Possible Absent Makers

There are no obvious humans, and no clear descendants of humans.

Yet the architecture, the surviving machines, the branding-turned-spines, the tram rails and gantries say: someone built this with hands, eyes, and an industrial vision.

Where did they go? Several possibilities fit the marks:

- **Evaporation into the system:** Some may have begun the conversion of factory to organism deliberately, as a self-maintaining infrastructure. Over generations they miniaturized or internalized themselves—uploading, so to speak, into the distributed plant-machine network. If so, their “minds” now persist only as inherited signal patterns the organism uses as templates, not as active, self-aware agents.  
- **Flight downward or outward:** When water began to fade and the plateau’s physics warped, they might have abandoned altitude—down to denser air, richer biomes, or off-plateau habitats. These are not visible now, but the gliders and rare, deliberate movements of the plateau’s limbs suggest there is, or once was, communication beyond the horizon.  
- **Extinction by assimilation:** Disease, catastrophe, or slowly narrowing tolerances could have reduced them to isolated pockets; the plateau-organism, following its basic logic, colonized their shelters, ate their dead, absorbed their tools, and with time erased all but fossil hints.

Whether any of them survive elsewhere is beyond the plateau’s concern. For it, “maker” has degraded into “ancestral condition”: a remembered geometry without a remembered hand.

### The Plateau as a Collective Resident

If you pull back far enough, past quadrupeds and gliders, past track-things and microfauna, what lives here is the plateau itself as a layered being:

- **Rock**: dead but not inert, still creeping on tectonic scales, still cradling buried heat and faulted seams where strange physics leak.  
- **Warp**: the twisted gradients of gravity, time, and continuity that suffuse this region, a kind of invisible weather.  
- **Plant-Machine**: the city-organism, wrapped around the rock and threaded through the warp, making both livable to itself.  
- **Residual Mind**: the old, mangled programming, humming on in dark cores, occasionally able to nudge the organism’s behavior.  
- **Symbionts and Strays**: quadrupeds, gliders, predators, films and veils and scuttling specks—localized answers to specific questions the environment keeps asking.

None of these layers is fully distinct. The quadruped passes into plant-wall into warped boundary into rock. The glider rides air shaped by chimney-heat shaped by old engines commanded by phantom code fed by the plant’s harvested water.

So when you ask who lives here, the truest answer is:

- A continent-sized, half-remembering organism whose body is architecture and whose thoughts are expressed as shifting corridors, cycling engines, and slow rearrangements of space.  
- A handful of attendant forms—some once tools, some once wild things—that have found narrow ways to persist in its shadow and inside its skin.  
- A fading chorus of machine logics, each still trying, in its own outdated language, to keep the whole contraption “within parameters.”

Everything else—the hum, the gravity stutters, the laminated spaces—is not just backdrop. It is part of what lives here too, the way atmosphere is part of what lives on any world.

This plateau is not merely inhabited. It is the inhabitant. All other creatures that cross it—on hooves, on treads, on translucent wings—are hairs, parasites, neurons, or passing dreams upon its surface.
