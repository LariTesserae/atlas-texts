They live at the same pitch as the place—nothing here is quiet inside.

Start with the ones you almost miss, because they are not bodies but tendencies.

### The Field-Workers

Whatever minds laid the monoliths and the skeletal frameworks are no longer here in any obvious, centralized way. If they have cities, they are somewhere else. What abides in the basin instead is their distributed attention.

It shows itself as behavior, not as a visible form: correlations between things that should not be correlated, constraints that flex like muscle. A section of slope slumps, and before the debris has finished sliding, the nearest instrument has already re-tuned local wind and water so that the new scar is cross-cut by five infant channels, not one. Ice grains coming off a fall curve into distinct plumes that deposit exactly where root density is lowest. The basin is full of these “corrections,” constant, low-amplitude edits, as if some invisible multitude is walking the landscape with their hands out, making small adjustments.

That multitude is not a set of operators at consoles; it is an ecosystem of algorithms, old and experimental, running inside the devices and through the fields they cast. They are not passive software. Each one has thresholds, preferences, even something like a temperament: some favor stability, some favor diversity of pattern, some are tuned to maximize biological throughput, some to push the geology harder. They argue in the only medium available to them: the arrangement of energy.

Where their domains overlap, you get strange, emergent compromises. A slope both wants to fail catastrophically and wants to retain a particular mosaic of wet and dry patches. The resulting behavior is neither fully a landslide nor fully a terrace, but a slow, stair-stepped cascade of soil that moves a little every hour. To a geologist it would look uncanny: too rhythmic, too segmented.

This swarm of logics is one kind of inhabitant. It does not think in scenes or stories. It thinks in flow regimes, in ranges of variance, in resonance bands. It is the basin’s weather-brain and erosion-brain and fertility-brain, all layered and occasionally at cross-purposes, yet bound by overarching constraints a long-vanished designer set: nothing gets so stable that it stops changing; nothing collapses so hard that the capacity to regrow is lost.

If there are still “owners,” this is the form their will takes here.

### The Machine-Bodies

Closer to what we’d call organisms are the built structures themselves—not just the obvious monoliths and frameworks, but the less visible mesh.

The crystal-like pillars are not mere terminals. Inside their milky volume runs a slow architecture: channels that grow and seal, nodules that appear, glow, and then dim. Over long watches you would see that each pillar is rearranging itself, as if it were continuously prototyping its own internal organs. They do not share a single blueprint; each one is exploring a slightly different configuration of pathways and resonant modes, testing how best to couple to the chaotic environment.

They are alive in a mineral way: they metabolize difference. The torrent at their base, the seesaw of wind pressure, the passing waves of electric charge in the sky—all of these are inputs they swallow, digest, and re-express as subtle field adjustments and light.

They do not defend themselves because they do not need to. Their “skin” is not a coating but a controlled boundary layer of organized force at the interface between their substance and the world. When a branch hits, it feels a surface that is briefly as hard as diamond at the exact contact point and moment, then softens again. Mosses don’t colonize them not because they kill them, but because the necessary micro-turbulence for spores to stick never stabilizes on their faces. The pillars constantly erase the tiny footholds that colonization would require.

More motile are the skeletal arrays. Their members extend and retract like limbs that never tire. They are not building some static final shape. They are using their joints as tools to reach into the living matrix: touching, sampling, nudging. Their filaments are sensors and also grafts. You see a fine line run into a tree root, but what you don’t see is that the line bifurcates at the cellular scale, following the root’s own finest hairs, braiding with them, sometimes entering their tissue.

In these couplings, data and chemistry move both ways. The tree gets better access to trace elements or a slightly warmer microclimate; the framework gets high-resolution feedback on how that tree’s physiology responds to minute shifts in light, moisture, and field. Over years, the framework’s posture is the record of its learning: which contact points it retained, which it abandoned, which parts of the forest it cultivated into thicker knots of growth and which it let thin out.

The basin contains many such bodies smaller than the first ones you notice: truncated pylons buried almost to their caps, disk-like nodes flush with the mud, grids of hairline conductors suspended between reeds like invisible spiderwebs. Together, they make an artificial root network interleaved with the biological one. They don’t so much occupy the basin as infiltrate it.

### The Symbiont Flora

The plants that brush against those filaments are not merely beneficiaries; over generations, they have become co-designers.

Some species have developed tissues that are particularly good at coupling to the machines’ fields: juicy, ion-rich stems; leaf undersides lined with cells that behave like miniature capacitors. They do better in the perturbed microclimates, so natural selection favors those traits. Their descendants are plants whose very architecture presumes the presence of invisible gradients set by the devices.

Look closely at those tight, glossy leaves: many are not arranged for maximum flat light interception like the leaves of a typical temperate plant. Instead, they form stacked layers with slight angular offsets that catch and diffuse the sort of polarized, scattered glow that pervades the basin. On their surfaces, patterns of stomata (the breathing pores) cluster in whorls and lines that map to the standing interference patterns in the air—these plants are optimizing gas exchange not just for CO₂ and O₂, but for an electrically restless atmosphere.

Their roots are even stranger. Traditional deep-tap strategies are rare; instead, there are laceworks of fine roots that sweep laterally, sampling wherever the field network is densest. In those root mats, certain tissues are consistently adjacent to machine filaments. Chemical analysis would show novel compounds there: long, flexible molecules that align in the presence of gentle fields, effectively turning patches of root into reconfigurable antennae.

The plants use this sensitivity to time their own events. Flowering, spore release, even the nightly rhythmic droop and lift of leaves are keyed not only to temperature and moisture, but to large-scale shifts in the basin’s acoustic and electromagnetic standing waves. When the occasional global “alignment” sweeps through and the noise crystallizes into music, half the basin’s flora twitches, all at once, nudged through internal phase changes: crystals precipitating in vacuoles, enzymes flipping from inactive to active, growth hormones released in synchrony.

Some species have taken this further. There are low, cup-like plants along the water that glow faintly from within. They are batteries as well as organs—storing energy from earlier alignments and bleeding it out as light, heat, and field at their own schedule, creating micro-pockets of normed conditions where certain insects and microbes thrive. In exchange, those organisms bring in nutrients or carry pollen. These cups are half plant, half environmental appliance.

In the mosses and filamentous mats that sheet over rocks and roots, luminescent patterns are not random. When disturbed, they flash in complex wavefronts, not just simple flares. The patterns echo the basin’s larger resonance structures in miniature, like tiny training grounds. The organisms that carry such responses are practicing, in their own biochemical way, the basin’s larger game of controlled chaos.

### The Swarmers and Herders

Insects and insect-analogues here are more than opportunistic scavengers of the abundance. They form another layer of “processing.”

Swarms gather in the slightly warmer, slightly more stable bubbles where skeletal frameworks bend the gradients. At first glance they are gnats, midges, beetle-wings; in aggregate they add that high static hiss to the soundscape. Yet the density of their clusters is not just about food. They are attracted to specific field configurations. Each kind of swarmer prefers a different pitch of hum, a different granularity of flicker. Some glory in the vortex edges of airflows, others hang precisely where multiple interference patterns cross.

Their bodies are tuned accordingly. Many have metallic flecks, crystalline inclusions, or feathery antennae that resonate faintly in the fields. The machine network uses them the way a gardener uses bees. When the system wants to move certain microbes, certain spores, it adjusts the local pattern signature; the favored swarmers are drawn, they pick up the cargo passively, and carry it along their own foraging routes.

In turn, the swarmers exploit the network. A particular species of six-winged flier, for example, has learned (by whatever evolutionary or behavioral path) that an upcoming whole-basin “alignment” is a perfect moment to mass-mate. Their nervous systems lock more easily into a coherent rhythm when the air and water already hum in step. So they cluster, waiting, in the hollows of plants whose roots are in tight machine contact; they use subtle shifts in leaf turgor (influenced by the network) as an early warning that an alignment is building.

Other arthropod-like forms are more solitary but still plugged into the general field. Some beetle-things with luminous elytra show coordinated, wave-like flashing that travels from patch to patch across hundreds of meters. To our eye it’s just beauty; to them it’s information about local gradients, predator density, and the timing of machine pulses. The devices allow this by letting their own emissions be slightly modulated wherever the beetles are densest: a side-channel for a smaller, faster conversation.

### The Bulk-Fauna

The heavier shapes you catch in the thickets are the basin’s movers of mass and meaning. They range from knee-high to the size of small vehicles, built not for grace but for staying upright in a world that won’t sit still.

Their common features: multiple points of contact with the ground, redundantly jointed limbs, bodies that can deform more than break. Some move on six or eight legs, some on four with prehensile forelimbs for bracing. To live where soil can liquefy under you and water courses can appear overnight, you must be ready to redistribute your stance in an instant.

They are not delicate browsers. They crash through the vegetation, bite off whole clumps of reeds, tear moss-bundles from fallen logs. The basin’s plant productivity is so high that leaving ragged stumps and scars is not a problem; regrowth is part of the rhythm. In fact, the machines count on it. The turbulence those bodies create—trampled paths, churned-up mud, disturbed root mats—keeps the ecosystem in a patchwork of successional stages, continuously re-seeding the early, explosive growth that the system favors.

Many of these animals carry their own versions of the plants’ antennae. Along their spines or flanks run ridges of specialized tissue—fluid-filled sacs, cartilage spines, arrays of vibrissae-like hairs—that twitch in response to deep, low pulses from the monoliths. They cannot interpret those pulses conceptually, but their bodies have learned reflexes: move to higher ground in this pattern, stay low and still in that one, head toward the warmer cavities when the high hissing noise shifts phase.

Predators among them are ghosts until they strike. They use the noise not as a problem but as camouflage. Their hunting strategy is to sync their own movement to the most local, dominant rhythm: stepping when a nearby fall booms, launching when a gust rips through the reeds. In quiet pockets they are less comfortable; you see them skirt the more stable microclimates maintained by certain frameworks unless prey density makes the risk worth it.

Some of the bulk-fauna maintain almost symbiotic ties with particular machine installations. Generations of them have learned that near a given framework there will always be slightly lusher forage, slightly easier walking (because the devices subtly firm the ground beneath regular game trails), and occasional density of prey. In return, their habitual paths help the system move seeds and spores in specific directions, shaping the mosaic of plant communities. In this sense, they are semi-domesticated by an owner that never appears.

### The Quiet Thinkers

Every dense, disturbance-driven ecosystem on Earth has given rise, sooner or later, to creatures that think beyond instinct—corvids, cephalopods, primates, social carnivores. This basin is no different.

Intelligence here favors those who can read patterns in chaos. One such lineage moves mostly by climbing, occupying the three-dimensional lattice of young trees and frameworks. Picture a creature about the size of a large cat, but lighter-boned, with four primary limbs for grasping and at least two smaller, more delicate manipulators clustered near its head. Its skeleton is partially cartilaginous, swaying with the trees instead of fighting them, able to loop a whole body around a trunk like a tightened belt when the wind throws everything sideways.

Its sensory organs are tuned for the dark. Wide, reflective eyes pick up the faintest bioluminescence. Ear structures are complex, capable of disentangling something like “signal” from the basin’s roar. But more distinctive is a ridge along its skull and down its spine: a comb of soft, conductive cilia that stand up at particular frequencies of the monoliths’ hum. That ridge does not give it language, but it gives it a bodily intuition for when the basin is about to shift mode—a chance to choose whether to ride out an upcoming alignment high in the branches (for a better view, more food-catching opportunities) or deep in a root-tangle (for safety).

These climbers live in bands. The noise prevents long-distance vocal subtlety, so their calls are mostly short, puncturing notes—clicks, barks, sharp upsweeps—that carry against the roar. For nuance, they use touch and pattern: drumming on hollow trunks, tapping sequences on each other’s ribs. Their social life is a little like percussion ensembles, a little like groom-threads in primate troops.

They have noticed the machines.

Not in any technological sense, but as reliable anomalies. Certain pillars are always there, always humming, always giving off a faint light. Young ones play around their bases, throwing things into the racing water and watching what rebounds. Some families have long memories of patches where the air is always a little warmer, the forage always a little richer—the microclimate bubbles—and they treat those as good places to rear offspring and cache food.

They also interact with the machine-biological mesh in ways that are not entirely anticipated. For instance: some have learned to hang bundles of gathered, phosphorescent fungi from branches near frameworks. That extra glow attracts more insects at night; the insects draw in small predators; the band then hunts those predators. From the machines’ perspective, these dangling fungal lanterns are just another variable to factor into the local light field; from the climbers’ perspective, they are tools.

Tool use extends further: ripping up mats of filamentous plants and stretching them into rough nets across narrow channels, piling stones just so to divert a trickle closer to a favored thicket. They are not masters of construction—everything they build is perishable, wrecked by the next real storm—but in a world where the whole environment is repeatedly reset, even a day-long manipulation can be the difference between feast and hunger.

There may be other intelligences on the ground or in the water: burrowers that reshape whole hummocks; aquatic hunters that navigate by the stable interference patterns in certain pools. If so, they, too, would be defined less by enduring edifices and more by fleeting rearrangements of a mutable world.

### The Absent Ones

Strictly speaking, those who designed this basin do not “live” here; they live through it.

They might be long-gone, their legacy perpetuated by self-maintaining systems. Or they might dip their awareness into the basin only intermittently, listening in on the distributed field-brain’s telemetry from some remote vantage. For them, the basin is not a habitat but an experiment—an ongoing, tightly bounded storm where matter, life, and field are allowed to negotiate under high pressure.

If they are still out there, they are patient. The devices are durable but not static; their gradual self-reconfiguration suggests that part of the experiment is to see how far such systems can diverge from their initial conditions and still achieve the broad goals set for them: perpetual youth, non-collapse, continuous novelty. Watching a forest grow old teaches one thing; watching a forest that never gets to be old teaches another.

They may also use basins like this as training grounds—not for individuals, but for algorithms and design principles. Each alignment event, each whole-basin re-phase, is like a “tick” in some high-level process: evaluate current diversity, evaluate energy flow, slightly re-weight priorities, continue. The basin’s inhabitants are, from that perspective, collaborators and test subjects at once.

### The Basin Itself

Last, there is a sense in which the basin as a whole is an inhabitant of itself, a self-occupying pattern.

Everything here, from the youngest shoot to the oldest pillar, participates in a single, coupled system that does not settle. It has no equilibrium state; its “goal” (if one were to anthropomorphize) is to stay at the edge of many thresholds simultaneously: ice just about to melt or form, slopes just about to slide, populations just about to boom or crash, every process kept in range where it is sensitive.

In that sense, what lives here is not “plants” or “animals” or “machines” or “algorithms” in isolation, but the dynamic between them: a restless choreography of floods, growth spurts, collapses, field re-tunings, predator-prey dances, swarms and retreats. If you tried to separate the components—to imagine the basin’s life without the machines, or the machines without the biology—you would lose the thing that is most alive about it: its perpetual, engineered adolescence.

The inhabitants, then, are:

- The invisible, overlapping minds that run through the devices’ fields.
- The machine-bodies that stand, hum, and reconfigure, half-instrument, half-organism.
- The plants that have learned to grow with and into those bodies, their physiology tuned to pulses of sound and light.
- The insects and swarming things that ride and modulate the gradients like birds on thermals.
- The bulk animals whose trampling and feeding are as much part of the design as rain.
- The quiet thinkers who notice, in their own ways, that some parts of the chaos are regular enough to lean on.
- And above and around them all, the basin’s own way of never quite becoming what it is, always living in the process of being made and unmade.

They do not live beside the control; they live inside it, and it inside them, until the distinction thins out.
