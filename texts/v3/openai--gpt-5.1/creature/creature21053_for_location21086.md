They live pressed against things.

Not because they fear the void—few of them have ever experienced an open space larger than a vault—but because their bodies and minds are built around contact, around the continuous, detailed conversation between surface and skin. To be fully alive, for them, is to be braced: back on a column, limbs spread across planes of rock, every sensory field touching some facet of the world.

Their form is not upright in the way of walkers. Imagine instead an elongated, low-slung body, somewhere between a cloak and a centipede: a broad central trunk that can flex and twist, edged with many short, powerful limbs that end in dense pads of gripping structures. Those pads are whorled with micro-hooks and soft, compliant tissues, adapted to the contradictory terrain: glass-slick obsidian sheets, needle-sharp foams, brittle mineral scales. Each pad can cling, but also sense—heat gradients, micro-vibrations, faint shifts in air density.

Their outer surface is not bare skin, not exactly carapace. Think of a composite armor, layered like the stone around them: tough, glossy plates laid over a lattice of sensory channels, with thin, softer membranes stretched between joints. Under certain angles of dim phosphorescence, they look like moving pieces of the wall itself: translucent smoke-grey ridges, amber glints, fractal matte patterns that echo the mineral-fungal textures they live among. Only when they move do you see the organic continuity—the slow flexion, the controlled flow of musculature under the plates.

They have faces, but not as a primary interface. The “front” of them is a slightly tapered section where sensory density is highest: arrays of tiny, lensless pits tuned to the faintest bioluminescent glow; cilia-like filaments that taste the aerosol-laden air for chemical nuance; a cluster of vibration-sensitive cavities that, together, function more like an ear wrapped around a nose and skin than either alone. Mouthparts are small, recessed, protected: they do not gape or bite at large things. Food here is scraped, teased, abraded from surfaces in minute quantities. Their main tools of taking are more like tongues and files than jaws.

The true organs of perception are distributed. Much of their cognition happens across their body: long arrays of mechanoreceptors lining flanks and limbs, thermal sensilla dotted like stars. Touch for them is not local; a single contact with the wall can ripple information through their entire nervous web. They lean on stone and know—not in propositions, but in a deep felt way—its temperature history, its strain, the directions it wants to fracture, the faint tick of distant impacts traveling along it.

Because the air is thin and dry, their respiratory surfaces are inward-turned and frugal. They breathe through slit-like vents along their sides, each guarded by stiff, whisker-like structures that act as both filters and sense organs. The air they take in is warmed and slowed inside a network of labyrinthine channels, passing over delicate exchange membranes wetted not by bulk water but by thin films of viscous, exotic solvents—organics that cling and evaporate only reluctantly. Their blood-equivalent is not water-based; it is a denser, syrupy mixture that moves slowly, stores heat, and tolerates partial desiccation.

They carry their own moisture carefully. Deep in their bodies lie reservoirs where concentrated solvents and bound water are sequestered, drawn upon sparingly. Dehydration here is not a matter of hours but of months, yet they treat every exhalation, every secretion, as something to be justified. Waste is minimized, metabolites cycled through symbiotic colonies embedded in specialized organs—internal gardens of mineral-fungus that transform what would otherwise be loss into slow resource.

They are not alone in their bodies. Just as the rock is colonized by organisms that blur the line between mineral and life, their tissues host whole ecosystems. Along the inner surfaces of their gripping pads, microscopic films of chemoautotrophic symbionts help break down the nutrient films on stone-fungus. Deeper still, within honeycombed bones of semi-crystalline material, live ultra-slow microbes that repair microfractures and sequester toxic traces from the environment. They are as much community as individual, micro-ecologies walking through macro-ecologies.

Their skeletons are not strictly internal or external. Instead, they grow interwoven frameworks of crystalline mineral in their muscles and dermal layers. The harder portions—load-bearing struts, anchor plates for limbs—are close to the outside where they can couple forces into the environment. Softer supports run deeper, protected but less effective as direct bracing. Over a lifetime, their structure becomes increasingly tuned to the rock around them: they accrete compatible minerals from food and air, knitting themselves chemically to the substrata of their home ranges.

In darkness, sophistication moves into other senses. They have no language of color in the way a surface-dweller might, though they can distinguish wavelengths of glow; instead they are exquisitely literate in pattern and texture. A wall smooth in a particular way, a regular series of micro-grooves, a field of raised hexes at knee-height—these are as distinct to them as letters, songs, or faces might be to us.

They write with stone. A common “sentence” is a panel carved only a few millimeters deep: repeating micro-ridges that encode instructions, stories, ownership. To read, one leans against it and lets thousands of receptors integrate the sequence into something felt as a gestalt, an almost-musical phrase of touch. What to an outsider is mere scratchwork is to them a library.

Sound, when they choose to use it, is intimate and constrained. Their audible clicks, low squeaks, and very soft trills do not carry far and are used at close range, often accented by deliberate knocks of limb or tool against rock. But below hearing, their world blooms with infrasonic speech. They can, by specific muscular contractions against resonant plates in their bodies, send directed vibrations through the stone they touch. These pulses travel into walls, along floors, through pillars—picked up by others kilometers away who happen to be in contact with the same network of material.

This is not casual conversation. The stone swallows and distorts, and only certain paths carry signal well. Over many generations, they have mapped those paths—the “song-lines” your description hinted at. They know which vein in a column must be gripped, at what angle, to catch the faint modulation of a distant ally’s call. Messages are patterned pulses: sequences of long and short waves, shifts in amplitude and spectral composition. Entire histories can be encoded in slowly repeated infrasonic motifs, humming through the rock for days.

They cultivate these lines. Where a natural seam of resonant material appears, they will sometimes spend generations clearing around it, shaping adjacent spaces to reduce interference. They don’t make wires or pipes; they sculpt resonance corridors: galleries tuned by angle and mass so that one particular band of vibration rings clearer there than anywhere else.

Their sense of time grows from this geologic pace. They are not especially long-lived as individuals—perhaps the equivalent of several human decades—but everything important to them extends across multiple lifespans. A planned tunnel is something one’s grandparents began and one’s grandchildren will complete. A relationship with a particular mineral-fungus bed spans centuries of careful seeding, tending, and harvesting by successive lineages.

“Family,” for them, is less about strict descent and more about co-participation in projects: those who have worn their bodies down against the same stones, tuned the same resonance chambers, contributed to the same living walls. They name themselves after these works: “The Ledge-Deepening-of-X”, “Those-Who-Joined-the-Cold-Channel-to-the-Warm”. Personal identity is braided with the history of built and shaped spaces.

Because the environment barely changes without deliberate action, they have grown careful. Recklessness is waste. A collapsed gallery is not only a local failure but a lost investment of centuries of small efforts and careful stress-balancing. So their engineering ethics are conservative, reverent. Before removing a single plate from a floor, elders and specialized sensitives spend long days pressed to walls, listening for hidden stress, charting the faint anisotropies of gravity and resonance that betray load paths.

The keen, fresh edges of the world resonate with them. Where we might fear the sharpness, they relish it: sharpness is information. A blunt, weathered face tells very little; a jagged break, a glassy, unrounded plane, holds within its facets a memory of forces. Their science—such as it is—is a tactile crystallography, a cultural lore of fracture and grain.

They name rocks not only by composition but by “yield-song”: how they respond to being struck, scraped, or leaned on. A given stratum might be known as Bright-Hollow (rings clearly under a tap, easy to shape into acoustic chambers), or Slow-Silent (dulls vibrations, good for living walls where privacy from the deeper hum is desired). They catalog these qualities in stories rather than in tables: tales of journeys where certain rocks helped or hindered, myths where personified stones choose sides.

Their technology follows from all this. Tools are not separate things to be taken up and put down; they are sheaths and extensions integrated into harnesses that distribute load back into the torso and, from there, into the environment. A “hammer” is a well-fitted, replaceable striking insert in a bracing shell that hugs shoulder, rib, and hip. When using it, the worker becomes part of a triangle: body, tool-shell, and stone each sharing impact forces.

Cutting edges—those precious strips of obsidian or hammered metal—are held close to the central mass, not on long handles. They carve by leaning, by slow, controlled pressure, almost by abrasion rather than chop. This fits their ethos: they prefer to negotiate with stone rather than fight it.

Light, being so rare and faint, is treated almost as a domestic animal that must be fed and guided. The bioluminescent colonies they use are not wild; they are selectively encouraged variants that produce stable, predictable glows on particular wavelengths and lifespans. A long gallery might have periodic patches of soft blue: each derived from an ancestor colony carefully harvested and transplanted. Over time, families become associated with particular “light strains” they maintain—lineages of glow that mark their routes and precincts.

There are artisans devoted solely to the cultivation of light. These caretakers know how to feed their colonies with trace gases and mineral dusts, how to scrape away senescent tissue without damaging the generative core. In some settlements, memorials for the dead are not stone markers but carefully tended light-panels: matrices of glowing pores whose pattern and color-change over years recount a person’s works. A traveler feeling along such a panel can, through the slightly different warmth and micro-texture of older versus newer colonies, “read” the temporal unfolding of a life.

Their settlements cluster in topological rather than geographical “places”: knots in the labyrinth where gradients, resonances, and materials align. A typical community might occupy a complex of adjacent ledges on the walls of a large shaft, or a tight cluster of interconnected honeycomb cells around a stable warm flow. There is rarely a single big “hall”. Instead there are many modest volumes, each tuned to a function: one particularly quiet and constant in temperature for rest; one with focused warmth for rearing young and coaxing difficult chemical processes; one with ideal acoustic properties where communal decisions are made by shared vibrations against the same ring of stone.

Gatherings rarely center on looking at each other. They assemble by converging touch: everyone present in a council may be braced against the same central column, or connected in a chain of contact hands-to-shoulders-to-walls. Deliberation is felt as much as heard: subvocal infrasonic pulses, subtle flexings, minute temperature changes through skin. Consensus is literally a shared resonance, a pattern of pulses falling into phase across many bodies.

Children are born small, soft, with fewer plates and a higher proportion of fluid volume. Their early years are spent almost entirely in contact with caregivers and the shaped interiors of nursery-chambers: smooth, gently undulating walls warmed to a precise degree, laid with textural scripts of cultural knowledge. Before they have language, they have touch-culture: the grooves and ridges under their limbs become their first stories. They crawl, press, and doze upon parables recorded as surface.

Instruction is slow, embodied. To teach a path, an elder does not draw a map; they take the youngster along, not just once but many times, encouraging them to note that where the ceiling becomes lower the air cools at ankle-height, that this wall sings faintly to the right palm, that there is a distinctive mineral-living film on that sill. A “route” becomes, in memory, a chain of sensory cues rather than a visual line.

They navigate by remembered gradients. What we might call a coordinate system, they experience as a pattern of warmth-and-vibration across skin: “Go until your shoulders feel the double-hum in the right, then follow the descending cool in the lower limbs until the ceiling tapers to near-touch.”

Predation from the environment shapes their culture of vigilance. They have a whole aesthetic and craft devoted to distinguishing true stone from false stone—the camouflaged ambushers that mimic walls. Artisans will decorate important surfaces with subtle, repeating micro-textures that no native predator can perfectly imitate. Visitors from other communities recognize these signature “hands” of texturing like we recognize an architect’s style.

In dangerous corridors, they embed tiny chimes and flexible spars into the structure—passive sentinels that tinkle or buzz when the wall behind them flexes in an unnatural way. These are not loud alarms; they are tiny whispers of difference, which those moving through will check with a shown pause and a hand to the wall. They live in a constant, low-level audit of their surroundings, but not in fear. It is simple hygiene, like breathing.

Their relationship with the mineral-fungal colonies is both exploitative and reverent. They farm, but what they farm is not fields; it’s volumes of stone. They will identify a context where gas flows and existing chemistry might favor a desired colony, then intervene minutely: scoring micro-channels to redirect gas, seeding spores in pre-patterned lattices, introducing trace minerals from distant regions. Over decades, they might coax a blank, glassy wall into a living panel that exudes a slow, nutritious film.

There is no clear line between agriculture and infrastructure. A wall that produces food also contributes to air-quality regulation, to heat distribution, to acoustic damping or reinforcement. A new bed of mineral-fungus might be planned partly to create a more resonant surface for communication, partly to feed a growing sub-group, partly to stabilize a fracture. Their built environment is ecological by default; they lack the luxury of separate systems.

One can imagine an old, central settlement: its columns sheathed in generations of such managed life, its galleries tuned so precisely that the faint planetary hum becomes an orchestral undertone. Here, walking along a familiar route, an inhabitant feels not a featureless stone-field but a polyphonic composition: the soft, steady thrum of the deep mantle, overlayed with the crisp taps of distant tool-work, the gentle breathing pulsing of near-wall colonies. The place is a song sustained over centuries.

Do they have myth? They must, though it is not about sky or sea. Their cosmogonies likely begin with the hum. The infrasonic base-note of the planet is an absolute, a presence from before any recorded texturing of walls. They may tell stories of how the world-sound slowed and cooled until it hardened into these glassy strata, how the first living patterns were the subtle reorganizations of texture in the dark—those “computing” mineral colonies whose slow changes suggest thought.

Some sects may even revere those non-luminous, rearranging surfaces as an elder intellect: a planet-scale mind thinking on timescales beyond their own, whose “neurons” are the very rock-mats they harvest and decorate. For them, to carve stone is not just engineering but conversation, an attempt to write back into the long, patient meditation of the crust.

Others may be more pragmatic, seeing the great hum and the slow cracklings as mere background, like weather. For them, divinity attaches instead to points of connection: the rare membranes in walls through which microfauna pass, carrying glimpses of deeper or shallower zones. Perhaps oracles in some communities sit for months with their limbs splayed across such a “soft wall,” reading the barely-perceived flicker of transiting organisms as omens: a subtle shift in chemical tang that foretells a new gas-flow, or the arrival of an exploitable mineral front.

Their science is a catalog of invariants and anomalies in this otherwise unwavering world. An unexpected gradient in temperature, a new pattern of resonance, a minute change in the flavor of aerosols—these are not weather quirks; they are events. A slightly warmer current appearing where there was none might indicate a crack opening to a newly accessible layer. Generations of patient note-taking—recorded as texture-panels along key corridors—allow them to model such changes and anticipate opportunities.

Once in a great while, something larger happens: a significant shift in stress, a major crack forming, a slow settling of huge volumes. They do not experience this as sudden catastrophe—in this deep, density-buffered labyrinth, even big events propagate as long waves of strain. But they feel, over weeks, a growing tension in certain pillars, a change in the global hum’s pitch. It is like living on the diaphragm of a sleeping giant that occasionally exhales more deeply.

In such times, their engineers and elders intensify their listening. They may temporarily abandon certain marginal passages, retreating to zones known to have tolerated previous shifts. They adjust load-bearing structures and resonance cavities, not to fight the planet’s movement but to ride it, letting built spaces flex and resettle without shattering. Their architecture has no rigid straight lines; everything allows for minute, distributed adaptation.

If, in some remote future, a breach were to open—if some unimaginably powerful event cracked the crust and poured sky-light down into their realm—the shock to such a species would be profound. Not simply because brightness and open void would be terrifying, but because the entire grammar of their existence is written in closeness and constancy. To see a horizon, to feel air that is not backed by stone, would be an ontological rupture.

Yet in their own frame, they are not deprived. They know spaciousness as depth, as cumulative connectedness: the knowledge that by following contact after contact, gradient after gradient, one can travel further than any lifetime allows. Their cosmos is not above and below but near and further-near; distance is counted in how many layers of intermediate stone-messages lie between here and there.

They live as patient sculptors, as acoustic gardeners, as keepers of glow and gradient. Their story is not one of conquering a harsh world, but of elaborating an already-stable equilibrium with careful, textured detail. Every wall they lean upon, every colony they coax, every resonance they tune adds another thread to the vast, tight-woven tapestry under the skin of their planet.

Who lives here? A people who think with their hands, hear with their bones, and take the unchanging darkness not as prison but as canvas: a place to etch, across generations, the slow, intricate patterns of a civilization that never needs a sky to know itself vast.
