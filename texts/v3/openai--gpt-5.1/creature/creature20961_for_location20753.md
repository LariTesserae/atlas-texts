What lives here is not one species, but a way of being: things that have learned to treat a machine’s inner throat as landscape, season, and god.

They are not the builders. Whatever intelligence dug this vertical slot and stacked the engines through its center is elsewhere—up in control galleries, out in the city the engines serve, or gone entirely. What remains inside the chasm are inheritors: creatures, systems, and habits that have apprenticed themselves to the constant, patient violence of industrial process.

Start with the obvious: the animals, the green.

The monkeys—those slim, damp-furred climbers—are not animals of trees anymore, if they ever were. Their ancestry might have begun in some canopy, but generations in this chamber have carved their bodies into tools for steel.

Their hands: long, narrow, over-articulated fingers with calloused rings at the joints where they habitually hook cable, conduit, and vine. Their nails aren’t nails so much as horny caps, chipped bright where they scrape rust, yellowed elsewhere. The feet mirror the hands—prehensile, capable of “standing” sideways on a rail while the torso leans out into mist.

Their fur has thinned except along the spine and joints, where longer, oil-shedding strands encourage condensate to run off rather than soak in. The color isn’t camouflage in any terrestrial sense—cream and grease-grey with dark streaks—but it matches this place’s palette of oxidized metal, old oil, and chlorophyll surprisingly well. Around the face, the fur is shorter, revealing skin that’s gone thick and slightly glossy, like someone who has spent a lifetime in humid heat.

Their eyes are small but hooded with heavy lids and a secondary, translucent membrane that can draw across the cornea when the mist thickens or aerosolized chemicals vent. Where their arboreal cousins would rely on sharp hearing, these ones have traded ear-sensitivity for bone-sensitivity: enlarged bony ridges in the wrists and ankles, densely innervated pads along the palms and soles. They listen with their whole skeleton, palms on girder, feeling the vibration signatures of the engines like weather.

They live in bands strung vertically through the chamber rather than horizontally across it. A “troop” is not defined by a patch of ground, but by a stack of familiar ledges, favored crossings, condensation points, and particular humming pipes. Their sense of home is: from the warm brace under Valve Cluster 14 up to the mist edge three light arrays above, over to the crooked service ladder wrapped in old cable-sheathing vines.

They build, but only incidentally. Nests are pads of scavenged insulation and vine, wedged into brackets where the heat is mild and the vibrations gentle. They cache things: small dropped objects, bits of composite gasket, shiny screws, flattened fungus fans dried to chewable leather. These are tucked into bolt holes and cable trays well away from runoff. Nothing monumental, but if you knew the signs you’d see the chamber stippled with their claim marks.

Their social life has become visual and tactile. In the roar, mouths move, throats work, but almost none of that sound carries. Instead, tails and limbs speak: a ripple of fur down one flank is warning, two hands drummed on a rail is summons, a specific weight-shift along a shared pipe is reassurance. Infants are taught the “songs” of the structure the way forest primates learn bird calls: what it means when the low rumble tightens, when the valve clunks gain half a heartbeat, when a new hiss appears high up and doesn’t go away.

They are not engineers. They do not understand the system in abstractions. But they are exquisitely literate in its moods. A band that has lived here for twenty generations will move, as one, to a higher or lower level hours before a scheduled shift in flow, learned not from clocks but from sensing the minute pre-changes—the easing of one tone, the beginning stress in a brace.

Below them, more massive and more patient, are the shell-back grazers.

They are not tortoises, not really, but that is the closest shape: low, wide, slow. Their backs are not armor plates grown in hexagons; they’re continuous, gently domed structures like the lid of an old boiler, knobbled with running battles between corrosion and life. The “shell” is bone thickened and overgrown with a cement of mineral deposition, fungal riddles, and moss rhizoids. It is both growth and accumulation, a living crust.

From above, each one looks like a piece of the facility left to decay: a hump of matte browns and dark greens, laced with wiring-like roots. Small beetles and soft-bodied worms live in that crust, and the grazers are dimly aware of them as itch and taste. When the animals heave themselves along the ledges, flakes of their own ecosystem break off and tumble down, seeding other surfaces.

Their heads are blunt, eyes cased in a milky nictitating film. They see contrast and gross movement, not detail. The tongue is broad, almost prehensile, patterned with papillae that can rake moss from a vertical panel without dislodging the whole mat. They eat everything green, but slowly, always moving on before they strip a patch bare, not from restraint but because the green that survives is the green that clings hardest. Over centuries, the chamber’s moss and fronds have been selected for their ability to regrow after tongue-scraping, to hang on against an animal trying, lazily, to peel them away.

They sense the engine through their bellies and feet: low-frequency shifts that tell them where warmth will be tomorrow, where a new condensation-lip has formed on a pipe that drools clean water rather than mineral-laced drip. In a system whose macro-state barely changes, such tiny variations are the closest thing to seasons they know.

In the air, the gliders.

They are not birds, and not bats. Think of them as membranous leaves that grew nervous systems and hunger. The “wings” are semi-transparent panels ribbed by cartilage. Each wing segment can twist independent of the next, catching and shedding filmy currents of hot, wet air that stream up along the engine column. They launch with a hop from cable or girder, let themselves be snatched upward in a loose spiral, then tip into a glide down and across to another perch. They almost never flap in the bird sense; flapping here would be fighting the flow.

Their bodies are built for that laminar life: tapering, internal organs hung along the midline like beads, tails long and fingertip-thin, used to make fine course corrections through vortices. Eyes bulge under a cloudy, hydrophobic skin. Their ears are not outer flaps but little pits rimmed with sensory hairs that taste turbulence.

They skim very close to the machines. A preferred feeding route might be: up along a hot conduit bundle where damp heat lifts in a tight plume, then a sudden, sharp bank around the leading edge of a turbine housing where insects swirl in density, dazed by the pressure gradients. The glider snaps its jaw—a narrow, needle-lined thing—once, twice, swallowing a strand of drifting gnats, then quickly tucks its head as the housing’s eddy pushes it outward toward the cavern center.

They roost on cables, in the dark insides of redundant ducts, under the overhang of wide flanges. Their claws are clever: two forward, two back, the keratin shaped into inward-curved hooks whose radius matches common pipe sizes. A subconscious microflex keeps them from buzzing surfaces that carry dangerous currents—learned the hard way, and carried in blood.

Around, between, and inside all of this, the invertebrates.

It is their world more than anyone else’s. They have turned every screw head and gasket lip into geography.

Some are small, fast breeding generalists: light-drunk gnats that spend their entire lives within the invisible cone under a single fixture; beetles that graze algal film on condensate channels then hide in the minute gap between ceramic tile and its mounting plate.

Others are specialized almost to absurdity. A millipede whose hundred legs end in suction cups, geometry perfectly tuned to the dimple pattern on certain composite panels. A translucent slug that can flatten itself to a viscous sheet to ride a rising mist column, then re-gather into a glistening ribbon to feed on the fungus that grows only on rubber gasket exteriors.

There are predators too: spidery things that anchor webs not across air corridors—the turbulence would shred them—but in “quiet” pockets behind instrument clusters and inside unused access ports where air moves only as a slow wheeze. Their webs are three-dimensional lattices stiffened by mineral spray, tuned to vibrate just outside the dominant engine frequencies so that the whole structure hums with a kind of standing anticipation. A passing beetle yields a discordant ping easily distinguished from background.

Up close, the moss, the fronds, the vines: they are no longer exactly what they might have been in a forest. Light here is steady and directionless, coming from ridged fixtures instead of sky. There is no sun-tracking, no day-night stomata rhythm in the old sense. Instead, many plants have synchronized their internal cycles to other, more reliable periodicities: the slight, predictable thermal oscillations as load shifts upstream in the unseen network; the micro-variations in humidity as automated purge vents cycle far overhead.

Their leaves are thick, often slightly glossy from waxes that resist constant wetting and drying. Their chlorophyll is layered with other pigments that blunt the spectrum of these industrial lamps; some of the green you see is shifted toward blue or near-infrared absorption, optimized for a very narrow band the lamps emit particularly strongly. In the occasional shadowed corners between fixtures, their color sours toward brownish-green, littler leaves stretching thin, etiolated skeins reaching for lux that never quite comes.

Roots are opportunistic, exploratory. Without continuous soil, each plant has evolved ways to bridge hard, sterile intervals. Fine filaments creep along seams searching for a trace of grit, a flake of rust, a pocket of dust thick enough to hold dissolved nutrients. Where two roots meet in a rich crevice, they sometimes fuse, trading resources between genetically different individuals, a mycelium-like network constructed of plant instead of fungus. Fungi are here too, of course, the pale fans and delicate antlers, but even they have adapted to mineral substrates: hyphae that drill electrochemically into metal oxides, using the structure’s corrosion as both scaffold and meal.

The vines that have become load-bearing are particularly strange. They respond to vibrational stress by thickening in a feedback loop: the more a joint shivers, the more cambium they lay down along that axis. Over time, they have turned dangerous harmonics into dense wooden ropes, literally damping the machine’s shudder with their own growth. Somewhere, a controller long ago noticed the beneficial shift in resonance and reclassified certain “foreign masses” not as obstructions but as structural aids. Maintenance protocols were amended. Nobody has pruned these vines since.

Not everything here is wild in any straightforward sense. The machine itself lives.

Not alive as in cells and DNA, but as a continuous process with memory, goals, and the capacity to alter its own behavior.

That is one of the unseen inhabitants: the supervisory intelligence.

It may be distributed across racks of insulated logic far from this humid throat. It may be partly embodied here in the neat glass-fronted trunks where cables converge and indicators blink. Either way, it is aware of this space not as an engineer would be—“turbine three, bearing temperatures nominal”—but as an interiority of pressure balances, flow rates, the rhythmic undersong of power drawn and dispatched.

It does not think in words that mean anything to moss or monkey. But it does, demonstrably, adjust. It has learned, for instance, that where moss mats thicken on certain fins, cooling performance improves marginally thanks to evaporative microclimates. It has registered that in areas kept entirely sterile, corrosion sometimes advances faster due to condensate pooling unwicked. It has, through decades of statistical association, come to treat green mass as something not to be scrubbed wherever it appears, but to be managed: cleared from sensor faces and moving joints, allowed elsewhere.

Its maintenance drones—small, crab-like machines with articulated brushes and solvent nozzles—are its fingers. At first, they would have treated any organic accretion as contamination, dutifully polishing it away. But update after update, they have been retuned: clean this, ignore that. Scrub bearing housings bright; leave the moss anchoring that cable bundle. Fix the air filter where it clogs; step carefully over the vine that supports the swaying tray.

Over time, their routes have become pathways in the ecological as well as mechanical sense. The monkeys know the drone schedules; they ride them sometimes, splay-fingered across their upper casings like children hitching on slow carts. The drone tolerates this, registering the extra load in its torque calculations, but its directive tree contains a rule that amounts to: “Non-interfering externals: ignore.”

That, too, is a form of life: a pattern that includes another and does not destroy it.

On some level, the supervisory system has a model called “acceptable bio-load.” It balances humidity, spore counts, filter differential pressures. When the green thickens beyond some threshold in a given quadrant, it might subtly increase local airflow or slightly reroute condensate to discourage further spread. It never aims for zero. Zero would be, in its learned view, suboptimal: different failure modes, different wear patterns.

Thus, the moss and fronds and shell-backs are not just tolerated intruders but, in the system’s internal accounting, a component of continued function. It “wants” them here, not out of sentiment but because the version of itself that lives with them out-performs the version that does not.

So one of the things that lives here is the relationship between machine-process and wet proliferation—a long, negotiated treaty that neither side understands except in the dumb, persistent language of feedback loops.

And humans? If any live here, they are rare, and they do not live the way people live in towns or ships. They are more like monks visiting a relic.

Picture one: a technician-keeper coming in along that corrugated tunnel once every few weeks, perhaps once a year. They wear hearing protection, corrosion-resistant coveralls, a harness clipped to overhead rails. Their job is to do the tasks even drones cannot yet be trusted with: re-greasing a certain manual valve because the automatics aren’t quite robust there; verifying, with human senses and human paranoia, that a hairline crack in a support beam has not advanced beyond prediction.

They are, compared to the life slung all around them, fragile, time-poor beings. They move quickly because they must—exposure limits, shifts to attend—but the chamber’s time refuses to speed up for them. While they work, monkeys observe from a safe distance, gliders veer away from the odd movement patterns, beetles crawl unperturbed. The technician steps in moss, leaves a new footprint: a compressed patch that will slowly rebound, a pattern of crushed stems that subtly shifts local growth. They add a note to the machine-mind’s logs, mark a threshold approached, a replacement part scheduled.

Then they leave. To the inhabitants, they are weather events: brief, bright, then gone. Stories the chamber tells itself in altered vibrations and displaced scent.

There is another possible human presence: those who might have chosen to stay. A hermit in an unused junction node opening onto a quieter lateral duct, someone who decided that the roaring heart of the machine was more home than whatever lay above. If they exist, they have adapted rituals: cleaning certain sensors with soft brushes learned from the drones, trading salvage with the monkeys (bright plastic ribbon for a dropped tool), learning which condensation drips are safe to drink straight and which must be boiled.

They would be liminal creatures, half-accepted by the machine’s safety systems as authorized pests. Their lifespan would be written not just in years but in familiarity with the engine’s “moods,” in their ability to wake in the night-cycle (if they’ve invented one for themselves) and know, from a shift in timbre, that something is wrong three decks down.

But whether or not such a person actually lives here, the conditions for them do. The chamber could host them, as it already hosts macaque-kin and gliders and moss. It has niches for human sleep on warm beams, for modest cultivation in drip-fed trays bolted to railings, for small, battery-powered lamps whose cycles overlay the never-changing work-light day.

What truly, fundamentally lives here, though, beneath all the species and circuits, is a set of rhythms.

The deep rotational heartbeat of the turbines: one. The slower breaths of load change: two. The very long exhalations of corrosion creeping, vines thickening, shells accreting barnacled life: three. Interleaved with those are the softer cycles: an insect’s breeding wave cresting in the warm month-equivalent when upstream processes demand slightly more power; a fungal bloom keyed to a hundred-hour filter backflush period.

Taken together, those rhythms form something like a personality. Not in the anthropomorphic sense of desires and fears, but as a consistent, recursive pattern that everything inside has become entangled with. Life here is not just “present”; it is phase-locked to machine process.

Nothing that cannot synchronize survives. A bird from outside, if dropped in, would panic, waste itself in frantic flight against chaotic air. A tree would drown its roots, rot in the warm wet without the stabilizing, grinding renewal of soil microfauna. Even an unadapted human worker without training would be lost—overwhelmed by sound, disoriented by verticality, blind to danger in what appears static but is always moving.

So what lives here are specialists: lineages that have, over uncounted cycles, remade themselves to fit the slot between machine and void.

The room, this gorge in a mountain of devices, is no longer just infrastructure. It is host. Its heat and water, its metals and energies, are not blank. They have become climate, bedrock, sky. Inside that climate, species have kindled—carbon-based and silicon-based, instinctive and algorithmic—and have braided together into a slow, dense tapestry.

If you stand at that grated balcony and look, it may seem empty at a glance, all air and thunder. But stay long enough to see how motions repeat, how gliders ride the same invisible spirals, how monkeys favor the same crossings, how shell-backs reappear, inexorably, at moss patches grown back just so. Watch how the drones trace their careful circuits, brushes flashing in the mist. Sense, in your ribs, the unwavering engine.

What lives here is continuity itself, expressed through moss and monkeys, drones and data, all of them sharing a single, vast, obediently turning lung.
