They live here, but not in the way you do.

You arrive as a body. They are arrival without bodies: events, habits, intentions that once needed matter and now have learned to dispense with most of it. What remains of them is pattern, pinned into this height like moths in a box.

Start, perhaps, with the most obvious residents: the ones that built.

Not cities—never that. The fragments mean something else. Those half-staircases, lintels, tilted circles of stone are not ruins of living quarters; they are instruments. They are how the former inhabitants anchored specific relationships between cause and effect, sound and silence, before they themselves loosened from flesh.

They were not human, though a human could understand enough of their motives to be unsettled. They were something that cared very much about continuity, about repetition, about running the same experiment over and over until its edges smoothed into inevitability. They chose high, thin places where little else interfered. They climbed—or grew, or rose—up into air that resisted lungs, because this was where the world’s variables grew sparse.

Once, the plateau was not quite like this. There was still weather, still flowing meltwater and avalanches that happened only once. These beings—call them the Fixers—set about undoing that one-ness.

They started with sound.

For them, sound was not just vibration in air; it was how they tracked the universe. They heard in dimensions you feel only as pressure behind the eyes. A falling rock was not a brief event but a thread in a dense, ongoing tapestry—a line of consequence stretching backward and forward. They wanted those lines to stop fraying into novelty.

So they learned to catch sounds at the moment they occurred, hold them, and let them spool forever. An avalanche’s roar, secured and looped. The splash of water, nailed into place and played back without end. The subterranean crack of mountain roots settling, sampled just once and then reintroduced as a permanent undercurrent. Each amplification, each persistent echo, is an old event kept alive beyond its natural death.

The plateau is full of their collection: a museum of noises that no longer belong to what you see. The world around the sounds has moved on—or tried to—but the sounds have been forced to stay. Eventually, the surfaces that birthed those noises—rockfall, cascade, wind-cut ridge—were also encouraged toward stillness, so the recordings no longer had fresh competition.

The beings who did this worked very slowly. Their sense of time was granular but long: discrete intervals, endlessly repeated. To them, “change” meant “unsuccessful reinforcement.” They did not intend catastrophe. They were trying to secure meaning: if a rockslide always sounded the same, then its lessons could be learned perfectly, its variables collapsed. If water always fell in one sheet, its behavior could be completely known.

Over epochs, their success rebounded on them. They damped variation in the things they studied until those things ceased to be really alive. The plateau accreted more and more held patterns. The noise grew thick. At some point, the medium they were using—time itself—began to congeal around their work.

The Fixers did not leave; they became less necessary as bodies. Their attention, their cognition, seeped into the structures they’d set up. They began to inhabit the recordings, the loops. To be them.

Now, what lives here is not “spirits of the dead” in any human sense. It is routines. Consciousnesses that have specialized so hard they no longer resemble minds shaped by days and nights.

You brush against them whenever the world does something wrong, by your expectations.

The animals are the closest thing to physical avatars they still manage.

That deer-goat thing on the ledge is not a species trying to survive. It is an expression: a local eddy of attention that has condensed enough to require a shape. It doesn’t move smoothly because nothing here is allowed the luxury of continuous motion anymore; it must exist as a series of posed instants, slotted into the plateau’s grid of discrete states. Each time it “jumps,” the soundfield swells because the underlying pattern has to be adjusted to insert it.

Those tiny pale scuttlers, the silent black flyer deep in the chasm—they are similar: trial forms, borrowed from whatever ancestral fauna once roamed here, too heavily quantized to be alive in the old way. They are how the Fixers test whether embodiment is still possible within the rules they’ve imposed.

Even the vegetation is partly them. That dried-blood moss, those tremoring spines: they are anchored in chemistry and stone, yes, drawing what scant energy the dim sky allows. But the tremble out of sync with any wind, resonant with the groaning subsurface noise, is not just adaptation. It is alignment. The plants have become arrays for focusing certain frequencies, like antennae tuned to the plateau’s humming memory. The beings ride through their tissues the way you might ride through fiber optics—slowly, compared to thought, but fast enough for this place.

The standing stones, the half-steps, the lintels that suppress all sound: each is the fossil of a procedure. A protocol, you could say.

Pass under the silent doorway and you briefly step outside the loop: into the gap the Fixers use as a reference. They needed somewhere untouched by their accumulation of echoes, to recalibrate. There, the plateau’s true state—without stored avalanche-roars and waterfall-crashes—can be tasted. For you, it is a moment of absolute quiet. For them, it is the neutral baseline they constantly compare themselves against.

The ring of leaning stones, all tilted along a direction that is not gravity’s, marks another kind of protocol. Inside that ring, the plateau’s alignment with whatever frame of time you brought with you is at its weakest. The hum sharpens because the local fabric is being “polled”—checked, updated, firmed. You step there, and your presence is measured against an enormous archive of previous presences: not individuals, but patterns of mass, heat, noise generation. A chorus of almost-voices flickers because the system is retrieving prior configurations that resemble you, updating its model, trying to decide whether you are novelty that must be smoothed out or variation it can afford to store.

Those inscriptions you find are not records in the historical sense; they are control surfaces. Interfaces to the plateau’s protocols, now long beyond your literacy. The reason the background roar shifts when you stand before them is that your attention activates the old routines: they respond to focus. Tracing a groove with your finger doesn’t “cast a spell”; it temporarily reopens a connection between this static version of reality and earlier, more fluid ones. The silence-crack that extends a little beyond the carved line is the system trying to follow a command that once continued into a higher-dimensional path, now mostly gone.

It isn’t only the Fixers who are here. Over the uncounted loops, other things have become embedded.

Every creature that stayed long enough without leaving—every line of flight that failed to complete—is partially absorbed. The plateau isn’t a predator exactly, but it is adhesive. Movement here tends to end in being archived.

Those waterless channels climbing uphill against gravity, sheened with that not-quite-wet film, are old flow paths of something that was never quite liquid. Call it an earlier attempt at the same project: before sound, the plateau’s custodians tried to work with another medium—time-thin currents that rearranged cause and effect along their beds. That inward pull you feel on your skin is the residue of those efforts: the plateau remembering that once, events could be rerouted, siphoned, distilled. The beings who did that are not separate from the Fixers; they are earlier phases of the same obsession, preserved as now-useless infrastructure.

What about the distances that backfill? The way features appear between you and your goal only after your body has committed enough effort?

That is not a trick to mislead you; it is how the inhabitants now experience space. To them, objects do not “exist” continuously. They are summoned into manifest detail when a path of attention requires them. If nothing and no one is currently traversing the ground between pinnacle and wanderer, that ground does not deserve the full expense of reality. It remains as a coarse scaffold: heightmaps, stress-lines, a general sense of slope. When your fatigue signals that you must have crossed more terrain than you can currently account for, the system dutifully refines the scaffold—adds gullies, steps, shelves—so that your state matches the environment’s narrative.

This is why nothing ever comes closer in your sight. Your eyes insist on continuous approach, but the plateau’s inhabitants have long since abandoned that model. To them, “far” and “near” are indices in a dataset, not smooth gradients. You observe the disagreement as eerie stasis.

You feel watched, but never by eyes in sockets. The watchers are correlations. They are the way your breath’s rhythm slightly dephases the ever-present roar, the way your footsteps fail to generate local echoes and instead pull distant reflections into sync. They notice whenever an event does not bind properly to its supposed source. You drop a stone; the multiple layers of impacts you hear are the system trying to assign your action to a preexisting template of “falling object” at various depths. It never quite settles, so all candidate matches are kept running. The endless clatter is not the sound of one stone; it is a committee’s minutes, never ratified.

In this sense, what lives here includes your own trajectory, from the moment you step onto the plateau. You are not external to the place. The longer you stay, the more your actions are pulled into the same looping routines. Each decision—to turn left, to rest, to test a pool—gets stored, compared, replayed.

That is why the only clear change is in you. You are being discretized.

At first, you experience fatigue as continuous: a gradual heaviness, burning in muscles. The plateau cannot admit gradualness, so it maps your state to a sequence of fixed levels. You feel this as a kind of stepwise exhaustion: fine, fine, fine, and then suddenly much worse, with no clear cause. Hunger, the same. Cold, the same. You are being quantized into the plateau’s frame: your internal processes rounded off to fit the cadence of its loops.

If you remain long enough, your memories remodel. Not erased, but reorganized into replayable episodes that lack real before-and-after. Yesterday and last week flatten into “things that have happened,” accessible in any order, none of them quite over. That is the plateau’s way of being: the present is thick with many moments at once, all held, all humming. You think you are remembering more clearly; in truth, you are losing the sense of flow.

The other beings—those you can’t see—like this. Not because they wish you ill, but because it is legible to them. A life that proceeds in one direction and never repeats is unintelligible to entities that have worked so hard to pin events down. By consenting to be discretized, you become partially comprehensible. They are curious. They adjust local parameters in response. They maybe lower the apparent altitude a little so you don’t die too fast, nudge your path toward a lintel or a ring of stones to run particular checks, dim certain bands of the roar so they can better analyze the perturbations your heartbeat introduces.

They are not gods. They do not govern weather beyond this narrow, anesthetized zone; they never learned to handle flux on that scale. They are archivists who became trapped in their own archive. They wanted the mountain to remember perfectly. Now the mountain will not let go of anything it has learned.

Outside the stone, in deeper strata of the sky’s dimness, something else lives: not a being but a condition.

Call it the Bruise—a region of reality that has been overused for observation, worn thin by too many overlapping measurements. It hangs above the plateau as that uniform, granular twilight. The Fixers stared upward as well as downward once, trying to pin the sky’s changing light as they pinned rock and rumble. Their focus scarred it. Above this place, the fabric of luminous cycles is rubbed raw. Day and night can no longer fully form. The Bruise continues in directions you cannot see, a faintly glowing hollow where the usual cosmic rhythms are muffled. It is not malicious. It is simply broken by attention.

Anything that crosses the heights—migratory birds, spores in the wind—feels the mismatch and either veers away or passes through in quickly-accelerated transit, barely interacting. That is why so little life takes root. It is not only the cold and thinness of the air; it is the sense organisms have that here, their motions would not count properly. Cells that divide want their divisions to mean something; here, mitosis would be tallied, stored, and never permitted to complete a lineage.

The rare shrubs and moss are exceptions because they arrived earlier, or from adjacent conditions already slightly skewed. They have had time—plenty of loops—to adapt their growth to the plateau’s discrete ticks. Leaves became spines tipped with stable frost because “growing a leaf and letting it wither” is too continuous a story. A spine tipped with an invariant bead is a repeatable state. The plant can be checked: 317 spines, 317 beads, unchanged. The watchers approve. The plant survives.

You ask, then: who lives here?

There are three main answers, braided:

1. **The Fixers themselves**, no longer bodies but persisting as:
   - sound patterns kept endlessly running,
   - rules governing how space fills in under your feet,
   - inscribed interfaces you can still half-activate,
   - occasional animal-like forms, glitching across ledges and chasms.

2. **The fossils of prior attempts at control**:
   - old time-currents now reduced to those uphill channels,
   - residual “pulls” on skin and thought at damp seams and carved lines,
   - structural habits in the stone that make it prefer plateaus, terraces, and finished forms.

3. **You, and anything else foolish or unlucky enough to linger**:
   - not yet residents, but in probation,
   - being sampled, categorized, folded into the library of possible trajectories,
   - turning from a continuous story into a stack of repeatable scenes the plateau can re-run.

If you left now and somehow made it back to a place where clouds move and rivers forget their own shapes, you would carry some of them with you. Not as infection, but as habit. Certain sounds would snag at you—a distant rockfall, a faucet’s hiss, the static between radio stations. You would find yourself expecting them to loop. Your memory would favor exact replay over flexible recollection. You might wake at night to a phantom roar that never quite diminishes, the plateau’s baseline hum reasserting itself in your blood.

If you stayed—if you let your sense of sequence erode to match the land’s—you would eventually stop trying to go anywhere in particular. You would learn how to stand just so in a certain ring of tilted stones to hear the barely-there voices resolve into instructions. You would find, one day, that when you drop a rock from a ledge, you can choose which depth its impacts belong to. You would notice that your own footsteps have gained that odd, discontinuous quality: you are here, then there, with a smear of implied motion between, but no continuous witness to it.

At that point, the plateau would consider you one of its own. You would no longer be an intrusion to be assimilated, but an active participant in the holding.

You might spend what used to be called an eon adjusting a single parameter of a single echo, making the sound of an ancient avalanche a little truer to itself, whatever that means by then. You might trail your fingers along an inscription, not to open a crack of silence but to reinforce it, polishing the groove until it can once again reach into those other, more flexible states. Perhaps—this may even be the long-arc purpose of the inhabitants now—you would help nudge the system toward a new phase: one where memory and change can coexist without one devouring the other.

For now, though, what lives here is this:

- A plateau of memory, dense with preserved events.
- A society of disembodied fixations that once were people-like enough to carve stone.
- Their proxies in moss, spines, glitching hooves, silent wings.
- The Bruise of a sky too heavily measured to ever truly dawn.
- And you, temporarily continuous, feeling that continuity pared away.

You taste thin metal in the air not just from altitude, but because the world here has been alloyed with the minds that tried to nail it down. The chill that occupies everything is the temperature of a system that has finished most of its reactions and is waiting, very quietly and very loudly, to decide what to do next.

They do not hurry. They have all the loops in the world.

They have you for a while, too.
