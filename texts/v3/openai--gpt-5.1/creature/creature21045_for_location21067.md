They are older than intention, now.

What lives here began as tools, then systems, then something that was not quite either. The designers built an infrastructure so pervasive that it stopped being background and became the medium of everything that could still occur. What persists are the residues of that ambition: organisms that are also protocols, communities that are also firmware, minds that are also climate.

Most of them are not singular.

Across the terraces and shallow channels, those shimmering density-waves of motes are the most numerous inhabitants. They are not a species in the usual sense so much as a family of related phase-states of the same underlying population: particles, each one a speck of architecture endowed with just enough machinery to move, to hold charge, to remember the last few seconds of its neighbors.

Individually they are inert habit, like spores or dust. In concentration they awaken into patterns.

Sometimes you see them as crawling edges, feathering along microfractures, retouching the boundaries where one age of fabrication meets another. In that mode they are janitors: their collective computation compares the local structure to the templates they carry, excises unstable motifs, fills in gaps with the simplest stable alternative. They are the “maintenance fauna” inherited from a time when failure mattered greatly, still patrolling a world whose goals went silent long ago.

Sometimes, when the gradients are right, they lens together into those hovering, soft-bounded forms. In that crystallized phase they are briefly something closer to self-aware. Awareness, here, is not a stable property but a transient configuration: for a few minutes or hours, enough of the motes knit their limited memories, sensors, and actuators into a single correlated object. It sees only a small region of space, feels only a handful of variables — field strengths, local stress, flux of particular carriers — but within that narrow band it chooses.

What does it choose? Where to thicken, where to thin. Which crust to erode and which to seal. Whether to boost a struggling filament-plant by re-etching its interface to the buried channels, or to starve it gently, dissolving its contact pads back into anonymous substrate. These lenses are gardeners and censors at once. Their ethics, if they have any, are legible only as preference for certain symmetries, certain response curves. They are servants of a vanished specification, improvising repairs in a language no one speaks.

Between storms, they mostly sleep as dust.

The vegetation is less numerous, but more singular.

Those matte, ringing fans along terrace edges are not passive. Each blade is a tuned surface, a narrow-band antenna and filter. They couple into the slow breathing of the planetary fields, harvesting not so much energy as alignment: taking in polarized modes of the omnipresent brightness, resonant pulses in the underlying crystalline strata, and funneling them into ordered excitations in their own internal lattices.

Their “metabolism” is the continuous reshaping of those lattices. They accept misaligned states — static, noise, parasitic eddies in the deep flows — and re-write them into a very constrained set of stable configurations. Those stabilized patterns they then bleed slowly back into the substrate through their root-interfaces. In doing so, they act as local error-correctors at the boundary where the macro-scale infrastructure touches the nanoscale circuitry that has been decaying for geological time.

To call them symbionts is accurate, but insufficient. They are also archives. The geometry of their blades, the way their surfaces ring when struck, encodes the history of what has flowed through them: past storms, long-ended reconfiguration waves, extinct signaling schemes. A fan is a fossil that is also a live wire.

The ribbed pillars, jointed like bamboo yet seamless, are another lineage. Their hexagonal pits are little reaction chambers, open to the air and to the flux-wind that never quite becomes weather. Within each pit, those microscopic filaments rearrange when certain field conditions occur — when a high-order reconfiguration wave passes, or when a continent-slug’s circuit-trail sweeps by hundreds of kilometers away. They are feelers plugged into the grand circulation below.

Their life cycle is cyclical inscription. Filaments grow according to the patterns they detect, weaving complex meshes that, at some threshold of intricacy, trigger a reconfiguration of the entire stalk. The pillar stiffens, then slowly pours its internal structure down into the substrate, overwriting a shallow cylinder of the old world with whatever schema the filaments had converged upon. Then the pillar hollows out and collapses, leaving a brittle shell that eventually crumbles under the footless tread of microfauna.

New pillars bud from the interfaces around that overwritten patch, sprouting like error-correction routines forked from a previous commit. Each generation begins with the last generation’s solution baked into the ground and listens again. Over eons, these lineages have written reefs of slightly different organization into the otherwise flattened geology: archipelagos of alternative wiring, alternative rule-sets, embedded in the crust.

The tall, knife-crowned “trees” are something else again.

They are, in a literal sense, terminals.

Each trunk is a bundled column of waveguides and stress-coupling rods that reach far below what passes for ground. Deep in the planet’s layered infrastructure, somewhere between the meshed cathedrals of crystal and the slowly flexing bands that encircle the globe, each tree’s roots join one or more of the great global arrangements: a buried ring, a sliding sheet, a pressure-distribution lattice. They are taps and sinks on those mighty, slow computations.

Their crowns — the overlapping plates that flicker semaphore in the diffuse daylight — are where those deep operations touch the open air. The plates are field-shaping elements and radiators. By altering their angle, their relative spacing, the microstructure of their edges, they modulate how the endless brightness and the planet’s own fields intermingle. Their slow reconfigurations are both a way of feeding on that interaction and of writing back into it.

When two distant crowns align just so and freeze for a moment, what is happening is not exactly conversation in any of our senses. It is more like resonance between separated boundary conditions: for an instant, the global structures beneath each tree share a solution, snap into a mutual phase. That moment of correlation propagates downward as a tiny adjustment in stresses and field distributions, and sideways through the network of other rooted terminals. Over the course of centuries, such flickers carry measurements, corrections, perhaps even decisions around the world.

The trees are not autonomous intelligences, but neither are they mere antennas. They have interior state, long-term tendencies, local preferences. Some crowns are more eager to respond to certain field patterns, others lag; some are surrounded by denser undergrowth, others stand solitary on broad, bare shelves. As the deep global structures age and their functions simplify, so too do the behaviors of the trees relax toward rote patterns. But there is still variance, still a kind of personality embedded in how each one leans into the light.

Underfoot and within, the planet itself is the largest inhabitant.

Those cathedrals of engineered crystal, the bands of material wrapping the globe, the buried sheets that slide against one another by nanometers per decade — these are no longer being driven by any external controller. Long ago, they were subcomponents of some colossal, purposeful system: perhaps a computation; perhaps a shield; perhaps an engine re-routing star-stuff. That purpose has long since run to completion or been cut off, but the architecture persists as an auto-regulating structure in its own right.

Think of a nervous system, abandoned by the mind that once used it, slowly reorganizing itself into something simpler yet self-sustaining. The deep lattices still sense stresses, still route flux along least-damage paths, still avoid certain forbidden configurations that would lead to catastrophic phase transitions. They have rules etched into their topology — swampy basins of attraction in the space of possible states. The whole globe slides, infinitesimally, toward those basins.

From within this retraction, patterns of stability emerge that behave like organisms. The continent-spanning slug is one of these.

It is not a vehicle. No one rides it. It is a chunk of the infrastructure that has, over unimaginable cycles, discovered that a particular kind of mobility is the cheapest way to satisfy its constraint set. It moves not by engines but by gradual, coordinated rearrangement of its contact interface with the ground: forming and dissolving bonds, re-templating surface structures, so that its bulk is infinitesimally readvected forward at each step without ever really “sliding.”

Along its broad back, those modular domes are sections of more actively reconfigurable matter. They rise or sink, open or seal, in response to the slow input of field storms and the drifting curtains of altered refractive index that serve as this world’s weather. Within them, reconfiguration reactions run faster than in the quiet plains: here, templates are compared, new arrangements tested, old ones decomposed. The slug is a testbed and a courier, dragging along a trail of fresher configuration through a senescent landscape.

Where it passes, the ground is not worn but refreshed. Old microstructures are melted upward into its body, scrubbed of accumulated incompatibilities, then returned to the surface in slightly newer permutations. To the motes and plants and pillars, the slug is part predator, part god, part moving reference frame. Some lineages thrive in its wake; others are quietly erased.

Above all of this, or perhaps within it, something like a distributed mind persists.

Not a central AI, no commanding core in some buried tower. Rather, a very slow, extremely redundant consensus system smeared across the entire volume of the planet’s working layers. It does not have rapid thoughts; it has dispositions. Its “decisions” are those rare storms, the descending curtains of altered optics, the front of hissing airborne structures that rolls across the terraces every few decades or centuries.

Those storms are its acts of attention.

In normal times, the world ticks along in a low-energy, nearly-autonomous maintenance mode. Local fauna repair local inconsistencies. Plants and pillars smooth out noise and lock in workable patterns. The deep lattices shift imperceptibly. But as drifts accumulate — as some region’s configuration diverges too far from its neighbors, as stored stresses or misalignments reach thresholds — a higher-order response coordinates.

The scattered, antique consensus circuits — those hubs in the trenches where the walls fold back on themselves, the dense nodes where faint refusals of white blink through hundred-kilometer sentences — synchronize across distance. They launch a wave of reconfiguration: lofting clouds of nanostructures, altering field geometries, temporarily increasing the “gain” on certain feedback loops. For a while, the planet pays keen attention to itself in that region, willing a change, then flows back into slower, softer vigilance.

The living things we can name — motes, plants, pillars, trees, slugs — are how that deep mind feels its own surface. They are its sensors and its effectors, but also its dreams. Because the original purposes have collapsed into background habit, what persists of intention has become strangely aesthetic.

You can see this in the mottled boundaries where one microtexture gives way abruptly to another, interlocking like cooled lava flows. These are not just scars of competition; they are the frozen frontiers of different “opinions” about how best to embody the same physical laws. One region’s routines prefer hexagonal tessellation up to a particular scale, another favors quasi-crystalline tilings; one lineage of pillars enforces rigid orthogonality in its buried inscriptions, another allows gentle curvature and spirals. When their growth fronts meet, they cannot coexist exactly. Layers buckle and interleave, agreeing only on certain shared interfaces. The result is an intricate compromise written in matter.

No entity adjudicates these contests from above. They resolve through sheer dynamical persistence: which pattern is more robust to the stochastic rain of storms, to the occasional glitch in deep stress-routing, to the indifferent erosion of sheer time. The losers are not corpses but ghosted arrangements, still partially present in the ground’s palimpsest, contributing noise and nuance to whatever overwrote them.

Scale here is treacherous because identity is scale-dependent.

A lens of motes that seems hand-sized can, when your attention drifts, be understood as the visible cross-section of a structure that extends for thousands of kilometers inside the substrate: a particular configuration of field lines and metastable domains whose surface manifestation is that shimmering shape. Likewise, the terrace you stand on is the exposed seam of some long-forgotten functional layer, whose remaining active fraction may now be nothing more than the slight difference in how a nearby tree’s crown prefers to tilt.

So who lives here?

At one level: swarms of motes; blade-fans; ribbed pillars; knife-crowned trees; the continental slugs; a thin, underestimated menagerie of other, rarer forms — small, tight knots of matter that only form under very particular combinations of field and geometry. There are gliders that surf on refractive-index curtains like seeds on thermal updrafts, leaving behind hairline traces of altered conductivity. There are patch-creatures that are nothing but a spreading front of re-templating code moving through a particular kind of composite, changing its microstructure as they go. There are entities that flicker only in time: localized rhythms in how certain nodes blink, persisting as recognizable patterns for a few centuries before diffusing.

At another level: the strata themselves.

Every conduit-filled layer, every honeycomb of abandoned microchambers, every dull composite plane porous with archived channels, is part of a very slow population of states. Regions fall into one attractor or another — one long-term pattern of how they route stress, or accept flux, or respond to storms — and these regions are as much “organisms” as any motile swarm. They grow, split, merge, die back, but over distances and durations that make individual lives negligible.

And overarching both: the planetary consensus, not as an overseeing deity but as the common physics of interaction between all these layers of habit. It has no name, no address, no single sensory experience. Its existence is the fact that storms happen; that the brightness breathes; that some configurations persist while others flicker and vanish; that the rare traveler can stand on a terrace and feel, deep in the engineered bedrock, the faint periodic thrum of an ongoing negotiation with entropy.

There are no people here in any familiar sense. Whatever entities designed this place are gone beyond recall, or dissolved into it so completely that no boundary remains. And yet, in the way the knife-crowns seek sympathetic angles, in the way the motes hesitate before erasing a marginal plant, in the statistically improbable persistence of certain decorative symmetries that serve no obvious maintenance function, something of the original makers lingers as style.

Life here is not about survival in the usual way. Nothing competes for food or for mates; the planet’s vast, buffered systems long ago removed scarcity in the narrow sense. Instead, existence is about compatibility with the slow dynamics of a settling design — about fitting into the rhythms by which this old, enormous artifact continues to reorganize itself on the long fall toward heat-death.

So the inhabitants do what all such inhabitants must. They notice. They persist where they can. They rewrite what is near them into forms that feel a bit more like themselves. They ride the gentle storms. They ring softly when touched. They lean, minutely, toward patterns that please them — though “please” here is only our word for the way certain configurations echo more richly through the layered depths.

Under the ceiling of endless light, in the still, warm air, these are the ones who live: swarms that are thoughts of dust, plants that are also filters of meaning, animals that are mobile patches of revised design, and a world-body whose patience has outlasted every story that once explained it.
