They live there, but not in the sense of having arrived.

They are as much in the middle of becoming as the mountain is. When you first see them, they seem like workers hanging from their own unfinished idea of a world—compact figures on cableways and balconies, more silhouettes than citizens. But the longer you watch, the less they resemble any single species. They’re a polity of things learning how to be people in a place that hasn’t decided what “solid” means.

---

### Their bodies

They tend toward the vertical, like everything else here. Limbs a little longer than you expect, joints slightly over-articulated, built for ladders, gantries, sheer drops. When one of them walks a flat span they have a faintly careful gait, as if unconvinced by level ground. Their muscles are corded and spare, not starved but economical, never needing to sprint in a world where nothing truly hurries.

Skin tone shifts along a continuum of stone and sky: grey-brown, iron-red, smutty white with the faint sparkle of mica or salt. Many bear matte patches across shoulders and backs, as if dust has decided it would rather be part of their flesh than lie on the rock. Under the twilight their colors collapse towards charcoal and umber, but when light pools, you notice subtle opalescence in the thin skin around their temples and along the backs of their hands, recalling the plates of the cliff-fliers.

They are not uniform. Some are tall and spare, their fingers long and tendon-strong for gripping cable and rung; others are blocky, their joints thickened, better for anchoring equipment and wrestling with slowly turning machines. The very old or the very changed may have asymmetries: one arm slightly elongated, a leg that seems a little too many-jointed, a spine with a faint second curve. The city’s topologies do not stay consistent; the people who grow up in its overlaps do not either.

Eyes are adapted to reluctant light. Large irises film over when brightness concentrates; in the usual dim, the pupils spread wide and narrow only when they look straight into places where light has pooled. Their gaze is steady. You see them stand at the edges of ledges, looking down through sixty meters of nothing with the same calm interest they give a loose bolt.

They breathe the metallic air like it’s familiar rather than harsh. The tang of machinery and new stone is part of their internal chemistry now; their blood carries fine particulate, trace alloys, microscopic slivers of the glassy conduits they maintain. It does not seem to hurt them. If anything, it lets them read the city by taste and smell alone—a whiff of iron, a drift of ozone, and they know which generator district the wind has passed through.

### Their dress and tools

Clothing clings more than it drapes; loose fabric is dangerous in vertical infrastructure. You see layered wraps, banded around limbs and torso, reinforced at knees and elbows with patches of composite. Their colors are almost always pragmatic: muted blacks and greys stitched with slashes of paint or stained cords in high-contrast hues so bodies can be seen against stone and shadow. Straps and harnesses are standard: webbing that buckles directly into certain walkways or anchor-posts, so a misstep results only in a jolt, not a fall.

The most common accessory is the hook-glove: a partial gauntlet that sheathes the last two fingers and palm in a textured, almost gecko-like material. It lets them cling to rock and metal a heartbeat longer than friction should permit, a small buffer against the mountain’s indecision. Paired with this are pitons that don’t quite pierce the stone so much as persuade it to accept them; their tips vibrate at a frequency that the young rock finds comfortable, opening a brief, local receptivity into which the piton slides.

Everyone carries tools. Not just workers—children, elders, the ones who seem to do little but sit and watch all have something: a narrow-headed hammer, a coil of conductive filament, a set of flattened tuning forks tuned to the voices of different machines. There is no strict division between civilian and maintenance equipment; the city is in constant minor adjustment, and any hand might be the one nearest when a joint needs loosening or a beam re-pinned to a fresh reality.

They carry lights, but sparingly. Hand-lamps emit not a beam but a slow-rolling sphere of more-committed illumination, convincing local twilight to thicken for a few meters at a time. These globes are not thrown so much as coaxed forward, riding along rails of air their bearers seem able to feel. Sometimes a lamp will drift off along a catwalk, following an echo or a faint vibration, and someone will saunter after it, more amused than alarmed.

### How they move and navigate

They travel as much by habit as by map. When a street can misalign itself by a meter in the course of a month, static diagrams get out of date. Their routes are partially memorized sequences of anchors: “three turns past the double-barred stair, across the black-patched girder, under the split pipe where echoes bend left, then follow the smell of hot stone.”

Their sense of direction is layered. They perceive “up” and “down,” yes, but also a kind of sideways-through—the difference between two overlapping versions of the same ledge. They talk about these with words that don’t translate neatly: a path is not only longer or shorter but also “thicker” or “thinner,” meaning how many potential alignments it brushes. The experienced favor thicker routes; they offer more ways to slip past a blockage by stepping slightly out of one reality and into another without ever feeling the moment of crossing.

This is not magic to them, merely familiarity. You’ll see a worker approaching a catwalk that from your perspective ends in open air. Without breaking stride, they adjust their harness, tilt their head as if listening for something, and step onto the nothing. For half a heartbeat their body is stretched, double-exposed—two outlines contesting the space. Then they resolve against a bridge that, to you, wasn’t there until their foot demanded it.

Children learn this early. They play games of proximity with the mountain’s half-formed spires, racing to touch the one that is “most here” without slipping through a thinner patch. They skim along the edges of double-exposed stairs, laughing as their hands come away tingling and briefly translucent, like fingers dipped into cold glass. Adults object only if they do it near a load-bearing column or a pipe whose contents should never be encouraged to consider alternate paths.

### What they are doing here

They are not colonists. They are not miners in the conventional sense, though ore does move along the conveyors. They are midwives to geology and machinists to an infrastructure that is also a teaching apparatus.

Their work has three intertwined strands:

1. **Stabilizing the choice to exist.**  
   They read the mountain’s slow decision-making and offer it incentives. Where a new ledge is thickening from hand’s breadth to foot’s, they install small weights, hanging them on long, taught cables that reach down into the ravines. The drag encourages follow-through: “You have started to be a ledge. Be more of a ledge.”

   Conversely, when a spire is budding where it will interfere with planned conduits or flight paths, they attach resonant dampers, subtle devices that sing a counter-note into the rock, persuading it to remain only a ghost. Their touch is light; they do not carve massive changes, only nudge. They want the mountain to grow into itself, but along patterns that allow cohabitation.

2. **Weaving energy into form.**  
   The slow industry is not merely utilitarian; it conditions the place. The huge flywheels, the trams that slide in and out of reality, the presses that take hours to close—all of these are rhythmic signals, habits being taught to a young world.

   The viscous light that creeps through glassy pipes? They cultivate and refine it from ores that do not quite exist anywhere else. It is less fuel than an instructor: wherever it lingers, stone learns how to hold charge, how to remember the passage of current. The citizens monitor its flow with great attention, sampling the glow at different stations, checking that the mountain is not over-saturating, not learning too quickly in any one place.

   Currency here, insofar as they use such a concept, is measured in “commitment” of light and time: how much of the city’s slow, stored motion you are authorized to invoke, how much solidness you can request from the cliffs for your projects.

3. **Cultivating the biota that will outlast them.**  
   The pocket gardens, the strapped-on terraces of imported soil, the vines threading up support struts: none of this is ornamental. They are seeding future ecologies, seeing how life behaves when fed on condensation and erratic illumination instead of steady sun and rain.

   They track which mosses grow best under engine-breath, which shrubs drink from the light-pipes’ stray glow and remain edible, which creepers can anchor microfractures in stone without shattering it. Every plant that thrives here is a test of compatibility with a world whose geometry is unreliable. They breed hardiness not just against drought or cold, but against shifting adjacency. Roots must tolerate discovering that the patch of stone they entered is now, faintly, also somewhere else.

In their records—etched into composite slates, cached in whispering machine-cores—they maintain elaborate histories of what has been asked of the mountain and how it responded. They treat cliff faces like students, noting which ones accept reinforcement, which resist, which begin to echo too eagerly and must be let rest.

### Their social shape

Their society is layered like the cliffs they occupy, not strictly hierarchical but arranged by depth of engagement with the city’s processes.

**The Edgers** live and work out along the furthest scaffolds, where spires are still half-virtual and platforms are most likely to wake up in a slightly new configuration. They are the testers, the first to cross a fresh bridge or re-anchor a beam whose fastening has drifted sideways in existence. They favor redundancies of rope and hook, speak in a slang rich with geometry-as-verb: “That ledge is thickening forward,” “The support under 7-East is thinking about leaving.” They are the ones most likely to fall, and also the most reverent: they treat the mountain’s whims as weather—respected, never fully predicted.

**The Keepers** tend the huge, slow machinery. They live nearer the mid-level cores where the flywheels turn and the throat-deep generators hum. Their work is not frantic maintenance but long, meditative attendance. Their training includes not only mechanics but also the reading of cycles: a sense for when a press that takes eight hours per stroke is “off” by a few minutes, or when the rhythm of a conveyor’s faint clatter indicates a misalignment several days before it would become visible.

They are the ones who listen at the bones of the city, pressing long hearing-rods against girders and pipes, eyes closed, parsing the layered groan of metal, stone, and the slower, stranger materials that mediate between.

**The Interpreters** occupy the quieter, inner walkways and small chambers from which many pipes converge. They are part engineer, part ecologist, part theologian if you need a word for people who argue about the right relationship between intention and matter. They study how echoes travel, how light stains linger, how a staircase’s destination changes as the surrounding structures mature. They debate—calmly, over years—whether to encourage a certain ravine to deepen or a particular twin-spire to fully condense.

Children move freely among these groups until adolescence, taking on small tasks across all three domains before gravitating toward one—unless they don’t. There remain those who never specialize, generalists who drift between edges, engines, and interpretive cores, knitting the culture together.

Authority is measured in **time listened**, not time spoken: how many continuous hours someone has spent watching a single ledge, a single machine, a single crack, without intervening. Those who have endured the longest vigils are deferred to when the question is whether to touch something at all.

### Their senses and speech

They rely heavily on sound, in a world where sound wanders.

Their language has two layers: a spoken tongue of clipped, compact syllables and a parallel vocabulary of knocks, scrapes, and pitched hums applied to metal and stone. A conversation might be held partly with mouths and partly with hammers against handrails.

Words are for nuance; the knocks are for distance. A double-tap at a certain tempo can ride a beam around a cliff and arrive at a remote platform as clearly as a ring from a direct strike. An apprentice’s training includes learning how different materials bend or carry these notes as the city and mountain shift.

They have dozens of terms for echo-behavior: a “clean return” that comes back along the same path, a “slip-echo” that arrives from a tangent route, a “ghost-echo” whose source has already moved out of alignment. It is common, mid-conversation, for someone to tilt their head and say, “That last word arrived twice; something’s thin near the west braces,” then mark the suspicion in a pocket-slate.

Their hearing is acute, but not superhuman. What differs is their patience: they are used to waiting minutes for an answer they shouted into a grille, used to believing that a noise they hear now might be the late arrival of last week’s dropped wrench. Causality in sound is stretched; they’ve grown a culture around that elasticity. Apologies, for example, are often spoken into metal rather than to faces, with the expectation that they will arrive later, in a different space, when both parties have cooled.

### Their relationship with the fauna

The cliff-fliers roost on antennae and unused gantry arms. The citizens encourage them, leaving out metallic grit and drying racks for shredded cable-sheathing. In return, the fliers carry signals: strips of colored fabric, glimmering bands of viscous light sealed inside thin shells, scent-marked discs. Released from one balcony, a trained flier can be trusted—over hours—to deliver its burden to another region of the cliffs, following air currents the people can only dimly infer.

The metallic beetle-things are tolerated in the way one tolerates a useful but inscrutable coworker. They strip rust where rust is unwanted; they expose it where a surface needs roughening for a future grip. Children collect their cast-off shells and mount them in strings near gardens, where the slow release of stored light nurtures certain sprouts. There is an ongoing, low-key dispute among Interpreters about whether the beetles are native to the place or somehow called into being by the corrosion patterns of the first metal installed here.

The horned quadrupeds are half-wild partners. The citizens recognize that the creatures’ grazing accelerates the aging of rock along specific paths—a service they need. They mark out “encouraged routes” with mineral licks rich in whatever latent substance the animals prefer. When new terraces need to age a little faster, they scatter trace attractants, drawing the herds there.

In lean water periods, there is evidence that some of the quadrupeds are culled for meat, but it is done with ritual care. Before the kill, a Keeper will press a palm to the animal’s horns, feeling the stored light’s gradual pulse, murmuring something that might be a promise to use well the time the creature has condensed.

The translucent floaters from vent-shafts fascinate everyone. Children chase them, leaping to tap their domed bodies and giggling at how sound thickens and distorts. Adults capture a few in fine-mesh cups when a particular piece of machinery needs quiet to be diagnosed, releasing them in banks above the noise source, using the temporary hush to listen more closely. There are strict communal taboos against harming floaters; when they die naturally, their delicate bodies are pressed into thin sheets and used as acoustic filters in listening-chambers.

### Their sense of time

They keep three overlapping calendars:

1. **The Mechanical Long-Count** tracks the cycles of major machines. A press that takes eight hours per closure defines a “stroke.” Ten strokes might be a “wheel-turn.” Fifty wheel-turns, marked in tally marks on a specific column, constitute a “span.” On this scale they schedule construction phases, infrastructure realignments, long-term experiments in rock persuasion.

2. **The Phenological Record** follows biology: first condensation-mist of a new period, emergence of a certain moss’s fruiting bodies, seasonal behavior of the cliff-dwellers. These events are logged with care; many decisions about water allocation hinge on them. To outsiders it might look like they lack seasons, but they recognize subtle cycles in the mountain’s breathing.

3. **The Alignment Ledger** notes topological shifts. When a stair’s destination changes, when a ledge thickens noticeably, when light-pools appear in new configurations along a wall—each is an entry. Some years the ledger is sparse; other years it fills quickly, and everyone feels slightly more alert, more willing to triple-check their anchor-lines.

These calendars rarely agree. A young citizen learns not to ask, “When will X happen?” but “How many strokes? How many mists? How many ledge-changes?” Time is not a single river but a braided set of slow streams, and planning is the art of balancing among them.

This makes them extraordinarily patient. A bridge half-built, its far end apparently hanging into void, is not an incomplete project so much as a statement: “We have done our part up to the current alignment. The rest will become possible when the opposite cliff finishes deciding where it is.” No one rushes the rock.

### Their beliefs

They do not worship the mountain as a god; they collaborate with it as a partner that thinks in stone. Still, there is reverence.

In a small chamber carved into a particularly decisive cliff—one that almost never slips between alignments—there is a wall covered in hand-prints. Each print is marked with a name and a small notation: “Held the crack under the Fifth Trellis until it turned inward,” “Watched Spire 3-B through three hundred spans of inaction,” “Allowed the south face to ignore a proposed brace.” These are not heroic acts in the usual sense. They are acknowledgements of restraint and persistence, of the kind of care that expresses itself as waiting.

The closest thing they have to prayer happens on balconies during what passes for evening. Individuals and small groups stand at railings, looking out over the cliffs and machinery, and quietly narrate their questions into the world:

“Will the northern ravine deepen or widen?”  
“Will the light in Pipe 12 continue its hesitation?”  
“Can we trust the far spire to bear a new garden?”

They don’t expect answers in words. They expect, perhaps, to notice, three spans later, that a crack has thickened in one direction, or that a machine’s hum has resolved into a more regular pattern. The answers arrive in form, not in speech.

They are wary of imposing too much will. Old stories—kept mainly by the Interpreters—speak of a previous attempt somewhere else in the world where humans tried to force a terrain of this kind into rigid compliance. That place, they say, stopped merely misaligning and instead tore, leaving a wound in reality that nothing could inhabit. Whether this is history or allegory hardly matters; the lesson is the same: guide, but do not command.

### Their births, deaths, and continuities

Children are born into mid-level dwellings that cling like barnacles to the more stable cores. Birthplaces are chosen for their acoustic and topological calm—rooms where echoes behave predictably, where the floor has not shifted in living memory.

From infancy they are carried in slings that clip directly into rails and anchor-points. They grow up understanding the city as something you fasten to, not something that merely supports you. Early games involve catching the wavering shadows that overshoot structures, teaching their eyes to recognize that light can lie about edges.

When someone dies, they are not buried in soil, which is too rare and valuable here. Instead, after the body has given its usable nutrients back to the gardens, a small, dense remnant—bone and certain extracted trace metals—is fused into the structure of a chosen beam or anchor-point. Sometimes the spot is marked with a carved glyph; sometimes only the close family knows.

The idea is not that the dead “hold up” the city. Rather, they become part of the network of constraints and reinforcements that continue to teach the mountain how to keep this shape. Names of the dead become shorthand for specific choices in engineering: “We’ll set it as Kera-lean,” meaning, “We’ll permit a little drift in alignment but insist on load-bearing integrity.” Kera was someone who, long ago, made that specific balance work on an early trellis.

Memory is not separate from infrastructure; it is embedded in it, materially and linguistically.

---

### How they think of themselves

They do not call themselves pioneers. The place was not “empty” before they built here; it was unmade differently. They see their presence as one possible solution to the mountain’s question: “What could exist in a world where position and duration are negotiable?”

Ask one who they are and they might answer with a gesture: a broad sweep up and down the cliff-face, encompassing rock, pipe, bridge, garden, beast, mist. They have a word that might best translate as **co-holders**—those who hold with, never alone.

They are wary of leaving. Out beyond the zone of misaligned cliffs and slow machines, in lands with steady horizons and definitive days, they feel strangely unmoored. Stories circulate of individuals who wandered too far into such regions and became ill: their senses over-committed, their patience turned to apathy. The returnees speak of sunlight that insists on being singular, of echoes that die correctly, of roads that refuse to be other roads. It is beautiful, they say, but painfully simplistic.

A few do go and stay away for long spans. They bring back seeds, occasional tools, and always stories of how other people understand solidity. The city listens with interest but rarely with envy. Here, fluidity is not a failure of the world to stay put; it is the very medium in which these people have learned to live.

---

### In the balcony twilight

At what passes for evening, they gather along railings, perched on beam-ends, sprawled on the warm concrete ribs of power housings. The dim light has thickened in certain valleys, left others lean. Slow machines turn barely perceptibly. A quadruped herd climbs a near-vertical run, their horns dimly glowing like a migrating constellation pulled sideways.

A child points at a half-real spire far along the cliff-line. Today it seems slightly more opaque, its edges less hazy.

“Will we build there?” they ask.

“Probably not us,” an elder replies, not sadly. “But someone who knows how we thought. Someone who can listen to what we taught the stone.”

Nearby, an Edger tightens a joint on a brace whose far anchor has drifted just enough to require an extra step. They paint a warning stripe, test the new configuration with their weight, and nod, satisfied. The city has changed, minutely. So have they.

Above, the layered sky refuses again to become day or night. Its glow pools on certain cliff faces, runs like slow water into certain cracks. Below, the mountain continues its almost-imperceptible decisions.

Between them, hanging in the ongoing choice to exist, live these people: patient, spare, and precise, part-engineer and part-ecologist, part-steward and part-student. Not merely inhabiting the city, but co-authoring it, teaching a young world the art of being solid enough to hold life without ever forgetting that it once could have been anything.
