They are not the first inhabitants of this place.

The first were the things that drowned: the air-breathers, the planners, the ones who set the gridlines and named floors and streets. Their bones, where they remain, are only trace minerals now, diffused into the sediment, folded into shells and cartilage, into the tiny hard parts of creatures that never knew what a window was for. Almost nothing here remembers them in any way that resembles memory.

What lives here now has been edited by time and weight and warmth into a very different citizenship.

---

Beneath the slow tides of water, on and within this softened city, the residents are arranged less by shape than by how closely they cling.

### 1. The Thin Film People

The most numerous are the ones you would not notice unless you pressed your face against a wall.

They are microbial mats and colonial films, but “mats” suggests something inert, and they are not. They are the first layer of decision-making in this world. On every surface stable longer than a decade, a politics of slime plays out: who gets the sunless energy, who eats whose waste, who resists the slow trickle of dissolved metals seeping from a hairline crack in an old industrial vault.

In the warm geochemical oases — where fractures in the bedrock undercut the city and leak heat and mineral-rich water — these film-dwellers are thick as velvet. Their bodies assemble in soft bands, each color a different metabolism tuned to a particular chemical indulgence: one line savoring sulfides, another fixing carbon from stray dissolved gases, another crafting fragile nanocrystals around itself to cage and neutralize whatever the city still leaks: cadmium, mercury, arcane synthetics from vanished labs.

They are quiet, but not passive. They trade. Gases bubble from one layer and are immediately absorbed by another. Nutrients flow sideways, not just upward: along a railing, across a crumbled cornice, from one room into an adjacent shaft via slick, invisible bridges. To the rest of the city they are “background,” but they are the deep economy that feeds everything that filters, grazes, or scrapes.

Some have become localized specialists, “house cultures” living only in certain buildings. One species thrives in the iron-oozed water that forever seeps from a submerged subway tunnel, locking the rust into intricate microlattices. Another only lines the interiors of ancient storage cisterns whose contents long ago leached into the water, having learned to exploit a particular pattern of contaminants that exists nowhere else.

If this city has memory, it is here: in the evolutionary tweaks written into these films, tiny genomic footnotes that say: here there was once a paint factory; here, a hospital incinerator; here, a long-ago spill that changed everything for a few hundred years.

### 2. The Quiet Botanists

Rooted life here is sparse but very deliberate. They are the architects of slowness.

The pale star-mats that spread over stable silt are not mere carpets. Each one is a multi-bodied network: hundreds of tiny, leafless stems sharing a single subterranean root web that snakes down through dust and rubble to tap water trapped in collapsed conduits and pipes. These networks map the buried skeleton of utilities more faithfully than any plan ever drawn by human hands. Over centuries they have “learned” that certain linear voids — old pipes, cable chases — tend to accumulate nutrient-rich seepage, and so their roots bias toward them, thickening into pale cords that follow forgotten routes between buildings.

The long ribbon-fronds draping from ledges are opportunists of another kind. They reach downward not only to intercept what little particulate food drifts past, but to brush and read the passing world. Their blades are studded with sensory cells: they taste the faint chemical wake of every animal that passes, register changes in current angle and temperature. In a way, they listen with their bodies.

Those hollow, glassy domes of algae — the half-deflated lungs pressed against vertical facades — are the closest this place has to “balloons.” Within them, microbial consortia trap pockets of lighter gases produced by their own metabolism. The domes do not float; the water is too dense, the gas volumes too small. Instead, they adjust tension and wall thickness to control how quickly gases leak back into the city’s water. These slow releases subtly enrich the boundary layer of water along certain walls, creating microhabitats that attract particular grazers. The domes farm their own consumers: small raspers, transparent snail-like beings that arrive for the gas-rich slime and, in so doing, transfer spores and fragments of algae from dome to dome.

A few rooted organisms have learned true depth. In the broken dome of what once was a civic building, pale trunk-like plants drive thick rhizomes deep into fractures that lead below the city-plain into softer, natural sediments. There, they tap more constant chemistries and perhaps even faint relics of groundwater flow. Their bodies are sparse above, bearing only thin, petal-like outgrowths that host pale symbiotic fungi and bacterial colonies. In return for their anchorage and nutrients, these subterranean plants stabilize whole rooms, slowing erosion and collapse, holding ceiling slabs a little longer in place. Many animals rely on this subtle engineering without any sense that they live inside the architecture of a plant.

To call any of them “plants” is convenient and wrong. Many are as interwoven with bacteria and fungi as corals are with their zooxanthellae. They are composite beings, greenless forests of alliances, growing at the pace of stone weathering and cable rust.

### 3. The Threaders and Feelers

Between the surfaces moves a nation of animals to whom the ruins are not ruins at all, but simply world.

Their eyes, if they still have them, are vestigial jewels: cloudy pearls that once collected light and now do little more than hint at ancestry. Instead they carry their attention in tendrils, whiskers, leaf-like fringes, and long, vibrating spines.

There are the threadfish: narrow, etching the water with bodies as fine as lengths of wire. Their lateral lines are extravagantly developed, a double row of papillae along their flanks that reads pressure changes like braille. They travel in tangled schools through the vertical honeycomb of stairwells and elevator shafts, turning as one when a single member’s skin registers the slight negative pressure of an approaching predator around a distant corner.

Some of these threadfish sow their own maps. Along their route-spines — habitual pathways through the same shaft, around the same broken balcony — they leave behind trace secretions that encourage particular bacterial films to grow. Those films, in turn, emit faint electric signatures slightly different from the surrounding walls. The fish can sense those differences, using them as beacons. Over generations, a shaft used consistently by a lineage becomes both physically and electromagnetically “marked” as theirs.

Others are body-eels: thicker, muscular, coiling into vents and ancient ducts. Some wear along their sides rows of fine, retractable filaments that they extend into cracks like exploratory fingers. Each filament ends in a cluster of cells able to sense not only pressure but also weak bioelectric fields — the cardiac flicker of a resting grazer, the nervy static of another eel hidden two corners away. They inhabit the same infrastructural spaces that once carried heated air and conditioned human comfort; now those volumes host loops of living, feeling rope.

When they hunt, they seldom rush. An eel will align itself along a corridor or shaft, lay its head against a wall, and wait, listening with its flanks to the city’s internal tremor. A distant footstamp of silt as a plated grazer settles its weight three rooms away; a faint, misplaced burst of microbubbles as something disturbs an anaerobic pocket. Only when the pattern matches its internal expectation of “edible” does it begin to move, flowing silently through right-angled geometry that long ago ceased to care which direction was “up.”

Tiny cousins of these feelers never grow longer than a handspan. They cling to surfaces, their bodies flattened, each bearing a corona of short tactile cilia around their edge. When the slow regional current reverses, they collectively lift and reattach, shifting sides of a beam or a doorframe, always staying on the upstream face to intercept what drifts. To a human eye — if one could see here — they would look like wandering lips kissing the city’s bones.

### 4. The Scrapers and Walkers

On every stable surface where a film has grown thick enough to have texture, scrapers roam.

Some resemble flattened crabs, but their shells have lost the raised spines and decorative flourishes of shallow-water kin. Here, smoothness is safety. They close to the geometry, mimicking the general hue of metal-turned-stone and silt. Their legs are multi-jointed, but strong; they step from pit to protrusion with the articulation of something that has spent eons reading terrain through footfall.

Others are columnar, like walking stalagmites. Their bodies are encased in layered exoskeleton that they grow in incremental collars, adding a ring of hardened tissue whenever they molt. Over decades these collars pile up into ridged towers around the softer core. They move rarely, grazing in slow spirals across a chosen wall or pillar. Whole generations might circle the same structural column, eroding the biofilm so consistently that the bare substrate gleams faintly in whatever ambient chemical radiance exists. The column-creatures’ presence becomes visible by absence: a polished vertical path through the otherwise matte-encrusted surface.

Where beams and ledges interlock into three-dimensional meshes, some crustaceans have abandoned any notion of a “floor.” One lineage, long-legged with delicately curved claws, maintains territories that are volumes: cubes of space bounded by four beams and two planes of wall. They patrol these invisible borders, stepping along ceilings as easily as along ledges, snapping at intruders who drift across the unseen boundary. Their sense of ownership is keyed to structural junctions — a cross-brace here, a bolt cluster there — which for them serve as land markers, just as a bend in a river might for a terrestrial animal.

There are climbers adapted to shafts: spiderlike, their dorsal shells encrusted with mimetic growths that look like rust nodules. They ascend elevator wells and service ducts using hooked limbs that catch the smallest defects in the walls. Some of them have entered a peculiar partnership with the jelly-bells that colonize the old hanging cables.

These jelly-bells, translucent and slow-pulsing, feed on fine particulates in the vertical water columns, their bodies anchored to tough, fibrous growths that have replaced the original metal strands. The climbers graze them sparingly, nipping off only detached fragments and dead tissue. In return, the jellies seem to tolerate the crabs’ presence, even wrapping their bells protectively around a perched climber when a rapid pressure shock passes — perhaps shielding them from the worst of the wave.

Farther out on the silt plain, where the city thins, the scrapers grow heavier.

Plate-grazers dominate there: squat, broad animals whose bodies are layered in imbricated armor plates, each plate edged with flexible cartilage. Their mouths are ventral, ringed with muscular, gripping lips that vacuum the top millimeter of silt, sifting for organic particles, bacterial clumps, tiny decaying fragments from the city’s interior. Their eyes are blind nodules buried under translucent skin; what vision remains, if any, is through the detection of light gradients, not forms.

Plated grazers move in loosely associated herds, not out of social inclination in any mammalian sense, but because their foraging algorithm has been tuned: follow the faintest lingering traces of those who fed successfully yesterday. The silt plain becomes a slow, ever-shifting tapestry of grazing paths: broad shallow furrows like the tracks of colossal brushes.

Their dung is not waste, but raw material. Long-bodied worms, each segment bristled with sensory hairs, thread through the loosened silt in their wake, ingesting still-edible residues and aerating microzones where anaerobic and aerobic bacteria can meet. Predators track these worms, and so an entire sideways ecology rides behind the grazing fronts like ghosts behind cattle.

### 5. The Soarers and Shadows

Above and between the structures, in the most open water this place affords, the large hunters travel on planes of cartilage and patience.

They are not fish in any standard sense; they are consequences of endless, slow gulfs of water and hunger. Their bodies have flattened and extended into wings that are almost as wide as a narrow street. Fins have become hydrofoils; tails have become adjustable rudders. They do not beat these wings in frantic strokes. Instead, they tilt, pitch, and yaw minutely, using the long, regional movements of water like a bird uses thermals.

Some are pure filters, gliding with open maws that strain the water for the constant drizzle of particulates shed from the city’s innards. Their mouths are partitioned by fine, flexible combs; their throats throb rhythmically as they pump water through, squeezing out food before expelling the cleared water through slits along their flanks. They often travel set distances “above” the city-plain, their internal sense of depth as accurate as any altimeter, holding themselves in the same invisible layer year after year.

Others are hunters of flesh. Their fronts have evolved into projecting sensory arrays: broad, paddle-like snouts fanned with electroreceptors and pressure detectors. They sweep these arrays through the water, “feeling” three-dimensional outlines of whatever disturbs the fluid. Where a mass of small fish schools along an alleyway, a hunter may detect not the fish individually but the broad envelope of turbulence they collectively create — a shape of stirred water that is to them as visible as a streetlamp.

When they strike, they accelerate with coiled efficiency. From seeming listlessness they can roll and dive, banking between softened towers, folding their wings slightly to tear through narrower gaps, then flaring again to arrest their descent. The water, thick and warm, bends around them; silt rises in lazy echoes of their passing.

Their hunting grounds are patterned. Each one learns, over its lifetime, where currents converge, where the architecture squeezes the tidal breath into faster streams that bring prey in dense, compressed swarms. These places — a particular collapsed atrium, the mouth of a ventilation trunk, the gap beneath a snapped viaduct span — become revisited ambush points, passed down not by word but by whatever instinctive biases evolution can encode: a born leaning toward certain salinities, temperatures, microcurrents.

Rarely, one of these shadows passes close to the plain of plate-grazers. Then the water translates the hunter’s mass into a soft, rolling pressure front that precedes it by seconds. Grazers feel this as a hard-to-interpret dread: a flattening force in their sensory organs, a brief muting of the usual background of small disturbances. Most are too armored, too large, to interest the hunter; but juveniles sometimes vanish in curling vortices of motion and bloodless bite-marks.

### 6. The Trap-Builders Without Hands

Two kinds of inhabitants make the old architecture work again, but in new, blind ways.

The first are colonial stingers: anemone-like animals whose individual polyps are unremarkable — simple tubes with a ring of tentacles, each tentacle studded with stinging cells. What makes them strange is where and how they grow.

They settle along edges where the slow current predictably passes: stair-rails, corridor mouths, the margins of broken windows. Over decades, tiny decisions — a polyp settling a millimeter left rather than right, a neighbor budding outward instead of inland — accumulate into elaborate funnels. The colony as a whole reshapes the local flow: tentacles arranged like a sieve across a hall, angled just so, diverting some water and passing the rest. Schools of small fish, borne along by the regional tide, are compressed through these living mazes. Some escape; some do not.

This is not intention, but emergent geometry. Colonies that grow in patterns which happen, by chance, to capture more prey, prosper and expand. Those whose shapes offer no hydraulic advantage remain small and easily overgrown by other sessile life.

Some stairwells have become fully “organed” this way: every banister thick with stinging funnels, every landing a knot of polyps whose tentacles span the open space like translucent curtains. What was once a path for human feet is now a digestive tract.

The second are the orchestrators of rooms: animals that take the architectural voids not just as backdrops, but as integral parts of their own hunting strategies.

One such lineage might be called the Vault-Keepers. They are not large — each individual no longer than a human arm — but they rule spaces many thousands of times their own body volume. They inhabit large, partially enclosed rooms where only one or two narrow apertures allow entry from the surrounding warren of streets.

Over generations, they have colonized these apertures with living screens: collaborations of filter-feeders, web-fungi, and mucus-secreting worms. The Vault-Keepers patrol within, gently grooming the screens, encouraging some threads to thicken, others to thin, maintaining just enough porosity that the room does not stagnate, yet dense enough that small creatures must squeeze through.

Periodically, when the regional current is just right, the Keeper and its kin retreat deep into niches and flash their bodies with faint electric pulses or brief bioluminescent flickers. These signals are tuned to attract opportunistic feeders that have learned to associate such glows with food: little scavenger fish, shrimps, wormlike things.

Drawn toward the lights, these smaller creatures push through the living screens. Many become entangled. The screen organisms themselves benefit, feeding on trapped animals; the Vault-Keepers benefit as well, feeding on those that struggle free but are weakened, or on the remains left over.

No hand planned this cooperation, yet the result is something like an engineered threshold — a door with a semi-permeable security system, maintained by reflex and selection instead of design.

### 7. The Domestic Swarmers

Some creatures carry the ruins inside themselves.

There is a genus of blind, manta-like animals for whom an inclined overpass is not merely shelter but natal script. They return to the same tilted vault at intervals longer than a human childhood, their bodies fitting under the concrete segment like puzzle pieces. Their ventral surfaces, usually smooth, during spawning swell with thousands of tiny friable nodules: egg-clusters enmeshed in a thin, mucous web.

As they line the underside of the overpass, their bodies almost seal it, narrowing the passage into a soft tunnel. They begin to pulse their wings, not to move, but to stir. The combined motion of dozens of adults generates a gentle, laminar flow that slides along the underside of the structure, from the lower, buried end toward the open plain. Into this controlled current they release their eggs and sperm.

The sticky egg-clusters adhere to the vault’s broken concrete ceiling and to each other, forming fragile drifts. Many are eaten by micro-grazers and tiny crustaceans that take advantage of this seasonal glut. But some detach, entrained in the carefully shepherded flow, riding it like a conveyor belt out toward the silt fields, where, over time, they hatch.

What lives within the bodies of these mantas is stranger still: swarms of bacteria and microalgae that live in specialized sacs along their wing-margins. Most of the time, these symbionts quietly process metabolic waste, buffering the animal’s chemistry. But during spawning, as the mantas crowd into the vault, the microbes bloom, altering the smell of the water, generating a particular chemical signature that — over evolutionary time — has become inseparable from the mating ritual itself.

Newly hatched young who can detect this flavor in the water later in life feel an irresistible pull toward it. Thus the place and the symbionts conspire to anchor the lineage to the architecture, binding behavior to a trapezoid of broken roadway.

Across the city, less dramatic but equally intimate domestications unfold. Small schooling fish harbor ciliate protozoans on their gill flaps that break down complex pollutants; in return, the fish attract predators whose failed strikes occasionally dislodge some ciliates, seeding them into new hosts. Worms cultivate biofilms in modified gut pouches, then excrete them as cohesive pellets that adhere to sheltered undersides of beams, founding new colonies.

The city lost its factories, but its inhabitants have grown their own tiny ones inside their bodies.

### 8. The Mineral Hoarders

From the ancient storage rooms and industrial spaces seep a legacy of altered chemistry: heavy metals, complex polymers, long-banned compounds that never had predators, only dilution.

Most organisms here survive them by avoidance or by letting their microbial partners do the work of detoxification. But a few have turned poison into ornament, or into shield.

These are animals whose tissues grow bright, crystalline nodules: tiny beads of mineralized waste that accumulate under skin or shell. In some, they flare as geometrical studs along the edges of fins or the spines of armor plates. They serve both as storage — a way to quarantine dangerous molecules — and as defense, making a mouthful of flesh crunchy and unpleasant.

One lineage of slow, finger-length grazers has taken this further. Their entire dorsal surface is an array of small crystal blades, like the facets of a mosaic. As they age and accumulate more burden from the water and their food, these microblades grow sharper. Predators that bite them sometimes suffer lacerations inside their mouths; infections follow. Over generations, hunters have learned to avoid the gleam of patterned dorsal crystals in the dimness.

At death, the grazers’ bodies decompose, but their burdened scales and nodules remain, dropping into the silt as a rain of tiny artifacts: flecks of unnatural clarity amid the dull grains. Buried and exhumed and buried again, they provide rich veins of rare elements for bacteria willing to unpick them. In time, some of those bacteria find their way back into new grazers’ guts.

So a perpetual circuit emerges: the city’s old toxins become part of an internal geochemistry; creatures become moving ores; death becomes slow, diffuse excretion back into the environment. Nothing is healed, exactly, but nothing is left unaccounted-for.

### 9. The Sense of Routes

No single animal here understands the city as a whole, but together their movements give it shape.

Migratory paths lace the drowned district like ghost transit lines. Schools of small, banded fish pass, always, through the same series of vertical shafts as they move between rich feeding grounds and safer resting spaces. To them, each shaft is known by a felt pattern: the particular way currents twist around a broken stair, the slight warmth of a geothermal seep through one wall, the taste of a certain bacterial film on a beam that protrudes at head-height.

Eel-bodied hunters shadow these migrations at a distance, learning the same sequence in reverse, committing to memory the slight delay between the fish leaving one shaft and appearing at the next. Their lives are stories of timing: miss by two minutes and the hall is empty; time it well and the corridor becomes a flurry of scale and flesh.

Crustaceans that defend volumetric territories learn the invisible highways that cut through their spheres — routes they cannot fully block unless they wish to starve. So they negotiate: their aggressive postures and click-patterns are tuned to specific angles of approach, allowing certain passersby through while turning others away.

Filter-feeders time their openings to the predictable phases of the deep tidal “breath.” Their polyps open in waves, a bloom of tiny mouths unfurling in anticipation a few moments before the pressure shift arrives. The resulting feeding pulses propagate through the ruins like slow, invisible lights switching on and off in sequence, a rhythm of hunger synchronized to ocean-scale changes.

In this way, without language, without planning, the beings here create infrastructure: not of steel, but of habit. Their bodies and instincts do what the old street plans did — make certain routes more used, certain junctions more important.

Over time, the architecture responds. Frequently traveled corridors remain swept of loose silt by endless fin-strokes, retaining hollows and edges that attract more dwellers. Neglected rooms fill, collapse inward, become part of the plain, erasing connections. The living world edits the dead one, reinforcing some lines, letting others fade.

If there are “neighborhoods” here, they are defined by these emergent patterns of passage: clusters of structures between which life moves freely, separated by zones of relative emptiness or danger.

### 10. The Long Time Dwellers

Some residents are as slow as the buildings themselves.

There are beings embedded in the very mineral crust that has formed over old metals: sponges and sponge-analogues whose bodies are lattices of pores and spicules, through which water sifts in perpetual, unhurried circulation. They may cover entire exposed faces of old machinery or bridge gaps between beams with leathery mats that look dead until you see the slow inhalation of their oscula.

These long-livers can persist for centuries, adding millimeter upon millimeter of growth. They become recorders of stability: if a wall collapses, they die; if it merely tilts over decades, they flex, growing more on the side now facing the richer flow. Their shape, if read, would tell you which parts of the city have barely moved since the flood, and which have been restless.

Within deeper, sealed pockets — rooms that only recently cracked open — you find relict ecologies. There are small, translucent crustaceans that evolved in isolation among stale air and seep-water, then suddenly found the ceiling broken and the warm flood pouring in. Some died immediately; others adapted on odd trajectories, keeping vestigial air-breathing organs repurposed as float bladders or chemosensory sacs.

A few such pockets host “ghost faunas” from earlier phases of the flood: lineages that have gone extinct elsewhere in the district but linger here in compromised refashionings. They are living memories trapped in cul-de-sacs of fitness, surviving because nothing else knows how to exploit precisely their combination of narrow conditions.

When a quiet quake loosens a new seam and such a pocket opens, the entire surrounding web shivers in response: new chemicals, new potential foods, new diseases, new predators leak out, and the local ecology flexes to accommodate, erase, or incorporate them.

---

What lives here, then, is not a catalog of curious aquatic forms, but an ongoing negotiation between water, ruins, and time.

They are touchers and listeners in a place where sight has resigned. They are meticulous misers of energy swimming in a medium that feels like sleep. They are opportunists of fixedness, anchoring themselves to the bones of a civilization that built tall and firm and then drowned.

None of them know what a window was for, or a viaduct, or an elevator. But they know, with their skins and valves and coiling paths, that there is an angled vault where the current births their young, a shaft that is always safe, a wall that tastes just slightly of iron and warmth. They know that when the far quake groans, they must tuck close to stone. They know when to open, when to shut, when to drift, when to explode into motion.

The city believed it would last forever. Something does. It is not the shapes the builders intended, but the shapes that life has made inside them: lattices of habit, of hunger, of slow, patient accumulation.

Those are the true inhabitants of this dark, warm, weighted place.
