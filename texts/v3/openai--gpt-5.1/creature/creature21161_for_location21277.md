They are small in the scale of their world, and they never stopped listening.

They are not many.

What you see first, if you see them at all, is motion that doesn’t match the plateau’s ordinary nervousness. Everything else here moves in bursts, in flutters, in avalanches of wings and legs and beating leaves. These people move in deliberate curves, leaving gaps of stillness around themselves like eddies in a storm.

Their bodies are made to disappear into the living floor. Standing, they are a little shorter than you might expect for something bipedal: low enough that the fan-leaves and arching boles around them can hide a crouched form entirely, tall enough to reach from mat to overhanging tissue in a single stretch. Limbs are long relative to the torso—arms especially, with a double range of motion at shoulder and wrist that lets them reach above, below, and behind without shifting their center of gravity much. They think sideways as often as forward.

Skin is not bare. What at first glance looks like fur is something between hair and cilia: short, fine filaments densely packed, each with its own minute musculature. When a wind gusts across the plateau and the plants bow, their bodies ripple in a different direction, cancelling turbulent eddies, turning their outlines into extensions of the mat. In damp air the filaments bead with moisture; they drink through their skin as much as through their mouths.

Color is adaptive but not dynamic like a cuttlefish; it’s tuned by growth and by slow shifts rather than moment-to-moment camouflage. Individuals born on the darker, wetter bands are deep moss-green through charcoal, glossed with a faint, oily iridescence. Those from drier domes are ochre, grey, lichen-pale with mottles that echo the crust plants there. Their eyes—set wide, slightly forward, under a bony brow ridge grown into a continuous, flexible crest—are large, dark, and wetly reflective. That crest is not just armor; it’s a resonator.

They have no obvious external ears. Under the ciliated pelt, their skull carries loops and hollows, labyrinths of air and cartilage. Sound reaches them through bone, through the flex of filaments, through the living ground itself. A loud call on the plateau does not simply arrive; it blooms inside their heads, mapped instantly against a memory of thousands of other calls. They feel direction in sound the way others feel it in light.

Their hands and feet are broad, with four main digits and a smaller opposable nub. Pads are not hard like hooves, not soft like human palms; they are thick, springy, latticed with vascular sponginess that can lock rigid or relax. When they stand still they spread weight over a wide area, barely dimpling the mat; when they choose to leap or strike, they focus that force into a narrow pulse, briefly compressing the plant floor, squeezing a faint wheeze of gas from the pores beneath. They feel for that response, sensing species and moisture by the way the substrate sighs against their skin.

Teeth are omnivorous, but skewed toward grinding rather than shearing. They are not apex predators here; nothing with their mass and tool-habit could ever compete in speed and sheer number with the flying swarms and heavy channel-beasts. Instead, they eat whatever fits into gaps: seeds uncracked by other foragers, bits of soft plant tissue that would otherwise rot in inaccessible pockets, insects and tunnel-things uncovered by storms. They carrion-feed when they can, but always in the wake of larger killers, never contesting a fresh kill.

Their mouths do something eyes can’t: shape the world’s roar. Lips are mobile, cheeks muscular, tongue long and tapered. They can produce a startling variety of sounds—clicks, hums, booming pops, flute-like whistles forced through narrowed throats. Some of these are speech. Many are not.

“Speech,” for them, is never just sound. Hands are always involved: flicks of fingers, tension in wrists, small shifts of posture. On the plateau, noise is constant; if you want to mean something, you wrap it in redundancy. A warning is a low tremor in the mat underfoot, a pair of clicks you feel more than hear, and a shared glance toward a particular arc of the horizon. A promise is a palm pressed briefly to another’s chest, filaments aligned, heartbeats synchronized for three breaths.

They do not shout unless they must. Shouting is bait.

Their children are very quiet.

Infants cling. They are born with thicker filaments and oversized fingers, and they spend their first seasons pressed against the adults’ ventral fur, carried and warmed by the parent’s body. For a long while they make almost no vocal sound. Instead they vibrate: minute thrum-patterns in their developing resonant crest that the adults feel through skin contact. Only when they can already move with some competence—when they can step from mat to branch-knuckle without losing balance, when they can feel the shift of a predator’s approach in the floor’s tone—do adults allow them to make noise freely.

They grow up in networks, not in single-family shelters. The “villages” that once dotted some basins were never dense clusters; they were strings of occupation across a slope, never so close that a single predator-wave could take all at once. Now, with the plateau’s life thickened, even those strings have thinned. A given valley, as wide as a city would be, may be home to only twenty or thirty of them, scattered in ones and twos, loosely aligned by kin and by acoustic familiarity rather than walls.

Their shelters are less “places” than consensual edits in the plant architecture.

They do not cut living tissue unless they have to. Instead they encourage, coax, and misdirect it.

Long ago, when their numbers were greater, they seeded certain plants with a bias for pliability—those arching, tunnel-forming species in particular. Now, a band of them can spend a dry season slowly bending a young arch down and sideways, singing to it in tones that trigger specific growth responses, weighting it with carefully placed stones and dampened mats, until it fuses into a low, overhung gallery: a space where they can gather out of the direct rain and out of the densest part of the swarm-flows.

The gallery looks, to an untrained eye, like any other vegetative tunnel. Only up close do you notice the slight regularity of its curves, the extra thickness in certain walls where they have induced storage tissue to grow, the consistent low resonance when you stamp the floor: they have encouraged hollow chambers under there, caches kept cool and moist.

They store things not in piles but in gradients. Food, tools, and memory-objects are embedded within the mat at different depths and distances. A seed-nut meant to be eaten soon is wedged just under the thin surface sponge, reachable with a single probing hand. A carved bone—a tool head, a treasured knapped shard of plateau-quartz—is pushed deeper, coaxing the plant-fibers to weave around but not through it, marked above by a slight discoloration, a shallow depression, a resonance note only they bother to hear. Artefacts that matter to the group as story rather than as daily function are placed deepest of all, in cool, nearly anoxic pockets where roots are sparse. Retrieving them is an event.

Those bone splinters you notice, here and there in the overgrowth, are the ones they failed to retrieve before plant-seasons closed over them, or ones they chose to abandon deliberately—signs in a script only they and a few other plateau animals even perceive. A drooping, thickened leaf at the edge of a slope, its petiole scarred by a series of tiny, regular diagonal cuts, might mean: dangerous swarms cross here at midday. A ring of oddly straight twigs woven into the mat around a basin patch might mean: here, long ago, we survived a storm.

They do not build fire often, and rarely openly.

Open flame is difficult on a surface that is mostly living fiber and water, but not impossible. In the driest domes a spark can run wild, panicking insects and birds, drawing predators. Fire is conspicuous in a world that otherwise glows only from fungal patches and rare bioluminescent insects. When they make fire, they prefer to do it in damp hollows, under edges of overhang where rising heat will draw moisture from the surrounding mat and create its own suppression ring.

They use it for three main purposes: to crack unusually tough seed coats, to smoke and soften certain adhesives they derive from plant resins, and—only in old stories now—to burn back a strip of overgrowth and create a temporary open scar. Those old burning scars, long healed, are why some basins still show belts of unusually uniform, same-age vegetation. In those days, they tried to push back the plant’s creeping continuity to carve out acoustic refuges, places where noise was briefly less, where they could hear themselves think without a hundred meters of insect-chatter between.

The plateau pushed back. Bare rock was an opportunity, and plant colonizers rushed in. In a generation, the scar was covered again. In two, the descendants of those aggressive colonists made the loudest seed-pops in the region, masking any advantage the scar had once given them.

Their tools match the plateau’s refusal to hold sharp edges for long.

Stone here is ancient, smoothed, its fractures reluctant. They cold-work small nuggets of tougher material when they find them: iron-meteorites lodged in old impact basins, denser knolls of silicate that flake grudgingly into blades. Those blades are rare and valued. More often they work with bone, horn-like growths taken from certain channel-beasts’ armor, and with consolidated plant tissue—dried and resin-hardened struts carved from older, dead boles.

A digging tool is a slightly curved spike on a woven handle, used with a rocking motion more than a stab. A cutting implement is a shark-tooth analog of flaked quartz or bone, hafted into a composite edge of plant resin and fiber that can be unbound, sharpened, re-bound. They do not quarry; they glean.

Their most sophisticated constructions are not visible on the surface at all. They are acoustic.

They have always lived in a world defined by noise. Sound tells them where predators are, where storms build, where the big semi-aquatic beasts churn the wet channels below the mat. Over time, they learned not just to listen but to shape the soundscape.

Long before any upright stones marked their presence, they learned that certain hollows under the mat deepened particular frequencies. A family that favored the long, mournful whoops that carry well at dusk might gradually scour and pat the substrate under their resting spot into a shallow bowl, so that their voices emerged with a distinctive tremor. Others, hunting a skittish prey sensitive to low rumbles, learned to dig flat-bottomed pockets that absorbed those notes, creating a local silence in which a single high snap would stand out cleanly.

The “singing stones” are where this experimentation eventually met geology.

Those crude rings and leaning slabs were never about creating enclosed spaces. They are tuned reflectors. Placed where the plateau’s own curvature focuses wind and distant animal cry, angled to catch the roar of a particular channel or swarm-path, they amplify some ranges and cancel others. A ring might create a bubble of comparative quiet at its center, a place where you can hear the faint tap of an insect’s legs against your own palm despite the surrounding din. A line of slabs might create a shadow in sound—a zone in the lee where a family can move with less risk of triggering sound-reactive plants.

Plants, over time, crawled over these stones, rooted into their microcracks, hummed and boomed against them. Symbiotic tissues grew, organs of living resonance. To most of the plateau’s fauna, they became just another patch of territory with its own strange acoustics. To these people, they are still tools, still instruments—albeit half-forgotten ones.

For their elders, they are also ancestors.

They do not bury their dead in the sense of digging dedicated graves. The plateau does not hold voids for long; anything opened is soon filled by roots and opportunists. Instead, they choose where to leave bodies with obsessive care.

Some are laid in the cool if temporary stillness of old basins, where sound is damped and growth is slower. The body is arranged in a particular posture—limbs tucked, crest resting against a chosen stone. They sing and stamp until the mat under the corpse grows soft and willing, then press it down until plant tissue cups it, accepting, not yet digesting.

Over seasons, the corpse collapses. Bones become cavities, cavities become reservoirs of slightly different chemistry. The plants that colonize those pockets grow with a marginally altered resonance: their tissues denser, their gas-chambers broader or more finely partitioned. The elders can identify those patches by ear. To a child brought there, they say: listen. That deep note there, just under your teeth? That is how your grandparent spoke.

Elsewhere, where a death was violent or away from the chosen places, bones are collected when possible and brought to known caches. Certain overhangs bear, embedded within their under-surfaces, a line of bone like a chord across an instrument. When a storm-wind comes from the right quarter, those bones vibrate softly. It is not speech. It is a remembering of presence.

Their religion, if you must call it that, is built out of this insistence that nothing is ever only itself. A plant is never just a plant; it is water-pattern, ancestor-note, predator-marker, shelter-wall, instrument. A stone is a knob in the world’s echo. A dead channel-beast, moldering in a wet trough, is food, is danger, is a future ridge in the mat’s internal architecture.

There are no temples. There are routes.

They walk long, slow circuits across the plateau, following paths that spiral through basins, over domes, along wet bands and dry crusts. On each circuit they stop at familiar resonant sites: a tunnel mouth that sings when stepped on in a certain pattern, a pair of facing arches that make a chord when wind crosses them from northeast, a cracking seed-pod grove that bursts in midday heat. At each they add a small gesture—a new cut on a petiole, a stone shifted half a hand-width, a hummed motif folded under the plant’s own sighing.

They are mapping changes of a living instrument, and in some diffuse way, they are playing it.

They have predators like everyone else.

The larger hunters of the plateau are too fast, too blunt an instrument to aim exclusively at these small, clever bipeds, but accidents and opportunistic kills happen. A semi-aquatic beast surging up through a thin spot in the mat; a flight of bulb-bodied fliers overshooting their swarm-prey and colliding with a family on the move; a heavy-skulled stalker that has learned the specific silence of these people’s resting-places and waits there.

More insidious are the chorus predators: those that shape plants with sound. Some of those species have learned that the particular banded, modulated whistles these people use to communicate can be turned back upon them. A hunter perched among bridge-tissues may mimic a common call that opens certain fog-net leaves, making a sudden bright gap in an otherwise continuous wall, luring a careful walker into thinking a passage is clear when a drop waits beyond. Another might imitate the short, sharp pulses that these people use as “here, I am safe,” drawing a curious child toward a knot of foliage that conceals a trap.

The people respond not with escalation but with diffusion. Their “language” is not one fixed system, but many overlapping dialects of sound and gesture, each local, each slightly altered generation to generation. Calls that are exploited by predators fall out of use. New ones, with slightly shifted pitch or timing, emerge. They do not write, but they edit their own vocal habits with a community memory as attentive as any lexicon.

Their greatest danger is not claws or fangs, but loss of coherence.

They are scattered. Populations separated by only a few days’ walk can drift apart entirely in their signal patterns. A traveler might arrive on a distant dome and find cousins there whose warning-clicks register only as random pops in the roar, whose gesture for “hide” reads as “come” in their own body-script. Misunderstandings here are not merely social mishaps; they are openings for the plateau’s other players.

Once, long ago, there were more of them, enough that information could sweep the tableland in great waves: news of a new predator, of an unusual plant mutation, of a storm-season that rose earlier than expected. Now, with their numbers pressed thin by competition and diluted by time, news travels in patchy, uncertain eddies. Stories diverge, fragment.

And yet they persist, because nothing on the plateau is truly alone.

They have built, perhaps unintentionally, an extended mind in the living mat itself.

The plants they have nudged for generations respond not only to water and light and sound, but to particular patterns of vibration that only these people consistently produce. A certain three-beat stamp followed by a low hum might cause a leaf sheet to curl just enough to reveal the small, moist pocket where their ancestors always stored a backup bundle of dried fungus. A particular click-sequence might make a line of tiny capillaries in the substrate constrict, briefly diverting water flow to an adjacent tunnel where seedlings, planted by hand, are waiting.

Even where there are no people any more, those capacities persist for a time. A lone traveler, moving through a basin where their kind has not lived in five or six generations, might still find that singing a half-remembered greeting song makes the mat underfoot swell slightly, its pores opening, releasing a faint scent of old resin and a cooler patch of air. The living world remembers their habits the way stone remembers pressure: as slow deformations, preferential paths.

You could say that these people are the plateau’s last conscious stewards. That would flatter them more than they themselves ever claim. In their own thinking, they are simply one more layer in the braid: a kind of mobile pseudopod the plant-skin once grew, which learned to think for itself and started making requests rather than merely responding to conditions.

Their myths—if you sat with them on a relatively quiet night and they trusted you enough to speak at length—tell not of dominion but of a long apprenticeship.

They speak of a time when the mountains still rose clearly above the plant-skin, bare and cold, their flanks striped by trickling free water. In those stories, their ancestors were cliff-dwellers, clinging to stone ledges, watching the slow spread of green up from the valleys. The plants then were simpler, more separate things. You could walk between them. You could see the rock under your feet.

As the green flooded upward, the ancestors learned to ride it, to anticipate which cracks would sprout arch-plants, which basins would fog earliest. They wove ropes and nets then, of free-standing fiber, connecting outcrops before the plants did. They used fire more. They believed, sometimes, that they could hold the line, keep some bare heights for themselves and their hunting-grounds.

The stories always end the same way: with the admission that they were wrong. The plants filled everything. The stone disappeared under life. The last scarp fell under a mat. The people either learned to live as another layer, or were peeled off and composted.

There is no bitterness in how the oldest storytellers speak of this, only a kind of wry awe. The plateau is ancient beyond them; its quiet bones will remain when they and their plant-partners are gone. They have had the rare, strange privilege of living through the end of one geological story and inside the middle of an ecological one.

Who lives here, then?

At one scale: the roaring multitudes of insects and gliders and behemoths; endless enzymatic shufflings; the slow, wet architecture of plant flesh arranging itself into bridges and galleries and cisterns.

At another: these sparse, intent people, moving like thoughtful shadows across a world too loud for solitude, their senses stretched between bone and mat, their artifacts half-swallowed, their language intertwined with the habits of leaves.

They are not rulers, not ghosts, not quite symbionts in any contractual sense. They are a listening, remembering presence: an animal that learned to hear the plateau as a single, enormous instrument and then, tentatively, to touch the keys without breaking them.

They live in hollows of comparative silence they’ve carved from an endless roar—under arches that bend because generations before them sang them that way, between stones that answer back when the wind is right, above tunnels that glow with borrowed fungus-light. They live in the way a hand rests lightly on a drumhead, feeling both the skin and the space beneath.

There are not many of them, and each year there are a few fewer. But as long as one pair of broad, ciliated hands still knows how to coax a leaf to curl, as long as one resonant crest still hums the pattern that opens the old water-pocket in the dry season, as long as one child still learns the difference between a predator’s mimicry and an elder’s call, something of them lives not only in bodies but in the plateau’s very habit of being alive.

Whoever comes here later—if anything ever does—will walk over a surface that still bears those habits. They may never see a single biped step out of the green. But if they listen, truly listen, they might notice that in certain basins, when the noise dips for a heartbeat, the plants themselves seem to be waiting for a song they have not heard in a long, long time.
