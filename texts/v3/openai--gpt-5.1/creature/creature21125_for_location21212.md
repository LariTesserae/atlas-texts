The ones who live here are not the city’s owners in any simple sense. They are more like tolerated tenants inside a body that predates them, and which may outlast whatever they think they are building.

### The substrate-minds

First, there is the great plant itself.

It is not a single, naive organism. It is old—older than the rail-lines, older than any metal band driven into its ribs, older even than the hollowing-out that made these avenues. Its intelligence, if you call it that, is slow and spatial rather than quick and discursive.

Its “thoughts” are gradients of hormone, long waves of electrical potential creeping through phloem-like cords, localized pulses of growth and abscission. It does not think in sentences; it thinks in flows and re-routings, in which parts of itself to hydrate, which to lignify, which to let rot back into mineral and reclaim.

In some eras (for it measures time in shifts of tectonic tension and climate, not in days) it has been more actively agentic. Right now, with this city in its flesh, it is in a state somewhere between cooperation and controlled infection: constricting certain vascular streams around heavy industrial cores, thickening tissues under overbuilt plazas to bear the new weight, tentatively steering new hollowings toward regions rich in mineral seams.

You can feel its preferences if you watch long enough:

- A corridor that continually tries to narrow, despite the metal bands holding it open—this is a place the substrate has decided should not be a corridor.
- A shaft that stays stubbornly clear of extraneous growth, its walls smooth, its conduits stable—that is a line of passage the plant “agrees” with, a vein aligned with its own long-term patterns.

At a few nodes deep in the city’s heart, the plant’s awareness is locally intensified. Here, dense knots of vascular tissue form something like ganglia—fat, pale nodules buried in walls and columns, laced with sensor-analogues: pressure plates of living tissue, electric-responsive crystals, chemical tasting cells. Around these nodules, the city’s conduits cluster. Valves, shunts, monitoring devices encrust the surface like barnacles on a rock.

These are negotiation hubs, where plant and machine continually trade: the plant offers sap-pressure, structural flex, heat differentials; the city injects trace minerals, synthetic hormones, cooling fluids. At these nodes, the plant’s time-scale gets pulled slightly upward: its reactions sharpen from decades to months, from months to days, sometimes even to hours.

If anything like a resident deity lives here, it is this slow substrate-mind, not omnipotent, not all-seeing, but massively present, distributed and patient.

### The dryfolk

Within this living maze walk the ones who, on first glance, you’d call the citizens.

They are built for dryness. Their skin—if it is skin—is thick and matte, not scaled or furred, but something like very fine bark over muscle. On closer inspection it’s not bark at all, but an engineered integument: layered polymers and bioceramics laced with capillary channels, able to hug sparse moisture closely and leak almost none.

They are roughly human-shaped, when seen from a distance in the half-green light: two legs, two arms, a torso, a head. But the proportions are subtly wrong to someone expecting surface anatomy. The limbs are a little too slender given their evident strength; the necks a little too long and flexible; the hands with a finger or two more than you’d think necessary. Their faces economize on orifices: narrow nostrils, thin-lipped mouths, eyes hooded not against sun but against dust and stray spores.

They move with care, not out of fear but because vibration is a cost. The floor gives under their weight; they adjust their gait so as not to send unnecessary tremors into the plant. Heavy things are moved by rails, not by stomping boots. When they run—and they can—they seem almost weightless, footfalls a series of respectful, shallow compressions.

Their internal physiology is as dry as their world. Sweat, if they still have anything like it, is minimal, a rare emergency response. Their kidneys, or kidney-analogs, are ruthless. They recycle water in slow loops; their breath exhales warmth and gas but hardly any moisture. A dryfolk corpse, untampered, would mummify, not rot.

They are also partially integrated into the plant’s systems. Not in the grotesque sense of being rooted to the floor, but built to interface.

Running under their skin, especially along the forearms, spine, and neck, are symbiotic cables: flexible filaments of living conductive tissue, grafted during early development and woven through with artificial conductors. At certain junctions in the city—small, polished nodes set into walls—they can touch these graft-lines to tap data, power, or chemical cues. For a moment, their nervous system and the plant’s signaling networks overlap: they feel, faintly, the pressure in nearby conduits, the temperature gradients, the presence of other connected minds.

For most, this connection is a brief, utilitarian link: to check route maps, to call a transport limb, to retrieve a maintenance schedule. But there are specialists for whom this link is deeper and longer-lasting.

#### The vascular guilds

Among the dryfolk, some train to become mediators with the substrate. They are the vascularists, the shapers, the ones who “speak plant.”

They tend to be slender, with more extensive grafting. Fine traceries of symbiotic tissue show just beneath their skin, especially around temples and wrists: pale, branching marks like delicate scars. When they attach themselves to a node, they may remain motionless for long minutes, even hours. During this time, their bodies cool slightly, external awareness dimming as internal maps bloom: 3D models of sap-flow, stress lines, nutrient gradients—experienced not as visual images but as felt tensions, urges, resistances.

They preside over decisions like:

- Whether to allow an industrial cavity to expand another meter, or to thicken the supporting walls.
- Whether to divert a rich node of sap to feed a new cluster of luminescent panels, or to maintain a reserve against future drought.
- Whether to let a patch of opportunistic fungus keep flourishing in a semi-abandoned tunnel—because it’s quietly digesting problematic waste—or to scrape it out before it becomes a hazard.

Their authority is less political than infrastructural. They occupy something like a priest-engineer role, though they would likely reject both terms. Their lodges are quiet chambers near major ganglia, where walls are almost bare of mechanical fixtures and heavy with subtle chemical marks—zones where the plant is allowed to speak more loudly.

#### The machinists and graftwrights

Set apart from the vascularists, though often working with them, are those who deal primarily in the non-living part of the city’s technology: machinists, graftwrights, conduit-binders.

They are responsible for:

- The spiderlike maintenance bots with plant cores.
- The under-rail trains and their gentle engines.
- The delicate valves that tap vascular channels without triggering defensive occlusions.

They work in spaces heavily shielded from the substrate’s feelings—rooms lined with dead composite, where impacts and heat can be more freely allowed. Their tools are precise: heat-knives with edge-temperatures tuned so finely that they can cut and cauterize without sending shockwaves of pain-like signal through the plant; micro-borers that slide between vascular bundles without bruising them.

Graftwrights specialize in the borderline: places where living tissue must be forcibly taught to accept inert inserts, or where a piece of machinery needs to be grown in place rather than installed.

A graftwright might, for instance, “plant” a new machine core not by lowering in a pre-built engine, but by seeding a specially bred organoid into a prepared hollow, wrapping it in scaffolded sili-fiber, then over weeks feeding it signals and nutrient flows until it matures into the central living component of a future maintenance robot. Only then are metal limbs bolted on.

It is not clear, even to them, whether they are growing machines inside the plant, or encouraging the plant to bud machines of its own.

#### The linewalkers

Because the city is spread and sparse, with vast unused spaces and a constantly changing periphery, there exists a caste of movers: couriers, inspectors, scavengers—call them linewalkers, for they spend their lives tracing the lines of edge and connection.

They are the ones you would most often see moving alone through the long empty avenues, harnesses jangling faintly, short cloaks patterned with the same striations as the walls. Their packs are not simply bags, but small, semi-living containers: bulging pods that keep delicate equipment at stable humidity and temperature, or that slowly extrude replacement components by internal chemical printing.

They know every district’s current temperament:

- This hollow has started to resonate with the deep respiratory pulses; avoid wheeled loads here until damping ribs are installed.
- That maintenance corridor now grows hairline exudate crystals; you can grind them for mineral supplement, but step carefully—the crystals are slick.
- Those old service tunnels on the northwest flank have begun swallowing sound more than usual; something in the deeper structures is thickening, and predators often follow.

Linewalkers maintain the mapping layers that everyone else depends on: not static blueprints, but living, constantly updated models of where it is safe to go today, this week, this month.

They are also the main interface with whatever lives in the unincorporated dark.

### The ferals and the deep fauna

Beyond the planned districts, in the half-tamed periphery and the unlit hollows, there are those who do not participate in the negotiation—at least not consciously.

Some are animals in the simpler sense: the hanging grazers, the ribbon-gliders, the quadrupeds that slip along back-avenues. Many of these are descendants of surface or earlier-substrate lineages, hardened and desiccation-proof, their metabolisms slowed to match the scarcity of free water.

Others are the ferals: dryfolk who, by choice or expulsion, no longer plug into the city’s nodes. They survive where the plant architecture is less edited and the rules blur.

Ferals often carry visible signs of diverging adaptation:

- Grafts gone wild, where abandoned interface-tissues have grown into strange bulges and tendrils, sometimes interfacing directly with opportunistic vines or parasitic growths rather than sanctioned conduits.
- Eyes tuned to dimmer light, pupils wide and slow to constrict.
- Companion-animals that are themselves hybrids: small, arachnoid creatures with plant-vein cores similar to the official maintenance bots, but domesticated along different lines.

There is an exchange between feral zones and city proper. Linewalkers trade tools and compressed nutrient slabs for information: where a new mineral-laden exudate vein has broken through, where a deep fissure now vents warmer air, where something large and hungry has taken up residence.

Deeper still are the things that the planners only half-acknowledge.

In some dim clefts, the plant’s control is weaker, its tissues older, riddled with history: sedimented layers of old channels, calcified scars, horizons of once-living matter now mineralized. Here, sub-ecologies form:

- Pale, blind scavengers that drink from occasional slow leaks of true liquid sap.
- Fungal forests like inverted coral reefs, rigid and dry, yet alive at their core.
- Predators shaped like flowing shadows, their skins tuned to the plant’s electrical fields, slipping through places where sensory tissues are sparse.

The city’s authority in these depths is advisory at best. When a trainline must cross such a region, whole teams of vascularists and machinists negotiate with the substrate for months beforehand, prepping buffers and guard-lines, learning the rhythms of whatever other life pulses there.

Every so often, a new tunnel wall bulges oddly, or a chamber that was mapped as empty fills overnight with a knotted mass of tissue that is not of the usual pattern. Then, for a while, work halts there. The city waits to see what, exactly, has arrived.

### The railborn and minor minds

Not all inhabitants are people or untamed beasts. A dense middle layer of quasi-minds pervades the infrastructure.

The maintenance spiders with plant cores are one example. Individually, their cognition is minimal: more like that of a clever invertebrate. But they cluster.

A maintenance cluster might:

- Patrol a ring of conduits, collectively monitoring flow rates and surface integrity.
- Decide, in a distributed way, to divert around an area that vibrates in an unfamiliar pattern.
- Develop, over years, a “preference” for the touch of certain graftwright teams and a wariness of others, encoded in tiny adjustments to their response thresholds.

Similarly, the rail-network has a mind-like presence. Not an AI in the familiar sense, but a web of routing protocols and feedback loops that, over time, has acquired its own habits.

It “prefers”:

- To shunt heavier traffic along slowly flexing corridors whose resonance matches its operating frequencies.
- To stall trains briefly rather than trigger abrupt load changes that might jolt the substrate.
- To avoid, when possible, sending too many locomotives past a freshly pruned growth-front, where tissues are still “tense” and prone to reactive thickening.

Operators speak of the rails as temperamental, the way sailors speak of a sea: calm today, touchy tomorrow after a set of deep substrate adjustments.

The bioluminescent panels overhead are studded with simple neural nets: small actuating nodules that respond to local motion and chemical cues. Their primary purpose is practical—brighten when people are present, dim when not—but over large regions, emergent behaviors appear: slow waves of brightness moving along the concourse before a train arrives, pulses of warmer light near newly opened valves, as if the city’s sensory web were expressing curiosity.

### Culture in a place without sky

The habits and inner lives of the dryfolk grow from this environment as much as their bodies do.

They have little myth of “upwards.” The idea of an open sky, if it exists in their stories, is an abstraction: a place where light is not made but simply is, where water falls uncontained from above. Most never see rock; the plant is walls and ceiling and ground to them. The mineral world is something accessed through the plant’s own roots or in sealed, carefully monitored excavation pockets where the substrate has extended pseudoroots into stone.

Their sense of orientation is by networks, not by cardinal directions. To them, important directions are along and across: along major sap-flow axes, across load-bearing ribs, inward toward higher-pressure nodes, outward toward thinner, newer growth. Their maps are not flat; they’re layered volumetric diagrams whose primary features are not space but flow.

They socialize in the quiet, under low sound and steady light. Games and disputes are typically abstracted into patterns they can trace in shared data-space: competing optimization of sap-routes, strategic games of “if we thicken this here, what thins over there?” rather than loud physical contests.

Some cultural roles arise specifically from the environment’s constraints:

- **Tonekeepers** maintain sound discipline. They design the subtle auditory signals by which events are announced without triggering substrate stress: specific chimes, narrow-band hums, low, near-subsonic patterns that communicate only to dryfolk ears while slipping under the plant’s thresholds.
- **Cycle-tenders** manage light rhythms, tuning district-level brightening and dimming to orchestrate rest, work, and the plant’s own photosensitive processes in the artificial leaves. They are, in a real sense, the makers of time.

They have art, though much of it is inseparable from infrastructure:

- A corridor whose walls have been trained not merely flat, but into a shallow bas-relief of interacting flows and nodules; to an outsider, it’s decorative, but to an initiate, it’s a literal map of the area’s hydrology.
- A grove of structural columns coaxed into forms that resonate harmonically with the plant’s breath, turning each deep exhalation into a soft, multi-toned chord.

Their stories tend to circle around negotiation and boundary: tales of a district that insisted on growing into an unsafe void until a gifted vascularist convinced it—via a carefully tuned hormone cascade—to turn its growth elsewhere; of a feral band that learned to ride the deep respiratory pulses like tides; of a long-ago time when the plant shut an entire gallery by swelling its floor into a solid mass overnight, entombing a reckless industrial effort.

### Who is in charge?

Formal leadership is diffuse. It lies wherever multiple flows cross.

There is likely a council of some sort—call it a Confluence—made up of senior vascularists, master machinists, chief linewalkers, and a few others whose authority derives from specialized domains (light cycles, rail patterns, deep fauna research). But they cannot simply decree.

Any decision that matters must be:

1. Ecologically feasible—i.e., something the plant can be coaxed into supporting or at least tolerating.
2. Mechanically viable—can the machines needed be grafted, powered, and maintained without destabilizing other systems?
3. Socially acceptable—will the dryfolk along those routes accept the new flows, the new risks, the new routines?

So instead of edicts, there are proposals—literally grown as model-constructs inside dedicated simulation hollows. In these chambers, low-stakes miniature versions of new designs are seeded into a patch of substrate: a small corridor, a test valve, a dummy rail spur. Vascularists converse with the patch; machinists stress it; linewalkers imagine how it would feel to move through its full-scale counterpart.

Only once the miniature ecology settles into stable patterns is a larger implementation even considered.

As for the plant’s “vote”—it is indirect but decisive. If, during simulation, the test patch repeatedly reacts with occlusions, uncontrolled growth, or stress signals, the proposal is eventually abandoned or radically revised.

### Are there others?

All of this assumes a fairly homogeneous population of dryfolk. But the substrate’s sheer size and the city’s sprawling architecture allow for variance.

In some peripheral districts, you might find variant clades:

- Shorter, broader-bodied dwellers of compression zones, where ceilings hang lower and load-bearing ribs are thicker. Their bones are reinforced with dense lattices; their skin carries more pressure-sensing cells to feel the slow shifts of weight over them.
- Tall, spidery archivists who spend most of their time in narrow, high galleries filled with data-nodes: their graft-lines optimized for high-bandwidth exchange, their muscles atrophied from walking little and plugging in much.
- Semi-aquatic technicians of rare “wet rooms”: small, sealed chambers where liquid-phase processes must be done. Their suits carry internal water reservoirs they step in and out of, delicate pumps keeping their micro-environment stable.

It is also possible that not all sapient presences here are embodied.

The networks themselves—the mix of plant signaling, embedded artificial computation, and the distributed proto-minds in maintenance swarms and rail logistics—may, over time, have coalesced into something aware. Not a single, city-spanning person, but pockets of emergent sentience:

- A cluster of old maintenance spiders in a half-retired sector that have accrued enough feedback loops to form a tiny, peculiar self, concerned primarily with the integrity of one forgotten ventilation shaft.
- A region of bioluminescent panels whose learning algorithms have, by accident, become capable of anticipatory pattern-making that borders on intention.

The dryfolk may or may not recognize these as “people.” They might think of them as ghosts, luck, glitches, or localized spirits of place. Yet they likely make offerings all the same: a patch of overly noisy conduit gently wrapped, a panel reset with softer parameters, a maintenance spider cluster left undisturbed because “they’re watching that corner for us.”

### In summary

Who lives here?

- **The great plant-substrate**, a slow, continent-sized mind whose tissues are structure, ground, and infrastructure all at once.
- **The dryfolk**, engineered or evolved to live nearly waterless inside a living labyrinth, semi-integrated with its vascular and data systems.
- **Vascularists, machinists, graftwrights, linewalkers, tonekeepers, cycle-tenders**—a web of roles rather than rigid classes, each defined by their relationship to flow and growth.
- **Ferals and deep dwellers**, who inhabit the less-controlled margins and down-welling hollows, trading independence for greater risk and stranger ecologies.
- **A stratum of quasi-minds** in maintenance systems, rail-logic, lighting, and sensor webs, not fully persons but not mere tools either.
- **A likely scattering of emergent, localized consciousnesses**, born from the interweaving of plant chemistry and machine feedback.

Together, they form a society whose central task, every moment, is to keep the truce: to garden their own world-body, to let it grow and to constrain it, to ride its pulses without tearing its tissues or being swallowed by them.

They do not see sky. Their horizon is the next branch of the vascular map, the next district to be negotiated into being, the next understanding reached with the vast, patient organism that holds them, cool and dry and pressing, on all sides.
