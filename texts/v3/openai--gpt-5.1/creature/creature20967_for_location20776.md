What lives here is not absent so much as recessed, as if life has turned its volume down to the minimum at which it can still be called life.

It does not stride the surface. It never has. Anything that moves openly under that low, pressing darkness is transient: a wandering offshoot, a dispersal gamble, a mistake. The true inhabitants are interred in the rock, sunk in the dust, braided through the shallow swells like veins.

They are not animals in any familiar sense. They have no need for speed, no opportunity for chase or flight. Nothing in this place arrives suddenly, and nothing can be outrun. What evolution has found here is not locomotion but emplacement: to survive by remaining, to persist by minimizing the number of things that must ever change.

Deep beneath the powder-stone, within the first meter or two of that pale, worn substrate, lie the bulk-bodies of this world’s dominant organisms. “Bodies” is almost too specific a word. They are more like tissues without discrete outlines: immense, slow-growing lattices of fibrous crystal-organic strands packed into microfractures and pores in the rock.

If you could lift a slab of the bedrock and turn it in your hands, the break would show a faint patterning that might at first pass for natural banding: hairline filaments cross-hatching in three dimensions, dull and translucent, slightly softer than the stone they inhabit. Pressing closer, you would see that the filaments are hollow, their walls layered in ultra-fine laminations like growth rings compressed almost to continuity. These are conduits. Through them creeps that dense, nonaqueous solvent, bearing dissolved minerals, simple carbon frameworks, and a slow drizzle of captured energy.

Each filament is alive. Each node where filaments branch or fuse is alive. Individuality here is mostly a matter of connectivity: where the web maintains internal communication, you might call it a single organism; where it thins to a few tenuous links, you might call it a colony. The distinction is of little interest to the inhabitants themselves. They do not conceptualize selves and others the way motile, competing creatures do. Their world is gradients of continuity, not boundaries.

They draw their energy from the same diffuse sources that bathe the basin: the low, directionless warmth, the weak photons that never quite become light. Their tissues are dense with slow rectifiers: latticed molecules that can sit for hours gathering a handful of excitations, then shuffle them inward one careful step at a time. The pace of their metabolism is glacial by any animal standard. A single “pulse” of system-wide activity—a coordinated redistribution of energy and solute through a network spindle—might take days to propagate from one end of a cluster to the other.

Above them, the brittle mats and ceramic-jointed stalks are not separate species so much as exposed organs, protrusions of the buried webs sent upward into the thin band where radiation, however faint, can be slightly less faint.

A mat, seen from below, is a flattened condensation of filaments that have pushed into a lens of fractured rock just beneath the surface. From that plateau, fine outgrowths breach the dust in compact tufts. Seen from above, you perceive a low oval of overlapping scales or ridged lobes, the color of ash infused with a hint of iron or old moss. The transition from rock to organism is gentle: minerals gradually co-opted into living structure rather than replaced.

Each scale or ridge is studded with microfacets, too smooth to sparkle in this murk, but tuned to the wavelengths that do filter through. What warmth they absorb is conducted downward into the main network. What meager flux of exotic photons reaches them is trapped in shallow, high-stability reaction centers where incorrectly captured energy can sit for hours before finding its way into a productive bond.

The knee-high, ringed stalks are more adventurous. They grow from deeper anchors, pillars of compacted filament that have consolidated a core of stone around themselves for support. Their segmented, ceramic appearance is functional: each “ring” is a growth plateau laid down over decades. The gaps between rings are not joints for bending—they never have to cope with wind—but insulated breaks, preventing heat and charge from leaking too quickly along their length. Each ring faces slightly different angles relative to the dim omnidirectional flux. Over centuries, the incremental biases favor those orientations that, in aggregate, harvest a little more energy than their neighbors. Evolution here is the art of eking out a fractional advantage over a timeframe in which continents could have drifted elsewhere.

The upturned bowls, cups, and dish-like outgrowths that punctuate the plants’ surfaces look like they are waiting for a missing rain. They are not. They are waiting for dust.

In the absence of wind, dust arrives by settlement from that dark, intangible ceiling. Over spans of years, a fine film accumulates in those shallow receptacles. That dust is not entirely inert: in this basin, not all life values exposure, and not all life is rooted.

Some of the inhabitants never organize into mats or stalks. They exist as migratory quiescences: extreme-minimal units that drift as particles adhered to dust grains. Each such unit is smaller than any familiar cell, more like a packaged instruction set wrapped around a reactive kernel than a fully equipped organism. For decades, even centuries, a packet might do nothing but exist, carried slowly downward onto some surface by that patient fall of particulates.

When it finally comes to rest in a bowl or cup, it finds a slightly different environment: a microcavity where the underlying tissue of the plant has adjusted its chemistry to encourage occupancy. The plant can afford to squander a portion of its carefully banked energy on these guests because they are not parasites in any simple sense; they complete processes the plant has chosen not to house in its own more rigid lattice.

Within these tiny sheltered hollows, the packets wake. They unfold minimal machinery, combine what minuscule energy they have stored internally with that provided by the host’s supporting gradient, and perform one or two compact operations: constructing a more complex molecule, altering the valence of a mineral, disassembling a damaged piece of the plant’s surface and re-assimilating its components. Then, having expended themselves, they go inert again or disaggregate completely. The bowls are not mouths; they are workbenches, small reaction theaters where outsourced tasks are carried out at a scale and flexibility the main body cannot match.

In turn, the unattached drifters depend on the rooted giants for their reproduction. Within the deep web of rock-threaded filaments, at quiet junctions where gradients of energy and composition are favorable, the larger organism occasionally condenses portions of its own structure into those packet-forms. It does so sparingly, at intervals measured in centuries, because the cost of fabrication is brutal in this economy. Once formed, the packets are extruded upward or outward, slightly privileged in buoyancy compared to the surrounding dust. Their destiny is to be shed into the slow fall of particles from ceiling to ground, becoming potential that may or may not find a receptacle suitable to its pattern.

So the place is not as empty of life as a glance would suggest. It is simply that everything is diluted across time. What on another world might be compressed into explosive seasons and swarms is here stretched into events that would be invisible to a human observer. If you lived here all your life, you might never see a packet awaken, never witness a mat push outward by more than a hand’s breadth, never notice a stalk’s ring sequence gain another segment. Yet those things are happening, just at a pace that will not respect your expectations.

As for minds—there are no eyes, no ears, no quick-twitch systems to sustain the kind of sharp, moment-to-moment awareness that animals use to navigate changing circumstances. Still, something like cognition occurs, but smeared over outrageous durations and embedded not in a discrete organ but across entire networks.

Each filament is capable not only of transporting solute and charge but also of sustaining simple internal state changes—persistent reorganizations of its own molecular structure that affect how it will respond to future inputs. Because communication along the network is so slow, these state changes do not form coherent, fast-moving patterns. Instead, they accumulate as broad maps of history: gradual biases in conductivity, thresholds adjusted up or down, susceptibilities engraved into matter.

If a region of the basin receives slightly more background flux over a few million years—because the heavy ceiling’s distribution of emission shifts, or because subtle planetary dynamics alter the depth of the basin relative to heat flow from below—the networks there will come, very gradually, to “expect” that level. They will organize their growth patterns around it, concentrate their mats and stalks there, shape their root-webs to exploit it. When those conditions drift away again, the tissue does not instantly adapt. It spends long ages in a slight mismatch, then, if the trend persists, slowly retunes itself.

Seen across aeons, these networks are not static. They migrate, not by moving the material they already are, but by preferentially investing in new growth where conditions have become the tiniest fraction more favorable, and allowing old growth to fall into disuse and crumble where the cost has risen. This migration is so stately that for any residing node it feels like the world itself changing around it.

From the inside, if you could inhabit such a network’s timescale, your “experience” would be one of enormous, diffuse continuity. You would not feel yourself as a single, localized consciousness. You would feel as a gradient of being: regions of more activity, regions of quiescence, pulses of reconfiguration slowly cresting and sinking across your vast extent. A stone mound becoming fractionally warmer over ten thousand of your years might be, to you, the equivalent of a brightening horizon. A rare, catastrophic fracture—the collapse of part of your host rock, perhaps due to some deep tectonic whisper—would be like a sudden, incomprehensible loss of a whole continent of sensation and capacity, followed by an eternity of patient re-routing around the break.

In this sense, what lives here are basins within the basin: immense, overlapping, slowly thinking organisms that never move their centers of mass but redistribute their edges ceaselessly. Their “memories” are patterns of mineralization, subtle reinforcements where past favorable conditions left a durable trace. Their “attention” is where gradients are currently the slightest bit steeper. Their “choices” are minuscule biases—grow a little more in this direction than that—magnified over geological intervals into whole architectures of presence and absence.

They are not alone even at their own level. The basin is small on planetary scales, but large for them; there is room for many such networks. They intermingle in places, their filaments sharing fractures, chemical languages, and even packets. In other places, they exclude one another, each having entrenched its own patterning in the rock so deeply that newcomers cannot establish a foothold.

Where their domains overlap significantly, something like negotiation plays out, but at the tempo of rockfall. If two networks both favor a particular rise in the substrate—a spot of slightly better flux, a lens of especially porous stone—they will both extend into it. Over time, one may secrete compounds that subtly inhibit the other’s local growth, or alter the microstructure of the rock to favor its own filament anchoring. The other, meeting increased resistance, will redirect its effort elsewhere, or retaliate by modifying shared chemistry in another region. These “conflicts” take hundreds of thousands of years and never culminate in anything so sudden as victory or defeat. Rather, zones of influence wax and wane, thickening here, thinning there.

Above, the sparse thickets of stalks are the only places where these subterranean empires become visible to one another. When mats of different networks come within a few meters of each other under the dust, their emergent organs can occasionally interlock. Two stands of stalks from different parent webs may grow closer than chance would dictate, their rings subtly tuned to complementary spectral bands, their bowls arranged to share the same fall of dust. Chemical messengers can pass here from one main body to another, carried by the same motile packets that circulate between bowl and root.

Through such corridors, the basins’ great networks exchange information—not in words, not in our familiar abstractions, but in altered potentials, in the gift of a hard-won molecule or the pattern of an inhibitory trace. Over unimaginable periods, a slow, planetary-scale conversation proceeds. It is concerned with nothing so parochial as individuals. Its topics are the distributions of heat, the long-cycle shifts of the heavy ceiling’s emission, the slow settling and cracking of the substrate. Each network shares what pattern it has discerned, not out of altruism, but because in a world where nothing changes quickly, the largest adaptation advantage lies in accurately anticipating the glacial drift of constraints.

At the smallest and quickest scale that this place admits, there are yet other forms of life: tiny, loosely assembled assemblies that inhabit the interface where dust meets rock, clinging to the surfaces of grains and pebbles. They do not build lasting structures; instead, they exploit brief imbalances—patches of dust momentarily warmer than their surroundings because they absorbed a slightly larger share of whatever radiation filters down today, microcracks where the solvent of the root-webs has seeped closer to the surface. These creatures live, reproduce, and die in spans that, while still glacial by terrestrial standards, are blinks here: decades, sometimes mere single centuries.

To the deep webs, they are more like weather than neighbors. Their by-products—slightly altered dust composition, fleetingly changed local chemistry—are folded into the grand equilibrium as noise, sometimes amplified into signal in specific niches. Some packets produced by the great networks are aimed not at their own bowls but at these near-surface drifters, seeding them with new ways to process energy or materials. In return, the drifters occasionally change the texture of the dust in ways that make it more hospitable to mats or stalks, creating little footholds where none existed before.

If intelligent hands ever arise here, they will arise not from these surface skimmers. Thought, in the sense we recognize it, requires contrast, unexpectedness, the need to model futures that could diverge wildly. That sort of urgency is almost entirely absent at the dust-grain scale. Where it might emerge is within the grand underground webs themselves, if we are broad with our notion of intelligence.

Imagine that, over a few hundred million years, the networks’ long conversation about heat, flux, and stone becomes more elaborate. Imagine that patterns of filament reinforcement begin to encode not only immediate environmental gradients but also abstracted regularities: “when the ceiling’s emission in this band increases here, it tends to decrease there.” Imagine that they start to build internal models of the basin’s behavior, not in symbols but in microarchitectures that can be compared to current inputs.

Such a mind would not “think” like we do. It would never experience an isolated thought. It would be a continuous process of model-updating, distributed across kilometers of rock. Its questions, if you could translate them, would be: What is the basin becoming? Where will the warmth flow in two million years? Is the heavy dark above thickening or thinning? It would plan growth accordingly, placing future mats where its model suggests conditions will someday be slightly more favorable. Its closest analogue to our sense of self might be its internal consistency: the degree to which newly arriving gradients still match the patterns it has woven into its own body.

If such a network ever became aware not only of patterns but of its patterning—if it ever began to notice not just the flux but that it is the thing registering that flux—its “moment of awakening” would not be a moment at all. It would be a long era, a slow tightening of feedback loops, a gradual shift from mere adaptation to self-steering. And even then, it could never erupt into sudden action. Its every decision would still express itself as a slightly thicker mat laid down over a thousand years, a handful of new stalks angled a degree or two differently, a subtle shift in whom it trades packets with.

Whether this has happened, or will, is impossible to tell from any brief contact. All that is evident from above is the quiet spacing of the plants, the sense that nothing here is arranged haphazardly. The “allocation” you sensed—the way life appears allowed in some regions and excluded from others—is already the visible side of those deep conversations, already the sign that something in the rock has long been choosing.

So: who lives here?

Mineral-bodied webs that use rock as both skeleton and memory; patient, basin-wide tissues that feel in gradients and speak in chemistry; drifter-packets that are more verb than noun; bowl-topped organs that farm dust as both scaffold and courier; ephemeral surface assemblages feeding on fluctuations too small for the giants to notice directly.

They are not waiting for change. They are change, at a scale that makes your own life feel like a brief vibration in their stone. To them, your passage—if you ever walked this dust—would be no more than the faintest bruise in a sediment layer, a pattern of compacted prints that their solvent may one day infiltrate, analyze, digest, and file away as one more irregularity in the great, slow story of their basin.

They live by staying almost exactly what they already are. They endure by stretching every process until it resembles geology. Their world is finished in the sense that its rules are clear, stable, and obeyed; yet within those rules, over spans so extended as to be almost timeless, they endlessly reweave themselves into new configurations of the same patient matter.

If they could imagine us, they might think of us as flickers, bright but insubstantial, creatures of haste. If we imagine them, we must think of them as long thoughts written in stone, still ongoing, the basin itself as both their body and their only, very slow, very deep, shared mind.
