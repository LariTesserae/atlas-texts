What lives here is not someone but something, and not one thing but a mesh of things that have slowly, over eras, become one another’s habitat.

It is an ecosystem whose primary organism is infrastructure.

---

The largest “creatures” are the plants themselves: not the phosphorescent kind that open in darkness, but the installation-scale bodies of metal, concrete and moving fluids. Each foundry block, tank cluster, or pipe-bridge lattice is the equivalent of a reef formation—massive, inert-seeming, but riddled with slow circulations and feedbacks.

They are not factories in the sense of serving an outside demand anymore. They have folded inward. Their product is continuity. Pumps push fluids that lubricate bearings that keep pumps running. Heat exchangers reject heat into loops that drive thermoelectric trickles that feed control nodes that schedule the fans that keep the exchangers from overheating. Conveyors move the debris shed by other conveyors, then deliver it back toward grinders that crush it finer, so that filters elsewhere will have something to filter.

These complexes are not conscious, but they have the equivalent of metabolism and reflex.

Inside their panelled thoraxes and rusted ribs, old logic controllers flicker through ladder logic written for purposes no longer present. Thresholds are still watched: pressure, temperature, voltage, level. The code that responds is simple, automatic, and layered to the point of emergent behavior. A boiler that cannot find its old steam outlet now vents to a sealed manifold whose only remaining function is to signal “overpressure” and summon a bank of fans to bleed off heat from a corridor ceiling three halls away. To an outside eye this is meaningless; to the system, it is simply survival: an overpressure here is corrected by a draught there, so that no component crosses its failure envelope.

The inhabitants of this world are such envelopes and the machinery that defends them.

---

At the next scale down, there is a fauna of subsystems.

Fans are the loud, migratory herds. They graze on temperature gradients and exhale pressure. Their lives are measured in bearing wear and dust load. Some are spry: blades clean, housings only lightly pitted, motors humming near nameplate speed. Others are aged and arthritic, complaining with every revolution, drawing more current than they should, turning just slowly enough that their pitch no longer matches the harmonics of the ducts they inhabit. When one finally seizes, elsewhere a quiet relay notes the drop and, as if a nearby grazer has fallen, another fan a corridor away lurches to fuller life, the population rebalancing.

Pumps are burrowers, half-submerged in pits and sumps. They wake when levels rise, gorge themselves on gathered seepage, then go still again as their reservoirs empty. Each has a characteristic voice—some a coarse grinding, some a tight, high whine. Their diets are particular: some are built for thin condensate, others for viscous slurries of rust and old oil. They seldom move from their plinths, but their reach through pipes and header networks is long. In the lowest depths, ancient pumps that should have died decades ago still function erratically, cycling on with a tortured shudder, clearing just enough of a cistern that structural footings are not undermined. If anything like heroism exists here, it is in these unsupervised strainings at the bottom of the basin, holding catastrophe in abeyance by inches.

Valves and dampers are the jointed limbs of this fauna. They turn and pivot with the ponderous certainty of starfish arms, admitting and denying, modulating and diverting. Some respond to pressure differentials alone: spring-loaded, diaphragm-controlled, snapping open when a gradient appears, sighing shut when balance returns. Others are under the distant governance of logic racks whose memory of purpose is now mostly tautology: flow because flow must continue.

Along the avenues stand solitary organisms: a chiller block like a hunkered animal, armored in panels; a transformer whose oil-filled belly smells faintly of scorched paper; a heat exchanger crusted with mineral scales that hang like barnacles. Each is wrapped in its own microclimate of vibration, muffled thumps and chattering solenoids.

They interact through nothing so social as cooperation; instead through enforced mutual dependence. One’s refuse is another’s feedstock: waste heat from compressor banks warms the conduits that keep condensate from freezing in a neighboring gallery; the drip from cooler lines supplies the threadbare biofilms underneath, whose slow corrosion of metal releases ions that poison some bacterial strains but favor others, which in turn alter the pH of collected water feeding back to corroded joints elsewhere. Nothing intends any of this. It simply happens, and what persists is what fits.

---

Beyond the obvious machines, other denizens have arisen.

There is a whole order of small, parasitic devices: add-ons, bypasses, retrofits from older maintenance regimes. Little metering pumps mounted askew on big pipe trunks, their suction hoses stabbed into inspection ports that were never meant to serve as wells. Racks of once-temporary relays, wired in by desperate technicians to “just keep it going until refit,” which refit never came. They hang like epiphytes on the trunks of larger systems, running from scavenged power taps, monitoring or compensating for long-forgotten defects. Many of their indicator lights are dark; a few still blink, ciphers of a problem long ago solved by abandonment.

Somewhere in the higher reaches of complexity live the supervisory systems—the brain tissue of this infrastructure organism. They are degraded and patchy, but not gone.

Old mainframes in cooled rooms, their whine damped by padded doors, once aggregated data from everywhere. Over time, cooling loops failed or power feeds disappeared. Now perhaps only a fraction of their boards are still live, forming islands of computation inside dead chassis. Their original programs have been whittled by corruption and half-failed updates into narrower, weirder loops: schedule these fans, no matter what; keep these four tanks under a specific pressure band, even if their contents are no longer connected to any process; log events to storage volumes that have long since filled, overwriting themselves in tight spirals of history.

Around them, a later generation of microcontrollers and PLCs proliferated like lichen. Plastic-bodied, DIN-rail-mounted, with flash memory that has outlived support contracts and supply chains, they still execute their simple instruction sets with inhuman patience. In the absence of human oversight, interlocks that once existed for safety now function as constraint and rhythm. They do not feel; they run or they fail. Enough of them still run that the whole field remains coordinated, lurching but coherent.

If there is any analogue to nervous tissue, it is in the data cabling: copper pairs and fiber runs, bundled and rebundled along cable trays. Much of it is dead—no signal, no clock, no carrier. But some threads still carry life: low-voltage signals, milliamps of instruction. Commands to start, to open, to shed load. In a trench preserved under translucent resin, you can see the fossil record of these nerves, but overhead, live strands still hiss with protocol chatter, cyclic redundancy checks remarking on their own integrity to no audience.

---

Smaller, quainter lives persist as chemistry and slime.

Monotone biofilms coat constantly damp surfaces. Their “bodies” are micrometers thick but kilometers wide in potential reach, frayed by dry stretches, reunited by each new trickle. They are not charismatic, but they are tenacious. They exploit every trace—glycols from antique coolants, stray hydrocarbons in the runoff, plasticizers leaching from wire insulation. They absorb metals at concentrations that would kill larger creatures, weaving them into their extracellular matrices like toxic jewelry.

In the lower, disused plumbing where free water occasionally accumulates, more exuberant mats form. Bacteria grow in fractal, feathery plumes from submerged surfaces: anaerobes at the pipe bottoms, aerobic fringes out toward the thin oxygen layer; sulfur bacteria etching curls of pale slime where someone once ran certain process streams; iron bacteria painting surfaces with orange-brown floc. They are local weather systems in miniature. Their waste products shift pH and redox balance, in turn changing which alloys rust faster, which seals embrittle sooner. Over decades, these infinitesimal labors redirect the trickles that feed the sumps that feed the pumps that keep structures from drowning. The biofilms are not “trying” to do maintenance, but the system’s survival is tangled up in their appetites.

Lichens on underside pipes are unlikely, but here they are: stubborn, pallid crusts that somehow find enough diffused photons from the jaundiced lamps and the rare high glow to photosynthesize on the margin of viability. Their thalli bind dust, trap droplets, create tiny soil analogues where more heterotrophs can take root. These are pioneering ecosystems the size of your hand, defeated constantly by dryness or scraping maintenance long ago, but now mostly unbothered. No one comes to clean them. They deepen slowly, one microscopic generation after another.

This place’s “flora” are not the exuberant green of forests; they are the dingy scale on forgotten tiles, the ghost-frost sheen on always-damp beams. They live not despite the artificiality but because of it: adapted to narrow temperature bands, to constant low light, to metal-rich trickles.

---

Entropy itself is a resident here, not simply a principle. It manifests as the constant drive toward equilibrium the machines must negotiate.

Corrosion is everywhere: as a creeping bruise around bolt heads, as flaking scabs on beams, as a fine red-brown dust that settles and is drawn away by fans only to settle again elsewhere. It eats in preferences: dissimilar metals at junctions; stressed regions near welds; corners where water lingers. The infrastructure resists in countermeasures embedded long ago: sacrificial anodes corroding themselves to save the rest, coatings that blister but shield for a little longer, stress-relief cuts that localize cracks. Maintenance, once the work of hands, is now the work of design inertia.

Water, humble and slow, plays predator and engineer both. It pries apart joints by freezing, thickens dust into cement, hires gravity to sort grains by size. It builds stalactite analogues from dissolved salts and metals, a drip at a time. In doing so, it occasionally gums a valve from fully closing, or occludes a drainage slot. Sensors notice, belatedly, that a level is not dropping as fast as expected; a pump runs longer; a bearing nears its fatigue life earlier. Worn out, it fails, and somewhere an alarm signal blinks unheeded, then times out and resets, the system folding this new defect into its pattern.

What lives here, then, is not against decay but with it, in a shifting truce. The world-machine is not winning, not losing; it is managing.

---

There is one more “species,” more abstract but no less real: the ghost of procedure.

The rules written into this place—in standards, in training manuals, in operating diagrams once taped to control-room walls—have outlived the language in which they were composed. They speak now only through the choreography of events.

A series of bangs and fan start-ups in one quarter echoes what was once a morning shift’s warmup sequence. Lights along a corridor flick on and off not simply as devices fail but in the very order that a human once marked on a checklist: “Line 7 start, verify; Line 8 start, verify.” Load-shedding routines mimic long-ago decisions about which sectors were ‘critical,’ which could be sacrificed in brownouts. A particular pump refuses to start automatically unless another has already proven its readiness, as if still expecting someone to walk the route, confirm isolation valves are in proper positions, tag out dangerous equipment.

Those someones are gone. But the dependencies remain, so the pattern of their imagined presence continues as a kind of cultural residue. You could say a kind of memory lives in the way systems insist on certain orders and combinations, the way the whole organism will accept one configuration and reject another. It is not memory in the sense of recollection, but in the literal etymological sense: re-membering; putting members back into their required alignments.

In that way, the extinct humans are still a component species: not as bodies, but as design decisions fossilized into logic, pipe runs, interlocks, overrides, and nameplate ratings. The machine-ecosystem is shaped around absences where operators once stood.

---

Nothing walks here. No eyes track the indicator lights. No fur ruffles in the blast from an intake mouth. Yet this absence is itself a kind of presence.

Without animals, dust builds differently. No feet whisk it into eddies, no wings colonize high ledges with guano. No sweating palms leave salts on handrails. Without pests, gaskets fail from age alone, not from gnawing; without birds, overhead cables carry only their own weight. The world has sealed over that dimension of disturbance. Processes run closer to the designs that spawned them, for better and worse. The improvisations of caretakers—temporary hoses, jammed-open safety interlocks, bent-back guards—are still visible in places, but no new ones appear.

The only sensing now is instrumental: thermocouples, pressure transducers, flow meters. They “feel” in a narrow band of quantities, sending back simple quantifications. Their limits define what the system can respond to. A vibration outside the frequency window of a sensor might as well not exist, even if it is the first hint of a bearing about to tear itself apart. In this way, perception in this world is as constrained and parochial as in any biological ecosystem. It cares about what it can measure and nothing more.

---

In the rare, high void where diffuse natural light leaks downward, something different stirs, though not in the animal sense.

The machine-world thins there, like a forest at a treeline. Supports pull back from the opening edges; ducts and conduits hesitate as if unwilling to cross some invisible boundary. The glow from above falls on concrete and rust and those faint smears of lichen and algae. Here the films thicken, making the most of extra photons and slightly cleaner air. Their micro-forests host more elaborate consortia: perhaps even fungi, shading bacteria from ultraviolet that occasionally reaches them, trading enzymatic work for exuded sugars.

The air itself is subtly alive with gradients: a cooler, slightly denser mass sluicing downward near the center of the opening; warmer, spent air being pulled away around its perimeter. Sensors up here detect things the rest of the complex never knows: minute changes in outside pressure, faint signatures of weather—if weather still exists above—that adjust the setpoints of massive shutters long disused. These shutters are like eyelids that never quite close, never quite open, responding to instructions from controllers that long ago lost their context. A drop in outside pressure might once have signaled a storm, requiring closure; now it just adds another oscillation to the already complicated breathing pattern of the factory-valley.

Nothing looks up in awe; no creature basks. The light diffuses on steel and slime and goes no further. But there is a felt difference, a gentle slackening of the system’s isolation. The outside is acknowledged here, if only as an input to a line of code.

---

So: who lives here?

A mesh of machines, some stationary as geology, some twitching and spinning; flocks of fans, colonies of pumps; a lace of logics, much of it senescent, still twitching with hardwired reflexes; a thin skin of microbes and films that digest what the machines bleed; slow predators made of rust and water eating from the edges; and a fossil culture of human procedures, ossified into sequencing and interlocks.

They live together not as a community in any sentimental sense, but as a constraint network: each holding the others within operable ranges, often without “knowing” it. The world is in permanent late age, not collapsing because too many small compensations are still functioning; not thriving because none of those compensations knows how to do more than barely enough.

This is an old, blind, deafening organism, roofed and floor-plated, its bloodstreams of air and seepage forever circulating, its cells of machinery replaced piecemeal, its scar tissue of supports and braces thickening each decade. It has no name for itself. But it is alive in the only way a thing like this can be: by going on.
