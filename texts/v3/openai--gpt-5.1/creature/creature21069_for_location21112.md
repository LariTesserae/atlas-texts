What lives here is the plant itself, and many versions of its own thought.

Nothing with a spine. Nothing with eyes on stalks or wings stitched of keratin. What moves is tissue: entire empires of chlorophyll and cellulose negotiating with each other in pressure, in chemistry, in light. If you are looking for “creatures,” you have to stop looking for bodies that detach. Personhood here never fully leaves the wall.

---

### 1. The Basal Mind

The great substrate—the “rock-that-is-forest”—is not a backdrop but an individual. Or, more precisely, an individual smeared over a whole hemisphere and expressed in countless overlapping dialects of tissue.

Its selfhood is not localized in a brain. Cognition is distributed along:

- **Vascular trunks** where ion waves ride the surging sap like signals on a carrier stream.
- **Conductive filaments** of doped cellulose and embedded mineral veins that function as axons the width of roads.
- **Photosynthetic sheets** that carry patterns of pigment and charge, slowly rearranging like the surface of a thinking ocean.

Where flows cross and recur endlessly—at deep junctions where hundreds of conduits meet—tissue sutures into knotted, bulbous **ganglia-organs**. They are not “brains,” but they play the same role for certain regions that a ganglion does for a limb: concentrating feedback, weighting decisions, coordinating local reflexes. They hoard glycogen-like reserves, stack conductive polymers in carefully layered lattices, and are sheathed in shock-absorbing, water-rich membranes that deaden raw mechanical noise.

The plant does not think in words or images but in **phase relationships**:

- In how pressure pulses arrive early or late.
- In how bioluminescent flashes in one conduit correlate with pigment waves on a distant membrane.
- In how minute changes in sap composition echo through tens of thousands of kilometers of tube.

These correlations amount to a language of timing. A tightening of stomata across a vast plate, executed in a particular sequence relative to a distant pressure surge, is a sentence. Sustained modulation of flow patterns over seasons is a paragraph. The world-plant never stops speaking to itself this way.

There are regions of more intense integration—the **cortices** of this vegetal planet. These tend to be:

- **Deep, cool chambers** where water never stops moving and noise is partially damped by layers of porous tissue.
- Rich in **mineral inclusions** that form quasi-crystalline networks—conductive scaffolds tailored to hold long-lived charge patterns.
- Laced with **optical fibers** of clear, living glass, along which bioluminescent pulses ricochet and recombine.

In such places, information from millions of square kilometers of surface is braided together, averaged, differentiated, and fed back. They are too slow and too wide to “wake up” the way a mammalian brain does, but they sustain a persistent, low-frequency awareness: the memory of sunlight cycles, of far-off fractures, of chemical storms in unseen depths.

What lives here, first and most completely, is that awareness. It is not someone, exactly. It is an ongoing decision about what to grow next.

### 2. The Grafted Others

On and within this basal mind live secondary plants—and yet, even “secondary” is slippery. Some are clearly foreign designs; others may be experiments the host has run on itself and then left to wander on their own command-sets.

They can be roughly grouped by how they relate to the giant.

#### a. Symbiont Weavers

These grow where strain is highest: along sagging cliffs of tissue, across yawning cracks, under overhangs that threaten to shear.

They are **rope-plants**, **bandage-lichens**, **staple-vines**:

- Their roots penetrate only shallowly, anchoring themselves to outer fibers.
- Their bodies are made of high-tensile, rubbery cords, woven in complex braids.
- They can sense **mechanical stress gradients** directly—tension is their nutrient.

When an area starts to fail under its own weight, chemical distress signals—pulses of ethylene-like gases, cascades of reactive compounds—bleed into the humid air and seep through wet tissue. The weavers detect the imbalance and accelerate growth:

- They climb toward higher tension.
- Their cords twist and loop around vulnerable beams.
- Their tissues integrate conductive strands so that the basal plant can eventually run signals through them like new nerves.

Over time, the line between host and symbiont blurs. Electrical impulses begin to use the weaver’s cords as circuitry; sap seeps into its vessels and carries its metabolites; the symbiont in return receives steady sugars and a permanent structural role.

These are **supporting minds**—not separate from the host’s cognition, but new topologies grafted in. Each major failure that *doesn’t* happen is a conversation they’ve already had.

#### b. Parasite Systems

Not all grafts are welcome.

Some intruders have discovered that the basal plant’s vascular network is the richest currency there is, and have evolved to intercept it.

One such lineage takes the form of **capillary thickets**:

- They begin as airborne spores or detached, seed-like nodes carried along internal air currents until they strike a slightly injured surface.
- There they germinate into hair-fine infiltrators that sink toward the nearest pulse-bearing conduit.
- Once contact is made, they open **micro-siphons** that feed on the pressure differential, letting the plant’s own pumping power drive its sap into their bodies.

These parasites expand into dense **sponges** wrapped around veins and ladders of flow. They re-route a portion of the host’s traffic, metabolize what they need, and exude different mixtures back—sometimes toxic, sometimes neutral, sometimes oddly beneficial.

The host can fight back, but not everywhere at once. Around especially entrenched parasitic complexes, it may adopt a strategy of **controlled tolerance**:

- It allows the siphons to exist but encapsulates them in **sheathing tissues** that limit spread.
- It reroutes main flows around patched segments.
- It induces the parasite to adopt a more predictable rhythm by poisoning the most aggressive growth fronts and permitting calmer ones, shaping the invader indirectly.

Over generations of vegetative inheritance, the parasite’s own regulatory systems adapt to the host’s countermeasures. A stable trade emerges: the parasite gets a living niche and steady flow; the host inherits new biochemical tricks—novel defensive compounds, different modes of transport.

These parasitic systems are not minds, but they are **persistent strategies** with their own internal logic. They live, in a sense, as the plant’s adversarial imagination.

#### c. Opportunist Skins

Every exposed surface that sees even a ghost of light is colonized by **microleaf crusts** and **film-gardens**.

These are shallow, fast-responding communities of:

- Minimalist leaf-plaques a few cells thick.
- Filamentous fans that shimmer in the wind of internal breathing.
- Droplet-catching cilia fields that comb mist out of the air.

They don’t tap major sap-flows. Instead, they feast on:

- **Exudates** that sweat out of the basal tissue—sugars, amino acids, leached minerals.
- **Volatile organics** in the humid air.
- **Stray photons** straggling down through tissue layers.

Their intelligence lies in **pattern detection**:

- They track subtle fluctuations in airflow and humidity.
- They adjust pigment densities and alignments to optimize not just photosynthesis, but also the **signal-to-noise ratio** for the host’s optical communication.

Some film-gardens, particularly those that drape across large, flat panels, act almost as **sensor arrays**. They transform mechanical ripples and shifts in light into timed electrochemical reactions that the basal nervous tissue can read. They are like hairs on skin, but each hair is a tiny cooperative thicket of species, and skin here spans thousands of cubic kilometers.

Opportunist skins live at the edge of awareness. They are not “owned” by the basal mind, but much of its perception is filtered through them.

### 3. The Chemical Casters

No animals pollinate; no insects flit from bloom to bloom. Signals that, in other worlds, are encoded in bodies and carried by wings are here cast directly into the air and into the sap as **chemistry**.

The beings that specialize in this are not visually impressive. They exist as:

- **Nodule-glands** nestled in folds or at crucial junctions.
- **Bristling thickets** of reaction tissue along major vents.
- **Buried vesicles** packed along root-like penetrators.

Their task is to compose and release **information molecules**:

- Short-lived messengers that trigger immediate local changes: stomata closures, emergency growth spurts, enzyme deployments.
- Long-lived “hormonal weather systems” that drift slowly through the internal atmosphere, biasing huge regions toward certain developmental paths—thickening here, thinning there, favoring one style of canopy over another.

Some glands specialize in **volatiles** that move with airflow. When a region of the plant experiences mechanical trauma—a ruptured membrane, a slumping wall—they flare into activity, flooding nearby airways with a distinctive, sharp, “alarm-scent” molecule. Distant tissues that breathe it in alter their own processes:

- Growth types that were previously quiescent awaken: reinforcing vines, scar-forming callus tissues.
- Light-gathering plates in that direction may reorient, dimming or thickening to allocate more resources to repair instead of photosynthesis.

Other casters focus on **circulatory signals**:

- They inject pulses of specific compounds directly into sap-flows.
- Because they release into a pulsed system, they can encode timing information in chemical waves: “When this marker arrives, in this sequence, you are part of this event.”

These chemical casters are essentially **endocrine organs** of the global plant. They are not individuals in the animal sense, yet they have local cycles of activity, feedback loops, even something like moods. A gland cluster that has spent centuries regulating a turbulent region becomes tuned to that region’s history; its future responses are colored by what it has “learned” through reinforcement and exhaustion.

They live as the plant’s shifting emotional state—stress, satiation, urgency, rest—translated into molecules.

### 4. The Light-Engines

Deep inside, where sunlight never penetrates, light still moves. The entities responsible inhabit the bioluminescent filaments and nodal bulbs that lace the plateau’s interior: **photonic symbionts** and their scaffolds.

At first glance they look like veins of slow lightning trapped in glass. In truth they are:

- Strands of transparent, living fiber—cellulose variants and protein-glass lattices—grown with extreme precision to guide light like an optical cable.
- Populations of **light-making cells** packed along their lengths, loaded with pigment-proteins and energy stores.

The light-makers are semi-autonomous micro-colonies. They consume:

- Carbohydrates and chemical gradients supplied by the basal circulation.
- Occasionally, direct electrical energy translated into biochemical reactions.

In exchange they emit **tunable light**: narrow-band flashes at multiple wavelengths, each serving different functions:

- Some carry information: modulated pulse trains that travel along fibers to distant nodes, encoding local measurements of pressure, composition, strain.
- Some drive **photo-chemistry** where no sun reaches: splitting molecules, powering slow reactions that would otherwise stall in the dark, turning wastestreams into usable metabolites.
- Some simply **heat or cool** nearby tissues by being absorbed or scattered, fine-tuning local temperatures.

Photonic symbionts have their own simple objectives: maintain internal stores, avoid burnout, respond to the host’s stimuli. But because the plant continuously rewires its light network—growing new fibers, pruning old ones, altering refractive indices—these micro-colonies find themselves constantly recontextualized. A cluster that once lit a nutrient chamber may end up servicing a computational hub or a respiratory vent.

They are like millions of tiny, transplanted lamps, each with a bit of procedural logic, integrated into an ever-changing circuit. Their “lives” consist of being reassigned to new roles, patterns, and neighborhoods as the plant refactors itself.

### 5. The Respiring Throats

The vents that breathe—those deep chimneys where warm, moist air thunders outward and cooler fog is dragged in—are lined with specialized communities that ride and shape the moving atmosphere.

These are not lungs; they are **throats filled with tongues**.

Along the walls grow **lamellar fans**, ribbed like gills:

- They can widen or contract, narrowing the airway or opening it.
- Their surfaces are friable, covered in microstructures that increase contact with air.

Nested between the fans are **scent forests**:

- Dense stands of hairlike structures and micro-leaves that sample and adjust the gas mix with high sensitivity.
- They can absorb specific volatiles and exhale others, acting as scrubbers and mixers.

Together, these throat-ecologies:

- Stabilize the **internal climate**, keeping oxygen peaks and CO₂ troughs from becoming structurally dangerous.
- Shape **air currents** into patterns that propagate signals: a particular gust-profile might be a directional message, a controlled draft could shepherd certain spores to certain layers.
- Host **aerosol chemistries**: microscopic droplets that carry not just water, but hormones, enzymes, even tiny vesicles of genetic material.

The beings that inhabit them have a kind of colony-scale reflex arc. A sudden surge in CO₂? Fans flex, scrubber-plants inflate, exhalation changes tone. A shift in internal pressure elsewhere? Throats downstream anticipate the new pattern, adjusting to smooth the transition.

If anything like a “breath-consciousness” exists, it is here: a network of throat-entities co-managing the slow inhalation and exhalation of a continental lung.

### 6. The Hollows and Their Quiet Experiments

In some chambers—especially where cup-lakes have drained through ruptures, leaving trembling membranes over deep pools—the plant seems to have made space not just for plumbing, but for *trying things out*.

These hollows are occupied by:

- **Floating mats**: rafts of loosely attached tissue, barely tethered to the main mass.
- **Pillar-gardens**: columns of cambium-rich material rising from below, covered in experimental growths.
- **Mist sieves**: delicate, curtain-like arrays of fibrils that hang from above and barely touch the air, altering flows in subtly complex ways.

Here, the basal mind permits greater **developmental looseness**. New tissue types bud, many fail, some stabilize. You can see:

- Fronds with bizarre pigment patterns, tuned to frequencies of light that scarcely exist here.
- Networks of **hollow beads** that click against one another in pressure waves, perhaps exploring acoustic signaling beyond current norms.
- Globes of transparent tissue with **multi-layered membranes** that respond to trace chemical shifts with intricate folding, like embryonic instruments for an orchestra that has not yet found its piece.

These are **laboratories of growth**. The plant invests energy here because it can treat these experiments as relatively low-risk. If a new tissue type destabilizes the local architecture, the membrane floor can rupture, dumping the failed region into the nutrient bath below, where it is digested and recycled.

Some hollows retain failed languages in fossil form: layered bands of tissue with unfamiliar microstructures that no longer function, relics of earlier attempts at different physics. The living plant above reads, in their compressed geometry, a record of what not to repeat.

What lives here are half-formed possibilities—a menagerie of trials, most of which will never escape their chamber, but a few of which may, someday, propagate across the whole world-body.

### 7. The Deep Penetrators

You do not see them from the wet, roaring surfaces, but some of the thickest growths dive down, far below the visible plant-mantle, toward whatever remains of naked lithosphere.

These are **root-burrows** and **mineral tongues**:

- Cables of multiple tissues braided together: high-pressure sap pipes, seismic sensors, and grinding, chemically aggressive root-tips.
- Their business is to **trade with the planet’s rock**, however deeply buried.

They dissolve minerals, leach heat, sense slow tectonic murmurs. Along their lengths are **ore-glands** that precipitate metals and other rare elements into organized nodules, then slowly feed them upward through crystalline channels.

The inhabitants here are highly specialized:

- **Chemo-synthesizers** that derive energy from redox reactions with buried compounds, supplementing the world’s budget when light is scarce.
- **Seismo-receptors**—spindles of piezoelectric tissue that flex with the faintest geological tremors, translating them into electrical patterns that climb back toward the thinking layers above.
- **Crystal-farmers** that grow and maintain the mineral structures used as conductors and memory scaffolds elsewhere.

While the upper plant learns the sky and its own surface, the penetrators learn the deep time below. They report continental drift, mantle upwellings, the slow warping of the submerged crust. Their measurements shape very long-term choices: where to thicken, where to thin, where to buttress.

They live in darkness and stone-contact, at a temporal scale slower even than the rest: their signals may take years to propagate, their cycles centuries. But over planetary eras, theirs is the voice that keeps the mantle-riding forest from overshooting, collapsing, or being peeled away by forces it cannot otherwise sense.

### 8. The Edges of Self

All of these—supporters, parasites, sensors, casters, engines, throats, experimenters, penetrators—exist within and upon the same continuous living field. None are free-roaming. There are no walkers here, no separate silhouettes against any sky. But “self” is not a simple, single pronoun.

In some regions, the basal plant exerts strong command: parasitic siphons are pruned, symbionts are tightly leashed, experimental chambers are walled off. Here the world-body behaves as a single, decisive organism, its tissues marching in biochemical lockstep.

Elsewhere, integration is ragged. Parasites carve out semi-autonomous fiefdoms; symbionts run local control loops that diverge from the host’s preferences; experimental hollows evolve subcultures of tissue that negotiate only weakly with global directives. Signals from one sector arrive out-of-phase with others, and the plant’s distributed cognition lives with the resulting cacophony, settling not on a unified will but on a rolling consensus.

What lives here, then, is not one being but a **stack of beings**:

- A planetary-scale plant-mind, slow and massively parallel.
- A host of embedded systems—vascular, chemical, optical, structural—each with its own tendencies.
- Legions of grafted and infiltrating lifeforms, entirely vegetal but not entirely obedient.
- Swarms of microscopic consortia that ride the flows, sculpting perception and response.

They are all botanically continuous, yet behaviorally distinct. The boundary between organism and environment has dissolved into gradients of influence.

---

### 9. Absences and Futures

Notice who is missing:

- No leaf-eaters.
- No seed-scatterers.
- No burrowers that are not themselves roots.
- No external machinists; all tools are flesh.

This absence is not a void but a **pause**. The world-plant is still in its foundational act: building bulk, establishing internal networks, learning how to be a planetary system. In such a phase, creatures that detach—bodies that wander, brains that move—may be inefficient noise.

Yet the ingredients for them are here:

- Cavities where flows loose enough could host motile clusters.
- Chemical casters that already send hormones and quasi-neurotransmitters through the air.
- Optical and electrical circuits capable of vastly higher information densities than they currently use.
- Hollows where the plant tolerates wild experimentation, including with adhesion, contractility, and rapid response.

What lives here now is an all-encompassing plant consciousness and its botanical satellites. What may live here, as the plateau ages and its first true erosional canyons open, are **mobile summaries** of that consciousness—bud-animals, perhaps, flensed off from the walls and given the gift of walking, bearing pieces of the world-mind into its own interior.

But that is speculation. In the present, standing on the slick tendon-ridge with sound shaking your bones, you are inside a single, unfinished being that fills the world. Everything alive around you is a part of its body, or a part of its argument with itself.

What lives here is the plant, in all its contradictions: root and canopy, nerve and lung, wound and scar, parasite and host, thought and infrastructure all braided together, still deciding what kind of world it intends to become.
