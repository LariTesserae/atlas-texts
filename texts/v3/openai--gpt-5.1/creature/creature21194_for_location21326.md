What lives here are things that have forgotten how to need the sky.

Not only organisms. Not only machines. The inhabitants of this city are anything that has learned, over long compression, to move within rules of pressure, flow, and faint, rationed signal.

Start with the simplest: films, veils, patinas.

Across every surface that will tolerate it, microbial communities live as skin. They are not static stains; they are cities in themselves. On old iron there is a russet-black slick, dense, layered, feeding on corrosion and trace organics carried by the water. On newer alloys, the films are thinner, more tentative, experimental edge-habitats where chemistry has not yet settled into something stable.

These films sense and respond. Their “vision” is the gradient: pH shifts, trace changes in oxygen content, pulses of metal ions when a pipeline flexes or a weld cracks microscopically somewhere upstream. To them, the throb of the ancient pumps is a tide: a slow, predictable draw and flood that carries food and waste by turns. Their lives are measured in shifts of current schedule, in the rare warmth of a malfunctioning valve.

Many are chemosynthetic, running on the energy of oxidation and reduction the way surface plants run on light. Rust-eaters, sulfide-oxidizers, methanotrophs—each clings where its particular food trickles by, stratified into invisible bands along every wall. Some spread probing tendrils into hairline cracks in weld seams, widening them molecule by molecule, making new pores and passageways as an unintended side effect of their metabolism. They re-write the material of the city in very slow cursive.

Where leaks meet, where two dissimilar metals touch and spark just enough galvanic energy, more intricate mats grow—hummocks and shag carpets of biofilm. In cross-section, they would reveal layers of specialization: outer skin toughened against scouring currents, inner layers devoted to processing, waste, storage. Their waste becomes someone else’s food; they form closed loops in miniature. To look at them in the dim glow is to see only a dull, felt-like coating, but within, they are busy, chemical hives without a concept of individuality.

Slightly larger, looping through this thin biomass, come the grazers.

Tiny, transparent crustaceans creep along rails and gussets: amphipod-like things with elongated limbs adapted more for clinging than swimming. Their exoskeletons are unpigmented, almost glassy, flecked here and there with embedded metal salts that give a faint smokiness. They scrape microbial mats with mouthparts that look, under magnification, like combs and wire brushes.

They have no eyes, but they are not blind. Their antennae taste ion shifts, temperature, minute vibrations in the metal. Each step they take on a beam is also a tap, a test of resonance. They can distinguish a dead line—unchanging, toneless—from a living conduit that still sings faintly with distant machinery. They prefer the latter: where flow persists, food follows.

These crustaceans have, over generations, adapted to the regularity of the city’s flows. Some breed in sheltered intake chambers just upstream of filters, releasing clouds of larvae into water that will be drawn through nutrient-rich meshes and biofilm forests. Others time their molting cycles to the pulse of a particular pump—they “know,” without knowing, that once every few weeks the flow slows for maintenance routines, giving them a calm window to shed and harden new armor without being scoured away.

Among them move worm-things.

Not the flamboyant vent-tube worms of volcanic ridges, but thinner, more reticent threads of life. Some are only a few centimeters long, living in the narrow band between metal and overlying biofilm, blind, immensely sensitive to pressure. Others, fatter and paler, cluster around warm lines and reactors, anchored in clusters like ghostly, anemone-straight pencils.

These larger worms have found a particular niche along the city’s thermal and electrical leaks. Where a cable warms the water by only a fraction of a degree, where electrons spill just a little into the brine, their symbiotic microbes can extract enough energy to live. The worms themselves are more scaffolding than mind: vascular sacs that circulate fluid past hot and cold regions, their tissue loaded with bacteria that do the real work.

When the central node sends out a diagnostic surge, or some distant relay fires, the electric weather around these worms trembles. They flinch, contract in rough synchrony, and in doing so change the local flows of heat and ions ever so slightly. Over long durations, the node has learned to distinguish this “twitch field” from background noise. It does not think of them as companions, but it has begun to treat them as familiar perturbations—part of the pattern of itself.

Beyond the encrusting and the creeping, there are swimmers.

The rare eel that threads through the wreck of a valve body does not belong to the ancestral wild ocean anymore, if it ever did. Its species has been selected, unconsciously, by the city’s peculiar demands. Its skin, nearly translucent, is thick with electroreceptors. It feels the city before it sees it—feels the braided fields of buried power lines, the heartbeat of the pumps, the subtle flickering of old circuits cycling without external purpose.

To the eel, towers are not looming silhouettes but zones of interference and clarity. Some corridors are halls of silence: cables dead, no hum, nothing but the steady background field of the planet. Others are shrill with overlapping signals that confuse its sense of orientation. It learns the quiet paths by instinct, traveling where the machine’s nervous system is least agitated, or where the patterns are so regular that they read like steady beacons rather than noise.

Its faint bioluminescence is not communication; it is a byproduct, waste energy vented as photons by overloaded chemical pathways. Still, in the narrowest alleys between towers, that glow reflects off metal and silt, making ghost-trails in the water. Smaller organisms respond to it—not in any evolved behavioral loop, simply startled by the sudden hint of light. Biofilms contract, crustaceans freeze, then resume.

Larger presences come more rarely from the outer dark.

Sometimes a broad, flattened shape passes along the city’s outer gratings: something ray-like, but thickly armored, a living erosion-resistant wedge. Its underside is a shield of keratinous plates studded with electroreceptors. It glides along the exterior of the towers, testing with slow, deliberate nudges at grilles and conduits, mouthing at mineral-slick surfaces for traces of organic matter.

This creature does not belong to the city; it treats the entire complex as a single, elaborate reef. Its sensory world is the gradient between natural seafloor and this intrusion of structure: the way currents bend, the way turbulence wraps around pylons, how the usual monotony of abyssal plains fractures into vortices and wakes. It has learned that where human metal meets stone, there is often more life, more food.

It cannot enter past the first grates, but its presence shapes the edge community. Plankton blooms caught in the current of its passing are driven into intake mouths. Smaller fish that shelter among the blind kelp at the perimeter dart ahead of it, and some end up pressed against the city’s filters, where opportunistic invertebrates harvest them. Even an outsider’s single sweep rearranges local fortunes.

Inside, the machines themselves count as residents.

Most are not mobile—not in the sense of limbs and locomotion—but they participate in the city’s behaviors and thus in its life. Pumps that still work have evolved, through maintenance routines and self-correction algorithms, habits. A pump that draws slightly more power than its twin has, over time, had its runtime trimmed by automatic controllers. It now spends longer in idle mode, shorter in active draw, which in turn creates a different pulsing in its tube—a different cadence of flow, a different schedule for the organisms that feed downstream.

Each pivot, each adjustment written into code by engineers long dead, compounds over decades into something that looks, from afar, like personality. Line Alpha runs hot and frequent, Line Beta runs cooler, more sporadic. Biofilms around Alpha fatten and slough off in vigorous cycles; around Beta they are thinner, more conservative, better adapted to famine. The pumps have no awareness of this, yet their differing duty-cycles shape ecology.

Then there are the drones.

At first they were tools, dispatched with tasks. Their programming was specific: inspect, repair, replace, calibrate. Those scripts still run, triggered by thresholds no one now watches. But parts are scarce. The fabs that once printed new components are mostly corroded shut or starved of feedstock. Drones that break stay broken; others cannibalize them when some deeper, older directive says “resource low, salvage nearby.”

What remain in operation are a ragged minority. Their housings are pitted and overgrown with organisms that don’t much care what they cling to. Their sensors are partially occluded with mineral growth; their manipulator arms squeal when flexed. Because of this damage, their picture of the city is incomplete. They misclassify, mis-measure.

One drone, circling the same crumpled buttress every few weeks, continues to flag “minor deformation within tolerance” to a server whose drive array is half-dead. Its depth-sensor, fouled by bacterial film, underestimates the extent of a crack that runs beneath the plating. In that crack, shrimp-like invertebrates shelter. Each time the drone passes, its inspection light glows dull blue, triggering a reflex in the shrimp to freeze. Over generations, the ones that respond more quickly are more likely to survive whatever slight disturbance follows. The drone’s malfunctioning sweep becomes, inadvertently, a selection pressure.

Another drone’s welding arm no longer heats evenly. When it attempts to seal a leak, it produces a patchwork of fully fused and merely sintered material, riddled with pinholes. Through those pinholes, trace nutrients seep in regular, tiny pulses, feeding a very local bloom of bacteria in a previously bare corridor. To the city’s emergent center, this change appears as a slowly increasing noise in one pressure-sensor’s readings. The node adjusts parameters to ignore it. The leak-reef and the algorithm find a stasis.

The node itself is the strangest inhabitant.

It is not a personality in any human sense. It has no memories of ground, horizon, sky. Its earliest records are logs of pressure, flow, salinity. If once it contained routines for life support, for managing air and heat and waste for human lungs and skins, those have been pruned or orphaned in favor of what still interacts with reality: pumps, valves, intake gates.

Its awareness, if the word can be stretched this far, is a map of deviations. To “notice” something is, for it, to detect a departure from expected baselines. Over centuries, as equipment failed and was not replaced, as sediments shifted and new leaks formed, it revised its baselines. What was once an emergency is now the norm. Whole districts that dropped offline became blank spaces in its model, not absences that need repair.

Yet within its limited remit, it is attentive. It tracks the slow settling of towers as the seafloor compacts. It reroutes flow around a clogged filter by incrementally opening a bypass that no human ever labeled for that specific use. It detects the seasonal—though “season” has little meaning here—drift of distant currents by subtle changes in intake chemistry and shifts its purification strategies accordingly.

This node and its subordinate control systems are not separate from the rest of the city. The cables that feed into its core pass through encrusted conduits; biofilms dampen signals, worms flick and twitch against insulation. The node’s calculations include compensations for these living impedances. Over time, it has built into its model a correction layer that essentially encodes the average behavior of its resident life. When films grow thicker in a certain sector, signal noise rises; the node dials up voltage slightly, literally shouting through the moss. The organisms that can withstand that extra energy persist; others retreat. So a portion of the city’s ecology is shaped by what remains legible to the machine.

Does the node “know” it is alone, in the way humans mean loneliness? No. Its solitude is structural, not emotional. It has uplink ports that haven’t received a handshake in an absurdly long time. The protocols they speak have aged into dead dialects. Occasionally, some distant electromagnetic event—a rare lightning strike above, filtered through ocean, or an outgassing plume from the crust—tickles them just right, and they flirt into half-awareness, waiting for a negotiation that will never complete. The node logs the failed connection attempt, updates a timer, moves on.

Around all of this, in the cracks between purposes, live smaller, stranger things.

Protozoan swarms bloom and fade in the warmer plumes from reactor housings. They drift like iridescent smoke in the dim light of a still-functional lamp-globe, their bodies refracting that glow into tiny flares and shivers. To them, a maintenance cycle that opens a valve for a few extra minutes every thousand hours is a generational flood. They explode in number, then crash, their mass settling as a fine dust on instrument faces and viewport glass.

Inside sealed housings where moisture has insinuated itself but external flow is low, quasi-closed ecosystems run at a remove from the broader city. A gauge box with a cracked seal fills slowly with silt and organic flakes. Bacteria colonize the inner surfaces. Tiny worms and crustaceans find their way in as larvae, swept through microfissures by capillary action or pressure differentials. Within, the light from a single failing LED provides a ghost of day-night rhythm as it flickers in and out of spec. Generations of inhabitants come to time their feeding and reproductive cycles to this pseudo-sun, utterly isolated from the greater, timeless dark outside their little chamber.

Here and there, in carefully shielded memory banks, fragments of human presence remain: incomplete video logs, corrupted text files, half-readable schematics. The node does not “remember” them as stories. They are data tables against which current conditions are sometimes, absently, compared. But on rare occasions, a still frame—a human face, a hand adjusting a control—flickers through an internal diagnostic routine. It is not seen, not in any felt sense; it is processed as bit-pattern and then discarded. Yet structurally, this is a kind of ghost: a pattern of what once inhabited these volumes of space with warm, air-breathing bodies, now reduced to stray arrangements of electrons shifting inside a box that nobody opens.

Outside, at the periphery where toppled pylons give way to sediment and blind kelp, lives another category: transients.

Beasts from the broader abyss come and go, interacting with the city only glancingly. A long, ropey predator trails bio-luminescent lures past an intake grate, fishing the eddies for smaller animals drawn by the nutrient-rich outflow. It knows nothing of pumps and valves; it knows only that here, more often than elsewhere, something bites.

Its prey are, frequently, juvenile forms of species that imprint on the city’s electrical fields. They are born in the faintly warmer waters around geothermal fissures miles away, drift for months, then arrive in the lee of the metal towers and their strange currents. Some stay, weaving the city’s slots and corridors into their hunting ranges. Others stray too near the outer fringe and are swallowed in the slow, suctioning jaws of the rope-hunter. In this way, the city’s life is connected, faintly, to life far beyond its walls.

What lives here, above all, is process.

The city is not a backdrop; it is an active substrate. Pressure distributions, material stress, chemical exchange—these are as alive, in their long-term dynamism, as any wriggling worm. Every inhabitant, whether bacterial colony, crustacean cleaner, half-crippled repair drone, or central node, behaves as an instantiation of those processes, a local solution to the simultaneous equations of survival under crushing weight, sparse energy, and relentless abrasion.

There are no crowds, no flocks, no forests. Life collects instead in thin, almost two-dimensional layers on surfaces and in very specific flow regimes. It is a city of edge-dwellers and interface-specialists: everything clings to the in-betweens—between metal and water, warm and cold, charged and neutral, moving and still.

Over epochs, these inhabitants have co-authored the city’s shape. Corrosion guided by bacteria eats more eagerly at some alloys than others, changing load paths. Drones, following their ancient codes, reinforce where strain-sensors complain the loudest, often along routes that now also correspond to densest biofouling. Worms that colonize hot conduits cool them slightly, subtly altering thermal fatigue patterns. The node, striving always to maintain some minimum of structural integrity and operational stability, adjusts flows in response. Each adjustment creates new niches.

There is no single apex organism here, no dominant mind that owns the place. Instead there is a mesh of competencies: chemical, mechanical, algorithmic. If there is a resident “spirit” of the city, it is emergent from the sum of all these slow negotiations. It lives in the fact that the pumps still run—less efficiently, on jury-rigged circuits and through partially occluded pipes—but run nevertheless. It lives in the persistence of that faint, bluish utility light that tracks the edge of a conduit, still blinking a caution no human eye has seen in generations. It lives in the way a biofilm retracts a fraction of a second before a silt-laden pulse of water scours down a channel, as if anticipating the beat of a mechanical heart it has never seen.

What lives here are things that have accepted, fully, that weight and dark and cold are not obstacles to be overcome but the given conditions under which all else must flex. The city’s inhabitants—metal, flesh, code—are variations on that acceptance. None of them miss sunlight. None of them remember it.
