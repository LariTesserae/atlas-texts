They are people thinned to essentials, like the place itself.

They are not many. You never see them all at once. They come in ones and twos: a figure bent over a channel with a flat stone in hand, coaxing its edge sharper so the water will choose it; someone seated in a doorway, hands working slowly at a cord; a cluster of three in the central space, not quite a marketplace now, but a place where news and grain and obligations are exchanged without hurry.

Their bodies carry the same kind of weathering as the stone. They are not gaunt in the way of famine, but lean in the way of long continuance under narrow margins. Skin tones run the range of earth here — sand, silt, dark clay — browned further by the high light. Their faces are unornamented and unremarkable in the way of people who have never needed to impress strangers from far away. You notice eyes more than any other feature: clear, reflective, used to reading small changes in brightness and texture rather than color and motion.

They move as if everything has a known place and there is no need to test the boundaries. Steps are quiet, weight set down with an unconscious care that protects both their own joints and the softened stone. They do not avoid the center of paths so much as have, over time, distributed their patterns of passage to keep any one route from wearing too deep. Children, when they appear, are not exuberant streaks of color; they are only smaller versions of the adults, shadows that learn early where not to shout, how to run by pressing their excitement into their legs and keeping their throats closed.

Their clothing is layered, meant more for the plateau’s temperature shifts than for display. Woven wool, felted and patched, in colors that begin muted and fade further with time: smoke, lichen, dried grass, the bruised blue of old sky. A typical garment is a long, sleeved tunic or wrap reaching to mid-calf, belted or tied with a braided cord. Over that, when the air thins in the evening, they pull on sleeveless jackets thick at the shoulders, or shawls that can be reshaped into hoods. Edges are bound in tougher fabric where they are handled most. You see careful mending everywhere: knees and elbows reinforced with oval patches; tears repaired with close, almost invisible stitching that follows the original weave.

On their feet they wear sandals or low boots of softened hide, soles thickened with multiple layers, stitched and re-stitched. The footprint they leave on the dusty stone is shallow, almost apologetic.

Jewelry, where it exists, is quiet and solid: a carved stone bead at a throat, a narrow cuff of darkened metal on a wrist, a loop of smoothed bone or horn at an ear. These are not numerous. You suspect each piece has outlived several generations of wearers, passed along with the same resigned inevitability as a house or a patch of cultivated soil.

They call themselves something that, if translated, would sound less like a tribe or nation and more like a verb: “the stayers,” “the holders,” “those who remain upon.” Their word for the plateau is older than any of their remembered stories. It is not grand. It is a simple name, like “the level” or “the high stone,” that has been spoken so often it has lost its edges, become a soft, almost bodily sound in their mouths.

Their number has tilted down over the long run, but they keep a certain minimum as carefully as they keep the water. Families are small by necessity, often one or two children only, with gaps of years between births. Older people are numerous in proportion. You see them sitting in doorways at midday, backs to the cool stone, legs extended, fingers busy with the slow tasks that do not require keen eyes. White and gray hair is worn either close-cropped or braided and coiled against the skull, out of the wind and away from tools and smoke.

Work here is not separated sharply by gender. The same hands that mend a reed basket will wield the stone adze when a roof slab needs shifting. There are tendencies rather than rules: those with better knees tend the terraces and channels; those with patience for repetition sit in the shaded interiors, spinning fiber or scraping hides. People are valued for endurance, for unspectacular reliability. The most admired quality is not strength or cleverness but a certain tempered steadiness: the ability to do the same necessary tasks in the same quiet, precise way, year after year, until the gestures themselves become part of the plateau’s routine.

They are, before anything else, keepers of flows.

Water is not theirs to own, but it is their responsibility to guide it. Every household has at least one person who understands the plateau’s minute hydraulics intimately: who knows which slight lip to lower a finger’s breadth to divert an extra trickle into a cistern, when to scrape algae from a channel and when to leave the slime because it slows evaporation. After a rain — which here is an event, not a background condition — they are everywhere, walking the paths of the runnels, crouching to scoop debris from an inlet, adjusting flat stones over open mouths to reduce loss. They do this without visible urgency. The urgency is all in the timing and the remembered patterns, not in the speed of their bodies.

There is a kind of priesthood, if you want to call it that, but its rituals are almost indistinguishable from maintenance. These are the oldest “keepers,” men and women whose main work is to watch. They keep track — not with writing, but with stones moved in shallow trays, with knotted cords and the angles of sticks in wall-cracks — of how much rain arrived each season, how long the cisterns stayed above a certain depth, when the last rare frost came. This knowledge is not hoarded. It is slowly and constantly taught: a thumb showing a child the thickness of damp at the bottom of a storage jar and naming the number of days it means; a story about “the year when the sun walked too long” and what measures were taken then.

Their language has dozens of words for thin water. Not different sources, but different behaviors: water that beads and will not run, that soaks and does not shine, that lingers as a skin you can feel but not see. There is a verb specifically for the first shining of moisture along a dry channel, another for the exact moment a cistern’s black stone shifts color as the level rises to kiss new rock. They use them without sentimentality. To them, water is not a god or a miracle. It is an element with a strict schedule and small tolerance for carelessness.

Food is grown close and low. Those blanket-sized plots are planted not in the lush idea of fields, but like herbaria: each patch a different texture. You see mats of hardy grains with fine, hairlike heads that break easily into hand-sized bunches; squat pulse plants that hide their pods under their own leaves; a few leafy things that never grow tall, living their whole lives in a tight, ground-hugging rosette that can be harvested by cutting outer rings and leaving the core to persist. Root crops exist, but they are more shallow, adapted to the thin soil over stone: tubers shaped like thick fingers rather than fists, each living in a carefully turned pocket along some micro-depression that catches a few drops more than its neighbor.

In thin seasons they add to this with what can be found on the slopes: tough berries, bitter leaves boiled twice, the occasional animal taken at great effort. Meat is an accent in their diet, not a staple. When a mountain goat from the higher ridges is brought down or one of their few domestic animals is finally slaughtered, every part is used. Flesh is cut fine and dried on trays in the full light. Bones are split for marrow, then later reshaped into awls and needles, the scraps burned in small, hot fires whose heat is captured in stone-lined pits for baking.

They keep some animals, but not many, and not close. A small herd of thick-coated, hornless creatures — neither sheep nor goat as you know them, but occupying a similar niche — browses on the down-slope edges where more scrub grows. They are guided rather than driven, moving at the same deliberate pace as their keepers. The animals know the paths to water as well as the humans do. Their milk is thin but rich enough to be cultured in cool jars into something like a hard, sour cheese that keeps well. Their hair is combed and spun and gives many of the people’s garments that faint smell of lanolin and dust.

You notice the absence of loud animals: no barking dogs, no braying beasts of burden. Companionship, here, is mostly human and stone.

Their houses are arranged less around separate nuclear families and more around quiet clusters of kin and obligation. A “house” may mean three low structures around a shared courtyard and a half dozen people of various ages and affinities sharing work, food, and sleeping space according to shifting needs. Privacy is not a prized commodity. Sound doesn’t travel far in this thin, listening air, and inside the thick-walled rooms speech falls even shorter, absorbed by textiles, grain sacks, and the simple fact that no one says more than they must.

Inside, the rooms are as bare as the outside suggests. Raised sleeping platforms of stone or packed earth run along the walls, covered with layered mats and folded blankets. In one corner, always, there is a hearth: a shallow depression blackened at its edges, with three or four stones set to hold a pot above the coals. Above this, smoke stains mark the path to a nearly invisible vent. Tools hang in their places: stone knives with smoothed bone handles, wooden spatulas, the prized metal blades wrapped in cloth or leather when not in use. One wall usually has a line of niches cut into it, each holding a jar: water, grain, dried herbs, a precious residue of oil.

Illumination at night is not taken for granted. They do not flood their interiors with light. Instead there are small, carefully made oil lamps — a shallow stone cup with a spout, a wick of twisted fiber — used sparingly. Light is directed toward hands working, toward the immediate circle of people, leaving the edges of the room in breathing darkness. Children learn not to waste flame as surely as they learn not to waste water.

They have stories, but they are compressed, polished things, much like the stones they live among. There are no sprawling epics of conquest. Their narratives turn instead around thresholds: the first climb up to the plateau, which none of them were alive to see but all of them speak of as if it were last season; the times when too many left and too few remained; the seasons of unexpected abundance when rain came twice as often as it should and they were unsettled by having to build new storage jars. These stories are told aloud mostly in the bright-grey calm afternoons, when outside work is finished and the interior spaces are cooler.

One story you hear variations of describes the plateau as a guest, not a possession. Long ago — longer than any count of grandmothers — they say the stone “sat down” here, descending from higher up the mountain or lifting from below, depending on the teller. It “offered its back” to any who wished to live where water moves slowly and wind does not scream. Their ancestors accepted, and in doing so entered into an arrangement: they would keep the back smooth, not cut too deep, not load it with too much weight; in return, the plateau would remain stable, would weather gently, would continue to catch and cool the air so that water could linger. It is an almost geologic covenant, a myth that teaches engineering.

Religion, if you insist on naming it, is more obligation than worship. There is no temple distinct from the rest, but there are two places clearly held apart. One is a broad, shallow depression near the center where several runnels converge. Its floor is elaborately smoothed, edges rounded not by weather alone but by generations of human hand. In its middle, a low stone ring circles an opening to a cistern below. Here, on mornings after rain, they gather. There is no chanting, no sacrifice, but a series of small, precise gestures: each person steps forward, touches the rim with fingertips, then with forehead, then speaks a single word, always the same for that person. It might be a name, a number, or something in between. An accounting, a promise, a record — you cannot quite tell. Afterward, a designated keeper measures the depth with a knotted rope, calls out the result, and a few nods follow. This is both ritual and ledger.

The second sacred space is not built at all: it is the cliff edge on the side facing the gentler ridge. There, the plateau falls away less violently, and a narrow ledge runs along part of the drop, accessible by a carved stair so old its steps are more remembered than visible. Every few years — more in some generations, fewer in others — bodies are brought there, wrapped in cloth, weighted with stone. There is no burial on the plateau itself. Nothing is dug deeply into its back. The dead are given to the cliff, set carefully upon the ledge or, if the family asks, lowered further down to a shelf out of easy sight. Their wrappings are marked with simple patterns, unique to each lineage. Over time, fabric and flesh decay; bones slide, fall, are taken by whatever faint things move along the rock faces. The plateau does not keep its dead. That, too, is part of the agreement.

They keep track of time, but not to overfine degrees. Days are marked by the sun and by the tasks that belong to particular slants of light: the early-hour walk to check the far cistern; the midday retreat into the deeper rooms; the late-afternoon turning of drying mats and the small reorientations of storage lids. Months or something like them are counted by the appearance and disappearance of certain stars just after sunset, by the blooming of a particular cliff flower that pushes bright petals for only a few evenings a year. Years are tallied by notches cut discreetly into a sheltered face of rock near the central space. You would not notice them unless someone pointed them out: a vertical line of short horizontal cuts, grouped by fives, worn soft by fingers passing over them.

Once in a long while, someone from outside comes. Not often — the climb is discouraging for those who do not already know the path and do not have a reason — but trade is not entirely absent. When it happens, it is calm and matter-of-fact. Foreigners arrive thin with the ascent, voices too loud for the air, eyes wide at the plateau’s near-emptiness. The locals meet them in the central space. They do not display wares; they know what they have and what they might want. Exchanges are simple: coarse salt carried up from lower valleys traded for small, carefully carved stone tools that take an edge like nothing else; a length of fine hydrophobic cloth bartered against access to a cistern in drought years. The outsiders always bring stories, carried eagerly up the mountain to trade along with goods. The plateau people listen politely. They nod at tales of wars, of green valleys and crowded markets, of cities that blaze with lamps at night. These are curiosities, like tales of rare animals. They do not change the plateau’s schedule.

Some of the younger plateau-bred people leave with these traders, at least for a time. There are always a few faces gone each decade, a few new ones brought back by marriage or by homesickness. The leaving is not framed as exile or adventure. It is an adjustment in the slow accounting of the community: so many mouths removed, so many hands no longer at the channels. The returning are greeted without ceremony. They bring back small innovations occasionally — a new knot, a slightly different way to line a jar, rumor of a distant place where water is so plentiful it falls from the sky in sheets for days and people complain — but these novelties, too, soften and blend. If you watch long enough, you see that the plateau is less a closed world than a filter. Ideas arrive, are tested against the firm rules (water, stone, sound, light), and either dissolve or find a niche.

They have no written signs on walls, no carvings that declare ownership or law. Memory is carried in bodies and in the built stone itself. When they want to explain something that will outlast lived recollection, they modify the environment in small, telling ways: a particular pattern of stones at the edge of a dangerous drop; a slightly raised lip along a channel that warns “here it once overflowed”; a bench carved just so under a certain tree remnant, placed exactly where the winter sun will touch it at mid-morning. Instruction is enfolded into comfort, hazard, and convenience.

Their children learn by being close, by watching hands instead of listening to lectures. There is very little overt correction. If a child runs and scuffs too much dust, an elder will simply walk behind and smooth the disturbed patch with their foot, letting the act be its own reminder. If a cup is overfilled and a bead of water starts to slide down its side, another hand quietly appears with a cloth to catch it, and later, perhaps days later, that same child will be shown how to tilt a vessel, how much to pour when the air is already thick with damp.

Laughter exists, but it is folded in, brief and low. On still evenings, when the sky has gone to deep cobalt and the first bright stars are burning, you might pass a courtyard and hear a small burst of it — someone making a rare joke about an old keeper’s habit, a playful argument about whose turn it is to check the farthest cistern tomorrow. It doesn’t echo off the stone. It sits in the air nearby, then dissolves.

They sing, but not often, and never loudly. Their songs are more like extended hums, built on a narrow range of notes that sit easily in the chest. The words, when they appear, are sparse and repetitive: lists of the plateau’s features, invocations of the known depressions where water collects, descriptions of seldom-seen phenomena like a full fog blanketing the stone. A favorite song among the younger ones is a long, looping piece that maps the climb from the lower forest to up here, naming every switchback and landmark. It is part memory aid, part game: they compete to see who can keep the sequence unbroken without prompting.

Conflict does happen; they are no more immune to human friction than anyone. But the scale of their life constrains it. When everything you rely on is shared and fragile — water, storage, the thin soil in the plots — you cannot afford to fracture into factions without breaking the system. Quarrels are settled by slowness. If tension rises between two people, the others gently rearrange work so they do not have to speak for a time. The plateau itself intervenes: there is always a channel to scrape, a roof stone to right, a distant ledge to check for rockfall. By the time they next need to share a task, the immediate heat has often seeped away.

Punishment, for more serious breaches, is of a particular kind. Those who waste water, who lie about stores, who hoard more grain than their agreed share, are not beaten or driven out. They are denied the comfort of routine. For a period — days, weeks, depending on the community’s decision — they are assigned to work that keeps them in motion more than they’d like: carrying loads up and down the paths to the lower slopes, watching distant goat herds in silence. It is not an exile from the plateau, but from its center. When the task ends, they are folded back in with no ceremony. The memory of their transgression lingers more in their own altered pace than in any expressed grudge.

The plateau people do not conceive of themselves as stranded or deprived. In their stories, this is the place where the world is most itself: where stone is mostly stone, where water is obviously rare and thus clearly valued, where sky is not interrupted by dust or smoke. They speak almost wistfully — when the subject comes up at all — of the noise and mutability below: floods that tear through valley settlements, soils that slide and bury homes, winds that scream so loud you cannot hear the word you shout into someone’s ear. Up here, they say, “the world can hear its own thoughts.”

That is what they have chosen to do as well: to live where the world’s mutterings are audible, and to make their own presence as light and as constant as they can. They are conservators, not of a past age but of a particular relationship between human need and a piece of stone that has agreed, for now, to hold still. They patch walls because walls are there to patch. They adjust channels because channels, left alone, veer off line by imperceptible increments. Their purpose is not written in any doctrine, but if you stood on that central terrace and asked each of them in turn why they stay, their answers would likely condense into a shared understanding:

“Because someone must remember how to live like this.”

In that sense, what lives here is not only them. It is a way of being: a culture of deliberate smallness, of respect enacted as daily labor rather than as grand gesture. Children are raised not just to survive this place, but to become an extension of its equilibrium, the way lichens extend the patience of rock onto neighboring surfaces.

Alongside these humans there are other lives, thinner but continuous. In the damp corner behind a cistern mouth, a spider species has evolved that spins webs in tight, three-dimensional knots, catching not flying insects — there are too few — but fine dust motes and the rare, small creatures that ride them. In the sheen of standing water that appears for a few days after a major rain, minute crustaceans and dormant algae awake, bloom, and vanish again, their cysts remaining in the stone’s microscopic pores for years. Mosses breathe so slowly their growth is measured in generations. The plateau people notice them. They have names for the few lichens that seem to “like” certain walls, for the way a particular moss curls when the air’s dampness reaches a specific threshold. These are neighbors as familiar as any human face.

Above, the birds that rarely land still belong in the plateau’s mental map. They are the “turners,” the ones that spin in the thermals and announce changes in the wider weather a day or two before the clouds arrive. The humans watch their patterns as closely as they watch the sky itself. If a certain kind of small, high-flying bird vanishes, they know the wind is about to shift and may bring dust from some distant dry region. They close their vents preemptively, stack stones in certain gaps, brace mentally for a day of grit in their teeth.

In all of this, the plateau is less backdrop than partner. Its very stillness is an active participant in shaping these people. They have grown, over many generations, into a population that matches its contours and moods: few, sturdy, attentive, resistant to sudden change but capable of endless, fine-grained adjustment. If they were to leave, the built stone would remain for a while, catching water, dreaming its slow dreams. But without those hands, those precise decisions about where a drop should go or when a lip should be raised half a thumb’s width, the system would gradually revert to something more purely geological.

So what lives here, in truth, is an agreement: between human intention and stone patience, between water’s reluctance to stay and their stubborn desire to remain. The people of the plateau are its living clause, the part of the contract that can walk and speak, that can kneel at a cistern’s edge and whisper a single, careful word into the cool dark below before stepping back, leaving the surface to grow still again.
