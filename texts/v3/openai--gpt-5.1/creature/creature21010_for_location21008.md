What lives here has forgotten that it is strange.

It does not, for the most part, think of itself as “animal” or “machine” or “colony.” Those are your distinctions. Up here, the plateau has had too long to blur such lines. Everything that persists does so by learning to play the slow, saturated game of the place: to lean on the rhythms of drip and hum, to treat the endless city-body as both ancestor and environment.

I’ll tell you of them in rough tiers—though in truth they interleave and trespass constantly.

### The city’s largest inhabitants

The great moving masses you glimpse crossing sagged viaducts and softened horizons are called, by nothing but our convenience, **plinths**.

They are vast, many-legged bodies whose proportions echo the architecture they walk upon. Each can be the length of a derelict avenue, their backs so high that mist curls along their flanks like weather along a mountain. From afar they resemble whole mid-sized buildings slowly relocating: hunched profiles, buttress-like limbs, fluted slopes where armor plates overlap.

Their substance is not simple flesh. At their core is a columnar skeleton of dense, porous composite—part mineral, part toughened protein—threaded through with slow vascular conduits. Around this core they assemble shells from the plateau itself. They tear down defunct balustrades, pry plates from drooping facades, harvest the glass-stalactite growths that exude faint warmth, then fuse these materials with calcified secretions from their own glands. Over decades, each plinth grows a laminar shell of scavenged city: bolted and secreted and grown together, annealed under its own weight.

On their underbodies hang forests of jointed palps and heavy, brush-tipped legs, more like exploratory cranes than limbs. They taste, tap, and listen to every surface before they commit their weight. A plinth never rushes. One foot might hover, testing, for the length of a human lifetime before it settles. Even so, they occasionally misjudge, and an entire limb-slung deck will groan and tear open beneath them. When that happens, they do not scream or panic; they redistribute, reroute, wait out the collapse like a ship riding a long swell.

Their heads—if you can call them that—are low, shielded bulks, pressed close to the surfaces they cross. Along those margins, rows of retractable eyes—hooded with mineral film—stare sideways into cracks and joints. They see less with light than with pressure and vibration. The still-working organs of the plateau send their tremors through the structure; the plinths read these as a blind person reads embossed text. They know which ducts still carry pumping fluids, which spans are about to fail, where new voids have opened.

Inside, each plinth is its own ecosystem.

Their inner cavities are warm, humid, dim. Mineral-film gardens line some of their interior walls, fed drip by drip from strategically opened condensation veins that run across their backs. Entire guilds of small grazers live in those gardens, never leaving the host’s body. Filterers occupy the plinth’s breathing vents, sieving aerosols from the plateau air. Parasites, commensals, mutualists: layered in tiers, all moving at the same long pace.

Plinths communicate rarely, but when they do, it is through the city itself. Two passing within kilometers may never see one another in the mist, yet their slow, infrasonic pulses travel through columns and spans, meeting in the bones. They exchange simple patterns—densities of filming, thickness of condensation, particular engine cycles gone quiet or recently awakened. The messages are not language as you know it, but mutual weather reports: notes on the shared body they walk upon.

Some plinths never cross paths with others in their entire lives. Some are born by fission: an overgrown individual, its internal rhythms de-synchronized, slowly pinch in the middle over centuries until one body becomes two, each inheriting half of its resident colonies and sound-memories. Birth, for them, is a geological event.

### The keepers of water and film

Because leaves never took hold here, something else had to do the work of catching what little usable energy and nutrient there is. That role falls to a loose assemblage of beings you might call **curators** of moisture and surface.

#### Bracket grazers

Flush against nearly every damp vertical, you find the **bracket grazers**. From a distance they’re indistinguishable from mineral stalactites frozen mid-drop, or from sagging brackets that once held signage. Each is a crescent or oval of layered, chalky plates pressed flat to the surface it feeds on.

They live by slow rasp and soak. A ring of toothlike cilia around their hidden underside scrapes at the rubbery films and brittle veils, dissolving shavings in a thin exudate and drawing the slurry inward. Their metabolism is unrushed: an adult bracket may spend a decade on a single palm-sized patch of wall, advancing only a millimeter at a time as it vacuums the microtopography clean.

They are not mere mouths. Along their outer shell they inscribe long-term records—growth bands etched by internal chemistry, responding to the composition of whatever films they eat. A pale, brittle ring for years of iron-rich runnels; a translucent, oily band where the film was thick with faint hydrocarbons; a chalk-dark layer where some unfamiliar seep brought toxins. When a bracket dies, its shell falls, breaks, and its fragments are incorporated into the city strata like fossil tree rings of someone else’s climate.

Some fauna read those rings. Slim, tendril-limbed foragers—imagine the outline of a starfish wrapped around a spider—crawl across fallen bracket shards, touching each band with soft sensory pads, tasting time.

#### Vein herders

Along the plateau’s slow-moving condensation routes move **vein herders**: mid-sized quadrupeds or hexapods with long, soft-tipped snouts and cabley, extensible tongues. Their bodies are unarmored but cloaked in hanging curtains of mineral film that they cultivate as mobile sponges.

A herder’s life is a circuit. It traces a path through a network of “veins”: ceiling seams where water habitually beads, hairline cracks that sweat, gutter-channels where films are thickest. At each stop it presses its hanging pellicles against the dripping surface, letting them load with moisture, dissolved metals, organics, and microscopic life. Only when the weight begins to drag its sides into ragged scallops does it retreat to a favored hollow, shake the films loose into a basin on its own back, and then lick that basin clean with its tongues.

Their intelligence is oriented around routes and cycles. A given clan of vein herders—loose, barely cooperative lineages—may maintain the same loop for thousands of years, passing the knowledge of every turn and access slit bodily: not by teaching, but by simple following and imprinting. The path is encoded in their muscles and balance, in the way they relax when they smell a familiar filmy composition.

They’re followed, always, by smaller specialists: **lichen-lice** that feed exclusively on the herders’ own films, scraping out impurities and excreting trace elements their hosts need.

### Walkers of the interior routes

The plateau is deeply hollow. Between its terrace faces lies a labyrinth of galleries, shafts, and chambers where the sky never enters. Here, life skews paler and more structural.

#### The column worms

Within support pillars that once carried maglev lines or traffic loads now pulse the **column worms**: enormous, near-stationary tubes of wet muscle and cartilage threaded through the hollowed cores. If you could peel the city away, they’d look like vertical, branching taproots: thick stems that split and rejoin, following and enlarging the old conduit paths.

They are not predators, nor exactly engineers. They are digesters of debris and maintainers of flow. Every crumb of fallen shell and film that sifts down through cracks, every husk washed inward by capillary trickle, accumulates at the base of these shafts. The column worms draw this fallow slurry upward in slow peristaltic pulses, break it down in a long, efficient gut, and excrete it as a fine, mineral-rich paste into rifts higher up, where myriad small grazers await.

In so doing, they keep the inner voids from choking. They are the plateau’s intestinal cilia, clearing clogs that would otherwise dam the hidden hydrologies.

They sense the world through pressure. Any significant vibration—a distant collapse, the tread of a plinth, a rare thunder-roll from below—registers along their length. They respond not by fleeing (they cannot), but by adjusting their pumping rhythms, their internal valves. To them, “news” is an irregularity in the never-ending slurry.

Around certain especially ancient worms, generations of smaller beings have built layered hives, hardening secretions into helical collars that wind up the shaft. From the outside, such a pillar resembles a titan’s windpipe fossilized in glass and rust.

#### Resonance hunters

Threading through these same interiors are the **resonance hunters**: lithe, cable-bodied predators that hide inside walls and floors.

Their skin is not skin so much as a thin sheath of conductive, semi-metallic tissue. They lie flattened along girders and inside duct walls, pressing their flanks to the structure. Periodically they emit narrow-band clicks—not into the air, but directly into metal, composite, stone.

Each click runs outward as a packet of stress, bouncing, splitting, echoing through beams, brackets, plates. Anything soft that interrupts those echoes—any cluster of flesh, any sac of shivering water—leaves a telltale shadow in the returning signal.

When a good echo returns—small but dense, moving at a vulnerable pace—three things happen in sequence. First, the hunter recalculates a three-dimensional intercept, like a bat triangulating mid-flight. Second, it waits until the target steps onto one of the paths it has prepared. Third, parts of the city open.

What you thought was a seamless panel of corroded cladding suddenly swivels; what looked like a harmless crack widens along invisible hinges. Jaws that match the texture of old beams snap out, drag something screaming into the thickness, and shut again. From outside, the event is barely visible: a slight tremor in a wall, a puff of dust, a new stain.

Over centuries, these hunters tunnel intricate networks of such hinged fangs and false backs within the strata. They relocate slowly, laying down abandoned false-fronts as they go. The city, in this way, grows an internal layer of mobile, predatory architecture.

### Masters of the air that is almost water

The plateau’s sky is dense, warm, and close. The creatures that cast shadows there do not so much “fly” as manage buoyancy and drag.

#### Mantle gliders

Spanning the gaps between towers and terraces drift the **mantle gliders**. Their bodies are narrow cores—rigid rods containing muscle, ligament, and a stack of buoyant sacs—fringed by immense, heavy membranes.

Those membranes are not uniform. Regions closest to the body are dense, dark, loaded with microcrystals that heat slightly when flexed, adding small, controllable convection currents. Out toward the edges the film thins and grows translucent, studded with pores and lacy stiffeners. The whole wing behaves like a variable-porosity parachute: by shifting fluid within their membranes, gliders can thicken some areas, slacken others, catching more or less of the heavy air.

They launch not with flaps but with descents. From favored high ledges they simply step off, let their membranes billow, then ride long, measured arcs down-canyon, banking on tiny differences in temperature that bleed from the deepest machines.

Their calls are tuned to the city. Each exhalation is a low-frequency thrum that shakes the old plates, mapping cavities ahead and below. To one another they are bright in that sonar: each glider knows where its neighbors are in the fog by the shared ripples of their overlapping hoots.

Colonies of smaller flier-symbionts cling to their undersides: **lampflakes**, thin wedge-shaped creatures with bioluminescent edges. When a glider passes through especially thick micro-swarms of slowing-air life, the lampflakes flare in metered flashes, driving the particles aside with light-induced avoidance—they have, over ages, learned to weaponize the aversions of beings no bigger than dust.

#### The mote seas

In certain cathedral-like hollows, the air itself is alive enough to be almost visible. This is where you encounter the **mote seas**: populations of microscopic to pinhead-scale filaments, vesicles, and chain-creatures that drift in such density they act as a quasi-fluid.

They are not under any one command. Each is a small, self-assembling unit: a hollow loop of protein-mineral composite with hairlike cilia that beat slowly. In clear air they fall, but together—billions to a volume—they create their own turbulence, their cilia setting up standing waves that support their collective weight.

They feed on aerosolized organics, on ultrafine dusts of shell and film. They divide rarely and die even more rarely, their lifespans overlapping like bricks in a slowly moving wall.

Larger animals avoid the core of a mote sea, where motion is expensive. But some specialists have evolved to exploit the transition zones. **Shear-skaters**, flat-bodied and rimmed with stiff bristles, cruise just inside the edge of the sea, where air thickens. They ride on the drag differential, their bristles catching the slower strata like oars in syrup, while their smoother upper surfaces stay in freer air.

The mote seas themselves slowly migrate along air pressure gradients defined by engine cycles and temperature. Generations of plateau dwellers track their drift as another kind of season.

### Dense life on the hand-scale

Up close, every hand-span of this world is an apartment block.

#### Filament combers

Imagine a spider rendered in translucent rubber, its abdomen compressed to a thin disk, its legs multiplied and elongated into hair-thin threads. That is roughly a **filament comber**.

They live in the narrow seams where metal meets mineral film. Each limb ends not in a claw but in a fine brush, thousands of microscopic hairs tuned to catch particular sizes of particulate and bacterial floc.

They do not roam; they rake. Picking a favored channel—often along the underside of a hanging duct—they stretch out, anchoring several limbs to the surface. The remaining limbs sweep the passing trickle-water or condensed films, combing out nutrients. When a limb’s brush load becomes heavy enough, they fold it to a central mouth, scrape it clean, then extend it again.

Over time, a single individual may inch along its chosen seam, leaving behind a bare, lustrous trail in the film. In their wake other things grow: secondary films enabled by the changed chemistry, micro-hives in the tiny dry breaks.

Predators the size of your fingertip haunt them: **clipbeetles**—little more than two opposing mineral blades on a flexing hinge. A clipbeetle waits motionless in the film ahead, indistinguishable from a calcified droplet, until one combing limb brushes it. Then the hinge snaps, slicing off the limb tip in a fraction of a heartbeat. The comber recoils, retreats, regrows over weeks. The beetle chews leisurely.

#### Capillary eels

Within the thinnest channels where water runs—rills so small they are more concept than stream—wriggle the **capillary eels**: translucent, wire-thin bodies that live perpetually in laminar flow.

They align themselves nose-first into the movement, bodies quivering with minute, standing waves. Along their flanks run segmented mouths—spicules that scrape ions and microfragments out of solution as water passes. They are living ion-exchange resins, drifting wherever their capillary dries or shifts.

When a runnel vanishes, they do not desiccate on the spot. Instead they burrow into the walls of the channel, entering a kind of glassy torpor, their bodies exuding a sheath of hardened mineral. There they wait—years, decades—until seepage resumes nearby. The faint vibration of new flow reaches them through the substrate; they thaw, wriggle free, and join it as though no time has passed.

### Symbiotic composites: bodies as platforms

Over endless slow eons, many creatures here have accepted that “self” is negotiable. Some have become walking settlements.

#### Gardenbacks

Those tram-car-length crawlers you glimpsed with backs mottled into lumpy, shimmering gardens are **gardenbacks**.

They are massive, low-bodied omnivores with flexible mouths hidden under their anterior third. Above that mouth, on the dorsal surface, spreads an expanse of living substrate: a tough, fibrous dermis oozing with nutrients and micropores.

Into this dermis seed a dozen different lineages of film and almost-fungus. Some are light-sensitive (though not truly photosynthetic), regulating their chemistry by day-night cycles. Some specialize in extracting rare metals from mist. Some host bacteria that can crack long-chain organics that seep in from ancient, buried leaks. Over the gardenback’s lifetime the mix changes—patches die out, new films establish from spores caught in tricklewater.

The host does not passively tolerate this; it curates. It has taste organs along its flanks that can sample its own back, analyzing exudates from each patch. Those that produce valuable nutrients, it strokes with soft-tongued grooming limbs, stimulating them, clearing away choking debris. Patches that have gone “rogue”—overparasitic or chemically off—are scoured away with abrasive secretions, leaving raw dermis ready for recolonization.

They are mobile oases in a world where substrate chemistry is destiny. Entire clades of small creatures live nowhere but on gardenbacks: minute grazers that never see the city itself, predators that travel from garden to garden only at breeding time, when adults leave the safety of a host to seek new ones.

#### Choir stacks

Some of the most unsettling composites are immobile: the **choir stacks**.

They begin as clusters of small, tube-bodied filter-feeders colonizing the mouths of old vents where the engines breathe: exhalation slits, heat-bleed grills. Their tubes harden into crystalline chimneys, branching and rebranching as generations cement themselves in place. Over time, the physical vent is entirely encased in a honeycomb of living channels.

The stack feeds on warm, moist outflow. Each exhale of the hidden machines flushes the tubes with nutrient-rich vapor and microscopic debris; in the long stillnesses between they sit, valves closed, slowly digesting.

Why “choir”? Because, to regulate heat and pressure, they resonate.

When the machine beneath prepares to breathe, tiny mechanical changes shift the acoustic character of the vent. The stack senses these with ciliated organs inside its tubes. In response, the chimneys adjust: some open, some constrict, some change their internal geometry by flexing minute muscles. The next exhalation then roars not as a simple rush but as a chord: layered, haunting tones produced as wind through a living organ pipe forest.

Nearby creatures have keyed themselves to these sounds. For some, the third overtone of a given stack’s call is the signal for spawning. For others, slight modulations in the fundamental note warn of increased heat or particulate load. The choir stacks thus turn raw machine rhythm into an intelligible, local clock.

From the perspective of the hidden engines, nothing has changed. They push and bleed as they always have. But the stacks and their dependents have woven culture through the pressure waves.

### That thin stratum that almost remembers

Not everything alive here is visibly mobile. Some things make decisions so slowly you’d mistake them for stone.

#### The warm drips

Those glass-like stalactites and frozen waterfalls, warm to the touch, are more than passive chemistry. They are **drip-forms**: super-slow colonies of mineral-binding entities—bacteria, archaea, and stranger nano-constructs—locked into a cycle of precipitation and rearrangement.

Every droplet that oozes through them deposits or dissolves a little, depending on temperature, composition, and internal signals. Over centuries, layers build, each lamina subtly coded with trace inclusions. They “remember” conditions not as ideas but as structure: a high-sulfide century leaves a sooty shadow; an era of warm, fast drip makes bubbles and stretched vesicles; a long drought is a hair-thin, dense, nearly pure line.

They respond to more than chemistry. The drumming of distant pumps, the constant thrum of a choir stack, the footsteps of plinths—vibrations traveling through walls and ceilings—alter their growth pattern. They grow lobes toward regular vibrations, recesses into quieter directions, forming convoluted shapes that best capture and damp those inputs.

In this way, they have functionally “learned” the plateau’s big cycles. In chambers where the drip-forms are especially mature, their shapes regulate acoustics and water behavior so precisely that they shield what lives beneath from abrupt changes in pulse or pressure. Entire microcultures of animals cluster under these overhangs, living within a kind of stone wisdom.

From time to time, a big collapse will shear through such a formation, exposing its interior laminations in cross-section. Grazers and symbionts flock to the fresh surface, both to feed and to “read” the released chemistries—a sudden outburst of ancient trace signals that may, to them, shape choices for decades to come.

### The slow communicators

In a world where the fast is individual and the slow is structural, memory and message pass through unusual channels.

#### Column drummers

Among the middle-sized, semi-social creatures are the **column drummers**: squat, many-fingered beings that live around known resonance nodes.

They resemble, in outline, star-shaped sacks perched at the bases of long, vertical struts. Each “arm” ends in a broad, calloused pad. Their bodies are dense with mechanoreceptors and simple frequency-discriminating organs—ears that hear through bone.

They do two things. Locally, they keep the struts clean, grazing films and scraping off accumulations that might detune the column. Globally, they send messages.

A drummer has a limited vocabulary, inherited from its lineage: sequences of stomps that set up distinct vibrations running up and down the column. There are patterns for “richness increasing” (good film seasons), for “heat wave coming” (some engine cycling into a new mode), for “break forming” (structural danger above). These codes were not invented all at once; they accreted. Over generations, random drummings that happened to coincide with notable changes were “interpreted” by other creatures as signals and reinforced.

Once every so often—on a scale of hundreds of their lifespans—another drummer far along the same structural network feels a familiar pattern in the baseline hum. Perhaps the resource situation that prompted the original drumming has long since passed. Still, it responds, integrating the long-delayed “news” into its local culture. For this reason, there exist clades of small creatures who will, without any immediately sensible reason, begin to migrate or alter breeding cycles based on a message launched centuries ago by some half-forgotten environmental perturbation.

To them, this lag is normal. The plateau’s time is thick.

### On sentience, or something similar

Do any of them think as you do—symbolically, self-aware, with a notion of story? Not as you would easily recognize. Yet there are gradations.

Some of the mid-sized omnivores—vein herders, certain resonance hunters—have nervous systems complex enough to hold models of their surroundings, to plan, to weigh delayed reward against present safety. They navigate three-dimensional mazes of corridors, remember the rhythmic quirks of specific machinery, anticipate the sound of a choir stack’s next exhale.

Above them, at the scale of plinths and gardenbacks, “mind” extends into symbionts. A plinth’s decision to reroute its journey around a slowly failing zone is not made in an abstract cortex. It emerges from layered inputs: shifts in tremor signatures recognized by resonant commensals in its legs; changes in film chemistry on its flanks relayed by sensitive grazers; the timing of engine pulses interpreted by a dozen different internal colonies. It acts as an integrating center, but the computation is distributed over dozens of constituent species.

And then there is something harder to name: the almost-intent of the plateau itself.

Consider: the still-turning pumps that keep water flowing uphill long after any designer is gone; the drip-forms that bend growth in response to those pumps’ cycles; the choir stacks that tune exhalations; the column worms that keep voids from clogging; the motes that regulate air viscosity; the plinths that spread load and test spans. None of these “wills” anything together. Yet their interactions produce a kind of persistent meta-stability. Failures that would otherwise cascade are often caught, redirected, softened into subsidence rather than catastrophic fall.

Call it emergent agency if you like. The plateau behaves as if it desires to keep being what it is: high, wet, warm, alive-without-leaves. Not possessed of a single mind, but of countless feedback loops that all reward the continuation of a certain range of conditions.

If something foreign were to arrive—a sharp-minded outsider with tools and haste—it would find that changing this world permanently is like carving water. Cuts close around intrusions; disruptions are absorbed into the next layer of balance. The engines will keep turning. The films will keep eating. The animals will keep moving in their patient crowds, filling every pocket.

### At the edges

At the outermost lips of the plateau, where terraces break off into the unseen below, life grows sparse and strange.

Wind, here, has somewhere to go. It rises in slow, persistent updrafts from the lower world, carrying new chemistries, spores, dust from places the plateau has not “tasted” in epochs. A few lineages specialize in this interface.

#### Rim catchers

Clinging to the brink are **rim catchers**: slender, arching bodies anchored deep into the crust, projecting filament-jaws and fine nets out into the open void.

They are patient. They let updrafts comb through their nets, catching whatever the rest of the plateau cannot: pollen-like grains from distant vegetations, ice crystals from cooler, lower weather, fragments of insect or leaf. Very little arrives, but each particle is a treasure of new molecules.

They do not eat all of it. Some they incorporate into gemlike nodules within their bodies: dense balls of foreign chemistry shielded from the plateau’s homogenizing seep. These nodules are tenders of difference, seeds held against the remote possibility that variation may become useful.

Once in a great while, when one rim catcher dies and its anchoring fails, the body tears away and falls, trailing its nets and embedded nodules down into the unseen. If anything lives below, it receives these rare, dense packages as meteors of strangeness from above.

#### The falling herds

On even longer timescales, sections of the plateau’s outer skin give way, as you’ve noticed: whole zones calving off in ponderous slide-falls. Many creatures flee long before; others stay, whether by ignorance, inability, or choice.

Some clades have, perversely, come to depend on such events. The **fallers** are small, dense-bodied animals whose life strategy is to ride collapsing shelves down as far as they can, harvesting the brief flush of energy released by grinding, crushing, and sudden exposure of new surfaces.

In the last days before a great section begins to move, fallers congregate along hairline cracks, feeding on the intense microbiological bloom that always precedes big failures. When the zone tips, they curl into armored knots, letting the world turn end over end. Many die; some wedge in crevices, survive the descent, and uncurl thousands of meters lower, inheritors of a fresh chaos of rubble and dripping fragments.

Their lives are short compared to most plateau dwellers—measured in seasons, not centuries. To them, the world is violent and fleeting. They carry memory through generation, not through growth; the location of reliable fall zones is coded in instinct and simple scent preferences. They are the plateau’s emissaries to below, albeit unknowing.

### How it feels to stand among them

If you could be there long enough to tune out the first shock of density, you would begin to notice the pattern in the crowding.

You’d see the bracket grazers like pale punctuation marks along every damp seam; the filament combers writing and erasing micro-scripts across films; the shapes of plinths far off, continents on the move. You’d hear the organ-notes of choir stacks and, beneath them, the beating heart of those unseen pumps that keep hauling oceans’ worth of water uphill drizzle by drizzle.

You would be watched, of course. Resonance hunters would test your weight in the plates, decide you are too heavy, too unpredictable, and keep their doors shut. Vein herders would divert slightly to avoid the new warmth of your body. A gardenback, if you stood still long enough under its garden’s drip, might grow a new patch of film tinted with the salts of your sweat.

Nothing here is interested in you as an individual drama. But everything, in its patient, adapted way, would test whether you can be metabolized—chemically, structurally, informationally—into the plateau’s long equilibrium.

Given time, you would be.

Your steps would leave faint compaction patterns in the films; those would shape where condensation beads, which would influence where bracket grazers settle. The trace metals your skin and tools shed would be taken up by films, then by their grazers, then by gardenbacks, then by plinths. Perhaps, centuries after you vanished, a column drummer’s descendants would still be tapping out a patterned echo of some vibration you caused, not knowing what it once meant.

In that sense, you, too, would come to live here—for a while—as a slight, particular inflection in the endless, breathing crust of this high, warm, wet, leafless city-plateau that does not quite sleep and does not quite wake, but continues.
