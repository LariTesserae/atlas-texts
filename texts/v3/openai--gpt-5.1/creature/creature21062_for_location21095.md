They live in layers, as the place does.

Start with their bodies, because those are the most negotiated things here: bargains struck between stone, air, dust, and the hard minimum of fluid they can afford to carry.

They are not tall. The space they inhabit is vertical, but it is crowded, and a tall frame would be a liability on the near-vertical stairs and hanging ladders. Most adults are compact, their proportions tending toward the sturdy and close-knit: long torsos, strong hips and thighs, arms that are a touch too long by “human” standards, with thickened forearms and wrists. Children are all limb and eye, but they grow inward rather than upward, packing density into bone and tendon, never wasting height that cannot be braced, balanced, or used as leverage.

Their skin is not one color, but all are some muted fraction of stone. The spectrum runs from dust-pale through ochre and umber to a deep, old-blood brown. Whatever their original ancestry might once have been, generations in this slow, smoky dusk have converged them toward tones that do not glare against the rock. Sunlight is a story; they do not need melanin for it. Instead, their skin has another property: a faint, dry sheen when seen up close, as if a very thin layer of cured resin has been laid over it. It helps with moisture retention. If you brushed your fingers along a bare forearm, you would feel almost no give of sweat—just warm, matte dryness.

Hair persists, but it is functional more than ornamental. On the head, it tends to be kept short, braided into tight cords, or shaved entirely. Long, loose hair catches dust and spores; it is something babies might have, and old story figures, but working people keep it bound. Body hair is scant on limbs and torso, but grows in notable bands where the air is breathed and filtered. Their brows are thick; their lashes dense. Fine hairs rim the nostrils, fringe the outer edges of their ears, trace faint, symmetrical arcs along the jawline. These hairs move with each breath, catching particulates before they reach deeper.

Look at their faces, and you see the adaptation most clearly. The bones of their cheeks are wide and high, as if the skull has grown out to make room for expanded sinuses. The bridge of the nose is strong; the nostrils are narrow slits they can flare or pinch almost shut. Many wear light masks—a web of fine, layered fibers and dried fungal felt—that rest just over nose and mouth, held by cords looping behind the head. They are not opaque; they do not silence speech. They simply add one more level of filtration, one more chance for dust and drifting spores to be trapped before they reach vulnerable tissues.

Their eyes are large. In the firelit, fungus-lit dusk, seeing is never effortless, and so the iris has given ground to pupil. Most eyes here are deep, nearly black discs, with only a thin ring of muted color—gray, rust, amber—around the edge. The pupils seem always a bit too wide. If you watch carefully, you will notice that they do something odd: in addition to narrowing and widening, they sometimes constrict in a subtle cross-shaped pattern, four short spokes closing toward the center. That is a reflex against sudden local flares of light—resin fires, bioluminescent flares from disturbed growths—that might otherwise dazzle them. To someone from outside, these eyes would look strange: over-attentive, their pupils flexing in small, almost conversational ways as the light shifts.

Their hearing is fine, as it must be. Ears lie close to the head, but the outer surface is carved by delicate folds that, like the textured walls of their home, subtly channel and distinguish directions of sound. Many individuals ornament the cartilage not with metal, which is rare, but with tiny beads of polished bone or compressed fungal resin, worked thin, each etched with familial markings. These decorations are flat, more like scales than rings, so they will not snag on climbing bands or root-ladders.

Hands and feet are tools as much as limbs. The hands are broad, fingers a little shorter and thicker than you might expect, but with prominent pads at the tips. The nails are toughened and used as much as simple blades as for fine manipulation. In many, you can see callused ridges along the sides of the index and middle fingers, where rope has burned and stone has abraded over a lifetime. Their grip is tireless.

Their feet are more interesting still. Centuries of climbing have broadened them. Toes splay a bit wider than in other humans, not so much prehensile as pre-bracing: able to wedge into carved footholds, grip the roughness of woody trunks. The soles are thick but not deadened; sensitivity remains so they can feel the micro-textures carved into path-markings. Children learn early to climb by foot as much as hand. One of the first games they play involves navigating a low, rough wall using only their toes and the patterned ridges cut into it, while their hands are occupied carrying a fragile object.

They do carry water inside them—blood cannot be otherwise—but they have made peace with keeping it there. Sweat is rare; when they overheat, they move into shade, rest against cool stone, breathe differently. Their kidneys are overdeveloped by outside standards; their urine is thick, almost syrupy, heavy with reclaimed salts and excreted resinous byproducts. Waste is precious information: its color, its smell, its residue tell healers how a body balances its meager fluids. Infants’ first wastes are watched with the attention outsiders might give to first words.

Their clothing moves across a narrow palette of purpose. Against the ever-present dust, most wear layered wraps of woven root-fiber and treated felt made from fungal growths. These fabrics are thick but surprisingly light, crisp rather than soft, crackling faintly when flexed. Tunics are sleeveless or short-sleeved to leave arms free but cover the torso where organs lie; leggings are close-fitting, cross-bound at knees and ankles for support on ladders. Over this they add harnesses: bands over shoulders and across the chest and waist that take the strain of carried loads, hooks and loops for hanging tools, pouches for small goods.

Colors are earth and dusk: smoky browns, dried-blood reds, dull ember-oranges, the pale bone-gray of certain mats. Bright pigment is expensive—distilled from rare growths or animal secretions—and is used sparingly as sign rather than fashion: stripes on a leader’s shoulders, a dot between the brows of someone in ritual office, a band of color marking a particular guild.

They do not go barefoot often, not because the ground is harsh—though in places it is—but because the fungal felt that carpets much of the floor is alive and slow. Constant direct contact, the oils of skin, the wear of nail and callus, would damage it. So they make sandals and soft, high-wrapped shoes from treated hides and thick woven mats, the soles patterned to distribute weight and minimize harm. Only children, and then only in designated play-areas, run bare-footed, learning the feel of substrate and stone on their skin before they are taught the taboo of careless tread.

They move with three habitual orientations: floor-ward, wall-ward, and air-ward. No path is simply “straight.” When they step out of a doorway, the head turns not just left and right, but up and down. Balance is not an afterthought but something woven into posture. Muscles around their ankles and hips are always making micro-adjustments; their center of gravity is low and fluid. Watching a group of them navigate a steep set of carved steps with nothing but a faint fungus glow for light, baskets slung across their backs, one hand holding a child or a tool, you would see very little wasted motion. They place the foot; they test; they commit. Hesitation elsewhere might be caution; here, it can kill. Decision must come in the same breath as care.

They are not, as a whole, particularly young or particularly old. The constraints of the basin do not allow for great, ever-swelling numbers. There is a visible balance: enough children that you hear their voices on the balconies and market floor, but not so many that they swarm. Old age exists; elders with deeply lined faces and slow gaits sit on favored ledges, watching; but the truly ancient are rare and, when they appear, are almost mythic. Many die not of infirmity but of slow accidents: a misjudged climb, a sudden weakness when crossing a swinging bridge, a rare fall of old stone. Death is not a stranger, but it is not an everyday spectacle. It is a quiet subtraction, a new layer in the undergrowth.

They call themselves a word that, in their tongue, means something like “those who hold fast” or “the clingers,” a name as much about behavioral stance as about physical grip. To translate too literally—“Ledge-Folk,” “Holdfast People”—would miss the sense of patience and endurance in it. In their stories, the name was taken, not given: an ancestral group who chose the hollow and refused to leave, even as the waters dried and the outer world went thin. Another story says the name came from the plants themselves, from a pattern of root-growth that looks, when seen in cross-section, like four fingers spread and pressing into stone.

Their society is as layered as their home, but less rigid. There are lines, but they are drawn along functions rather than castes.

Near the basin floor live many of the traders, craftspeople, and those who tend communal functions. This is not poverty or privilege, but proximity: the market, the hearths, the storerooms, the grafting troughs. To have a home whose doorway opens almost onto the bustle of the market platform is to always be in the thick of things: good for trade, bad for privacy. Those who work in sound and communication—the singers who keep the mnemonic chants, the speakers who arbitrate disputes—often dwell here, where their presence is required.

Higher up, along the mid-level ledges and in the bony growth of houses that cling there, live families whose work is more dispersed: harvesters, climbers who prune and shape the woody trunks; carvers who maintain and extend the ladders; tenders of the wall-mats; keepers of the gliding creatures’ nesting-routes. Their homes open onto rope bridges and hand-carved tunnels that lead both inward and outward, up to lesser-used routes. The mid-level people know the basin as a web rather than a hub.

Higher still, near the places where the stone vault presses low and the flying creatures roost, live those whose duties require them to be at the edge of the known: watchers at the sealed exits, keepers of the highest growths, hunters who study and, when necessary, cull the heavy-bodied predators of the mid-gloom. It is not exactly an elite—or if it once was, time has softened that into myth. Living there is both hardship and strange privilege. The air is thinner of dust, the quiet deeper, but falls from that height are seldom survivable. For children of those households, play includes learning the hum-patterns of the four-winged fliers, practicing the calls that startle them without stampeding them into dangerous clouds.

Within all levels, work-possession is more important than lineage. Their units of belonging are not nuclear families alone, but “rings”: small, flexible groups centered around a particular long-term task. There is a ring that tends the resin-hearths, its members spread across several ledges; another that oversees the fungal felt, measuring its thickness and resilience; another that charts, in painstaking braids of knotted cord, the slow cycles of growth and gas and the timing of ceremonial prunings. You are born into a ring—your parents belong to one or two—and as you grow, you either stay, marry or bond into another, or apprentice to a function that catches you.

These rings overlap, interlock, and knot the community together. At gatherings, people identify themselves first by ring, then by immediate kin. Names come third. A typical self-introduction before a trade or agreement might translate as, “I tend the north mats and climb with the Silent Roots; my mother burns resin, my co-parent shapes ladders; I am called Hanu.” Rings police their own knowledge: how deep to cut, how long to leave a mat fallow, which growths are safe to burn, how far to push a root into a crack before it will no longer support a house-beam. This knowledge is shared, but slowly, cautiously; an error can unbalance an entire cycle.

They speak in a rough, low-carrying tongue, evolved for echo and privacy in a dense acoustic environment. The consonants sit thick at the back of the throat and palate; the vowels are short, clipped. Long, carrying notes—sung or hummed—are reserved for ritual or for long-distance signaling. Much everyday meaning is folded into tone and rhythm rather than raw lexicon. The difference between a command and an invitation may be only a little downward dip in the voice at the end of a phrase, or the substitution of one glottal catch for another.

Because light is never plentiful, they gesture widely, but not wildly. Hands are part of speech, shaping it: tapping patterns into stone or beam as they talk, tracing spirals or straight lines that modify the spoken words. A trader’s fingers drumming twice on a market stone might mean “this year’s growth” as opposed to last year’s. Drawing a fingertip across the palm from thumb to little finger while naming a trunk tells the listener that you mean the outer rings only, not the core. Children learn these gestural modifiers early, and outsiders—if any came—would miss half the conversation without them.

They also speak with the environment. Surfaces all over the basin are inscribed with low-relief patterns: swirls, cross-hatchings, nested chevrons. These are not simply art; they are maps and texts. To run your hand along a wall while walking a corridor is to be reminded: three long ridges, then a gap, then a square of roughness—that pattern means, “two ledges above market, approaching drop; slow your steps.” On a house-threshold, a carved spiral might encode the ring affiliation of the inhabitants, their primary work, and a record of major changes (births, deaths, bondings, splittings). These carvings are meant to be read under fingertips, not eyes.

They remember with song. In a world where paper never existed and carvings take labor, the most flexible storage medium is lungs and throat. Their ritual songs are not primarily about gods or heroes, though such figures appear; they are about the place’s slow pulses. One long chant, taking most of an evening, recounts in sequence the last seventy-two cycles of the wall-mats’ thickening and thinning, keyed to a pattern of claps and steps that mimic the flow of unseen gases through cracks in the rock. Another song, taught to children as a game, encodes the safe and unsafe animals: each verse imitating a sound, followed by the children naming what it belongs to and what you must do if you hear it close.

Religion—or whatever fulfills that function—is not centered on an external, intervening sky-dweller. They have not seen a sky in so long that the concept, while present in myth, does not carry awe so much as a kind of speculative melancholy. Their reverence is more geological and metabolic. They speak of the Warmth Beneath as a kind of impersonal ancestor: the deep stone heat that never quite leaves, that makes this place possible. They speak also of the Exchange: the unending cycle by which bodies and growths and shed skins and burnt ash become each other.

Their rituals mostly mark points in larger rhythms. When certain woody trunks darken at the edges of their rings, when the hum of a specific growth grows louder or shifts pitch, when a particular flying species begins its moulting chorus, the people hold the Rite of Thinning. Sections of overgrown mats and overcrowded fronds are cut away in a carefully choreographed practice: some to be burned as fuel; some to be dried and crushed into nutrient powders; some to be laid in designated decay pits where specialized fungus will break them down into forms that can be re-fed into grafting troughs. Participants daub their foreheads and hands with ash from older rites, then wash it off with a precious splash of aromatic resin after the work is done. The ash and resin together are collected for future use—a closed loop in miniature, a reassurance that nothing is lost forever.

They have few formal taboos, but those they have are absolute. The one that comes closest to moral law is about waste. To pour out drinkable liquid onto bare stone is not an act of carelessness; it is an accusation. Only the very gravest wrongs are expiated that way. On rare, solemn occasions, you might see someone convicted of sabotage of the Grove or a gross violation of harvest rules kneel on the market platform, holding a skin of stored liquid. Before the eyes of all, they must open it and let it flow onto the rock, watching it vanish. It is a kind of social suicide: declaring that they have broken the Exchange’s rules so badly that they must themselves be cut from the cycle. Afterwards, they cannot live here; if they are not killed, they are taken to the sealed upper ways and sent outward, carrying an exiled share of the hollow’s precious moisture into the unknown.

They tell stories of the outside, but these are as much cautionary as curious. In some tales, “above” is a place of raging change—storms of grit, violent shifts of temperature—that would tear apart their carefully balanced ecosystem. In others, it is an empty sandstone bowl, frozen in bright glare, in which nothing grows and sound goes nowhere. A few quieter stories imagine distant hollows like theirs, connected by thin, dangerous tunnels, each with its own people and growths. In the best of these, a wandering exile from their basin finds such a place and is taken in, bringing with them a fragment of a song that matches an old, half-forgotten motif in the new hollow’s lore, proving that once the inward worlds were not so isolated.

Whether such other basins exist or not, the idea of them lives in the play of children. They tie knots of rope and dangle them from balconies, each knot a “world,” each segment of rope between knots a “tunnel.” They send small gliders—made from treated leaves and fronds—sailing from ledge to ledge, naming them after imaginary hollows. A successful crossing is a story about travel; a failed one, a miniature disaster, mourned with mock-solemnity and repurposed as material for the next attempt.

Their work with animals mirrors their work with plants: not dominance, but entanglement.

The small, many-legged shell-creatures at the basin floor are not precisely domesticated, but they are managed and culled. People encourage them where their foraging cleans detritus from important walkways or decay-pits, drive them away from grafting troughs and sleeping-places. Children learn to flip a hand-axe and rap it twice on the stone to startle them from a particular path into a safer crevice. Occasionally they are climbed back out of those crevices to be harvested: cracked open, roasted, their mineral-rich tissues ground as seasoning or paste.

The furred gliders share a more intimate relationship. They thread the same three-dimensional routes the people do, darting up past heads on a rope bridge, skimming low over the market to steal a shred of dried paste. Some rings have learned to trade with them: leaving small bits of food on specific perches, always in the same pattern. In return, the gliders nest there, their droppings and shed bristles collected as fine fertilizer or fibers; they also serve as early warnings. If gliders are avoiding a particular ledge, something is wrong there: predators, or a shift in the load of stone or growth that has made the structure subtly unsafe. Certain children find they can mimic glider calls so well that flocks will follow them; these youngsters are prized by the watchers and may be drawn into rings that monitor the mid-level predators.

The four-winged fliers are more remote. They are never kept, but always watched. Their breeding choruses and moultings provide major time-markers. Their vibrant hums also respond to sound in complex ways: a sustained note at a certain pitch will cause them to shift roosts; a sharp, triple beat of drum on stone will startle them into flight. The people exploit this. There are established “hum-calls” used only for emergencies: deep, throat-sung notes that, when repeated by enough voices on enough ledges, set the fliers trembling and shifting in great curtains. The resulting flutter and wing-roar can mask other sounds—useful, for instance, when a hunting group wishes to approach a mid-gloom predator without it hearing the scrape of their tools.

Those predators—slow, patient, heavy—are both danger and resource. They thin the gliders and shell-creatures; they trim any population that grows too bold. But when one starts to take too many people—ambushing a climber too often on a certain route, dragging bodies from shallow shelters on the same level—it becomes marked for culling. This is a serious undertaking, involving coordination across rings: harvesters to study its habits; stone-workers to maybe reshape a path as a funnel or trap; singers to plan distraction-hums for the fliers; hunters themselves, armed with carefully hardened and serrated bone spears tipped in fat-soluble concentrates derived from certain bitter growths. If they are successful, the predator is brought—slowly, piece by piece—to the market level, where its hide, bones, and meat are distributed according to older-than-memory rules. The kill holds a quasi-ritual weight: an acknowledgement that while they live intertwined with their non-human neighbors, there are moments when they must insist on a boundary.

They are not a violent people, but neither are they gentle in the sentimental sense. The place has no room for endless growth or unchecked impulse. Children are disciplined early to move carefully, to respect others’ holds on rock and resource. Quarrels arise, of course—over access to a particularly rich mat, the rights to a newly discovered pocket of useful mineral, accusations of taking more than one’s share from a common pit. But there are strong incentives to resolve them with speed and minimal breakage. Broken fingers mean a climber cannot carry; a cut mat cannot regrow quickly.

They have a practice that substitutes for blood-feuding: the Slip. Two parties in serious conflict present themselves at a mid-level ledge chosen by elders, its outer edge unwalled, a drop beneath leading not to certain death but to a deep, unpleasant fall into the Grove’s thickest tangle of growth and detritus. They stand side by side, harnesses lashed to a single safety-root. At a signal, each must try—without striking blows—to unbalance the other with movement alone: shifts of weight, feints, shoulder-checks. The one who steps back first, closer to the drop, “loses” and must undertake a period of low-status work in service of the other’s ring. The act is witnessed by many; the danger concentrates the mind. It is rare for anyone actually to fall—those who do are pulled up, bruised and humiliated, but alive. The memory of that near-drop tends to cool tempers.

Their understanding of time is neither linear nor cyclical in any simple way. It is stratified. The short layers are daily: the pulses of market, hearth, chores, sleep. The medium layers are the cycles of growth: when to graft, when to prune, when to burn. The long layers are carved into stone and song: the tales of when water still whispered in the old, dry channels; when a section of the vault cracked and dropped, killing dozens and changing all future plans for routes and dwellings; when a great, slow sickness crept through the Grove, hollowing trunks from within until a new strain of fungus adapted and countered it.

They glance upward when they speak of those long layers, even though there is nothing to see but the dim arch of stone. They speak as if the past is physically above them in the rock, pressing downward with its accumulated weight, while the future lies somewhere beneath, in the deeper warmth, yet to emerge. Their metaphors of aspiration and despair both point “inward”: to go “high” is to be closer to the old things; to go “deep” is to risk untested change.

From the mid-level balcony, watching them, you see how this sense of layered time lives in ordinary acts. A pair of adolescents on a narrow bridge pause in their banter to touch, in quick, unthinking sequence, a series of carved grooves in the arch they pass under—a rote acknowledgement of some event recorded there generations ago. An elder tending a grafting trough pauses before pressing a root-knot into a crack; they murmur one line of one of the long chants under their breath, matching root to remembered pattern of gas-flow and older growth. A child, fetching a bundle of dried fronds, stops to run their fingertips along the polished indentations of ladder rungs: the ghost-grips of those who climbed before, a tactile reading of unspoken history.

They dream, individually, as all humans do; and collectively, they dream of more subtle expansions than conquest. A young carver might sketch, in charcoal on their house-wall, the outline of a new ledge bridging a gap that has always been empty, imagining how it would change traffic and sound. A ring of growth-tenders might experiment with grafting a mat-shred into an untried type of rock in a deep corner, wondering if a new kind of felt will take. A storyteller invents a tale of a hollow where the Warmth Beneath pulses more strongly, where growth cycles are faster and life can afford to be a little more wasteful. These are small transgressions against their world’s slowness, permitted and even encouraged in youth, then tempered by the demands of real maintenance.

Now and then, someone pushes further.

There are a few who cannot accept the sealed exits as mere boundaries of prudence. They study the old stories more closely, hunt in the highest reaches for any hint of fresh air, any sign that the beyond is not entirely dead. They cultivate gliders that are more adventurous, train fliers to carry tiny, resin-coated markers that might survive brief excursions into harsher zones. They are not revolutionaries, exactly; their rebellions are private and quiet. Older members of their rings shake their heads but do not always interfere. After all, someone must, every few generations, test the assumption that everything important is inside. They make their attempts; most return with nothing but coughs and rock dust. A few do not return. Their names shift into a different register: no longer just individuals, but small, cautionary stars in the firmament of song.

For the most part, though, they are at peace with their enclosure. Not in bland contentment, but in a practiced, deliberate acceptance. They have built a civilization appropriate to a world that has narrowed and slowed. They have made economies that do not assume endless growth, ethics that center balance and return, aesthetics that find beauty in the rough feel of a well-used ladder-rung and the soft glow of a fungus lamp tended for three generations.

They live in that basin like a nested organ: not separate from the stone and growth, but continuous with it. Their breaths feed the mats; the mats’ exhalations feed them. Their dead become part of the felt beneath the Grove, their bones ground to dust to fill small cracks where new roots may take. Their stories wrap around the great trunks and old stairs, making them more themselves.

If you could step in unseen and stand on one of those mid-level balconies long enough, the initial alienness would wear off. The filtered light, the musk, the omnipresent dust would settle into background. You would begin to hear distinctions in the hum of the fliers, to recognize the difference between the crackle of foraging shell-creatures and the cautious scrape of a climber’s foot on a rarely used route. You would see how a tilt of the head means deference here, challenge there; how a touch on the wall beside a doorway signals arrival in place of a knock.

And you would come to understand that what lives here is not just “a people” but a particular answer to a particular question: How does mind behave when the world has grown old, dry, and small, but remains warm enough, rich enough, to sustain intricate life—if that life accepts that nothing comes from outside anymore, and so everything that exists must circulate, deepen, and be held fast?
