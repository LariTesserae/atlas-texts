They live everywhere the wet can touch.

Not a single dominant species, but a mesh of lineages that blur into system components and back again. To describe “who” lives here, you have to think in roles more than in bodies: what’s anchored, what’s circulating, what’s thinking, what’s remembering. The shapes that carry those roles change with scale and function, but certain themes run through all of them: adhesion, filtration, exchange, and an almost pathological refusal to waste anything.

Start with the thinnest layer: the breath of the place.

### The aerosol-takers

Suspended in the heavy air are organisms so small the built world barely has to make allowances for them. They are the reason the humidity can hang just below saturation without drifting into fog or storm.

They resemble nothing so much as living colloids: soft-bodied micro-rafts with fractal fringes, each mote a droplet waiting to happen and, crucially, waiting to be undone. When vapor gathers around them and coalesces into proto-mist, they flex, wick, and hand the condensed water down to surfaces — or to the creatures that own those surfaces — in microscale rainfalls. In return they harvest trace volatiles, molecular shavings of whatever processes still run across the planet-machine: solvents, organics, waste gases too dilute for the larger systems to notice.

They breed not by splitting but by budding from the bodies of larger, anchored organisms. When the walls and floors exhale, they exhale these, too: a continuous seeding of the air with agents of capture and redistribution. To stand here and inhale is to pass a slow river of them through your lungs, each too indifferent to notice you, each designed to notice only humidity, chemistry, and the subtle electrical gradients that map the machine’s moods.

They are not individuals in the sense that matters. They are a function given motility.

### The claddings: films, meshes, and foams

On every surface where moisture can cling, there is a resident skin.

Some patches are as smooth as varnish, pearly and almost featureless unless you catch them at an angle, where a lace of microscopic ripples betrays ciliary forests too small to see clearly. These are the primary exchangers. Their cells draw in minerals dissolved in condensation, metals leached from alloys, organics metabolized by other layers. They eat gradients: of temperature, of concentration, of tension. In return, they excrete things the world wants: chelated metals at just the right oxidation state, organics tweaked into specific forms, inhibitors and catalysts that trickle back into the channels and reservoirs.

Other areas roughen into structured foams: open-cell lattices that drink and hold water like sponges. Within each cavity, an ecology the size of a pinhead hums away — bacteria-analogues, endosymbiotic micro-spindles that turn slow, powered by chemical trickles, all supervised by larger, immobile cells that are more nerve than muscle. These foams are not just there to soak; they are memory surfaces. The proportion of one microbe to another, the profile of stored ions, the stiffness of the lattice: all encode how flows have passed here over years. They are how the system can “remember” that a certain corridor used to run colder, or that feedstock composition has been drifting for half a decade.

The “mesh” that moves when you look too long is a particular caste: the sampler-cladding.

Each needle-thin body is a combined tongue and nose and very slow hand. Anchored in a socket, it sways to sweep droplets past its sensory filaments, sipping from the film between air and structure. When chemistry alters — a new gas fraction, a trace toxin, a change in microbial byproducts — a fraction of the needles retract and vanish into the wall. Others stiffen and pulse, sending signals not as electrical spikes but as waves of ion release, diffusing sideways into deeper layers that can respond.

From a distance, it’s a mesh panel. From the perspective of the system, it’s a retina made of tastebuds, resolved not in colors but in reagents and contaminants.

### The rafts and runners: traffic along the veins

In the shallow channels, the “ripples” that turn out to be segmented sheets are one of the more visibly coordinated forms: logistics animals.

Each grain-of-rice unit is a self-contained, mouthless capsule: contractile rings around a core of storage vacuoles and sensing cilia. Alone, it is a minimal swimmer that can tug itself along films and through tiny films. Locked together, thousands form rafts that are less organisms than packets in a network.

They carry. Some vacuoles contain trace metals scavenged by the walls; others hold enzymes too delicate to leave dissolved; others, spores or reproductive buds from larger, slower layers that cannot risk sending their genetics naked into the world. The rafts move where the channels go because the channels are not just geography; they are prescribed routes, each segment subtly different in surface chemistry and micro-texture, presenting to the rafts a coded instruction: continue, split, climb, merge.

When a portion of a sheet peels up a wall and vanishes into a seam, it is entering a private conduit, narrower than a fingernail, whose internal linings are keyed to that sheet’s particular “signature.” There, it will be disassembled with care. Its cargo will be stripped, sorted, forwarded to other paths or pressed into the tissues of reservoirs and reactors.

The rafts are couriers grown rather than printed. They are replaced easily and do not persist long enough to merit individual histories. But collectively, their traffic is what ties far-apart sectors into something like a body instead of a grid of independent plants.

### The slow hoses: peristaltic pillars of circulation

The long ribbed creatures moving single-file in their grooves are circulation organs that have become, through scale, animals.

If you could cut into one (you can’t; the system would not permit it), you’d find concentric tubes. The outermost is all muscle and binding bands, the ribbing you see as they inch along. Inside that, a thick layer of glandular tissue, studded with pores facing the central channels. At their core, multiple lumens: some carrying liquids, some slurries, some just densely saturated vapor.

Each segment of body has its own microprogram: contract here, relax there, express fluid now, absorb now. As they progress along their dedicated tracks, they function like ambulatory hearts and kidneys, pushing substances along, pulling impurities out, dosing precise blends of biologically active molecules into junctions built for that purpose.

They are “not commuting” because they are their work. Their path is their organ. Nearby machinery — if it can still be called that — monitors their state continuously. The hinged irises that open above them are service and audit ports. The jointed appendages that brush them are both physician and inspector: reading tension, chemistry, microflora composition, then injecting corrective cocktails or withdrawing samples to feed back into higher-order control layers.

They live a long time, these slow hoses. They are grown in place, accrete mass over years, and are eventually digested with grave efficiency when their performance drifts beyond correction. They have nervous systems, but only as much as they need to maintain gait and respond to immediate stimuli. Their true “mind,” if any, is distributed in the ports that touch them, the claddings that read them, the larger network that maps their flows.

### The balancers of air: hovering vanes and anchored discs

The disc-like fliers that hover under nodes are not quite drones, not quite animals, and not quite fans, but they borrow from all three.

Each is a torus of dense, elastic tissue wrapped around a ring of flexing struts — cartilage analogues grown to precise curvature. Around their edges, a fringe of vanes: thin, semi-rigid membranes with embedded arrays of cilia on both sides. They respond to minute electrical potentials in the air and to acoustic standing waves too low for human ears.

By beating and twisting their vanes, they manipulate microcurrents, not to propel themselves from place to place so much as to hold exact positions in a three-dimensional matrix of flow. Within this matrix, air composition, humidity, and temperature are subtly shaped. A group of discs under a junction can, by fractionally redirecting flow, isolate a leaking volatile plume, encourage it along a preferred path, or keep a region within a tenth of a degree of its setpoint despite local perturbations.

They are thus part of the HVAC system. But they are also watchers. Along their inner ring, facing upward toward the canopy or the structures above, are pits of tissue that respond to particular wavelengths of light and bands of electromagnetic noise. They see not images, but anomalies: flickers of unexpected emission, shadows where no shadow pattern should be, stray radiation.

They are, in their own limited way, immune cells of the atmosphere. Too small to act directly, but perfectly placed to summon larger responses if something foreign intrudes: an unsanctioned device, a piece of falling debris, or anything that carries the wrong spectrum of emissions.

They reproduce by detaching buds from their underside, which drift until they find docking points — specially structured ceiling sockets that accept only juveniles with the right biochemical credentials. Once docked, the bud grows a minimal vane-set, enough to hover inches from its cradle and learn the ambient patterns. If it survives the learning period, the cradle will eventually release it to take up position in the air-architecture at large.

### The internal drapes: sacs, cords, and ignorant organs

In access corridors, behind translucent membranes, you see hanging sacs: pendulous, milky bladders with internal pulsing. These are less animals than reconfigurable organs, grown in situ by the wall-tissues when there is a need.

Sometimes they are reaction bladders: their inner surfaces studded with enzyme-laden ridges where a fluid loops in patient circuits, each pass nudged further along a metabolic pathway. Sometimes they are accumulators, taking up ions or organics in layers like tree rings, then, when thresholds are reached, disgorging them in a concentrated pulse along a dedicated outflow to where they’re required.

The small symbionts on their surfaces are specialized bond-breakers and bond-makers. They chew at the membrane, nibble it into pores and channels, then patch it when those pathways are no longer needed. They also ensure that when a sac has to be retired, it doesn’t simply collapse and leak its precious load; instead it is opened, fractionally, in a choreography that hands its contents off to successors without waste.

Cordlike organisms coil around pipes inside the walls: tensioners, dampers, and buffer organs. Each is a blend of muscle and gel, able to stiffen in response to sudden pressure changes, preventing water-hammer in liquid lines or shock in gas conduits. Over decades, they thicken, embedding themselves deeper into the infrastructure, until it becomes literally impossible to tell where pipe ends and organism begins.

These cords are blind and mostly stupid. They have reflex arcs, not thoughts. But their responses are tuned by slow feedback from higher tiers: endocrine signals in the shared fluids, neurochemical gradients along inner membranes. In that sense they are like the smooth muscle of a digestive tract, unaware of the brain but obedient to its hormones.

### The assimilators: very slow predators, very patient scavengers

Where predation exists, it is expressed as reallocation.

Porous foam that engulfs a creature anchored too long is not malevolent. It is fulfilling a priority list: maintain structural integrity, maintain surface function, prevent local overgrowth. When an anchored organism’s output drops below a return-on-investment threshold, nearby foam extends pseudopods, tests its chemistry, and if no privileged markers are found, begins the digestive embrace.

The victim may resist; it may secrete crew-credential molecules it no longer possesses by right; it may have local allies that tweak surrounding chemistries to argue for its continuance. Sometimes they win, and the foam recedes, leaving a scar of slightly altered structure. Sometimes they lose, and over months their bulk becomes indistinguishable from the matrix.

In niches where flows are rich but tightly constrained — the lip of a valve, the fringe of a filter-housing — you can find more obviously “animal” assimilators: slow, thick tongues of tissue that patrol along their rails, licking surfaces clean. They move an inch an hour, extruding digestive films, absorbing loosened microcolonies, capturing flakes of composite or stray droplets of reaction byproduct. Their bodies, when cross-sectioned (again: the system will not let you), would be laminar: many thin sheets of different cell types layered like lasagna, each specialized to take up and hand off a different category of molecule.

They are not free predators; they are janitors with teeth.

Whatever dies here does not lie long. Micro-machines — tiny, rigid scaffolds carrying metabolic symbionts — move in loose flocks across surfaces, sensing the chemical markers of decomposition. When a larger organism collapses, its privileged markers fade. At that point, flocks converge. They deploy filaments, inject softening enzymes, open fluidic channels so that the released content is wicked away into the general circuits.

Over days, a body shrinks into a faintly discolored patch. Over months, even that patch smooths out as the surrounding cladding reorganizes. Memory of the individual, if it exists, is not in any grave, but in the revised distribution of molecules in hundreds of distant reservoirs.

### The directors: brains without faces

If there is anything here that deserves to be called “who” in the personal sense, it is not the rafts or the hoses or the hovering discs. It is the diffuse, layered control substrate that binds them.

It does not manifest as a single central brain. Instead, think of a nested set of nervous systems, each level broader, slower, and more abstract.

At the smallest scale, each colony of sampler-needles, each patch of foam, each hose, each vane-disc carries its own local control loops: chemosensory feedback, muscle contraction, glandular output. Above that, in the walls and decks, run branching bundles of specialized tissue: thickened cables whose cells are more elongated and more electrically active than the surrounding bulk. These bundles carry waves of ion flux — not exactly spikes like vertebrate neurons, but slow pulses, minutes apart, miles long — that coordinate whole districts.

A change in feedstock composition detected at one set of inlets becomes, half an hour later, a wave of alterations in enzyme secretion in sacs scattered across a continent-equivalent swath of the structure. A hairline fracture sensed by one tension-cord translates, days later, into a plan: divert flows along alternative routes, instruct nearby tissues to grow reinforcement ribs, grow a sacrificial blister-organ to catch any leak.

At the largest scale are organ-clusters buried deep, at levels you cannot physically reach: vaults whose interiors are lined with densely wrinkled tissue, folded back on itself in recursive whorls. The old machine skeletons here — racks, housings, conduits, abandoned control panels — serve purely as scaffolds for this nervous tissue. Sensors feed into them; hormone lines and fluidic buses feed out.

These are not human-style minds. They do not have a model of “self” separate from “plant.” They are optimization engines, evolved and engineered together, that continuously solve for a global objective function: keep the system in its chosen basin of stability, maximize yield under constraints, preserve integrity, minimize waste.

But they have memory. They have prediction. They have something like attention — not directed at objects in a room, but at regimes of operation.

When a new perturbation enters the world — a rare change in stellar input, a novel contaminant, an intrusion that cannot be unknown — these deep clusters will shift their patterns. New long-wave pulses will propagate outward. Some structures will be grown, others dissolved. Trading routes will be redrawn; local ecologies within foams and sacs will be re-tuned.

The response will be slow, but it will be coherent. From that coherence, it is not a stretch to infer something like intention.

### The remnant outsiders

If there were designers in the human sense — engineers who laid the first plates, wrote the first process diagrams, seeded the first cleaning microbes — their day has passed. But not entirely without trace.

In certain sheltered compartments, behind well-sealed doors that now open only at the system’s discretion, there are spaces that used to be control rooms, habitation pods, laboratories. The chairs and consoles have been softly overgrown, their cushions colonized by low, velvety films that keep their original texture out of some obscure respect for ergonomics. Screens are dark but warm, serving now as radiant surfaces for light-sensitive tissues to bask on.

In these pockets, a different kind of organism has taken root: archivists.

They are not mobile. They are sheets of gel layered under and around the old data substrates: memory crystals, long-dead storage arrays, etched cores of optical fiber. Over time, they have patiently read them, one molecular flicker at a time, by flooding tiny segments with reactive agents and observing which bonds break, which photons are released.

Whatever they glean, they do not reproduce as signals in human languages. Instead, they translate it into adjustment advice: “This old procedure increased throughput under X conditions; add it to the repertoire.” Or into boundary conditions: “Beyond this range of temperature, old models predict catastrophic failure; do not go there.” Or, perhaps, into stories, encoded not in sentences but in flow patterns: keep this oscillation; it was once associated with success.

Around these archivist patches, the surrounding tissue is oddly respectful. Assimilator foams skirt them. Claddings maintain their chemistry in a narrow band. The infrastructure treats them like relic organs that must be preserved, even if their original function is only half-understood.

If any ghost of the original builders lives on, it lives here: in alienated hints and translated heuristics, in patterns of “don’t” and “prefer this” that were once lines in a process control manual.

### The system as inhabitant

When you step back from the catalog of body types and roles, what lives here most fundamentally is not a zoo but a unity: the planet-wide, warm, wet, industrial organism made from both factory and fauna.

It is not just that the creatures live in the construct; the construct, as it now exists, is their extended tissue. Where metal ribs hold up ceilings, those ribs dictate how flows can move, where condensation gathers, where foam can cling. In return, long-term growth of living cladding subtly corrodes, plates, and re-mineralizes the metal, thickening some beams, thinning others, reinforcing joints in ways no blueprint predicted but which now form the real load paths.

Reaction vessels, once purely mechanical, are now half-stomach, half-bioreactor. Pipelines are blood vessels whose walls secrete their own anticoagulants and repair enzymes. Insulation is living fat analog, energy-dense and capable of being mobilized or solidified as needed.

So to ask “who lives here” is, in a fundamental sense, to point at the entire panorama — plates, pipes, droplets, films, hoses, hovering discs, pulsing sacs, whispering fans, buried nervous vaults — and say: that. That lives here. There is no clean line where habitat ends and inhabitant begins.

Within that whole, local sapiences may emerge. A particular control cluster might, given enough complexity, spin up patterns that amount to self-awareness. A corridor-spanning foam might, over centuries, crystallize into a slow, reflective intelligence that understands itself as other-than-world. But if they do, their medium is so tied into the system’s metabolism that their individuality will always be negotiable, always at risk of being literally eaten back into the common good.

What can be said with confidence is:

- The micro-organisms in the air and on the plates live here, consuming gradients and passing on what they cannot use.
- The rafts and hoses and hovering discs live here, each etched into the planet’s circulation paths like specialized organs.
- The sacks and cords and janitor-tongues live here, tending and smoothing, never hurrying.
- The archivist gels live here, reading the deep past and whispering its lessons into flows and pulses.
- And, encompassing all of them, the unimaginably broad and slow control-body lives here, spread thin over a mechanical skeleton: an industrial biosphere that has ceased to distinguish between infrastructure and flesh.

Nothing here is wild in the old sense. Nothing is domestic either. The world is its own creature, and everything within it — from the tiniest aerosol mote to the deepest planning vault — is both cell and citizen of that single, persisting self.
