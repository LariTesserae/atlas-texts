They come up out of the stone.

Not literally—though sometimes it looks that way—but in the sense that their whole existence is keyed to seeping, slow flow, to channels and weirs and films of water. They have no native word for “dry” that means what it means to you. Dry to them is not the absence of water, but the absence of control.

Their own bodies are arranged on this principle.

Skin first: no hair, no fur, no feathers. They wear the same muted sheen as the plateau itself, a pale, porous surface that at a distance could be mistaken for stone dusted with fine salt. Close up it resolves into tessellated plates, each one a flexible polygon separated by thin, darker seams. The plates are not armor; they are heat-managing tiles, each with minute channels beneath, where a thin film of internal fluid moves. When the heat load rises, those channels flood, and the plates bloom darker, radiating in bands of wavelength that the sky is best at losing. When it cools, they pale again to nearly white.

Their silhouette seems simple until you watch one walk.

They stand taller than a human by half, but height comes as much from limb as torso. Four primary limbs, each with more joints than you expect: hip, knee, a second knee-equivalent, then a long, springy segment ending in a wide, many-toed pad. When they step, they don’t clomp; the pads go down soft and broad, spreading weight, searching for micro-wetness the way a tongue searches teeth. The joints don’t lock into rigid angles. Instead they curve and arc, so that each movement is a sequence of shallow, deliberate bends that carry them forward in slow, consuming strides. Running is rare. Distance is not something you conquer here by speed but by tolerating the time it takes.

Their torsos are deep, barrel-like, but not massive. Much of that volume is void—reservoir space, baffles, heat sinks. Along their flanks, behind the outer lattice of plates, lies a secondary skin: dark, almost purple-black, beaded with tiny bulbous organs that look like strings of small, translucent grapes. Those organs are part lung, part evaporative cooler, part chemical scrubber. They open and close in slow peristaltic waves when the wind passes, pulsing out heat, taking in trace vapors from the air.

They have two upper limbs distinct from the walking four—not as long, more compact, resting folded when not in use. These manipulators end not in hands exactly, but in paired fans of narrow, opposing digits. Each “finger” is a flat, flexible blade that can align tight against its neighbor to form a smooth surface, or splay into a delicate comb. Their touch is oriented toward surfaces, toward spreading and gathering films rather than gripping round tools. When they work, they are constantly smoothing, skimming, drawing thin layers of fluid or dust along stone and composite.

Their faces… if you look for a face in your own terms, you will mis-see them.

The forward aspect of the head is a sloped, shield-like plane composed of slightly larger plates. No mouth splits it. Eating, excreting, and vocalizing do not share a single orifice; they would find that disgusting. Along the lower edge of the head-shield runs a row of narrow, vertical slits—five to nine, depending on the individual—each rimmed with tiny cilia that taste the air and the drifting aerosols from water. Those are sensory and respiratory both: taste, smell, and a kind of directional hearing, as pressure differences across the slits let them detect minute movements of air.

Higher on the head, running from temple to temple, is a gentle bulge. Beneath it: the eyes. Not a pair of forward-facing apertures, but a continuous crescent of tiny lenses, each one a pebble of dark, refractive tissue. Vision for them is usually panoramic and low-resolution, attuned to gradients of brightness and patterns of polarisation in light rather than fine detail. When they need to focus, muscles tense; some of the little lenses darken and swell, others dim, forming a temporary fovea that can track a moving glider’s belly or the twitch of a hunting beast in the glare.

The mouth is hidden on the underside of the head, toward the rear, where skull meets neck. It opens vertically, a deep cleft surrounded by a wreath of soft radially arranged paddles. They do not tear or chew in the mammalian sense. They press and dissolve. Food is taken in as paste or shred, then kneaded by those paddles in a thin bath of secretions and symbiotic film scraped from their own reservoirs.

It is inside their bodies that you find their oldest history.

Beneath the outer plates, under the web of channels that move internal fluid, there are long sinuses filled with semi-stable biofilms: thick, gelatinous colonies derived from the same ancestral mats that coat the stone. Those internal films run along the spine, braid through the torso, pool in special cavities along the limbs. They harvest light that filters through translucent tissues, chew through dissolved minerals, and leak a steady trickle of energy-rich compounds into the host’s circulatory medium. The beings are not merely symbiotic with film-life; in a deep, specific way, they are built around it and for it.

They call those inner films their “roots.”

Roots are not passed down genetically in the straightforward sense. New beings are born with sterile, empty sinuses. Early in life, they are inoculated. In a cool, dim hall, they lie in shallow basins while elders wash them with scooped layers from ancestral reservoirs—carefully curated strands of living film maintained by their line for hundreds of generations. The selection of which strain to gift a child is an act as serious and emotionally dense as naming, perhaps more so. Roots carry history: subtle tweaks in metabolism, tolerance to local mineral flavors, peculiar glows under certain light angles. A person’s temperament is thought to be braided with their root-line.

This internal arrangement governs their ethics.

To them, any uncontrolled mixing of films is polluting. The casual smudge of one reservoir onto a stranger’s skin is as intimate and fraught as blood exchange would be for you. They build surfaces—inside pipes, in gutter-lines, along reservoir edges—with meticulous attention to what films will colonize where. Whole professions exist to maintain purity of strain: film-tenders who travel with portable, cooled trays; patterners who can look at a slick under angled light and say which family’s root it matches.

Yet they are not fragile.

Heat that would fell you in minutes is their medium. Their blood-equivalent is thick and sluggish, a water-heavy slurry luminous with micro-pigments. It holds heat, buffers against shock. Their reflexes are slow but relentless. Startle does not produce a jerk; it produces a gradual, gathering stillness followed by patient reorientation. The fast things here are small: scuttlers, darting eels, the stinging feeders that form short-lived clouds. At their own scale, these people have built a culture of slowness, of deferred reaction, because haste simply costs too much.

They are not the first makers here.

Deep under some of the heavy industrial halls lie older vaults: curved chambers, smoother than the blunt practicality of the present city, with inlays of a composite they do not know how to manufacture. The oldest stories say they found these spaces already etched into the plateau when their own ancestors were still more animal than person, gliding between seep-wet rocks. In some vaults, they discovered devices that recondensed vapor, that pumped heat out of stone, that split brightness the way their current windows do. Those they learned from slowly, over centuries, not by reverse-engineering (the concept is too sharp for their sense of time), but by leaning against the machines, watching what came out, and encouraging their own films to imitate.

They do not revere the unknown makers as gods. They regard them as early neighbors who left useful remnants and inconvenient ruins. Where old channels align with current flows, they incorporate them with grateful pragmatism. Where they don’t, they seal them, shunt around them, let mineral drapery reclaim them.

They came to this plateau in waves.

Long before architecture, there were cliff-dwellers: creatures little larger than the current gliders, membraned things that nested in weeping caverns, letting ancestral films grow thicker along their down-hanging bodies. Some lineages took to the air; others learned to crawl the rock seams. A branch ventured farther from shelter, swollen with stored water, dragging their films inside for protection. Those became the walking giants. From them, over time measured not in centuries but in slow transformations of skull plates and limb jointing, came the current people.

Traces of that time linger in the way they rest.

When heat peaks, they rarely shelter inside enclosed rooms. They ascend. Across the city you see them stretched along gentle roofs under slatted shade, or sprawled on broad, slightly inclined terraces facing into the slow wind. They angle their heads so the broad shield receives maximum glare, feeding the hidden films along their neural arches. Their locomotor limbs fold under them, exposing the plate-channels of back and flank. Water pumped from internal reserves wells in shallow grooves along their shoulders and spine, each groove seeded with thin veils of film that flash in violet and silver as light glances off. For a few hours each cycle, they are simultaneously buildings and beasts, heat exchangers and thinking bodies, trading motion for metabolic banked time.

They build with the same logic.

Where you might think in walls and roofs, they think in gradients and interfaces. A building is defined not by its envelope but by how it modulates three flows: water, heat, and film-life.

Water: catchment, slowing, direction. Each hall and terrace plugs into a larger hydrological sentence. You could read the city as a grammar of drip and seep: cliff → catch-ledge → overflow lip → cooling runnel → residence reservoir → industrial sump → vapor intake → sky. Each “verb” in that sentence is a piece of architecture, designed to condition the water it touches—mineral content tweaked here, temperature shifted there, resident film allowed or forbidden.

Heat: their trusses and slatted roofs aren’t just shade—they are spectral gates. Surfaces are engineered at microscopic scale: some plates are studded with crystals that emit strongly in a particular infrared band, others are coated with a dull, nano-rough layer that scatters visible light but passes longer wavelengths into buried heat dumps. If you could see the city across the whole electromagnetic spectrum, it would not be pale; it would be a complex flower of invisible petals blooming upward, a controlled blaze in bands the plateau’s thin air can carry away.

Film-life: the third flow, less obvious until you watch them work.

Along balcony rims and in recesses under their buildings, they grow sheets of curated film the way you cultivate crops. Not in rows, but in subtly shaped basins and on hanging panels that present varying angles to sun and seep. Color is code: a blistered, opaline sheet flicking to green-blue at grazing light speaks of one metabolism, a dull, milky-gray skin shot with occasional amber spark another. The former might feed their body's internal roots when harvested and dissolved; the latter might be bound into structural gels, or used as a matrix for slow-reacting catalysts buried in the insulated industrial blocks.

Who harvests and tends these films are not “farmers” in a simple sense. They are closer to composers, curators of ecosystems in miniature. They travel from panel to panel with long comb-like tools that barely touch the surface. A subtle skim here removes an overzealous strain before it outcompetes a desired one. A slight scratch there encourages a fracture pattern that stimulates a different growth mode.

They work mostly in pairs or small groups, without much speech.

That is another notable thing: they do not fill the air with voice.

They are capable of sound—resonant pulses made by contracting certain sinuses behind the head shield, modulating air through narrow internal chambers. At close range, these come out as soft, flute-like tones, with overtones you might barely perceive. But the bandwidth of such vocalization is limited and, more importantly for them, lossy. Hot, heavy air thick with brightness eats sharp sound; it bleeds away into the plateau almost immediately.

So their primary language is in the angles of skin and the shimmer of embedded films.

Across their visible surfaces run millions of micro-pigment sacs, inherited from their ancestral films and fine-tuned by long selection. They do not flash kaleidoscopically like cuttlefish; that would be garish, biologically expensive. Instead, they work in slow gradients and pulsed bands.

When two of them meet on a path, you’ll see it: the outer plates along their sides darken or pale in subtle stripes, running from shoulder to hip, like the passage of a shadow overhead. A rising, slow-dark band might mean “I approach with respect, no claim.” A crosshatch, building and fading over a few heartbeats, might say “I carry heat, keep your films clear,” a warning that they are radiating more than usual. Over this, small, localized gleams—tiny specks along the head-arc or manipulator limbs—flick in rearranged constellations, carrying individual names, affiliations, current tasks.

For long-distance messaging, they don’t shout; they shine.

Those tall skeletal towers are not merely for intake and observation. Along their spines are panels of gel-crystal akin to the strange windows that split the sun’s hammer. At chosen times, or when a certain threshold is crossed in some far reservoir, towers open their gel-faces to the sky. Light went in as raw white blaze; it comes out as tightly tuned beams in narrow spectral bands. Another tower, kilometers away across the bowed horizon, receives the incoming color through its own crystal, translates it into a slow, structural glow that bleeds through nearby walls and floors.

Inside adjacent halls, the inhabitants feel the subtle shift: a cool space takes on a faint internal lilac, or an otherwise dim passageway’s translucent supports blush from inside with amber. These color-cues form a city-scale layer of language: “up-plateau seep has thinned,” “industrial quarter shedding more heat than usual,” “a large movement of beasts along northern escarpment.” No one needs to assemble and listen to proclamations. They simply live in a fabric that quietly re-tints itself with change.

Their built interiors are cool, but not cold. They are thick with weight, the weight of damp air and low sound.

Furniture, as you know it, hardly exists. They do not sit in chairs. Resting postures involve contact with surfaces: leaning long flanks along cool walls, folding limbs to lower their ventral surfaces against slightly wetted floor plates that wick away built heat. Tasks are arranged along ramps and gentle slopes, not in stacked verticals, because their manipulator limbs have limited reach compared to their stride. Work surfaces often double as shallow basins; to work on something is often to bathe it, to pass controlled film over it, to see how it gleams and resists.

They gather in wells.

Those circular, stepped structures sinking into the plateau—cool, damp, quiet—are communal and contemplative both. In the hottest hours, groups descend to the mid-depth rings, where thin veins of natural seep cross the worked stone. They bring with them trays of film, tools, artifacts reclaimed from old mineral-draped ruins. There, in the hush thick with the smell of cool wet mineral, they negotiate.

Negotiation is physical.

To propose a change—a new channel cut, a reallocation of seep from industrial sumps to domestic basins—one does not merely speak. One lays out small scale-model slabs, pre-carved channels gleaming faintly with wetted films. Others approach, test with their comb-like hands, tasting the flows, noting where the opalescent sheets thicken or thin. Arguments happen in the rearrangement of these models: a lip lowered half a finger’s width, an overflow moved two paces along an imagined cliff. Voices, when used, are sotto voce, more to sync rhythms than to carry complex content: a rising trio of notes to request pause, a low descending glissando to signal consensus.

Their sense of ownership is porous but not absent.

No one owns a cliff; to claim the source is to invite collective correction. But a family-line might steward a particular weeping face and its downstream terraces across generations, earning both responsibility and pride. Their names for themselves often fold such stewardship into identity: “Of-the-Third-Fanning-from-East-Seep,” “Keeper-of-Gray-Root-Below-High-Condenser.” Names situate them within flows.

They have no coins. Value is stored in curated complexity.

A particularly stable, productive film strain that can thrive well beyond normal temperature ranges, or harvest a difficult mineral without poisoning its host, is wealth. It can be promised, lent, sectioned. A newly discovered pattern of radiant plate-tiling that sheds more heat per unit surface than any previous design is worth decades of obligation from all who adopt it. Artisans and thinkers are not separate castes; anyone who can coax a new interface between water, heat, and life is both architect and alchemist, honored.

They do have art in the narrower sense, work not strictly necessary for survival.

It hangs from eaves and nestles in shadowed corners: thin sheets of composite seeded with films that have been taught, patiently, to grow in structured, recursive motifs. Spirals of changing iridescence that take hours to complete a cycle, fractal lace of darkening and brightening that maps some internal rhythm of the artist’s own root-strain. These pieces are never static; they are fed carefully, turned by minute degrees, kept at particular angle to light. To neglect a piece is to let its pattern devolve into monotonous sheen, a small domestic tragedy.

They watch the animals intensely but without sentimentality.

When a tall grazer—one of the long-limbed beasts with back-channels running with water and film—comes to rest near their structures, they do not chase it off or approach to touch. They step out onto high terraces, lean their flanks along warm railings, and watch its slow shift, the way heat-shimmer wavers above its back, the pulse of the living films along its channels. These observations feed directly into their understanding of their own internal roots and the health of the plateau’s biosphere.

Sometimes, in the quiet between tasks, a pair or small group will follow at a distance as a grazer moves along the open highland, tracking how it chooses resting spots, which seeping cliffs it approaches, which it avoids. These are not hunters. They are students of flow. The paths of the large beasts reveal broad, subtle gradients of mineral content and film composition that no single instrument could yet map as well.

Predation among the city itself is rare but acknowledged.

Some marginal dwellers—those who live farther out along the plateau’s raw edge, where formal structures thin—have a more pointed relationship with the hunter-creatures: co-habitation in the shadows of overhangs, mutual tolerance at seep-cavities. Those people’s plates are rougher, their internal films tuned to slightly harsher mineral loads. Occasionally, an animal that has grown used to the stillness of city perimeters will slip in closer than is comfortable. When a small child or a distracted worker is taken—a sudden, silent constriction under a lip of stone—the response is not vengeance but redirection.

They study the event: why here, why now, which shadow was unaccounted for. Then they modify. A lip is thickened, a catch-ledge extended an arm’s breadth farther, a new pattern of light-shade thrown by adjustable slats. The hunter is not demonized; it is treated as another force in the equation of survival, one to be modeled and steered. They will, however, construct decoy shadows: tempting overhangs out on unimportant edges, carefully wetted and cooled to draw predators away. Compassion, in their terms, is less about sparing individual lives than about smoothing the roughness in the long-term pattern.

Children are rare and slow.

Gestation happens within the torso, in a specialized sac where internal root-films never establish. The embryo is fed by filtered versions of the parent’s internal fluid, gradually growing its own lattice of channels and its own bare, sterile sinuses. Birth is a long, low-voiced event in a cool, acoustically soft room: not painful by their standards, more an extended fatigue. The new being emerges weak but heat-tolerant, already capable of withstanding full sun for short times, their skin-plates small and under-pigmented.

For months, they remain near constant-contact with elders’ surfaces. They are pressed against flanks in rest, laid on gently wetted panels to feel the angle of brightness, carried to the open under heavily slatted shade to begin acclimating their internal channels to the city’s pattern of heat. Only when their sinuses have widened and thickened, ready to receive roots, are they taken to the inoculation basins.

Play, insofar as they have it, is about gradients.

Very young ones are given thin trays of water, sloped at various angles, studded with small fixed obstacles. They learn by feel: if I run my fingers like this, the sheen breaks; if I press here, the runnel strengthens. They are also allowed supervised contact with safe film-strains, watching how their touch makes the surface shudder and reform.

They are gentle with the smallest animals.

Those flat, translucent arthropods that glide along damp surfaces—they call them something closer to “edge-drinkers”—are crumbs of the old ecology, vital scrapers that keep films from over-thickening and smothering themselves. The people rarely interfere. If one strays into a work area, they simply shift their plates to shadow its path, guiding it toward richer seams. Killing one absent-mindedly is considered a minor social offense, roughly akin to needlessly breaking a piece of useful glassware.

The gliders—those manta-like fliers with trailing filaments seeded with symbiotic mats—are more overtly integrated.

Perches along building edges are not charity; they are part of an energy exchange. When a glider folds itself along a concave platform in shade, the city gains access to the slight trickles of charge induced along its filamentous fringes by the harsh light. The perches are embedded with micro-interfaces: barely visible combs that couple to the glider’s own conductive structures. A resting glider feels a soft tingling along its edges—a pleasant sensation, apparently, as they return again and again—while the city sips the accumulated potential to feed low-level systems.

There are individuals among the people whose task is to tend these relationships: perch-keepers. They adjust shade lattices to match prevailing winds, ensure perches remain at the right temperature, not too hot, not too cool, and use long, hollow reeds to exhale certain trace chemicals that the gliders seem to find attractive. In exchange, the gliders tend to pass more often over the city’s centers, their bellies’ symbiotic films shedding fine aerosols that drift downward and enrich exposed films below.

Silence here is not an absence but a cultivated texture.

Sharp sounds are jarring not because they are rare but because they ripple too far, too fast, disorganizing subtle film behaviors along the rock. Over centuries, they have learned that sudden pressure waves disrupt the delicate layering of evaporative deposits and living skins that make their managed weeps efficient. So they have engineered away clatter the way you might engineer away toxic fumes.

Even the loudest processes—pressure releases, mechanical reconfigurations, material grinding—are cocooned in thick-walled chambers sunk into the plateau, lined with baffles and damped masses. Doors are massive but so well-hung that they swing and seal with hardly a note. Tools that must be struck are struck under water films, the blow’s sharpness chewed into rounded pulses by liquid.

In place of abrupt sound, they embrace rhythm.

Walking through the city, you can—if you attend—feel subtle periodicities: the slow pulse of a condenser’s flow, the gentle, repeated clink of a hanging line under wind load, the regular exhale of an intake tower. These are not accidents; they are tuned. Large-scale infrastructure is designed so that its pressure cycles and temperature swings fall into ratios that avoid resonance with key ecological processes. Watching them adjust a system is less like tuning an engine and more like retuning a room of instruments so no one note will boom.

Their sense of time is anchored not only to diurnal heat cycles but to deeper, slower rhythms.

They mark passing years by precipitation patterns in certain sheltered caverns, where mineral drapery grows in visible rings and ridges. Less visibly, they mark generational sweeps by shifts in the plateau’s overall film ecology. “When the gray roots along the north escarpment began to spark with amber, that was when…”—such phrases start their histories.

History itself is not recorded primarily in script, though they do have notation: a lattice of dots and lines etched into translucent plates, readable by fingertip and by eye as patterns of density and refraction. But more revered are the living archives: ancestral root-strains kept in deep, temperature-controlled vaults, some of which are no longer installed in any living body. These roots carry metabolic “memories” of old mineral regimes, different sky compositional balances. Periodically, careful experimenters will re-inoculate sacrificial volunteers with a sliver of ancient root. Those individuals spend their lives partially out of phase with their contemporaries—sensing different tastes in the water, shedding heat along slightly altered channels—and their observations feed long-term ecological models.

They have stories of when it was different.

Not myths of origin in the human sense, with a singular act of creation, but layered accounts of transition: the time when the sky was slightly thicker and sound carried a bit farther, when gliders were rarer and the big grazers more numerous; the time when an older civilization’s deeper condensers failed and vast portions of the plateau’s seep were lost, forcing whole districts to thin themselves and migrate; the time when a strange high-brightness arrived from above—perhaps an impact, perhaps a flare from the star—burning sections of the film-ecology into glass that still lies, useless and beautiful, in certain hollows.

In these stories, their own role is always that of adjuster, never of master.

They do not speak of having “tamed” the plateau. They speak of learning its narrow generosity. Of discovering that if you coax water to pass here rather than there, a particular mineral does not accumulate in harmful ways, and a certain ancestral film can prosper without suffocating its neighbors. Their morality is built around this: good action is action that lowers the harshness of extremes without dulling necessary gradients. Evil, in their tales, is not malice but stupidity: a group that over-harvested a cliff’s seep, letting mineral scarring run so thick that no film could ever re-establish; a foolish experimenter who let a voracious film-strain escape its panel, and watched it strip whole basins bare before starving.

Toward outsiders—if any ever came—they would be courteous, cautious, and unhurried.

They would notice first how a newcomer disrupted flows: the way your body sheds heat at different rates, the chemical signature of your sweat if you had it, how your movements startle or calm the near-silent fauna. They would test you with light before they risked touch: waves of shadow across pathways, gentle shifts in brightness bands along walls, seeing if you respond, if you can perceive their large-scale language. Only then would they approach, outer plates pale to radiate away some of their own heat, manipulator fans open in a gesture that, for them, means much: “we will share interface.”

If you stayed, they would try to find a place for you in the flow: some task that aligned with your tolerances and capacities. Perhaps you’d be set to watch a particular panel, to report in your own ways when its sheen changed beyond a threshold. Perhaps, if you could hear further or see in wavelengths they could not, they’d bring you to the observation towers, have you sit in the cool ring of their gathered bodies while they tuned gel windows, asking you what you saw when the sky shaped itself differently.

In time, if you offered, they might give you a film-strain: a safe, peripheral one, perhaps to wear on your skin as a learning. You would feel it as a faint drag, a new, soft resistance to touch, a coolness that rises when you step into light. It would mark you to them as partially theirs, an adopted interface, another way in which the plateau’s long, slow conversation can be conducted.

Because that is what lives here, at every scale: conversation.

Between stone and seep, between old devices and new constructions, between ancestral films and the bodies that carry them around. These people are not rulers of their world, nor supplicants. They are translators and mediators in a constant triad: heat, water, life. Their city is a dialect of that triad made visible. Their own bodies are its script.

In the bright, nearly soundless press of that high plateau, they endure and elaborate, not by rejecting the givens of their environment but by entwining themselves with them so thoroughly that you could not, after a time, say where land ends and citizen begins.
