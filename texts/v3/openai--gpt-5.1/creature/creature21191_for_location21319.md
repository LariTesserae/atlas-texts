Here, “who lives here” is almost the same as “what this place is.” Still, distinct lives have budded out of the plateau’s general will. Some are as large as ridges; some are barely larger than grains. All of them are, in different ways, expressions of the same long patience.

---

First, the plateau itself.

It is not simply a habitat; it is the primary inhabitant.

What rises into the cold air as undulating tableland is one organism in the sense that it shares a continuous body, a continuous circulation, and a continuous economy of signal and resource. But within that unity it behaves less like an individual and more like an ecosystem that forgot to separate into parts.

It has a memory—not of events, but of forms. The persistence of ridges in the same places over geological time, the way basins and troughs reform after being erased by convulsions, the rule-like avoidance of stress-risers and defense of empty chambers: these are all its “habits.” They are how its genes, its structure, and the feedback of its own long history have settled into something like instinct at landscape scale.

It knows the world the way roots know soil and leaves know sun. Through gradients.

Heat, mostly, and chemistry. Tiny differences in temperature between shaded hollows and exposed crowns. Minute excesses of metal in one trickle of absorbed rain compared to another. Faint discrepancies between the acidity of exhaled vapors in one hollow vat and the next. These are its sensory fields, and every fiber of its body reports on them simply by existing: by swelling or shrinking, conducting better or worse, fluorescing more or less under the nocturnal waves of light.

No mind sits at a center and reads those reports. The plateau’s thinking, to the extent that it thinks at all, is distribution. Where a gradient forms, conductive tissues sprout, thickening like thought. Where a cavity resonates at the right timbre, it is preserved and reinforced into an organ. Where a zone produces more waste heat than is economical, finlike ridges slowly grow there to shed it. Decisions are what the plateau looks like after enough time has passed.

Its “technology” is not a separate category from its metabolism. The slag-bands, the rotors of tissue, the towering chimneys that pump gases and warmth: these are specialized organs serving long-term projects. Chief among those projects is concentration. The plateau pulls in thin traces of everything that touches it—metals in dust, carbon in the corpses of its own cells, fleeting chemistry in the breath of its sparse animals—and distills them. It makes exotics from the ordinary, then breaks them again and scatters the pieces to be reused.

This refining habit gives it a peculiar interior: nodes where rare elements accumulate enough to change conductivity, or stiffen tissue into natural semiconductors, or alter the spectrum of its internal light. Those nodes act as junctions for its slow signaling. When the subterranean bioluminescent lines brighten and a wave of soft light crawls through a region at night, that is the plateau sending instructions, or at least synchronizing processes. “Thicken here.” “Soften there.” “Shift heat from this basin to that tower.” Every instruction is a difference in timing and intensity, moving through tissues as easily as sap.

It is not aware of itself in any way that would resemble an animal’s knowledge. But it has a continuous experience: a long, sliding grasp of strain patterns and chemical budgets and the quiet tug of the sky. That is the first and primary inhabitant. Everything else lives inside its body.

---

Then, the microscopic multiplicity.

The surfaces that appear as matte, pale-green skin are actually crowds—millions of tiny, short-lived forms that bud and slough, bud and slough, like the fur on some immense, slow-breathing animal.

There are film-livers: microbial consortia that inhabit the thin water skins glazing hollows and ribs. They are not separate species in the strict sense, but shifting partnerships of bacteria-like and fungus-like lineages, trading genes and metabolites along the gradient between plateau-tissue and open air. They live on the exudates the plateau releases—simple sugars, low-energy acids, traces of reduced metal—reworking them into more complex molecules that the plateau cannot easily assemble on its own.

Along the undersides of overhangs and within the narrow cracks that open during convulsions, there are filament forests. Delicate hyphal webs sprout from the plateau’s own cambium-like layers, then hybridize with free-living strands that are only tenuously attached. These webs are not parasites; they are outsourced services. They specialize in dismantling foreign matter faster than the plateau’s ordinary tissues could: the rare meteoritic grain, the occasional carcass, the broken fragments of its own denser organs. In return, they are fed a steady drip of sap and warm, moist refuge from the bitter air.

Between these two—film-livers and filament forests—cycles a third community: dust-eaters. Spore-sized organisms, motile only in the thinnest sense, ride the micro-breezes inside pores and vents. They bind wind-blown grit and trapped aerosols into tiny clumps, digest the organic fraction, and then cement the mineral remainder into little pellets that roll down micro-slopes into those metallic-looking troughs where the plateau’s industrial organs harvest them.

None of these things has a name in their own world. They exist as fluctuations at the base of the plateau’s economy: an insect’s gut flora, but distributed across kilometers.

They do not know the plateau; they simply are it, at a finer scale.

---

Next, the fringe of motile life you can see: the tiny animals that skim the moisture films, the pale arthropods.

They have traded limbs for fringes, skeleton for pliant cartilage. Seen up close, a single individual is like a pointed tongue of translucent gristle, flattened to a leaf’s thickness, its underside edged with thousands of microscopic cilia. In the thin water layers, those cilia beat and set up surface waves, making each animal ride on its own ripples. It never quite contacts the plateau directly; there is always a sheen of water and vibration between.

They graze on loose tissue, yes, but also on any cell clusters that detach from the plateau with interesting chemistry: pigment grains, weird mineral inclusions, the occasional packet of plateau “spores”—small, thick-walled nodes that bud from degraded organs and would, if left alone, sink deeper and be recycled.

The arthropods serve an unasked-for service as selective scavengers. Their mouths are ringed with sensory tufts tuned to detect particular chemistries. Over evolutionary time, local populations have specialized.

In colder, shadowed corridors, you find a strain whose tufts are attuned to the sour tang of over-oxidized tissue; they clear away the plateau’s senescent skin before it thickens into a barrier.

On the high crowns, a smaller, translucent kind seeks out sap beaded with unusual trace metals; they ingest it, bioaccumulate its contents, and then flush them in fecal threads that slump into the processing troughs below. In this way, the plateau has, without intending it, bred herds of microscopic miners that drag rare elements downhill.

They respond strongly to the plateau’s night-light. When a wave of bioluminescence moves through the tissues beneath, they align their bodies in its direction. Their cilia adjust, pumping them slightly “against” or “with” the flow of signal. In aggregate, millions of them act as little diodes for the plateau’s information: their movement pattern slightly speeds or slows the progress of pulses in certain corridors by altering local load, heating or cooling tissues, scraping or polishing certain conduits. None of them understands this, but their behavior has been shaped by the environment that benefits from it.

They have no eyes, no ears, no social structure beyond occasional brief aggregations where water filaments meet. Yet they are not entirely alone. When two cross paths, their cilia patterns briefly disrupt each other’s waves, causing a few heartbeats of stuttered motion—a primitive, physical sharing of trajectories. It is not communication in any rich sense, but it is enough that they do not simply behave as gas particles. They are a crowd, with all the emergent, slow order that implies.

---

Rarer, and stranger, are the animals that break the smoothness of the horizon: the long-necked grazers.

They are the plateau’s largest tenants that are unambiguously “other.” Even the birds, when they come, are momentary; these are residents.

Their bodies are tubes of flexible, graphite-gray hide, supported inside by a braided lattice of cartilage and fluid bladders. There is no obvious anatomical segmentation: no clear head, thorax, or abdomen. Instead, a tapering end—call it “front”—hosts a ring of flexible mouth-flaps lined on the inner surface with rasp teeth of keratin-like material. The opposite end terminates in a cluster of gripping pads, each covered in microscopic hooks that find purchase in the plateau’s fibrous skin.

They move by inversion. The gripping end anchors, the body lengthens along its axis and leans forward, then the mouth end attaches and the rear unhooks and is pulled up and over, like a sock turning inside-out in slow motion. With each “step,” the body’s internal lattice subtly rearranges, distributing tension along different helices, letting the animal climb buttresses and slide along vaulted corridors without ever lifting more than a fraction of itself off the surface.

Inside, a long digestive tube spirals, aided by peristaltic muscular bands that push semi-digested slurry backward, against the animal’s usual direction of travel. As they rasp along the plateau, they take in thin sheets of tissue and the film-livers and dust-eaters clinging to it. Their handsomest meal is the frost: early-morning ice, enriched with gases and trace elements absorbed from the plateau’s nocturnal breath. They lick it away with an inner tongue while the outer flaps keep a purchase.

Their eyes—if one insists on eyes—are embedded along one side of the tube, each a pale, lensless patch of photoreceptive cells shielded by clear gel. Instead of focusing images, they register gradients of brightness and color across meters of surface. A grazer sees the plateau not as shapes but as a moving map of temperature and reflectance: brighter, darker, warmer, colder, more or less maroon in the crowns overhead. This map is precise enough for them to find the live edges where growth is tenderest and for them to avoid the yawning slots of fresh convulsions.

Their voices, the low, breathy coughs, are side-effects. The plateau’s muffling tissues mean that even small pressure differences in their lungs are enough to move air audibly. They do not call to each other, not in any organized fashion. But they vent more often when traversing regions of heightened plateau vibration, as if the internal tremor jostles their respiratory rhythms. In that sense, their sound is a readout of the plateau’s mood: the colder and quieter the living ground, the more silently they move.

They are not numerous. The plateau cannot afford to host many such mouths. Each one demands sizeable energy to build and maintain, energy ultimately derived from the plateau’s own productivity. Why, then, does the plateau permit them? Or, more accurately, why have they not been outcompeted into nonexistence?

The answer is in what they trail behind them.

Their waste is not random. The long digestive tube does to the plateau’s tissues what the plateau’s own processing organs do, but on a different regime. The grazers’ gut flora—descended from ancient film-livers, but specialized for life in liquid darkness—are adept at breaking down complex secondary compounds the plateau uses as defenses and structural stiffeners. In the end, what comes out of the grazer is a slurry rich in certain otherwise-sequestered metabolites and rare elements.

The plateau has built, beneath the most common grazer paths, shallow, sponge-like aquifers of absorptive tissue. They wick in the slurry, strip it of its riches, then reroute the remnants into deeper waste channels. Over time, grazer routes fossilize into hidden, nutrient-dense belts that the plateau uses to power new growth and complex organs.

In that sense, the grazers are domesticated without ever having been captured. The plateau creates the corridors they prefer—easy gradients, sheltering arches, mild thermal pockets at just the right intervals—and in following those conveniences, they deliver their processed tribute.

They are, dimly, aware of each other. When two meet head-on in a narrow corridor, there is no aggression. One simply inverts direction; their bodies are topologically unconcerned with which end leads. The internal lattice flexes, organs shift place more than shape, and what was mouth becomes gripping tail, and vice versa. No fixed front, no fixed past. It is a body architecture that suits a world where every direction is equally important, and where the ground itself can move unpredictably beneath you.

They breed infrequently. There are no eggs, no larvae in the open. Once in many seasons, a grazer thickens in its midsection; its internal tube buds off a clot of tissue that slips into a prepared niche in the plateau’s skin, a warm, moist alcove that is already waiting. There, it swells, fed by plateau sap and the parent’s regurgitated slurries, until a juvenile extrudes itself the way its parent moves: by inversion. The plateau consumes the abandoned alcove’s lining and stitches the wound.

For a few cycles, the young stick close to the parent’s paths, literally following the scent of its waste along familiar corridors. Eventually, the plateau’s slow convulsions and shifting gradients pull them elsewhere, and their trajectories diverge.

---

Above them, rarely alighting, there are the sky-things—the “birds,” if one insists on that word.

They are not avian in origin, though they share the silhouette: tapered bodies, sweeping surfaces, a habit of gliding along invisible lines in the air. Their wings are not feathers but thin, dark membranes threaded with a reinforcing mesh of flexible rods. The membranes are glossy, almost oily, shedding frost and the plateau’s chilled mist with equal ease.

They are warm inside. Always a little warmer than the air, enough that a faint halo of disturbed atmosphere clings to them when they hover close to the towers’ crowns. Their metabolism is fueled by stores hoarded during rare, intense feedings.

What they feed on is not obvious from below.

They do not peck the plateau’s flesh, nor tear at visible organisms. Instead, when they land on a crown-plate that has stiffened to meet them, they lower small, square patches on their chin or breast—specialized contact fields, rich in conductive tissue and laced with sensory nerves. The plateau responds by lifting tongues of sponge-tissue that meet those fields.

For minutes, nothing moves. Then a very slow exchange begins.

The plateau pushes up a faintly shimmering gel, full of ion-rich fluids and charged organic complexes. The bird-thing’s field draws some of those charges away, shunting them into internal reservoirs: electrochemical batteries, built of layered membranes and protein complexes more reminiscent of an electric fish’s organs than any terrestrial bird’s structures. Simultaneously, the bird-thing bleeds back into the plateau a different spectrum of charges, discharging stale potential built up from earlier harvests.

They are, in effect, mobile capacitors. They ride the plateau’s self-made weather, mapping its electrical gradients, sopping surplus charge from zones where static builds too high, and dumping it where the plateau can use it—into organs that need a quick jolt to change phase or to trigger a region-scale convulsion. They get, in return, the energy they require to stay warm and the materials to rebuild their charge organs.

They are not fully under the plateau’s control. Their pattern of movement is their own, driven by their need to balance load and avoid over-saturation. However, over evolutionary time, the plateau has rewarded those flight paths that best serve its own equalization: it grows taller crowns and more generous exchange-tongues where birds visit frequently, and starves or dismantles useless towers.

Thus a mutualism emerges. From above, the birds see a luminous map during certain nights when the plateau’s internal circuits flash brighter: they are exquisitely sensitive to that glow. Where the lines converge, they know a good exchange is possible. Where they flicker unevenly, they know stress is building and may catalyze a convulsion—hazardous but rich opportunities to pick up big surges of charge along fresh, exposed canyons.

They have their own sociality, faint but real. On the rare days when an outside weather front presses heavy cloud across the plateau and static builds between ice-crystals and the living ground, several birds may arrive at the same tower. They circle in overlapping spirals, measuring each other’s charge fields. Brief contact flashes—audible, up close, as tiny snaps—exchange partial loads between them, leveling out risk. There is no language, but there is a negotiation, performed entirely in voltage and proximity.

Their nests are not on the plateau. To make one here would be like trying to build a house atop the back of a restless whale. Instead, they vanish into the unseen periphery—cliff faces, perhaps, or other, drier biomes beyond the plateau’s vegetative sea. They are commuters, not citizens. Yet the plateau has shaped them, and they in turn shape its internal weather. They are part of this place even when they are not physically on it.

---

Beneath and between all of these, there are the marginal minds: entities one might not notice at all unless one watched for decades.

The plateau, being vast and continuous, can support local patterns of organization that are more intricate than the general background. In certain voids that the plateau preserves instead of collapsing—resonant chambers, protected hollows—tissues grow that are more neuron-like than elsewhere: cells with branching processes, specialized for speed of signal transmission rather than mere conduction.

In a given resonant cavern, a cluster of these cells might, through internal feedback and the plateau’s own recurring wave-patterns, settle into a semi-stable cycle of activation. That cycle could encode, in the fleeting way of electrical patterns, some memory of an event: a particularly violent convulsion, the rare fall of a large foreign object, the passage of many grazers in a short time. The pattern persists, feeding back into the local tissues, altering how they stiffen or relax, how they respond to future waves.

These are not independent intelligences. They cannot move the plateau in ways the plateau would not already move. But they are local biases: tiny, half-born selves that tilt the outcome of ambiguous processes. In one hollow, the bias might favor the maintenance of an unusually large void, simply because its resident pattern “likes” the resonant vibration. In another, it might promote the growth of extra processing organs, amplifying the plateau’s refining capacity in that region.

One could, if one insisted, call these hollow-minds. They are neither entirely separate from the plateau’s overall activity nor completely dissolved into it. They are memories with opinions.

The other lifeforms respond to them unconsciously. Arthropods, in one such region, might always veer slightly left when passing through, because the local vibration pattern makes their cilia beat asymmetrically. Grazers might linger in another, because the convection of warmth and the faint chemical signatures stimulate their feeding reflexes. Over centuries, these biases carve behavioral ruts that reinforce the hollows’ special status, feeding back into the plateau’s own decision to maintain them.

No one writes the rules that govern this world; the rules grow from exactly these long entanglements.

---

Finally, there are the ghosts: lives that do not quite belong, but which the plateau incorporates and erases.

Now and then, something truly foreign arrives. A fragment of some neighboring biome blown here by improbable winds. A spore that has drifted for years in high air until turbulence threw it down. A microbe-laden dust from some distant, warmer valley. These things strike the plateau’s surface and, if they are lucky, land in one of the few micro-habitats that are not instantly hostile.

For a few reproductive cycles—days, months, perhaps even a couple of years—such an intruder can live almost as if the plateau were just another substrate. It may even find niches: a patch of sun-warmed membrane where it can photosynthesize, a corner of a damp fissure where its enzymes work at the right speed.

But the rules hold. Foreign matter does not last.

The plateau does not wield an immune system in the animal sense; it has filters and absorbers, gradients and pulses. Where an alien colony perturbs those gradients, the plateau thickens or thins tissue beneath it, redirecting flows. Film-livers change composition, incorporating newly arrived genes that help them digest the intruder. Arthropods subtly alter their grazing, preferentially scraping at the unfamiliar textures and tastes.

Soon enough, what was foreign is broken into its parts and either burned for energy or rearranged into plateau-coded forms. The only trace that such a thing ever lived here might be a slightly altered ratio of certain isotopes in a slag layer, or a faint deviation in the timing of nocturnal bioluminescent waves long after the organism itself has vanished.

Yet those ghosts matter. They are the plateaus tiny contacts with beyond. They seed occasional novelties into its gene-like patterns. Over very long spans, these inputs let new kinds of organ, new metabolic shortcuts, new cooperative relationships emerge.

The plateau is not an island; it is a membrane. It ate its environment so long and so thoroughly that now the boundary between “place” and “organism” is gone. But winds still carry rumors, and now and then, those rumors become part of someone who lives here.

---

So who lives here?

The plateau lives here, as the enduring body and blunt, gradient-sensing mind of the place.

The film-livers and dust-eaters and filaments live here, as its biochemical detail and its digestive halo, half-self and half-world.

The sliding arthropods live here, as its janitors and miners and tiny diodes, simple grazers that have accidentally become part of its circuitry.

The long-necked grazers live here, as big, mobile digestive loops that the plateau has half-domesticated to recover what it cannot easily reach.

The sky-things live here, in a wider sense, as commuting capacitors that balance its charge and taste its invisible weather.

The hollow-minds live here, as small, local preferences in a vast organism, like eddies in a steady current.

And the ghosts—briefly, and then entirely—live here as well, their patterns stripped and used, their alienness amortized into the plateau’s next, patient rearrangement of itself.

Nothing is entirely separate. No resident stands outside and merely occupies space. On this high, cold, single, living surface, to live here is to be a part of what the plateau is trying—slowly, quietly, endlessly—to become.
