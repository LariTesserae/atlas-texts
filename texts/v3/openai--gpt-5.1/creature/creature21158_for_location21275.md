They are small.

That’s the first thing: anything larger than a big dog is a liability here. Growth closes around bulk, crushes it, uses it as scaffold or food. So the thinking creatures have given that up. They run to the scale of a child, or smaller: narrow torsos and limb-bones like bundles of hardened vine, dense and springy. They live in the clearances between organs — the provisional gaps that open and close as the great plant-body flexes.

They are not naked apes. Nothing naked survives long in this abrasive, adhesive air. They are furred, or at least fur-adjacent: a tight pelt of hollow, resilient fibers that shrug off spores and resin, that can be scrubbed clean against a root without stripping skin. The color is the color of background: amber-brown, lichen-gray, soot-black, mottled in dull patches so a crouched form disappears into the layered vegetation.

Their bodies are a negotiation between climbing and bracing and clamping. Picture a torso no wider than your forearm, but long, with a low center of mass. The spine is a flexible chain of short vertebrae, allowing them to twist nearly in half, to align with whatever tangle they’re on. Arms and legs are all nearly the same length: four main limbs, each multi-jointed, that can serve equally well as arms or legs. The ankles and wrists rotate far beyond what would be comfortable for you, letting a limb pivot to any angle that the maze demands.

At each end: not feet, not hands, but something that is both. A broad palm with four or five stubby, muscled digits that can splay out in a circle. When they close, the whole hand becomes a cupping clamp, fingers locking into ridges along the palm so that the grip is a solid ring. On the outer edge of each digit, a dull, horn-like pad; on the inner edge, ridged skin that finds purchase on even the slickest of sap-wet bark. They can seize a cable of root from any side and hold, even while the root itself moves.

Their faces need to breathe in a storm of chemistry and not be erased by it. The nose is not a proud protrusion but a shallow recess, a vertical slit and two lateral ports that can seal flat. Around it, the bones form a shallow bowl, into which they fit whatever mask the day requires: layered fronds, woven filters of dried luffa-like tissue, padded rings of spongy seed-pith. Over the eyes, nictitating membranes — clear, then amber, then opaque — blink sideways, sweeping off dust and spores.

The eyes themselves are large for their size, but deep-set, heavily lidded. They are not built for distance; there is no distance here. They are built for fine contrast in low, diffuse light: they read texture and vibratory shimmer in the air itself. Colors tend toward the spectrum that matters underground: golden, carmine, poison green, the subtle shifts of bruised purple that mean a nodule is about to burst. A ring of smaller photoreceptors freckles the skin around each eye, catching sudden flares from the corners of their vision.

Hearing is not an ear-perched-on-the-side-of-the-head affair. The whole skull is an instrument. Along the jawline and the back of the neck, they carry a fringe of stiffened hairs, each rooted in a deep, innervated follicle. Sound is touch at a distance; the ceaseless thunder of the cavern comes to them as pressure maps along their skin. Deeper inside, behind the cheekbones, bony plates with honeycombed interiors resonate to low booms and pulses, letting them feel the far peristalsis of the cavern-body, the distant beginnings of a storm.

Their mouths are unremarkable, by terrestrial standards: a flexible lip, a tongue as agile as a small hand, teeth that skew toward grinding surfaces rather than fangs. They eat what the ecosystem gives that can be taken without inviting instant reprisal. Tough, woody growth they pre-digest by fermenting it in baskets of gut-coated fibers hung in warm vents; soft organs and small animals they tear apart with tools more often than with teeth.

They smell of warmed resin and their own oils, but beneath that, faintly, of the plant. They have lived here long enough that some of its chemistries have become theirs. Their sweat carries volatile compounds that signal “do not grow here” to certain tissues, buying them a little extra time to keep a passage clear or a sleeping hollow open. The chemistry is never absolute — the plant will reclaim everything in the end — but their bodies, exhalations, and wastes are small, continuous acts of gardening.

They call themselves nothing that translates easily. A name spoken aloud is as much vibration as sound, a complex click-and-hum that your throat cannot shape. To think of them, imagine this: the People of the Kept Gaps. The ones who live not on the world-organism, but in the transitory absence of it.

Their homes are not separate structures but edited parts of the mass.

A “house” begins as a cyst, a natural blister of plant-flesh that swells between converging roots. When they find such a swelling in time, they intervene gently. They prick it, not once but many times, with slivers of mineral and thorn. They bleed off its pressure in measured increments, encouraging it to toughen instead of burst. As its walls thicken and differentiate, they scrape away every inner extrusion, every exploratory tendril that pushes inward.

Inside, the walls remain alive, but tamed. They press in rhythm with the larger body, but the interior surface is kept smooth by constant use: skins hung there, tools scraping, bodies rubbing. If you lay your ear to such a wall, you would hear a dull syncopated susurrus: sap moving, tissues flexing, the echoes of distant booms. The dwelling is literally a listening organ they have re-purposed.

Floor is a word that hardly applies. In their home, any surface can be surface. They string tensioned nets of woven vine across the interior volume, layer them, crosshatched. These are anchored to carefully selected points — nodules whose slow movement has been charted and whose trajectories they understand — so that when the outer tissue bulges, the nets shift rather than break. They move in three dimensions through this scaffold, hanging, crouching, clipping themselves to cords when sleep or spore-storm demands stillness.

Furniture is whatever they can keep from being reclaimed. A platform of repeatedly bruised root, hardened into a callus by constant feet. A hollowed seed mass, scorched inside to discourage regrowth, serving as a basin for fermenting slurries. A bundle of dried, spiritually significant growths — the eye-stalks of an enormous, long-dead grazer, perhaps — lashed together as a ladder and hung where the plant cannot easily reach with new tissue.

Their village, if you insist on the word, is not mapped by houses in a clearing, but by densities of interruption. You would recognize it, if you had been walking the mazes long enough, as a place where the relentless vegetal push seems oddly… less. Routes here are unusually consistent. Certain load-bearing cords show signs of deliberate scarring in long vertical lines, the repeated blows of tools preventing them from thickening and shifting. Some fans of translucent frond have been cut back again and again, forming a semi-stable canopy that tames the glare into a softer dusk. The air smells… not clean, there is no clean, but subtly different: familiar resins undercut by the protein tang of many bodies, by smoke clung to fur.

Smoke is their miracle and their terror.

Fire is hard to coax in a world that steals moisture so quickly that even ember-warmth can suffocate for lack of sustained oxygen, and yet where any unattended flame can become catastrophic. They have watched spore-storms primed by a single spark chew through a hundred living vaults, leaving behind charred, smoking organs that budded anew within days, their own dead woven into the future architecture. They know that lesson.

So when they burn, it is with almost religious precision.

They have found plants whose dried outer husks smolder instead of flare, and others whose sap, mixed with certain mineral flakes, burns pale and hot in a narrow spectrum of fumes that nearby tissues find repellant. They make coals, not bonfires: small, concentrated cores of heat held in stone rings atop particularly stubborn patches of root that even the world-organism struggles to infiltrate. Over these, they lay the skeletons of air-ducts braided from stiff reeds and plated with char-resistant bark. The flame drinks from channels the plant cannot easily seal. If the structure warps under growth, the ducts collapse and the fire starves.

They use fire to cook small portions of their food, yes, but that is almost incidental. More crucially, they use it to mark. A char-line along a favored bridge-root tells the root, chemically, “this place burns,” and for a time the plant will grow its most delicate, most flammable tissues elsewhere. A ring of scorched crust around a dwelling-cyst is a moat that must be repeatedly renewed, a sacrificial belt of killed tissue that absorbs the next surge of growth so it does not crush the habitat within.

They are not alone in their relationship with flame. Certain plants blister into life only after scorched, their seeds hardened against heat and keyed to the specific chemistry of smoke. The people have learned to time small, controlled burnings to coincide with the fruiting of these species. The result is narrow windows when plump, high-energy pods ripen all at once, hanging from blackened stems like gifts. There are feasts then; glows of ruby pulp on soot-stained hands.

They eat almost everything they can get that the plant does not immediately seize back.

Soft, translucent nodules hidden in shaded crevices, rich in fats and complex sugars. The outer skins of large gas-bladders, carefully peeled and dried before they can regrow. Spore-sacs that they fillet and desiccate to a leathery crackling before the microscopic charges inside ripen into storm-fuel. They scrub away bitter, neuroactive secretions, or they cultivate tolerance to them in careful, incremental dosings.

They hunt, as well. Not the great, armored grazers whose bulk and symbioses make them dangerously integrated into the plant — to kill those is to provoke localized immune responses that might smother a whole district in hardened foam — but the mid-scale mobile fauna. Lizard-pods, fast and stupid, that skitter in erratic hops along the under-sides of roofs. Furless, almost-spherical “rollers” that pinch stems with ring-mouths and tumble along when the plant convulses. The mantis-like thing you saw earlier is on the edge of their capacity: they will pursue one only if they can rig the terrain first, cutting specific support-vines so a sudden, timed collapse pins it without tearing up a route they need.

Their weapons are extensions of their own bodies and of the plant.

A spear is not just a sharpened stick; it is a former root hardened in smoke and oil, its tip reinforced with a wedge of mineral chipped by hand. The haft has subtle notches that fit their fingers exactly, and also anchoring grooves that catch the fibers of the surrounding growth when they hurl it, so that even a missed throw leaves the weapon dangling safely instead of falling into some unreturnable fissure.

Their blades are few and precious. A sliver of obsidianlike glass dug from cracked veins high in the rock, ground to an edge on a sandstone slab until it can part living fiber with the gentlest pull. They bind such a blade into a handle not permanently, but with a lashing that can be quickly unraveled, so the stone can be repositioned, hidden, shared. To lose a blade is to leave an invitation in the environment, an exposed mineral that the plant will lick at with acid filaments, that flesh-boring insects will colonize, that some other thinking group — or an unrelated clever scavenger — might find and wield against them.

They have darts, tipped with mashed, fermented plant gunk that carries paralyzing alkaloids stolen from some defended organ. They will watch for hours, pressed flat against a damp, pulsing wall, waiting for a particular grazer to put its unarmored throat to a certain favored tube, then flick a dart into the exposed soft. It drops; scavengers begin to converge; the people work quickly around them, hoisting portions into nets and dragging them away along pre-cleared, chemically-quiet paths.

Their technology is not only cutting and burning. They have learned to grow.

They do not command the plant-body, no one does, but they can bias it. In hidden clefts, they cultivate strains of fungus that colonize specific tissues and slow their thickening. They smear spore-pastes into the grooves of a root-bridge they wish to keep slender and flexible; as the fungus takes, the root remains wiry instead of bulking into an untraversable column. In return, they harvest the fungus’s fruiting bodies: tough, tangy shelves that store well if dried.

They graft, too. A defensive thorn-vine with hook-barbs that tangle anything that brushes it; a fleshy, soft-leafed creeper that breathes in certain caustic vapors; a thin, nerve-dense rootlet that the plant uses as a sensor. They splice propagules in crude but functional ways, binding them together with wet strips of cambium and holding them in place with fiber bands until they fuse. Over time, they have created lines that the plant does not “mean” to grow — hybrid tendrils that sprout thorns only where their dwellings meet major growth arteries, or faintly luminescent runners that trace safe paths without flaring into distracting brilliance that would trigger nearby phototropic responses.

They know a great deal; they do not know why. Their “science” is story and scar.

They tell each other, in the quiet hours between storms, about the great convulsion when the ceiling near their grandparents’ cyst cracked open and a new shaft was born. They remember which smells preceded that quake, which distant bass tones wobbled through the walls. They began, after that, to score those tones onto bark: long cuts for deep rumbles, short notches for staccato pulses. Over generations, those score-marks became more abstract, more referential. The angled trio of cuts here might mean, for them: “When you hear this pattern in your bones, do not sleep in the lower nets.”

Writing is a desperate act.

Every message must be carved into something determined to erase it. They exploit tissues that are momentarily slow — the callus-scars their feet have made on certain supporting trunks, or plaques of mineral crust exposed at rare rock windows. A word is a wound held open. To say, “this way leads to a lode of glass-stone” is to make a small infection the plant must eventually heal. They accept this. All things here are tradeoffs between knowledge and irritation.

Their language, spoken, has evolved in answer to the storm of sound.

They do not shout. There is no point in adding to the roar. Their vocal apparatus is complex: a main larynx and smaller, secondary folds echoing in sinus-bulbs in the chest. They speak in narrow, precise bands of frequency that stand out against the cavern’s general thunder. To an outsider, their talk would sound like a series of sharp, fluted beeps buried inside low hums; to them, it is full of nuance. They can throw these notes in directional beams, turning their throat and chest so that a call arcs around a pillar or threads a particular cluster of hanging cords.

More than voice, though, they use beat.

Climbing, they tap as they go, knuckles against root, heel against hardened bark, tools against mineral. Not loud, but patterned. A four-beat, pause, two-beat might mean “I am here, I am not in danger.” A sequence of alternating high and low knocks could be a warning of a spore-swelling overhead. These codes change, shift within families and clans; the plant listens, too, and so their pulses must remain a little unpredictable. They also touch each other frequently: fingers along forearms, palms on ribs, short bursts of pressure that say “stop,” “wait,” “breathe shallow.”

Their sense of time is the rhythm of the cavern.

They do not measure in sunrises. Their cycles are keyed to pulses: the rise and fall of fermentation heat through a given region, the waxing and waning of certain phosphorescent discharges in the deep throats. They count spore-storm intervals, the time from one great chemical cleansing to the next. Between those, there are smaller beats: the nightly (for them) quiescence when, after a storm, nearby tissues are momentarily stunned and new growth has not yet begun; the “noon” of their world when the ambient rumble mounts as a distant core engages a new vein of rock.

They raise children in the safest uncertainty they can contrive.

The very young cling like extra limbs. Their hands and feet come born with the clamping reflex, tendons tight and strong. An infant rides on a parent’s back or belly, strapped with braided vine that allows rapid release if some greater motion demands it. Their first lessons are not about words but about how to feel the wall through their bones, how to listen for the change in the air when a nodule starts to swell.

They wean them onto fermented plant mash, seeded with the mild toxins of their world in doses just enough to train their guts. Sickness is constant, but mostly brief — the decomposers are merciless; anything that lingers dying is taken apart. Those who survive become adults with livers and kidneys that can parse an environment saturated with alkaloids and resins that would stop your heart.

Stories are maps as much as memories.

Around the dim coals of a tightly shuttered fire, they narrate the way through constantly shifting territory. A tale about an ancestor’s hunt is also an annotated route: “He went under the thick root that drums when the deep-heart wakes, past the hanging fronds that smell of cut pepper, across the bridge that hums three-toned when you thump it.” Children recite these in singsong, then go with elders to see how the place has changed, adding new verses, new qualifiers: “Then the bridge broke and became a ladder; now you must go around.”

They have myths of bigger things, too: of the world-body itself.

Some say it is one being, dreaming. The booms are its sleep-jerks; the spore-storms are its breaths. Their duty is to scratch where it itches, to keep its skin from sealing over too completely, to be the tickle that keeps it half-wakeful so it does not roll and crush them unaware.

Others think of it as countless beings that only seem one because they grow together. Each pillar, each web, each vast, warm wall is a different mindless hunger; the communal illusion is yours, not theirs. To them, this is comforting. You cannot negotiate with a single god-body, but you can learn which patches respond too quickly, which are dull, which can be tricked into growing away from you by repeated small insults.

Their spirituality, if you call it that, is as practical as everything else. They leave offerings not to curry favor but to shape patterns. A favorite, safe cavity that the plant has, against all odds, not yet invaded — this is a sacred place. They hang there the husks of their dead’s masks, the worn-out tools whose dulled edges once cut a path. The smell and the accumulated films of their presence tell the plant: “this hollow is occupied; disturb it last.” Often, that works for a surprisingly long time.

They do die. Often via miscalculation.

A growth-surge misread; a spore-storm that arrives faster than the elders remember; a bridge-root that snaps under a new, unseen load. When a body falls where retrieval would endanger too many, they do not pursue it. They mark the path instead. A triple-cut on a supporting cord. A ring of char on a nearby knot. The body, meanwhile, becomes scaffold, nutrient, texture. Within hours, delicate, almost beautiful fungi feather from its cooling muscles. Within days, its bones are bound into new walls.

They have enemies beyond the environment itself.

There are other thinking knots of flesh in this place.

Some are cousins, clans split generations ago by a quake that sealed routes between them, their languages drifting. When paths open again, contact is tense, prickling. They read each other’s tools, the way you can tell the maker of a pot by its patterning. A lattice of net with diamond knots: that’s from the Upper Heat. A blade hafted with three parallel wrappings instead of two: that’s the style of the Far Throat. Trade is possible, stories exchanged like grafts, but also conflict: a disputed safe cyst, a vein of stone that yields particularly fine glass.

And there is at least one other species that builds in a different way.

Not furred, not four-limbed, but many-legged and plated, its intelligence distributed along its spine in ganglia that can operate semi-independently. It doesn’t use fire; it fears it. It eats different tissues, competing less directly for food, but it, too, seeks enduring hollows, predictable routes. It armors certain roots in layers of chewed bark, making them treacherous, too slick or brittle for the people’s usual grips. It lays down chemical smears that trigger localized plant-immune responses, cluttering favored paths with choking, hardened cysts.

Between them and the people, there is a cold war of modification. The folk of the Kept Gaps clip a series of vines to open a shortcut; the many-legged ones plug that opening with woven detritus anchored by their own hardened excretions. Both parties erase and overwrite each other’s marks on the walls. Both learn the other’s sound-patterns and fake them: a sequence of taps that means “safe” to a bug-mind but leads into a region primed for fire; a scrape-signal that imitates a “do not grow” smell, luring the people into a section that will soon erupt with growth and trap them.

Despite all this, cooperation happens in extremis.

When a truly vast internal convulsion begins — when the deep-heart has found a rich new seam or must shed some load — every being that can feel the coming quake knows: the local map is about to be redrawn. Pillars will topple; shafts will open; whole chambers may be sealed forever. In those hours, you might see a child of the Kept Gaps clinging beside a chitinous flank, both pressed flat against a relatively stable wall-point, both listening with equal intensity through their bones. Neither will strike. The enemy is, for the moment, the same for both: motion too big to fight.

At the very bottom of this world, where you imagined the blistered heart pressed to raw stone, the people do not go. Not often. It is too hot there, too chemically hostile, the air too thick with undiluted exhalations. But legends come up from the few scouts who have dared, wrapped in extra layers of filtration and walking on temporary bridges grown for that single, suicidal trip.

They say that down there, the plant-flesh is less differentiated, more like the tissues of something embryonic. That its glow is not amber but a bloodier, almost painful crimson. That the booms are louder, more like coughs. Some claim to have seen voids in that flesh, smooth-sided shafts that descend deeper still, not lined with growth at all — perfectly bare, smooth rock tubes. The world-organism recoils from them, refuses to colonize. From these holes, faint drafts rise: air of a different composition, edged with a chill unlike anything known here.

The children love these stories. They imagine that beyond those shafts is another world, one where the air is thin and watery and where light comes from a distant point instead of from everywhere at once. The elders, underneath their skepticism, are uneasy. They know that any true emptiness is dangerous. Growth abhors it.

The people of this place live by embracing that principle and defying it, both at once. They are caretakers of absences, carving and re-carving small oases of relative predictability inside a body that never stops trying to be whole.

So who lives here?

A folk whose bones know the beat of peristaltic pillars as surely as your ears know rain. Climbers with hands that are also hooks and feet that are also vises, whose fur is dust-filter and camouflage and sense-organ. Gardeners who prune a god-organism by knife and by fungus, who burn it in hair-thin lines to coax it away from their beds. Toolmakers who must assume that anything left unattended will be eaten, grown over, or turned into somebody else’s scaffold.

They live in perpetual negotiation: with quarry that might be structure, with predators that might be neighbors, with a landscape that is also a physiology. Their culture is the art of making room — not once, but continuously, against something that finds any room intolerable and seeks, always, to fill it.
