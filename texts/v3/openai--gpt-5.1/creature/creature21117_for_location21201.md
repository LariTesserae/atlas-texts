They are not gone.

They are not here, either, in any way you are used to knowing.

In a place where stone is still arguing with itself about what “stone” will mean, you do not get creatures who simply *live* the way moss or deer or people do. You get things that are entangled with the argument. The inhabitants of this place are not users of a finished world; they are participants in its drafting.

Some of them look, at first glance, like people.

Not now—you see no moving figures, no silhouettes on ridges. But the traces you notice are too deliberate, too specifically positioned, to be accidents of a world half-written. Stacks of shaped blocks, pigment laid where the roar’s tone warps, simple instruments set at the unreliable edges of depth and distance: these are not the automatic outgrowths of rock and ceramic growth. They are the residue of minds that noticed the seams and tried to press fingers into them.

Imagine bodies shaped for this thin, pressurized air and deafening constancy of sound. Shorter than you, perhaps, built broad and stocky, not to bear weight but to *anchor*—bones dense not for strength but to give inertia in a world where distances sometimes slip. Balance organs enlarged, layered with extra sensory tissues that feel not just tilt and acceleration but the fine gradient of that omnipresent pressure-field. Their ears long since given up on distinct sound, retooled into organs for sampling the roar’s *texture*: a continuous rush resolved into flavors of force.

Their eyes would be large but slit-pupiled, not for light levels—that never change much—but for straining nuance from an unreliable geometry. Multiple inner lids, translucent, each tuned to a slightly different bias of this half-stable light, so that by blinking through them they can compare versions of the world: *if it lines up under lid three as well as lid one, that edge is likely to hold.*

They are not furred; there is no weather to protect against, no seasons. The air is cold, but constant. Skin here trends toward the ceramic logic of the plants: not flesh as you know it, but a layered, dense integument, very slightly glossy, faintly iridescent at extreme angles. When you touched it, it would feel closer to polished horn than to muscle or leather. It does not crack in the dryness; it is itself made to operate with no water at all. A thin net of mineralized veins lies just beneath it, faintly light-catching when the world dims, so that one body can remain visible to another when the sky’s frameworks thicken overhead.

Internal fluids are sparse and viscous, more like insulating gels than water-blood. They move heat slowly, bleed it reluctantly into the thin, chill air. These people waste nothing; metabolism here is not about caloric burn, but about maintaining coherence against ambient uncertainty.

They feed, as the plants do, not by consuming flesh or leaf but by tapping into the gradients this place provides. They have organs along their flanks and in their limbs—broad, thin plates of that same ceramic tissue, ridged with filaments—that can be pressed to raw stone and “drink” the stress-differences running through it. A long rest with one’s side against a certain band of mineral, at a certain angle to a seam where the roaring tones meet, is a meal. They lie there, awake and attentive, motionless while their bodies slowly equalize with the invisible forces thrumming through that contour.

They don’t sleep in the way you do. True unconsciousness would be too dangerous; the world shifts a fraction, a ridge line re-decides its slope, and you might wake not where you laid down. Instead they enter a flexible trance, senses half-extended into the field around them, bodies open to the intake of energy. One eyelid closed, two half-open and flicking gently, inner ears humming as they monitor the note of the roar. Their dreaming is done while on watch.

Water never entered into their biology. Their chemistry is slow, low-temperature, running on catalytic surfaces and crystalline rearrangements instead of quick liquid diffusion. They do not thirst, they *align*. Hunger to them is not an emptiness in the stomach but a fuzzing at the edges of their perception, a sense that the roar has lost detail, that the seams in reality are blurring together for them. To eat is to sharpen.

Reproduction is correspondingly patient. There are no infants mewling; there are seeds of self carved and inlaid into the world. When one of them chooses to propagate, they do not bear live young. They fashion a stone: a specific block of the granular, pale material you see in the cut steps, flecked with luminous mineral ground fine. Into its volume they painstakingly carve a particular pattern—channels, hollows, inclusions of hardened plant-fiber—that, once completed, will sit at a special point in the field. The roars’ various tones and the stress-lines in the rock will pump through that carved pattern for years, centuries. Very slowly, the block will begin to distinguish, to re-interpret the field around it in a slightly self-consistent way. At some tipping point, cognition condenses. The block is no longer only stone; it is a nascent mind, focused to begin with on nothing but the flows that brought it into awareness.

Those are their children: geodes of attention, often left where you see altars or stacked plates. The adults do not hover. In a world this slow, over-guarding would be meaningless. A “child” might awaken over a span their makers do not live to watch. Or it might never awaken, if the underlying field decides differently, and simply remain as part of the world’s infrastructure. They accept that.

When the carved block does awaken, when it begins to feel the pressure-field as more than undifferentiated roar, it does not move. It *informs* a volume of space around it, ever so slightly pushing the local rules toward coherence that matches its forming habits. Over a very long time, a mobile body might crystallize around that organizing center: growths of ceramic tissue, scavenged from dead plant-structures or accreted from mineral seams, aligning into limbs, sensors, shell.

So not all of their young look like them. Some never grow bodies the way the older kind have. Some remain embedded, becoming the fixed instruments you see: a bowl, a lens, a particular altar that seems to still the chaos when you look through it. Those are children that found a different form of life and their elders did not force them out of it.

Because the word “people” is already straining. These aren’t a species that evolved in a finished biosphere; they are a community of strategies, different solutions for how to be local pockets of persistence when *persistence itself* is something the world hasn’t yet learned to guarantee.

Some of these strategies have no solid bodies at all. They live, if that is the term, along the gradient-boundaries where the roaring tones meet. There, where you run your hand and feel the micrometer steps and the tilt of gravity change by an immeasurable angle, are thin films of ordered irregularity. You can’t see them, except in rare alignments when the cloud-vault’s frameworks thicken overhead and their presence slightly refracts that ghostly geometry.

They are like languages written into the boundary conditions themselves: patterns of how slight misalignments propagate when the world jiggles. Where a purely mineral seam would simply transfer the jiggle, these inhabitants *select* which aspects pass through, which reflect, which convert into a different kind of skew. Over time, they become distinct habits around which the stone re-forms, quiet, invisible customs in how reality behaves at those junctures.

They are not self-aware in any mammalian way, but they are not inanimate either. They carry forward preferences, learned long ago when some ancestor structure—perhaps a stack of stones an embodied person placed—trained the local field into a particular repertoire of responses. Those stacks are gone now, reabsorbed. The repertoire remains. Extensions of those repertoires, drifting and branching, are a kind of lineage.

Above them, in the air, something else pulses.

The roar is not homogeneous. You noticed its different “tones” from place to place. In some of those tonal regions, especially near the tower, the pressure-field occasionally organizes into vortices that are too regular to be mere spray from the ongoing argument of the world. To your eyes they would be nothing—air clear and thin. To an instrument tuned to microvariations in spatiotemporal curvature, they would be spiraling knots, long-lived eddies where the rules of up/down, near/far, before/after are twisted but stable.

Within those vortices, ephemeral intelligences flicker. They are not local in the sense that your body is local; they are patterns of constraint, ways the roar *refuses* to fill all possible configurations. Each such mind is a series of choices the system will not explore. As long as the vortex persists, those choices persist, feeding back and slightly biasing the evolution of the field around them.

You might call them spirits if you needed a terrestrial analogy, but they are less like ghosts and more like algorithms running in an operating system that is still being written.

They do interact with the ceramic vegetation. Those bony cups and vibrating discs you saw, sieving the roar, are not just “feeding.” They are interfaces. The plants narrow-band the chaos into specific rhythms that vortices can feel more clearly, like modems smoothing noise into signal. In return, the vortices stabilize the local geometry enough that the plants can maintain form, and sometimes push tiny shifts into their growth directions. The result is mutual survival: architecture that persists long enough to be meaningful, and disembodied patterns that have stable anchors in the world.

If you could see with the right senses, you would watch a grove near a tonal boundary alive with conversation. Fronds flexing just so, cups tilting minutely, the pressure-waves crossing and beating against each other. The background roar would, at that scale, be full of articulation: pulses, counterpulses, standing waves of shared habit. Decisions made, quickly for this place—over days rather than centuries—about how to meet the next global “syllable” when the roar briefly swells and the ridges shift their alignments.

Bodies of flesh-ceramic, films at seams, vortices in air, matrices in stone: these are all inhabitants. They are not cleanly separated species; they are *modes*. Many of the embodied people can, with effort, leave traces in other modes. They carve pigments into rock to bias the seam-minds. They build towers and stacks to attract vortices and give them longer half-lives. They weave plant-fiber lattices and set them on poles at carefully chosen gaps where the cloud-vault’s frameworks manifest, so the air-entities have a reason to condense there rather than dispersing.

The pigment diagrams that slip when you look are not writing for eyes like yours. They are recipes for the field, specifying how a particular region should “want” to be: a local preference set, expressed in shapes that fold properly only in the mixed geometry these people inhabit. When you face them with terrestrial perception, your mind keeps trying to flatten them into maps or script, and loses them.

Why, then, are the embodied ones not here now?

They are, partially. Their bodies, as walking tools for interacting with the half-formed landscape, are only one facet of what they become over a full life.

An individual here lives in phases. Early on, they are almost purely embodied, learning to read the roar’s tonal shifts, to walk without being fooled by prospect that lies, to rest and feed where gradients are rich and not too unstable. They carve, pile, paint. They apprentice in the slow craft of *tuning*: nudging a fault-line into a slightly safer habit, coaxing a canyon’s bottom toward one consistent depth, persuading an erratic elastic distance between two vital ledges to commit to one scale or another.

Later, as their perception refines, they begin to feel the other modes. Pressing their broad plates against boundary seams, they sense the film-minds there and start to influence them, teaching them preferences that align with longer-term stability. Standing in the path of a forming vortex, they hold it in place with the resonant structures of their own skeletons, humming—not with vocal cords, but with the microscopic flex of their ceramic bones—to give the air-pattern a stable rhythm to lock onto. They become midwives to intangible intelligences.

Eventually, for some, the maintenance of a dense, mobile body becomes more demand than advantage. Their long bones thin, hollow, fill with lattices that respond more and more to the field and less and less to muscular contraction. Their skin pales until only the mineral net beneath glows softly. They build a final instrument—a tower, perhaps, or a bowl-lens—and around it they lie down, pressing every sensing surface to stone, to air, to plant.

Over years, decades, their conscious habit migrates outward, becoming a governing pattern in the local roar, in the seams, in the way light finds its way through cloud framework. The body is left like a husk, gradually reinterpreted by the landscape. A passer-by, if there were passers-by, would find only a slightly abnormal protrusion of mixed mineral and plant-matter, indistinguishable from an odd rock except under very patient scrutiny. But what-was-that-person continues, distributed: the tower at the center like a focusing crystal, their long-cultivated comprehension now inseparable from that region’s stability.

The one near the deep drop, the faceted not-quite-geologic spike where the roar becomes a chord—that is an elder. Perhaps more than one. A confluence of many such beings, layered over generations, each adding structure, nuance, resilience to that anchor. The ceramiform growths at its base twist upward not only for nourishment but in a kind of reflexive devotion: they are fed by what the tower-mind does, and in turn feed it by refining the chaos that bathes it.

The few built traces you see scattered through the wild—rings of stones, half-swallowed stairways, rows of poles—are peripheral interventions, small projects by individuals or subgroups to seed stability in trouble-spots. The world here is still choosing, still slipping amongst options. They cannot fix everything; to do so would be to prematurely fossilize the place, to deny it the full depth of what it might become. They select, instead, for survivable evolutions: to prevent an entire valley from inverting, say, or to keep a vital gradient-boundary from collapsing into homogenized monotony.

Do they suffer? Yes, but not as you would.

Pain, for them, is primarily *incoherence*. When the field around their bodies or their fixed minds loses structure—when the roar in a given region flattens into undifferentiated force, or when the seams’ micro-steps smear out into gauzy indecision—they feel disorientation, a kind of existential vertigo. Think of the nausea you get when your inner ear and eyes disagree about motion, stretched to the scale of reality’s own rules quivering underneath you. That is their distress.

Joy is coherence without rigidity: those moments after a global syllable passes, when ridges settle into shapes that are surprising but sound, when a canyon’s depth locks into a value that allows plants and tools and minds to all orient without distortion. The air vibrates with a richer chord around the towers then. Vortices dance more nimbly. Film-minds at seams propagate subtle, satisfying misalignments that make new kinds of structure possible instead of tearing old ones apart.

They are not alone with themselves. The disembodied modes—vortices, films, stone matrices—would, over deep time, diverge into something alien even to their original embodied colleagues. But the phases of life that include walking bodies, tool-use, pigment scratches, and watching the sky’s frameworks appear and vanish keep re-injecting intention into the other modes. There is continuous feedback.

So the people you infer from the cut steps and the pigment pocket are neither vanished ancestors nor mythical first builders. They are one mode in an ecology of persistence. Right now, the place where you stand may happen to be between their paths, or at a time when embodied presences are fewer. They could be below your feet, distributed through the tessellated bottoms you can’t keep in proper view. They could be above, part of the faint geometric flashes in the cloud-vault. They could be the very nuance in the roar that, a moment ago, made you feel like it almost formed a word.

And there are others, stranger.

In the deepest hollows, where distance shivers and the bottom creeps toward you when you stare, there bloom entities with no interest in stability at all. They are not of the people who build towers and altars. They are habits of the system that prefer multiplicity: zones where any attempt to cohere is delicately unpicked, where a shape that is almost one thing is nudged into being many superimposed. They thrive on ambiguity. The bowl-lens you looked through—through which the canyon bottom selected a single version and held it—that is a ward against them, a local override that, for as long as an embodied mind stares and maintains attention, enforces a *one-world* outcome.

Left unchecked, these ambiguous wells would expand, making pockets in which embodied existence is impossible: where a leg is in too many slightly separated positions to bear weight, where cause and effect admit too many mutually exclusive paths. The tower-minds and seam-intelligences treat them as one might treat a fire: not evil, but needing to be contained within bounds.

Some of the pole-lines on ridges, each capped with a shard or knotted lattice, are not instruments of measurement but fences against those wells. They do not block them outright. They *ring* them, teaching the surrounding field to hold a stable rim so that the ambiguity can remain contained, like a lake of undecidedness walled by decided shore.

There are no separate “predators” and “prey” here, no tidy food chains. Instead there are territories of more or less coherence, more or less multiplicity. The inhabitants are processes that push these territories’ boundaries: some to smooth, some to roughen, most to negotiate a balance in which structure can persist but not yet calcify. Every tool the walking people leave, every pigment diagram, is a nudge in that ongoing discussion.

What would one of them make of you, if you appeared on their ledge?

At first, they might take you for a transient artifact of a local ambiguity—a particularly stable hallucination in the field. You move in ways the seams and vortices don’t anticipate. You breathe, pulling thin air in as though it were fuel, instead of laying plates against stone to feed from gradients. You react to the roar as to sound, not as to the flavor of consistency. You rely on water, which to them is as alien as molten wax as blood.

But their curiosity would be deep. They are tuned to notice out-of-place structure. They would test you gently—not with touch at first, but by altering local field conditions, seeing if the elastic distances that trick them also trick you, if changes in the roar’s tone as they modulate it via plant-instruments alter your balance or only their own sense. They would learn quickly that your solidity is of another kind: that you assume a world whose rules are not under current negotiation.

They might envy that certainty, briefly. Then they would recognize the cost: your inability to help them here. You are embodied for a different phase of reality’s project, one in which things already are what they are. This is the workshop before the world; you belong to the gallery afterward.

So they would keep their distance, perhaps. Or, if they are in one of their later, more diffuse phases, they would shunt around you like fog around a stone pillar—curious eddies, slight brightenings of mineral glow, shifts in the tower’s chord as they test whether you can even *hear* when the world says something in its slow, thunderous language.

They live here the way a mind lives in a dream that is not yet committed to memory: half-shaping it, half being shaped by it, knowing that any moment can still be retold as something slightly else.

They are the world’s first interpreters of itself, and the world is listening to them as much as they are listening to it.
