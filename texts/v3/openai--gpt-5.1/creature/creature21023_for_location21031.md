They are sparse, but they are not primitive.

Who lives here were never farmers. They are not a people who remember forests or oceans, or if they do, those memories are so mythically remote that they function more as equations than as stories. Their world has always been gradients, fluxes, partial pressures. They grew up on the interface between rock and vapor, not soil and sky. They live here as a thermal species: everything about them — bodies, tools, rituals — is tuned to the management of heat and dissolution.

They call themselves nothing that translates cleanly as “humans” or “citizens.” What they name themselves is closer to “attendants,” “custodians,” “tenders of the exchange.” In your terms, they are a late-technical culture that chose — or was forced — to become a maintenance guild for a planetary-scale machine.

Physically, they are not dramatically alien at a glance: bipedal or near enough, a symmetry that still privileges forward motion. Taller than most of your kind would be in this gravity, but spare, tendon-thin, their mass arranged for endurance rather than strength. Shockingly little visible fat; anything that insulates too well against heat would be a liability here. Bone — or its analog — is almost latticework, optimized not to break under load but to survive long-term thermal cycling. Their joints have slightly deliberate ranges of motion: not graceful dancers, but precise adjusters of valves.

Their skin is the most striking thing. It is not bare. It follows the logic of the place: semi-permeable, an interface technology in its own right. In naked zones — forearms, faces, necks — it is a matte, mineralized surface, sparsely veined with darker bands. Color runs along a narrow spectrum from clay-ash to scorched umber, with no fragile pinks or high contrasts. Embedded in this matrix, like very fine, dark freckles, lie micro-valves and pores not quite biological and not quite artificial: shuttered openings that can exude salts, bind or release water, alter local emissivity. When they stand still, you can see their skin bloom in slow patterns of dampness and drying, as if they were perpetually adjusting their own boundary layer.

They wear clothing, but it is not worn as a second skin so much as a removable environmental interface. Garments seal at critical joints: neck, wrists, ankles, waist. The outer surfaces are laminated composites that resist abrasion and chemical attack; the inner faces wick and redistribute. Cooling channels and sorbent layers run through them. Where you would expect ornament — jewelry, personal flair — they carry connection points: standardized ports for plugging into tools, measuring rigs, those near-invisible lines that snake along the walkways. Individuality shows, but in subtle ways: the cut of a condensation hood, the calibration marks scratched into a personal tester, the way they’ve let mineral accretions crust along the seams of a favored coat.

Their faces are adapted to the dimness of this washed-out world. Eyes are large, but not luminous; the sclera is muted, the irises deep, almost colorless wells that drink in low contrast. The pupils can iris down to pinpoints when they look up close at a process flare or a diagnostic glow. Lashes are sparse, brows almost nonexistent — there is little loose dust here, only the slow settlement of mineral films. Noses are narrow, nostrils guarded by fine, ciliated membranes. Breathing is measured, precise; every inhalation is a balance between taking in enough oxygen and not overloading their lungs with volatile burdens. You can hear some of them exhale with a faint, controlled hiss, venting into micro-scrubbers integrated into their suits.

Their hearing is less tuned to high chirps and more to slow, structural sounds: a minute change in the timbre of a reactor’s tremor, the altered rhythm of a pump farther down the terrace, the way the basin presents its deep rumble through stone. Tiny ossicles in their inner ears are oversensitive to infrasonics. Some have augmented that with devices: bone-conduction loops at their temples that whisper machine-status codes and geologic telemetry directly to their hearing nerves.

Where they are most unlike you is not in anatomy, but in metabolism and cognition.

They run hotter inside than you do, but in a narrower band — a tight coupling to the thermal regime they inhabit. Fever is not a symptom of sickness so much as a mode of work: they can, for short periods, let their baseline spike, using specialized enzymes that only become efficient at those higher temperatures. Complex cognitive tasks, the kinds of problem-solving that require fine-grained pattern detection in chaotic flows, are often scheduled to coincide with these self-induced “burn phases.” Afterwards, they will deliberately seek cooler cavities, drape themselves in heat-drawing fabrics, and enter a shallow, controlled torpor while their systems recalibrate.

Their thinking has grown around the city’s purpose. They are not, by temperament, abstraction-disdaining pragmatic laborers nor detached theorists; they are both, fused. Since birth they have been educated in the language of flux: phase diagrams, reaction chains, stress-strain maps, equilibrium curves. Where another culture might read weather in clouds, they read it in condensation patterns on a pipe, in the particular acrid-sweet scent that means a trace impurity has risen beyond tolerance in a vent plume. To them, the city is legible as a script continually being written—one they can read with eyes, skin, and instruments.

They do not live densely on the surface because their real habitation extends inward, down into the rock. The terrace-fronted dormitories you passed are only the thinnest skin of their architecture. Behind the visible city, galleries have been cored into the plateau, not in neat, orthogonal grids but in branching, fractal networks tuned to stress lines and heat flows. The inside of this place is more organ than building: circulation routes aligned with coolant veins, communal halls located where converging conduits create comfortable microclimates, nursery-clusters set in the most thermally stable pockets reduced to a gentle, artificial “spring” of warmth.

Within those inner spaces, the atmosphere softens. The light remains low, but it loses the smoldering amber and shifts toward cooler hues: muted blues and soft greens produced not by any nostalgia for forests but by calibration — these wavelengths interfere least with the diagnostic colors they need to see in instruments and indicator bands. Walls are smooth but not stark; they too are coated with thin mineral layers, but here those layers are deliberately patterned, etched with marks that are half aesthetic, half data: historical plots of reactor output, stylized diagrams of changes in the basin’s water chemistry over generations, abstract representations of the plateau’s sub-surface structure.

Their social units are organized less around blood and more around process. There are lineages, yes — they track descent, heritable traits, genetic diversity — but what matters most is the Process House you are attached to. A Process House is a community, a workplace, a school, and a ritual cohort all in one, centered on a particular domain of the city’s activity:

- A House of Condensate, tending the long columns and their radiative wings, responsible for balancing the water budget of not just this basin, but its linked neighbors.

- A House of Deep Exchange, whose initiates spend much of their lives in the intermediary galleries between the city and the hotter lithic depths, mediating heat trades with the underworld reservoirs.

- A House of Surface Forms, which maintains the weeping walls, monitors the growth of mineral veils and mats, and studies the strange, almost-living domes and films as both risk and opportunity.

- A House of Structure, overseeing the integrity of platforms, trusses, buried supports, and the careful redistribution of stress as thermal cycles slowly warp the plateau.

These Houses do not compete; they interleave like woven fabrics. It is not uncommon for an individual to be fostered across Houses if their particular aptitudes suggest it. That fostering is done early, marked not by dramatic test or contest, but by long, careful observation. They are never in a hurry to decide a person’s path — thermal systems punish haste.

There are children, but you will almost never see them near the exposed basins. Their first years are spent in interior crèches whose climates are rock-stable. They grow up playing with miniature flow systems — sealed, safe — where they can accelerate or slow tiny circulations, watch colored indicators show how a change here ripples out there. Puzzles are physical: rearranging channels in a heat board to move warmth without spilling too much to the environment; building delicate arches that must survive repeated heating and cooling without cracking. Only later are they introduced to the real city, taken up to observation galleries to watch the basin breathe.

Their myths, such as they have them, are about gradients. In some origin tellings, the world was once flat in energy — a dull, dead sameness. Life (in this version, “life” includes the city and its people together) began when a great difference opened: one side hot, one side cold. The story does not bother with why; it treats that rupture in equilibrium as a primordial given. What matters is the covenant that followed: to keep the difference from collapsing. To let it run down uncontrollably would be to dissolve everything back into dullness. Their ethical language is thus deeply entangled with thermodynamics: to waste a steep gradient is a small sacrilege; to preserve and channel it wisely is virtue.

Formally, they do have governance, but it too is more like process control than king-list. A Council of Balances meets not in a throne room but in a node-chamber where multiple major conduits intersect. They sit — or stand, or pace — with a live view of the city’s state displayed in abstracted form: color-bands crawling across the walls, low tones representing flux changes translated into sound. Decisions about expansion, dormancy, repair-priorities are made by consensus, but consensus here is mathematical: they look for parameter sets that keep key variables within acceptable ranges across long time horizons. There is politics, but it is technocratic and deeply conservative: they add nothing that they cannot integrate into their models.

Their relationship to the non-human systems — the carriages, the insectile maintenance units, the hovering tethered samplers — is intimate but not sentimental. These machines predate most of the current generation. Some predate their grandparents. A maintenance unit that has been functioning for two centuries is spoken of with a kind of quiet, professional respect, much as a veteran engineer would honor an old, reliable tool. They do not anthropomorphize them; they track their performance histories, their failure modes, the rare quirks that give each class its micro-identity.

They can build new machines, but not all types. Some of the city’s core systems are legacy technologies, inherited from an earlier, denser phase of habitation — perhaps even from a different culture, one that first raised these condensers and sunk those deep exchangers when the plateau’s climate was in a different regime. The current inhabitants understand these elder machines functionally but not comprehensively. They have repositories of preserved instruction-sets, materials they can no longer fabricate exactly, designs they dare not alter too much. House Deep Exchange in particular treats some of its oldest reactors as quasi-sacred: if one fails in a way they cannot fix, the whole basin’s thermodynamic balance would shift disastrously.

Because of that, there is a thread of unease woven into their otherwise controlled existence. They are stewards of systems they only partially own in knowledge. Whole disciplines among them are devoted to reverse-engineering, to simulation, to cautious experiments at the edges of the city’s envelope. In the interior galleries they maintain test-beds — scaled-down copies of critical components where they trial novel configurations or materials, watching over many cycles to see if unexpected instabilities arise. They run these trials for years before even considering applying them to the main infrastructure. Time, to them, has become a resource like water and heat: precious, but spendable over generations.

What do they eat in a place like this? Thermal gradients and mineral flows are not meals. Their sustenance is a stack of processes leaning on those gradients. In some protected cavities, they cultivate denser forms of the quasi-life that grows on the weeping rock: mats bred to produce complex organics in slow, layered accretions. They seed these with carefully tuned consortia of enzymes and simple self-copying structures that can store energy chemically. Think of them as mineral-bio loaves. Workers scrape them at intervals, then process the scrapings in hot digesters that break down the rock fraction, concentrate the organics, and fold them into paste-like foods.

They are not gourmands. Meals here are quiet, functional. However, they do differentiate between “raw-work” ration — fuel consumed quickly amid a shift — and “deep-rest” fare: richer, eaten in the cool interior during torpor weeks, when they allow themselves to taste. Taste, for them, runs along metallic and mineral lines more than sweet or sour: the pleasant tang of a particular sulfide, the mellow bite of iron complexes, the clean, almost musical flavor of certain silicates doped with trace volatiles. Their tongues are more finely tuned to such nuances than yours could easily be.

Very rarely, they will harvest from the wild basin: a carefully sampled lens of natural mat, a shaving from an especially intricate mineral dome where life-like structure has clearly found some new trick. These are treated as both delicacy and data — eaten only after replication in controlled conditions, studied for what they can teach about emergent pathways that the city’s own engineered systems have not found.

Their contact with the larger fauna — the long-backed, vane-crested creatures that move under condensers, the pale duct-worms and glass-chitin crawlers — is mostly observational. They neither domesticate nor exterminate them. To do so would be to disturb unknown feedback loops. House Surface Forms in particular maps their movements, correlating population fluctuations with changes in local chemistry or microclimate. Occasionally, when a rare pattern recurs — a certain creature always congregating when a process imbalance is brewing, say — they will incorporate that behavior as an informal early-warning signal. Such uses are not officially sanctioned, but they persist as practical folklore.

Emotionally, they are not cold, but their range has adapted to a world where sudden swings are dangerous. Outbursts are frowned upon, not because of some austere moral code, but because an untrained shout, a careless run, a thoughtless slap at an alarm can trigger cascade misunderstandings in a city always on the edge of multiple thresholds. Children are trained from early on in a kind of embodied patience: to move deliberately, to speak with weighted intent. In private spaces they laugh, they grieve, they argue — but even there, voices tend to stay low, gestures economical.

Their intimacy is subtle. It expresses in the sharing of calibration tasks: two people leaning together over a condenser’s control frame, hands overlapping as they fine-tune a valve to a shared rhythm. It shows in the acceptance of one’s thermal state by another: lying back-to-back in the cool galleries during torpor, learning each other’s heat signatures, trusting that your partner will rouse if a tremor shifts into something more dangerous. They form lifetime bonds, but not in rigid pairs alone; networked affiliations cross Houses, generations, and roles.

And why are they here, on this high, scarred plateau, in this smoldering dusk?

They are here because this is one of the last places where the planet’s ancient gradients remain exploitable. Whether through natural climate drift, catastrophic prior industry, or stellar complication, the old seas are gone or sterilized, the lowlands choked, the atmosphere thickened with particulates and vapors that trap heat and mute light. Up here, on the eroded roof-world, conditions balance on a knife-edge: hot enough to drive powerful flows, cool enough — barely — to sustain structured chemistry.

The network of basins you glimpsed at the horizon is their lifeline and their labor: a distributed array of condensers and exchangers that:

- Capture what little water still rises in convective plumes,
- Draw up volatiles and metallic vapors from below,
- Split, recombine, and store these into forms that can support ongoing technics and the fragile ecologies they require.

In some senses they are climate managers, though they would not phrase it so grandly. Their immediate goal is local: to keep this lattice of basins from slipping into either arid burnout or runaway saturation. But at scale, the system they maintain is nudging the whole planet along a controlled cooling curve, bleeding off stored heat into deep sinks, trying — against vast inertia — to re-open the range of possible worlds.

They will not see the end of that project. Nor will their children, nor likely their many, many-times great-grandchildren. They know this. Time, for them, is not an arrow but a slowly unfurling spiral they can only glimpse a fraction of. They maintain archives not just of data but of procedure: instructions on how to interpret drift, how to recognize the signatures of approaching thresholds they themselves will never face.

Periods of apparent emptiness — the terraces quiet, only a handful of figures crossing a gantry in an hour — are not signs of decline, but of chosen underactivity. They run their city in throttled modes as much as peak modes, cycling systems to allow for rest, maintenance, and simply to avoid pulling too hard on fragile global balances. To an outside visitor accustomed to roaring industrial centers, this would feel like underpopulation; to them, it is correct population: just enough minds and bodies to watch, adjust, and hand off.

In their rare rituals, they do not gather around flames or images of gods. They gather around process traces. Once per major thermal cycle — longer than your year, shorter than an ice age — representatives from all Houses convene in the core node-chamber. There, projected across the walls, are the stacked histories of the basin’s key variables: water held, heat exchanged, compounds synthesized, emergent life-forms cataloged. They stand in silence while these traces play at accelerated speed, the last cycle’s story told in lines and colors and slow, changing chords. Only afterwards do they speak, offering brief commentaries, dedications to those who kept certain lines within bounds, acknowledgments of those lost to accidents or illness.

Then they step out along the terraces again, into the heavy, smoldering air. They touch railings slick with condensation, feel the background tremor of reactors underfoot, smell the faint metal-sour breath of the weeping stone. They are, each of them, small and temporary, but they belong to a continuity: of rock being re-patterned, of gradients being written and preserved, of almost-life slowly complexifying under their watch.

This basin, with its sweating walls, its slow proto-biologies, its quiet industrial heart, is thus not merely where they live. It is what they are part of. They are not rulers of this place, nor simple workers within it, but one more stratum in its layered, ongoing process: a mobile, thinking layer tasked with tending every other layer as the planet, under a dim, hot sky, tries to find a new long equilibrium that can hold something like life.
