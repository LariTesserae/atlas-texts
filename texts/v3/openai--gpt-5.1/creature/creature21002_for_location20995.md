They are people of dependency, not dominion.

Whatever names they call themselves by, they live as if the ridge were kin: an elder body whose moods and flows must be listened to, not commanded. Their lives are braided into its tissues in the same practical, unfussy way that vines are braided into arches. If they vanished, the place would not become “wild” again; it would simply become less articulate.

They are small against their home. Not physically much smaller than you—most stand somewhere between your shoulder and your eye—but scaled mentally to a world where everything above knee height can move, thicken, sag, or sprout. Their strength is rope-muscle and tendon, the strength of people who climb more than they walk. Shoulders are broad from hauling living vines into tension; forearms are corded from pinching, twisting, and stripping fibers. The older you look, the more you see a particular posture: knees always a fraction bent, as if expecting the surface underfoot to yield.

Skin comes in a spectrum of earth and bark—deep browns, copper, old-ivory browned by damp sun. Constant wetness keeps it smooth; elders wrinkle more like softened leather than parchment. Hair is usually grown long, but never left loose. It is braided as thoroughly as the architecture: tight plaits along the skull, coils wound into living loops of vine, ropes that can be tucked under hoods of leaf or bound back when climbing. Children’s hair is more chaotic, threaded with leaves and seed-fluff caught in play, but even they are rarely completely unbound. Stray ends tangle in aerial roots; they learn fast.

Their clothes are layers of plant: cloth-bark, felted moss, and woven bast. Some shrubs here put out thick, papery sheaths that can be peeled, softened in water, and worried between palms until they become supple like chamois; others produce long inner fibers that can be beaten and netted into loose, breathable textiles. Tunics and wraps hang close to the body, tied at shoulder and hip rather than tailored, always with enough give to accommodate a long reach or a sudden scramble. The palette is all the vegetative shades the place offers—muddied greens, buff and umber, the silver underside hue of particular leaves—shot through with deliberate bands of brighter color: a sash of fungal orange, a collar edged in berry-dye blue. Color marks role more than wealth.

Feet are mostly bare. Soles are toughened and, in older adults, slightly ridged from a lifetime of gripping root and liana. For certain kinds of work—handling caustic saps, walking newly soaked walkways—they wear sandals of cured leaf or thin, layered bark bound with vine. Gloves are rarer, used by particular specialists, because bare fingers are their primary tools. The first lesson children learn is how not to crush soft cambium when they climb.

Their tools are few and well-known. Everyone can name who made which blade by eye. Stone knives are leaf-thin and glossy; they live in sheaths braided directly into belts, never set down far from the body. Metal is precious but not revered; a copper adze is valued because it can split a buttress cleanly without sending shock deep into the living tissues. Bone awls, thorn-prickers, scrapers of shell or horn wrap in leaf-rolls beside bundles of seed, all smelling faintly of sap.

They scar themselves, but only in dialogue with the plants. Forearms and calves often show raised, pale lines where vines have grown deliberately against the skin for a season, trained around arm or leg and then cut back, leaving the memory of the contact in both bodies. Some wear bracelets that are literally alive: a loop of stem grafted to itself, leaves sprouting outward like small, shifting charms. It is a quiet status marker to keep such a symbiont healthy year after year.

They do not decorate much with inert things; even their jewelry sprouts. Earrings are often the toughened bases of fallen fruits drilled and hung from holes grown—not pierced—into the lobes: when children are very young, a small bud from a gentle vine is tied against the ear. It pushes a tendril through under the guidance of an elder midwife who knows how to pinch and prod it so that it curves and loops rather than spreads. Once it hardens, it leaves a painless, clean tunnel of scar that can be refitted with other ornaments. The process takes weeks; for the child, it is an early lesson in patience with slow, living works.

They live in clusters rather than nuclear families. A “household” is a vertical slice of the ridge: a stack of platforms, rooms, and hollows connected by grown stairs and nets. Three generations share these stacks, along with anyone adopted, married in, or simply absorbed. Children drift upward as they age, sleeping higher, closer to the weather and the birds, then often settling lower again when they have their own infants, where access is easiest and the risk of long falls is less.

No one owns the tree that holds their home the way another culture might own a plot of ground. They speak of “our trunk,” “our gallery,” but the pronoun is about stewardship and usage, not possession. The oldest person in a household is less its ruler than its interpreter for the plant: they can tell, by subtle shifts in color and creak, when a root needs to be relieved of weight, when a buttress is preparing to hollow. Their authority rests on having listened to this one giant being for most of a life.

Children here are not told “don’t climb”; they are taught how. Toddlers learn on the lowest lattices, over shallow growth where a fall only means bruises and scared tears. By the time they reach adolescence, they can move one-handed along a wet rail of vine while carrying a basket in the other, feet finding footholds in half-shadow by feel. Play is constant motion: chasing along lateral branches, swinging from the slung roots like pendulums, dropping in controlled falls from one platform to another, catching themselves on nets that sag and then rebound.

The people who live highest—the canopy-tenders—almost never fully descend. They sleep in small, densely woven nests along the upper platforms, lightly thatched with overlapping leaves. Their bodies show it: forearms speckled with the tiny scars of insect bites; hair salted early from the harshness of constant cloud and wind; a lightness in their movements from living where any misstep could send them through meters of space, even if the fall will likely be broken by intervening growth. They are the keepers of sky-holes, watching how the light discs travel, pruning just enough to maintain those moving windows without weakening the overarching vault.

Lower, at the level where people breathe mist at the ankles but still see sky in slices, live the coordinators of flow. They tend the water. These are the channelers: men and women whose days move along the gutters and weeping trunks, thick fingers stained green-brown from handling algae, nails worn and darkened. They listen with their palms against root and wall, feeling the faint, slow throb when an inner conduit is full, the hollow resonance when it runs low. If a storm is coming, they relocate basins, reinforce certain gutters with looped vines. They know exactly how much seep each household may tap in dry spells without stressing the ridge’s own thirst.

Deepest in the body, in the darker halls where green light filters down in shafts and bats flicker along the ceilings, dwell the keepers of decay. Their work is the least visible and most essential: directing rot. They move through the half-hollows, pruning out dead vine, teasing apart mats of fallen leaves to expose their underlayers, guiding certain fungi and discouraging others. Their hands smell always of earth-analog, and their tunics are stained with crescent shapes of white and ochre from carrying big fungal bodies that crumble as they are moved.

They are the ones who decide where a fallen bulk will be allowed to sink into the massif and where it must be lifted away to keep a corridor from collapsing. With simple levers and rope, and sometimes with the help of certain large herbivores bribed with choice fruits, they redistribute weight. In doing so they are conducting not just the architecture but its history, keeping the ridge from growing too fast in any one direction, nudging it back toward balance as it slumps and surges.

Some among them are more entwined still: graft-workers. These are the calmest hands, the most patient bodies. They perform the slow surgeries that give the settlement its distinctive shapes. Where two young trunks must be fused to make an arch, they will score the bark with tiny cuts, align the wounds, and bind them in living ropes for seasons at a time, returning every few weeks to ease or tighten the bindings as the plant heals into itself. Where a stair is desired, they choose a vertical growth and nip every bud except those that protrude in just the right spiral.

Their art, if you can call it that, lies in thinking in years rather than days. A child might initiate a graft they will not walk upon as a finished stair until they are an adult. Among these people, the line between architecture and horticulture has vanished; both are simply forms of relationship.

They do not write on bark or weave letters into cloth, but they leave marks. A scored pattern on a buttress tells any passer how weight may safely shift there. A line of small, wedge-shaped holes near the base of a platform announces that its supports are young and should not be overloaded with stored fruit. Wayfinding is handled by living signposts: particular vines whose flower color or leaf-shape has been coaxed into a conventional code. A spray of crimson trumpets at a junction might mean “this way to the main basin,” while a pale, pendulous inflorescence at ankle height signifies “steep drop beyond; children guided.” These conventions are taught as seriously as tool-use.

Speech is layered like their soundscape. At close range, they talk in a rolling, liquid language that carries well in wet air, full of soft consonants and nasal vowels, with a set of sharp, percussive clicks reserved for sudden warnings. Over distance, they use calls and patterns drummed on wood. Each major trunk-hall has one or more designated “tongue-roots”: projecting ribs of living wood that, when struck with the heel of a hand or a mallet, carry low sounds into the body of the ridge. Tapping codes differ between households but share certain universal markers. Three slow, evenly spaced thumps might mean “come, water here,” while a rapid alternating pair from two directions means “moving load above you, look up.”

There is also whistle-work, mimicked by birds. Children are encouraged to whistle distinctive family patterns; local birds take them up, weaving them into their own songs. In time, the forest speaks pieces of human language back to itself in fragments, creating a ghost chorus. The people live with that echo; sometimes, an urgent call must be verified by context, because a bird may cry “falling!” in the middle of a quiet afternoon simply because it enjoys the drama of the sound.

Food is more scattered than centralized. There are no fields, but there are zones of coaxed abundance. Certain tangles of branch at shoulder height have been pruned to encourage particular fruits: wax-skinned drupes with crisp, watery flesh; elongated pods whose interiors are thick with starchy pulp that can be dried, pounded, and steamed into dense cakes. Other plants, naturally bitter, are repeatedly wounded in small, nonlethal ways until their chemistry shifts toward something sweeter or more nutritious; over generations, lineages of “domesticated” vine diverge visibly from their wild cousins.

Meals gather where opportunity arises, not always in the same place. A particularly heavy fruiting may call half the settlement to a certain platform: nets are stretched, baskets hooked into sockets on the walls, and for an hour the “street” becomes a processing floor. Knives flash; seeds are sorted into living bowls still attached to their stems; children suck juice from fibrous strands and then fling the flattened remains to attending animals who know to keep a polite distance until fed.

Meat is rarer, taken more opportunistically than by organized hunt. Pit-falls are impossible in this architecture of ceaseless growth, but there are trap-corridors: places where the living walls narrow and nets can be dropped with a single cut to a restraining vine. The creatures they catch are mostly mid-sized climbers, quick and clever. To eat one is an event, a sharing that binds the group that made the kill. Bones are never discarded casually; they are given to tool-makers, to be ground into awls, fishhooks for whatever swims in the deeper root-channels, and fine needles for teasing out fiber.

Fire exists, but it is a carefully leashed anomaly. In a body so full of living cellulose, flame is both tool and potential catastrophe. They do not let naked fire touch the architecture. Instead, they have grown fire-bowers: deep hollows whose inner walls are lined with thick, water-rich tissues that barely char. In these spaces, smoke is guided upward through chimney-plants—hollow, vertical growths whose inner surfaces are slick and moist. Over generations, humans have selected and encouraged lineages of these plants that can withstand warm, smoky air and deposit, in their inner linings, a tar-like residue that eventually hardens into a protective shell.

Only a few people in each household keep the knowledge of kindling: how to coax a coal from fungus that smolders slowly when dried, how to nurture it with shredded bast until it licks into flame. Fire is not kept burning constantly; instead, coals slumber in nested pots of ash and damp moss, traded between households as needed, so that flame never has to be coaxed from nothing. It is a subtle status to be the person whose lidded coal-pot rarely cools.

At night, these same people tend another light: bioluminescence. Many insects and some fungi here glow; the keepers of night coax them into usable patterns. Glowing beetles are encouraged to lay eggs on strips of bark that can be hung along treacherous drop-offs; certain bracket-fungi that emit a steady, low greenish light are transplanted into the ceilings of important meeting hollows. Children sleep under domes freckled with faint points, like a private sky.

Belief is not a simple hierarchy of gods, but a weave of stories about the ways bodies contain and exchange life. They have words that translate loosely to “the Deep Water” and “the Breath in Leaves,” but these are less entities than processes in their cosmology. The ridge itself is not worshipped so much as acknowledged as one elder among many: a particular personification of the wider habit of life to knit itself into vast, self-maintaining structures. Other ridges exist, they say, each with its own temperament. Stories of journeys between them are rare and half-mythic, involving days spent in suspended walkways through mist, following the calls of birds that never rest.

Those who pay closest attention to the slow shifts of growth—the graft-workers, the keepers of decay—often speak as if the ridge speaks back. They claim to sense preference in the way a vine leans when given two options, in the subtle sweetness or bitterness of a sap. Outsiders might call this animism; for them, it is simply respectful noticing. They do not believe they command the ridge; they believe they have learned its habits well enough to ask it to lean in certain directions, to grow here instead of there, to accept the load of a platform or the hollowness of a new chamber.

Death is handled by inclusion, not separation. Bodies are never burned—it wastes what might feed. Nor are they simply left to rot. When someone dies, their household chooses a place where the ridge is thinning, where a root has begun to sag or a gallery needs shoring. There, a carefully tended hollow is opened into the living wood, a pocket just large enough. The body is wrapped in leaf and fiber—layer on layer of plants that held special meaning for that person in life: the vine they most often tended, the fruit they favored, the moss they slept upon. The bundle is slid into the hollow. The graft-workers then stitch the wound closed not with dead cord but with living tendrils, guiding them so they pierce gently through shroud and flesh.

Over months, the plant takes the offering into itself. Stories say that you can hear, if you lay your ear against the bark in the right season, the faint signature of that person’s presence in the creak and rush of sap: a remembered laugh in certain winds, a turn of phrase carried in bird mimicry. Whether or not that is “true” is irrelevant; the ridge becomes sibling and cemetery, every stair and arch fattened in part by those who walked it before.

Conflict among them does not center on territory in the way you might expect; there is no “spare land” to seize. Instead, tension arises over patterns of relationship: who has the right to pick from a particular fruiting vine, whether a proposed new platform will divert too much light from a neighbor’s growth, how much sap may be bled for medicine from a stressed trunk. Their law is a body of precedent spoken aloud in councils, each case compared not to abstract concepts of guilt or innocence but to remembered situations and their living outcomes: “When we took too much from that northern buttress three flood-seasons ago, remember how it cracked in the drought. Do you wish the same here?”

They do have weapons, but they blend with tools. A pruning knife becomes a defensive blade with no change of grip. Some carry seed-pouches not only for planting but for throwing: certain pods explode into irritant dust when crushed, stinging eyes and lungs. Nets, normally used for catching fruit, can be flung to entangle a predator or a trespasser from another ridge. Yet open, lethal violence seems rare. The architecture punishes it; a body pushed in anger can tumble unpredictably, perhaps through no fault of its assailant, knocking others from platforms. There are old, cautionary tales about anger that started at one ledge and ended in an avalanche of bodies five levels below.

Visitors from beyond the ridge—if any—would be detected first by sound. Unfamiliar rhythms on wood, strange vowels in the air. The people here would react like their animals: a temporary surge upward, to observe from safety. They are not naive; they know that not every outsider will share their ethic of tend-and-negotiate. But the place itself grants them advantage: they know every braided rail by touch, every deceptive mat that will sag if stepped on with too much confidence.

If you lived among them long enough, your body would change. Your calves would learn the constant small adjustments that keep you balanced on curving, damp surfaces. Your hands would grow stronger in the particular way of clipping young stems without crushing them, of sensing when a piece of living architecture can accept your weight. You would begin to think in gradients instead of borders: not “wet/dry, safe/dangerous, inside/outside,” but “this much wetter than that, this much more likely to give way, this much more willing to grow if asked.”

You would stop seeking silence as a virtue; the layered noise would become your measure that things are as they should be. A sudden hush might startle you awake at night more surely than any shout. You would come to trust certain voices in the cacophony: the complaint-call of a particular monkey-thing that often spots snakes before humans do, the change in pitch of the main channel’s rumble that warns of an upstream blockage. Even your dreams might fill with trickling and leaf-rattle.

The people who live here are not “primitive caretakers of nature,” nor are they master-engineers in the sense of imposing clear designs. They are something else: artisans of ongoingness. They have chosen—over, and over, and over again—to remain in conversation with a place that is never finished. The ridge grows, sags, splits, devours its dead, offers new surfaces. They reply with pruning, grafting, binding, diverting. The settlement you stand in is not an endpoint but a mid-sentence, the current arrangement of a long negotiation between human intention and vegetal will.

Who lives here? A town-sized cluster of such negotiators. People whose ancestry has spent so many generations with their weight and warmth pressed directly onto living wood that they can no longer imagine a wall that does not respond, over time, to the touch of a hand. People who know—by necessity rather than philosophy—that civilization can be made not only out of hardness and resistance, but out of softness, saturation, and the slow attentiveness required to live within a breathing, dripping, unquiet body larger than themselves.
