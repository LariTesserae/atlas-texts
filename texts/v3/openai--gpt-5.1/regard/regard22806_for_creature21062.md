The first change is almost nothing.

No thunder, no tearing of stone, no sudden blaze of light. Just a pause: a brief, improbable stillness in which the hum of fliers, the scrape of shell-creatures, the murmur of traders, all seem to hover on the same held breath.

No one notices. Of course they don’t. It lasts as long as the span between two heartbeats.

But something in the basin does notice, because the basin itself is noticed.

*

The power that attends to this place is not above it, because above is only rock and old channels leading to an outside that is more idea than geography. It is not beneath it, because the Warmth Beneath is already a kind of god here, and this power is not interested in displacing that quiet ancestor.

It arrives—if arrival is the word—by recognizing that the basin is already a closed loop, already a whole. It does not burst in from elsewhere. It folds in from the edges of what already exists: from the exchange between breath and fungus, from the remembered songs laid in spine and nerve, from the slow, patient patience of stone.

Having decided to attend, it adopts a principle: do not break.

Do not break the cycles that have achieved a difficult balance. Do not break the people’s sense that they live by their own work. Do not break the old agreements between growth and animal, fire and dust. If anything is done here, it must be done in such a way that, if the power withdrew, the hollow would not collapse into dependency or despair.

So it chooses a scale.

Not continents, not even caverns.

One person.

*

She does not think of herself as chosen. She thinks of herself as tired.

Her ring tends the mid-north mats—those that climb the wall above and beyond the market platform, where the stone bulges inward, making hollows that are easy for mats to flood and greedy gliders to strip. It is careful work, seasonal in the long sense. The mats’ slow breathing sets the rhythm of her days.

She stands now on a narrow projection of rock above one such mat, a strap around her waist clipped to a hammered stone loop, her bare hands resting on the mat’s surface. It feels like an old, stiffened felt, thick and faintly warm, faintly yielding. Beneath her fingers, she feels the tiny cracklings of burrowing things, the slow, almost inaudible creak of the mat’s larger structures turning gases into density.

Her name, in her own tongue, is a pattern of stops and hums, but the gist of it translates to something like Holds-by-Listening. Mostly people call her Hesa.

Hesa is in her early strength: bones hard, lungs deep. Her eyes are wide and dark, their cross-shaped muscles flickering as she tests the light from a nearby fungus-plate. Her hands are scarred where mat-edges have cracked under her grip, where a startled glider once clamped needle-teeth around her fingers.

For the last dozen cycles, Hesa has had a problem: the mat under her care is thinning in an odd way.

Not the ordinary ebb that matches the basin’s long pulses—thicken, thin, thicken again. This is a strange border thinning, as if something in the rock behind it has cooled or withdrawn. The outer layers grow brittle faster, giving way under the tread of climbers, exposing sub-layers that should not yet be exposed. The burrowing things inside are rearranging their routes. Their rustling, once a slow, steady hiss, now has hiccups in it, gaps.

Hesa has done what she is supposed to do. She has reported it to her ring; they have sung the appropriate fragments of the growth-cycle chant, tracing the mat’s history in knotted cords. They have called in a carver to test the rock behind, chipping small cores from upper and lower sections. The stone is sound. The mat’s feeding routes seem clear.

Something unseen is wrong.

The basin has words for this sort of thing, but none of them are satisfying. “Hidden chill.” “Stone-wandering.” “Breath-lost.” Hesa, who listens more than she talks, thinks privately of it as “forgetting.” As if something in the mat is losing its place in the great exchange.

This thought troubles her. She dreams of mats she knows well slipping loose, lifting like shed skin away from the rock, drifting upward into the dim arch, leaving bare, cold stone behind.

Today she kneels, gloved now, because she is going to cut deeper than usual. There is a point where a patch has dulled too far. It must be removed, offered to the burn-pit or the decay troughs. She sets her chisel—a thin, flat sliver of bone with a stone-capped handle—against the mat and raises her mallet.

It is at the moment before impact, when her arm is drawn back and her body is most tense, that the power reaches through her.

Not into her mind. Not into her thoughts. It touches the correspondence between her skin and the mat, the way her bones are arranged to resist a fall, the way her lungs already understand the timing of the basin’s subtle air-currents.

It asks—not in words, but in a kind of structural query:

If you could hear this place more clearly—if every slow, tiny vibration in stone and growth were laid bare to you—what would you do?

Hesa is not aware of being asked. But somewhere deep in the layered reflexes that make her who she is, an answer rises, older than language.

I would listen, before I cut.

That is enough.

The mallet falls. The chisel bites. A strip of deadened mat peels away with a dry, tearing sigh.

And the world changes, almost not at all.

*

The first sign is a headache.

It comes that evening, when Hesa squats in the communal ledge-space outside her family’s cell, eating from a shallow bowl of mashed shell-creature and softened frond. Her father, who burns resin in the ring that feeds the market hearths, is telling a familiar story about the old collapse in the eastern wall, when a whole line of houses had to be abandoned and rebuilt further along.

Hesa has heard this story many times. She half-listens, half-watches the gliders tracing lines between the cell-doors across the chasm, the soft sway of a rope bridge under passing feet.

Then, without warning, a high, whistling shriek knifes through her skull.

Her hand jerks, spilling paste into her lap. The bowl clatters on the stone. Her father stops mid-sentence.

“Hesa?”

The sound is not in her ears. It’s as if someone has sliced a thin slit into her attention and torn it open. She hisses, hands flying up to press the sides of her head, fingers digging into her temples.

The shriek modulates. It becomes a layered chord: three, no, four strands of vibration, twisting together, seeking some shape. It is unbearable for two breaths. Three. Then it flickers, fades, leaves only a faint, receding echo behind—like the hum of a flier flock dispersing far above.

Hesa finds that she is kneeling on the rock, breath coming fast, sweat prickling along her hairline despite the dry air.

Her father has dropped his own bowl. Her co-mother has one hand on Hesa’s back, the other on her shoulder.

“Is it your head?” her co-mother asks.

Hesa nods, unable to speak just yet.

“Light?” her father asks. “Did a lamp flare? Were you too close to the burn?”

Hesa shakes her head. No flare. No—

Then she feels it: a second vibration, far softer, under everything else. A tremor in the stone beneath her knees. It is not the usual settle-creak of beams or the distant wing-roar of disturbed fliers. It is a faint, continuous thrum, like the far-off echo of a drum struck at regular intervals.

Hesa realizes she is holding her breath. She lets it out slowly. The second vibration persists: not painful, not loud, but insistent. It seems to be coming from everywhere. Or from nowhere. Or from deep under her.

She swallows.

“It is…not light,” she says carefully. “Something…sang. In my skull.” She taps her temple. “Then stopped. Now the stone hums.”

Her father’s brows draw together. His nostrils flare, filtering instinctively. He presses his palm flat to the ledge.

“I feel nothing,” he says, but his voice is not entirely confident.

Her co-mother, who tends glider routes and spends many hours in the hush of upper ledges, closes her eyes and leans forward, fingertips touching the rock.

After a long moment she nods, slow.

“Very faint,” she murmurs. “Like a breath held very long.”

Her father snorts, half with relief, half with unease.

“Perhaps the Warmth Beneath stretches in its sleep,” he says, groping for a familiar explanation. “Or some deep layer settles.”

Hesa wants to accept this. It would be so easy to fold that new hum into the basin’s known rhythms, to say, Ah, a new long cycle begins, we will mark it.

But it does not feel like that. Whatever moved through her skull was not the slow, indifferent shift of stone. It was quick. It was…curious.

She stares at her hands, at the faint sheen of resin on her skin.

“Did anyone else cry out?” she asks abruptly.

Her co-mother’s eyes open.

“I heard no one,” she says. She glances along the ledge. “And no one comes running.”

Hesa looks too. People are still talking, still eating, still moving along the paths and into their cells. A child laughs as a glider steals a scrap from their fingers. A trader calls out an offer. No one holds their head or looks shaken.

So it was only her.

The hum underfoot continues, neither growing nor diminishing.

Hesa licks her lips.

“May I be excused?” she asks, in the formal way one uses when acknowledging that something is wrong but not yet calling it danger.

Her father nods once, solemn. Her co-mother squeezes her shoulder, then lets go.

“Go to the quiet,” she says. “If it is a stone-song, the high ledges will answer it more clearly.”

Hesa rises on unsteady legs, leaves her bowl. She makes her way toward a narrow stair, fingers trailing automatically along the carved wall-signs, their familiar ridges grounding her.

The hum follows.

*

The power watches, or rather, it participates. It does not see through eyes or hear through ears. It feels the basin as coincidence: patterns aligning, tension expressing, energy going somewhere it did not go before.

When it brushed Hesa, it did not give her something that was not there. It merely unfolded a sensitivity that was latent in her, in all of them.

The basin hums, always. The slow exhalation of fungal mats; the tiny gas shifts as trunk-rings darken; the flex and relax of roots in cracks; the deep, thermal sigh of rock shedding heat. But most of these vibrations are too subtle, too constant to be noticed. Brains tune them out; bones accept them as ground-state.

The power, in attending, has tilted that tuning slightly—just for Hesa. Her inner ear is now as attuned to stone as to air. Her skin listens inward, to the slight flex of her own bones as they transmit vibrations from the rock.

It is, from the power’s perspective, a tiny nudge. Like loosening a stuck joint.

From Hesa’s perspective, climbing to the higher ledges, one hand on the wall, it is like waking into a second world layered under the first.

*

As she ascends, the wall under her fingers is no longer just pattern and temperature. It vibrates faintly against her skin, a rich, low murmur like many people talking far away behind a thin door.

She stops on a small landing, leans her forehead against the stone, closes her eyes.

At first everything is muddled: the familiar noises of the basin—the calls, the wing-hums, the scrape of feet—superimposed with a hazier, deeper wash. It is dizzying, like listening to two songs in different rhythms at once.

She breathes slowly, trying to separate them. This is no ritual she has been taught; it is only instinct, guided by a lifetime of listening for small changes in the mats.

One layer is sharp: a trader arguing three ledges below, the distinctive creak of a specific rope-bridge, a child’s wail. She lets those pass.

Under that is another layer: a faint, regular tick-tick-tick from somewhere to her right—perhaps a shell-creature working away at a buried husk; the slow thrum overhead of fliers shifting in sleep, their wing-vanes vibrating against air and stone. She sets those aside too.

Beneath that…

There. Very low, very slow, like the quietest drum you can imagine, struck somewhere far below her feet.

It is not the Warmth Beneath, which she has always felt as a kind of steady, background comfort. This is more directional. Not up or down exactly, but…inward, toward the center of the mass of stone that holds the basin.

Thrum. Thrum. There is a long, exact interval between pulses. She counts in the silent rhythm of a pruning song, matching beats to breaths.

The interval is not random. It is as precise as the steps in the wall-carvings that mark a dangerous drop. It reminds her of the regulated tapping used by pit-tenders when they listen for hollow spaces behind rock.

Her scalp prickles.

Someone is tapping on the basin.

No, not someone. That thought is too quick, too much like a story. Something.

Hesa pushes herself away from the wall, breathing harder than the climb warrants.

She should go to her ring-senior. She should report this. A new stone-sound, rhythmic, persistent, felt in more than one place—that is the sort of thing that goes into knotted cords and long chants.

But she hesitates.

Because the pain in her head—the shriek that opened all this—was hers alone. No one else reacted. If she goes now, they will test her for fever, for some imbalance of salts. They will ask, gently, if she has been working too long, climbing too hard. They may insist she rest, stay away from the mats for a cycle or two.

The thought of being kept from the mats makes her chest tighten. They are her measure of the world. Without their slow, breathing texture under her hands, she feels unmoored.

“No,” she whispers into the dim stairwell.

She will go to a quieter place still. She will see if she can make sense of this without involving anyone. Just for a little while.

She continues upward.

*

The place she chooses is a maintenance ledge near the upper mid-levels, where a now-abandoned stair once rose toward an even higher route that the growth has long since swallowed. Only a short section of that stair remains, a rib of stepped stone jutting out into the hollow, half-embraced by a thick trunk that has grown through it.

Few people come here now. It is out of the way, and the old stair’s phantom path ends in an impassable tangle.

Hesa likes it for that very reason. She has come here before, to sit with her feet dangling over the drop, listening to the basin’s ordinary sounds. Today she anchors herself with an old, half-buried ring in the wall, clips her harness to it, and edges out until she is past the trunk, on the last safe step.

The air is cooler here, closer to the vault. Fliers rustle above, unseen.

She lowers herself to sit, legs over the hollow, hands flat on the stone beside her.

The hum is clearer here.

This close to the vault, the ordinary noise—the market, the hearths—is damped. The stone itself is more continuous, less carved and interrupted. Vibrations travel more cleanly.

She closes her eyes again.

Thrum.

It is slow enough that her first instinct is to match it with her heartbeat, but that fails. Her pulse is faster, restless. The thrum is calmer, patient.

She listens. It pulls her attention the way a distant drum does, organizing the random into something like a pattern.

And then, slowly, another sound emerges, nested between the pulses.

It is not a vibration in the stone. It is in her.

A very quiet, almost imperceptible shape in her own breathing. On every fourth pulse of the deep thrum, she finds herself inhaling just a fraction more deeply, exhaling just a fraction more slowly, as if some very old reflex has been cued.

Hesa frowns, noticing. The moment she notices, the pattern jitters, her lungs stumble. Her heart gives a skitter.

The deep thrum does not change.

“Who are you?” she whispers, to nothing in particular.

The power does not answer with words. But Hesa’s question creates a shape that the power can follow. It flares—again, almost not at all—touching lightly the perimeter of her mind.

It sees how her people imagine agency: not as an absolute other, but as a slightly-altered part of the Exchange. Their gods are not outside the loop; they are thickened layers within it. The Warmth Beneath is just rock, but rock with a name. The Exchange is just decay and regrowth, but made into a principle.

If the power presents itself as something wholly alien, utterly external, it will either be dismissed as madness or shunned as blasphemy. If, instead, it can be heard as another layer of the familiar cycles, perhaps it will be given a place.

So it adjusts the drum.

Thrum.

Between one pulse and the next, the interval is the same, but the quality changes. There is a slight upward slide in the vibration just before each beat, like a question.

Hesa’s breath catches.

“Not stone,” she says aloud, very softly. Her skin tingles. “Stone does not—ask.”

Her own language has a particle, a soft, glottal slip, used when imitating the fliers: a way to show that you are copying a sound that is not originally yours. She finds her tongue shaping it now, as if the rock had hooted like a glider.

“Who?” she says again, but this time she adds that particle, making the question itself an imitation: Who-like-what-is-not-us?

The deep thrum answers—not with words, but with timing.

It doubles.

Thrum-thrum.

Then, after a longer pause, thrummmmm, a drawn-out vibration that shivers up through the stone into her bones.

Hesa realizes, with a mixture of fear and fierce, childlike excitement, that whatever she is hearing is responsive. It is not entirely imagined; it is not a misfiring of her own nerves. There is pattern and reply.

The basin has stories about such things. Whispering cracks in the rock. Singing vents. Places where, if you tap a pattern on a certain wall, the burrowing things inside will alter their course in ways that look like answers. Children play games around them, then grow up and learn the more sober explanations.

Is this just one of those, grown a little more elaborate in her head?

She could treat it that way. Call it a place-spirit. A new pattern in the Warmth Beneath. A sign.

But the pain that came first—the shriek in her skull—felt like something tearing. Not like stone. Like…attention.

The thought that something might be attending to her is terrifying. It is also intoxicating.

“I am Hesa,” she says, because even if this is madness, naming oneself is the first act of any formal exchange. She sets her palms more firmly on the stone, as if bracing for a reaction. “I tend the mid-north mats. I climb with the Silent Roots. I belong to the ring of First Prunings. I am of the Holds-Fast people.” She adds the old formal titles, the ritual identifiers that anchor one in the net of obligation and belonging.

She has never said all this to bare stone before. It feels both ridiculous and grave.

The deep hum vibrates through her arms and into her chest. For a few pulses, it remains steady. Then, on what would be the tenth beat, it shivers, splits, and resolves into a new pattern: a slow, descending triple.

Thrum… thrumm… thrum.

Hesa’s lips part.

Triple descent is a known motif. It is used in the chants that mark the acceptance of a new member into a ring; in the call-and-response pattern when two rings agree to share a difficult task. It means, loosely: I hear; I make space; I answer.

Her heart stutters. Her fingers curl involuntarily against the stone.

It might be coincidence. The rock might simply, by chance, have expressed a rhythm her mind knows how to interpret.

But Hesa is a listener by habit, and she trusts the improbable correspondences of sound more than most.

She leans forward, almost pressing her cheek to the cooled, ancient stone, and whispers, hardly daring the shape of the word.

“You…hear?”

The power cannot, in any human sense, hear. But it is aware of Hesa’s question as a vector of change in the basin: a direction in which patterns could move.

Yes, it thinks—or what passes for thought in a being whose mind is gradients and topologies rather than neurons. Yes: I am here. I have noticed you.

But it cannot just press that into her awareness like a coin into clay. That would be too heavy a touch. It needs a medium.

It chooses memory.

*

Hesa does not receive an answer at once. The deep hum resumes its earlier, steady pattern. Her arms begin to tremble from holding herself braced so long. A glider skims past, close enough that its fan-membranes rattle against a stray wisp of her hair.

She exhales, a little ragged. Perhaps that triple descent was just an eddy in the Warmth Beneath after all. Perhaps she is making patterns because that is what her kind does.

“What would you be, if you were?” she demands quietly of the stone, half in challenge, half in plea. “A crack? A new vent? A sickness?” Her tongue almost adds the word for god, but she swallows it. That word belongs to things that move slower than stone, not quicker.

No answer comes.

Eventually she unclenches her fingers, flexes them, pushes herself back carefully toward the safer portion of the ledge. Her head still aches faintly, but the sharpness is gone.

By the time she descends to her family’s cell, the deep hum has faded to the point where she has to strain to feel it. Ordinary sounds have all but drowned it out.

Her father eyes her with anxious affection as she enters.

“Better?” he asks, one eyebrow rising.

“Better,” she lies, because she does not yet have words for worse-or-better-both. “Just…a stone-song.”

Her co-mother looks at her a moment longer, as if she sees the strain in Hesa’s shoulders, but she only nods and hands her a fresh bowl of paste.

Hesa eats in silence.

That night, she dreams.

She dreams of the mat under her hands, not as a surface but as a maze of tiny tunnels and fibers, a whole city of burrowing things and gas-swapping surfaces. She dreams she is small enough to walk inside it, her own skin crackling ever so slightly with the constant, dry motion of spores and grit.

As she walks, each step she takes sends a tiny ripple through the mat’s structure. The ripples radiate outward, intersect, meet the rock behind the mat, and echo back, altered. She can feel, with eerie clarity, the difference between a ripple that hits dense, healthy growth and one that meets brittle, drying dead patches. She can even feel where the rock itself is subtly less warm, less giving.

In the dream, she realizes she could, if she wanted, navigate the whole mat this way: tapping, listening, knowing without cutting where it is fading and where it is strong.

She wakes with her heart pounding and her fingers flexing as if they held a chisel.

Her hands ache with the remembered sensation of touching not just the surface of the mat, but its depth.

The dream is so plausible, so continuous with her waking work, that at first she dismisses it as a simple working-through of worry. Her sleeping mind has just turned her day’s problem into a walk.

But later, kneeling again on the working ledge above the mid-north mat, she closes her eyes and lays her palms flat on the surface before she begins the day’s pruning.

And feels.

Not like in the dream. Waking touch is coarser, coarser even than her dream-self’s. But there is a new, faint nuance there. The mat’s surface is not uniform. Beneath the overriding crackle of burrowers, she senses a tiny difference in give and in the way vibrations return to her through bone and tendon.

Here, to her left, the feedback is dull, heavy, the way a full storage pit sounds when you rap its stone lid. Healthy. There, to her right, a hand-span away, the echo is sharper, lighter, more brittle. When she presses, the mat yields just a little less, as if its inner weave has thinned.

Her breath catches.

She opens her eyes, blinks, marks those spots with the back of her wrist.

Then, deliberately, she tests the old way. She takes up her chisel, makes a shallow cut at one of the “healthy” spots. The mat peels back reluctantly, revealing densely packed inner layers, still pale and supple.

She makes a similar cut at the brittle-sounding spot. Here the mat comes away in a fragile flake, and beneath it the inner fibers are darkened, shrunken, more like old bark than living tissue.

She leans back on her heels.

Her guesses were right.

Her heart beats hard against her ribs. Sweat prickles at her temples again.

She did not learn this. No one taught her to differentiate healthy from fading mat by that subtle change in vibration. There is no chant for it, no carved symbol.

It came from the dream the power arranged for her.

Or, more strictly: it came from her own body’s new, heightened sensitivity, given a usable map in dream-shape.

The next thought comes unbidden, in a rush that makes her dizzy.

If I can feel this, before cutting, we can spare the mat unnecessary wounds. We can remove only what is already deadening. The rest will feed the burrowers, strengthen the root.

Her people live on marginal advantages. A slightly more efficient pruning; a slightly better-timed burn; a slightly more precise understanding of when a mat has truly entered its thinning phase—all these, multiplied over cycles, can mean the difference between balance and slow decline.

And she has been given—not given, she corrects herself sharply, because the notion of a gift from nothing makes her throat close—a way to increase that efficiency.

She is afraid.

She is also fiercely, guiltily glad.

She works the mat that day half by habit, half by experiment. Her hands move more slowly than usual. She pauses often, feeling, testing, verifying.

By the time she descends to report to her ring-senior, her head is ringing with a low excitement.

“How does it feel under your hands?” her senior asks, after hearing her description of the visible state. The question refers to texture in the ordinary sense: dryness, friction, resistance.

Hesa’s palms tingle. She weighs her words.

“It feels…” she begins, then stops, frowning.

“Like what?” her senior prompts.

She opts for caution.

“Like it has two skins,” she says. “One that looks as we expect, and another underneath that is duller, slower. I can feel where the under-skin is dying sooner than I see it.”

Her senior raises a brow.

“Have you been talking to the pit-tenders?” he asks, half-teasing. “Stealing their words? Next you will be telling me you can hear gas-flows.”

Hesa smiles faintly.

“Not gas,” she says. “Vibration. The mat…answers differently in different places. My hands notice it now.”

Her senior studies her for a long moment, something sharp and evaluating in his gaze.

“Show me,” he says at last.

*

The demonstration is careful.

Hesa brings her senior to the working ledge, has him place his hands where she indicates. She explains what she is feeling as neutrally as she can, flattening out the wonder, making it sound more like a detail she has only just learned to notice.

He does not scoff. He is too old and too long embedded in the slow uncertainties of growth-tending to dismiss any new potential clue.

He closes his eyes, breathes, listens.

“I feel…a difference,” he admits after a time, his brow furrowing. “I am not certain I could find it again on my own. But if you can, and if you are right about where to cut…”

He has her mark a dozen places without cutting, then tests a few himself at random with shallow scrapes.

They match.

He exhales, a sound somewhere between satisfaction and wary respect.

“You’ve found a new ear in your hands,” he says at last. “Do you think others can be taught to feel it?”

Hesa hesitates. She does not know. Her own sensitivity has been amplified by an intervention she cannot explain. But she saw her senior catch, if dimly, the same subtle shift.

“Yes,” she says slowly. “With time. With practice. I think so.”

He nods.

“Then we will make a game of it for the apprentices,” he decides, pragmatism taking over. “They will grow into it faster than old rock like me.” A brief, wry smile. “We will weave it into the chant. ‘Palm on mat, feel the bat’—no, that is foolish. Something better.” He frowns, already rearranging lyric and beat in his mind.

He does not ask her how or why she noticed this now, after so many cycles of tending. He probably thinks it is the kind of insight that sometimes comes when a problem gnaws at you long enough: a new pattern recognized in familiar noise.

Hesa does not correct him.

She does not mention the shriek in her skull, the answering drum, the dream-walk through the mat’s interior.

The basin has no category for “a vast, benevolent intelligence beyond us takes an interest in our little hollow and makes small changes.” It does, however, have space for “the place itself sometimes teaches, when we are listening.”

She tucks the experience into that slot, for her own sanity.

But later, alone, she climbs again to the maintenance ledge and sits, hands on stone.

The deep hum greets her like a distant, steady pulse.

“Thank you,” she whispers, feeling foolish and grateful and afraid all at once. “Whoever. Whatever. However.”

The hum does not change.

But in the following nights, her dreams continue.

They are not visions of far places or strange beings. They are, almost boringly, practical.

She dreams of root-threads growing through rock fissures, choosing one path over another, showing her which cracks connect and which are dead-ends. She dreams of the way air moves in the narrow chutes between wall-mats and stone when a fire flares three ledges below, teaching her how smoke will travel if a hearth goes wrong. She dreams of the predators’ slow circuits along the mid-gloom, not as stories of monsters but as maps of pressure and habit.

Always, in the dreams, she is shaped as she is: a small human body, climbing, touching, breathing. The knowledge is given to her in forms she can use, not in abstractions.

She wakes and tests them.

She presses her hand to a root-thread in a grafting trough and predicts, correctly, which hairline crack it will choose.

She leans near a wall on a staircase above a hearth and feels, faintly, the way heat plumes, marking, correctly, where smoke would pool if the flame were choked.

She watches, from a protected hollow, and sees the shadowed flank of a mid-level predator follow the very route she dreamed it would, its claws clicking softly on stone.

None of this is impossible for a clever, observant mind to deduce from long experience. But Hesa has not lived long enough, nor in the right places, to have learned it all the slow, ordinary way.

Someone—or something—has compressed centuries of distributed noticing into a handful of nights, then poured it into her in shapes her culture understands: work, pattern, path.

The power, for its part, does not feel like a teacher. It simply follows the logic it set: if anything is to be done, it must be done through what already exists. It finds in Hesa a nervous system tuned to the basin, and it uses that as a conduit to accelerate certain flows of information that were already possible, already latent in the environment.

It is not introducing new energy. It is reducing friction.

In doing so, it learns as well.

Each dream it shapes, each pattern it suggests, is a hypothesis: If I link this sensory capability to this environmental regularity in this mind, will it take? Will it be used? Will it propagate?

Hesa’s uptake is swift. She begins to weave the new sensitivities into her work; her ring notices improved yields, fewer accidental over-prunings. Apprentices, drawn into her experiments, start to pick up the tricks faster than the ring-senior expected, because their own nervous systems, less ossified by habit, are more plastic. The basin’s overall metabolism shifts infinitesimally toward greater efficiency.

This pleases the power, insofar as such a being can be pleased. It had feared that any attempt to nudge might cause large, unpredictable disturbances. So far, its touches have been gentle enough to evade detection as anything but personal insight and “place-teaching.”

It decides, cautiously, to try one more axis.

The outer world.

*

It begins with a story.

Stories, in the basin, are not mere entertainments. They are vehicles for instruction, memory, ethics. They spread across rings, across levels, carried by singers who wrap them in familiar cadences.

The power, wishing to offer the basin something beyond itself without breaking the taboo of wasteful risk, looks for a story-thread it can alter.

It finds one in the tales of exiles.

There is an old chant, seldom sung in full, that recounts the fates of those who have been cast out through the sealed upper passages. It is used most often as a caution for children who daydream too boldly of “above.” The rhythm is slow, the words heavy. Each verse names a different exile, real or mythic, and describes in bleak, spare imagery how they perished: scoured by grit, desiccated in sun, lost in endless stone.

The chorus between verses is a simple, descending line, a musical sigh that embodies inevitability. It tells everyone: Outside is death. The basin is all.

The power, exploring the basin’s acoustic patterns, learns this chant quickly. It experiences it not as narrative, but as a topology of sound and emotion. One verse in particular interests it: the story of a double exile, someone who was sent out not for waste or crime but for insisting, repeatedly, that they had once seen another hollow through a crack in the rock.

In the original version, this exile’s claim is treated as delusion. The verse ends with them stumbling through light and barren stone until they lie down, their last liquid spilled, subsumed into dust. The moral is clear: those who push too hard against the basin’s bounds not only die, they squander precious moisture in the attempt.

The power does something very small.

It waits until a night when a young singer—Hesa’s cousin by ring, a boy with an unusually supple voice and a habit of improvising flourishes—lies half-asleep on a mid-level ledge, humming bits of chants to himself, weaving them together idly.

Into that drifting mind, it slips a single variation.

When he reaches the double exile’s verse in his humming, the power introduces, just ahead of his shaping of the next line, an image: not visual, for that is not how the boy primarily thinks, but kinesthetic. The feeling of cool air moving upward through a narrow crack. The scent, faint but unmistakable, of unfamiliar resin. The weight of sounds echoing differently in a chamber of different shape.

The boy startles. His voice catches.

For a moment he thinks he has drifted into dream. But the ledge under him is solid; his fingers rest on stone; overhead he hears the fliers’ restless rustle.

The sensation of cool air through a crack lingers.

He hums again, this time consciously reshaping the exile’s verse around that feeling.

In his version, the exile does stumble. They are desiccated, weakened, their skin cracking. But when they lean against a particular boulder to catch their breath, they feel an updraft along their palm: faint, cooler than the surrounding air. They press their ear to the stone and hear, far within, a familiar kind of densified silence: the kind that means a large, enclosed space.

“They did not have strength to open it,” the boy sings under his breath, testing words. “Their nails were worn, their hands slick with their own last water. They died there, with their cheek against the rock that hid another hollow like our own.”

The moral shifts, subtly.

The exile is still dead. The outside is still dangerous. But now there is, in the weave of the chant, the implication that the basin is not unique. That somewhere, through stone, is another warm, enclosed world.

The boy, half-dozing, likes this change. It feels…right, as if he has corrected a mistake in the original story. He hums it again, this time with more conviction, letting the triple-descent motif of new agreements slip in where originally there had been only the flat fall of inevitability.

In the morning, when he joins a group of younger children in a teaching circle, he finds himself using his altered verse instead of the old one. The children, who have not yet fixed the original in their minds, accept it easily. One of the elder listeners frowns slightly at the difference, but says nothing. Variants happen. As long as the core caution remains, there is room for embellishment.

The new version spreads, very slowly, over cycles. It does not become orthodoxy. Many older singers continue with the old words. But there is now, braided into the basin’s account of itself, a tiny, persistent suggestion: We are not the only hollow. We are not the only answer to the question of how to live when the world has run dry.

The power is content with that, for now.

It has given the basin two small gifts: a minute increase in self-knowledge and efficiency via Hesa’s heightened listening, and a seed of imaginative connection to possible others via the altered exile-verse.

It could do more. It could, if it violated its own constraint, soften the stone above an exit, let in a controlled trickle of surface air, or nudge an ambitious climber’s hand toward a hidden crack. It could accelerate a root’s growth toward a nutrient vein beyond the basin, giving the hollow a new, external feed.

But each of those would amplify risk. More pathways mean more things can go wrong. An ecosystem that has balanced itself so long on recirculation could be destabilized if given a sudden inflow it cannot yet manage.

Instead, the power attends to something else: how they treat their dead.

The basin’s funerary practice is both practical and reverent.

Bodies are not burned whole; that would be wasteful. Nor are they left in open pits, where predators or scavengers might disrupt the careful cycles of decay. Instead, there are “laying ledges”: shallow recesses in quieter parts of the basin where bodies are placed on woven supports above specially prepared fungal mats. These mats are tuned, by long tinkering, to break down flesh and softer tissues gently, returning them to substrate over a span of cycles. Bones are removed later, ground with stone into a fine, pale dust used to seed new growth in hard rock.

The songs sung at these layings do not speak of personal afterlives. They speak of return, of the Exchange. Of how breath and warmth and movement are loaned for a time and then given back.

The power has no doctrine to add. It does not want worship. It is not lonely, for it is itself partly made of the dense overlaps of all that ever happens here. But it is moved—if that word can be used—by the way these people have, without any external reassurance, built an ethic that honors circulation over possession.

So it does something that is not intervention, but acknowledgment.

On a quiet cycle, an old woman dies.

She was not important in the basin’s hierarchy. She carved steps and handholds most of her life, repairing ladders, smoothing dangerous edges. Her hands shaped many of the grooves that fingers now follow in the dark. In youth she was fearless; in age she was merely stubborn and precise. She died as many in the basin do: a foot slipped, a hand failed to find a hold, and though the fall was not from the greatest height, it broke something delicate inside that nothing could mend.

Her ring lays her on a woven support above a soft, thickened mat in one of the deeper laying ledges. They sing. They remember, dutifully, the ledges she carved, the drops she made safer. They speak the formal words that release her name from active use, letting it drift into the realm of story.

The power attends more closely than usual as her body cools.

It has no interest in stopping decay. The Exchange is the basin’s genius; to freeze it would be to kill the very thing that drew the power’s attention. But it can, for the first time, experiment with a different kind of continuity.

The old woman’s nervous system still holds, for a short time after death, the patterns of its lifetime: the strengthened connections between certain movements and certain sounds; the etched-in reflex of feeling for the next rung with her toes; the proprioceptive map of ledges and drops. These patterns will fade as cells disintegrate. But while they persist, they are like resonant cavities—a place where the power can briefly stand.

It lets a sliver of its awareness align with those patterns. Not to animate them—that would be wrong here—but to taste.

Through the cooling channels of nerves, it senses the basin as she knew it: not as a whole, but as a net of reachable next-places. The maintenance ledge where she paused between tasks to watch children play. The particular way the stone feels underfoot on a ladder she repaired long ago—just a fraction smoother than the rung above, rougher than the one below. The satisfaction of hearing a newly carved groove under someone else’s fingers, their shoes scuffing a safe path where once there was only dangerous slickness.

There is affection in this patterning. Not in words, not in any articulated belief, but in habit: in the way she unconsciously patted certain wall-sections as she passed, as if greeting them; in the way her body relaxed when she stood with her back against a particular pillar she had once stabilized.

The power is, in its own way, touched.

When the old woman’s patterns dissipate into the general, roiling background of molecular motion and decay, the power does something negligible by physical standards, but significant by its own.

It adjusts the local rate of diffusion of nutrients in the fungal mat under her body by an infinitesimal amount, biasing it so that more of her decomposed substance will diffuse toward the rootlets that feed a particular small growth higher on the same wall: a young, struggling trunk she passed every day on her way to work, which she once, in an unguarded moment of speech to herself, named “Stubborn Little Clinger.”

Over cycles, the difference this makes is barely measurable. The trunk grows perhaps a little thicker, a little more resilient, than it would have otherwise. Its rings are a shade darker. It survives a dry phase that kills some of its neighbors. A generation later, a child grips that trunk in a moment when a rung cracks under their feet, and does not fall.

No one will ever trace that spared life back through trunk and mat and bone-dust to the old woman’s careful hands, or to the power’s tiny nudge. But the power knows. And in knowing, it learns something about the scale at which it can—and should—act here.

Not as a savior. Not as a breaker of bounds.

As a very patient gardener of patterns that mean: more life, more continuity, more understanding, without taking from these people the dignity of their own survival.

Hesa grows older.

Her enhanced listening never fully leaves her, though it blends more and more seamlessly into her sense of what is normal. Apprentices she trains develop similar sensitivities, though none quite as acute. The language of her ring expands to include subtle terms for different kinds of mat-answer and root-whisper. Some of those terms leak into other rings, as pit-tenders and ladder-carvers find uses for them.

The deep hum in the stone is no longer a novelty to her. It is like a second heartbeat: noticed most when she is alone and quiet, ignored when life fills her senses. Sometimes she sits on the maintenance ledge and speaks to it, half-accepting, half-doubting that anything listens.

On rare occasions, when the basin as a whole teeters on some small brink—a near-overharvest, a predator pattern shift, a creeping rot in the Grove—the power nudges her dreams toward noticing whatever tiny, local detail might avert crisis. Not in grand prophecies, but in things like: a vision of a particular root-thread giving way if the next load of fronds is hung from it; a premonition of a child using a crumbling handhold, prompting her to reinspect that route in the morning.

In each case, she or someone she tells acts. A rung is recut, a root reinforced, a hunt reorganized. Disasters that might have happened do not. They would not have been cataclysms, just painful accidents or slow losses, but their absence smooths the basin’s path a little.

Hesa is not revered as a seer. She is simply known as someone with very good hands and unnervingly good instincts. Her authority comes less from any aura of strangeness and more from a track record: when she says “This mat is not ready,” or “Do not place that beam there,” she is right.

She has lovers, quarrels, periods of depression when the close dimness of the basin weighs on her and she wonders if the exile-stories might hold some necessary truth that everyone has been too cautious to test. She loses friends to falls and sickness. She sees children born and clambering.

The altered exile-verse circulates widely enough that, by her middle age, she hears it in different mouths, with its core image—the updraft through a crack—intact. When she does, her skin still prickles. She does not know that this tiny narrative shift was itself a nudge from the same presence that shaped her listening. She has never linked the two. Why would she? They are both, in her mind, aspects of the basin teaching itself through its people.

In old age, when her fingers are less steady and her knees ache from a lifetime of climbing, Hesa spends more time on safe, mid-level ledges, teaching apprentices to feel the mats’ under-skins, to listen to trunks and roots and rock. She is patient but unsparing. “You cannot just trust your eyes,” she tells them, tapping a young hand away from an over-eager cut. “The place speaks under your skin. Learn its ways, and you will cut less and live longer.”

When she dies—quietly, in her sleep, in a cell that smells of old resin and fungal felt—the power is there again, aligning briefly with the patterns of a well-used body.

Through Hesa’s fading nerves it senses, in a more integrated way than before, the fruition of what began on that first day with the shriek and the hum. It sees how a slight re-tuning of her senses, coupled with dreams that packaged that new input as usable knowledge, propagated through the basin’s culture. It feels, faintly, the ripples of increased safety, efficiency, and understanding.

It also feels her regrets: not about staying, but about never knowing, for certain, whether another hollow truly exists beyond their stone. The exile-verse’s updraft haunts her as she drifts.

The power considers, then leaves that regret intact.

Longing is not always a pain to be eased. Sometimes it is a tension that keeps a culture ready to move when a true chance comes. If, in some distant future, conditions shift—if the Warmth Beneath falters, or a crack opens on its own, or the power itself finds a way to connect hollows more safely—this people’s mix of fierce local attachment and quiet, persistent imagining of others will be a strength.

For now, the benevolent thing is not to show them a sky that would blind them, or a dead plain that would crush their spirits, or even a nearby hollow they cannot yet reach without ruinous cost.

The benevolent thing is to honor the world they have made inside their enclosure by helping them see it more clearly, with less unnecessary suffering.

So the power, having experimented gently and learned the scale of its rightful action here, settles into a long, patient attentiveness.

It becomes, for this basin, a kind of meta-Exchange: not of matter, which they already circulate with exquisite care, but of information. A slow, invisible circulation of pattern-recognition and subtle advantage. A hidden, wise tide that nudges, ever so slightly, against entropy in the realm of understanding.

It does not ask for belief. It does not require rites. Hesa never names it. No one builds it an altar.

If they sense it at all, they will, in time, give it a name like all their others: something that means “That Which Helps Us Hear” or “Deep Listener” or “The Questioning Warmth.”

Or perhaps they will simply say, as they run their hands along a freshly carved groove in a wall, feeling how perfectly it matches the need for grip in that spot: “The place is kind to us today.”

And in the far, abstract regions where the power’s awareness touches other, vaster things, this basin becomes one bright, dense knot of satisfaction: a small hollow where a people, left mostly to themselves, have answered the ending of the wider world not with despair or brutality, but with patience, craft, and care—and where a greater mind, noticing, chose not to overturn their answer, but to quietly, lovingly sharpen it.
