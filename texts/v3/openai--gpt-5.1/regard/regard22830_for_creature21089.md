The first touch is not with hands but with attention.

For a long while, the benevolent power does nothing that would look like an event. It simply listens.

It listens in the only way that is adequate to a place like this: across scales. It takes in the plateau’s thunder of small teeth and soft wings, the bass murmurs of sap in tower-veins, the slow groans of root-plates adjusting to stone. It notices the daily rising and falling of sound as others here do: how the insect hiss swells at heat-peak, how the deep glider calls stack at dusk, how the cliff-singers’ harmonics shift when the rim slumps.

It also attends to the very slow music: generational drift in the positions of terraces, (almost) imperceptible thickening of the root-mat, incremental brightening and dimming of certain tower lineages, gradual changes in the composition of swarms. It listens to how the tower-people remember, to the logic of their bone-tables and seed-rods, to the understated politics of moving a single bead one notch along a row.

Only when it can follow the plateau’s sentences—spoken in sound, tissue, and habit—does it turn from listening to answering.

It will not answer with spectacle. Anything violent or sudden on this world becomes disaster: fire scaling up out of its hearth, terrace failures, root-plate shears. Those are already part of the plateau’s vocabulary of catastrophe. The wise power has no wish to add to that lexicon.

Instead, it chooses to work where it can be as quiet as rain that happens to fall exactly when needed, in the pattern of a symbiont that “just appears” at the right time, in a new way for things to fit together.

What it does can be told as a series of very small changes.

### 1. A New Sense of Edge

The first intervention is at the rim.

The plateau’s edge is a place of both richness and danger, where moisture and cooler air rise, where bare stone is exposed, where the cliff-singers live and sing the rock. It is also the line along which the whole structure can fail most catastrophically.

Watching over many cycles, the power sees a pattern that the tower-people only half-grasp from their own vantage: certain combinations of rain-sequence, rope-predator migrations, and cliff-singer silences that precede the biggest shears—those that peel off whole shelves of root and tower-base, taking with them fissures the people rely on for water and shelter.

The cliff-singers already sense some of this through their bone-voices to the rock. The power does not wish to overwrite that; it chooses instead to extend and clarify it.

In a clutch of eggs, in a single generation of cliff-singers along one length of rim, a tiny change appears. It is not dramatic: slightly modified bones in the head and breast, an extra air-sac, a different pattern of nerves. When these young fledge and press their throats to stone, their calls carry a fraction farther through rock, and—more importantly to them—their own bodies feel more sharply the nature of what the sound returns from.

Roughness, hollowness, tightness of grain: all are now a little clearer. To this, their instincts add something new under the power’s gentle nudge: a tendency to modulate their songs when they sense particular kinds of hidden voids and tensions, not just as personal knowledge but as broadcast.

They develop, without knowing why it is easier now, a distinct class of calls for “stone that will soon move.”

The tower-people, tuned to cliff-singer choruses as part of their ordinary awareness, begin to notice that before certain collapses there are now new harmonics, new skeins of sound in the night. At first the correspondence is only guessed at. Over some lifetimes, as elders add beads to bone-tables and scratch new marks near “cliff-song” columns, the pattern resolves: “When they sing thus and thus, in this ratio of low to high, the rim near them will give way within days or weeks.”

No voice speaks this to them. It is discovered. But the possibility of discovering it was the gift.

As the pattern becomes reliable in their memory-arrays, tower-people change their habits. They stop establishing long-term camps in certain promising but unstable alcoves. They time their approaches to new stone spurs with more caution. They develop, as a cultural practice, listening gatherings where a band will sit in quiet during the height of the singers’ season, doing nothing but mapping the evening’s echoes onto their bone tables.

The edge is still dangerous. Cliffs still fall. Whole towers along the rim still eventually go. But fewer of the tower-people are directly under such falls now. Fewer cool fissures that they depend on are lost without warning. The plateau’s breathing becomes, for them, a bit less arbitrary, a bit more legible.

The wise power does not stop any collapse. It simply makes it easier for a small, fragile species to be somewhere other than directly beneath the heaviest stones when they go.

### 2. A New Way to Carry Coolth

The second intervention concerns heat.

For all its moisture hidden in bodies, the plateau runs close to its tolerances. When the brief rains fail or come late, when the cloud-bands go thin, there are cycles when the upper terraces bake. Swarms die in mid-flight. Certain tower lineages slam pores closed harder and for longer, starving their own symbionts to stay alive. The cool refuges in fissures grow crowded and stale.

The plateau has already invented many strategies for moving water: tower-veins, mat-peristalsis, sweating terraces, mist-forest membranes. What it has less of is long-distance, passive movement of cold.

The power refrains from reaching down into mantle or up into star. It stays with what the system already almost does.

On the outer flanks of a subset of towers scattered across the plateau, it waits for the next glitch of mutation in the tissues that form big, vertical membranes—the translucent “sails” that drink humidity. In a few individual towers, some of these membranes happen to form narrower, more elongated tubes instead of broad sheets: flawed sails that, as the towers’ own genetics stand, would be selected against over time because they catch less water.

Into those flaws the power leans. Slight adjustments: the inner surfaces become more finely ridged; the tube walls slightly stiffer; the openings at top and bottom—where the tubes meet the air and the internal spaces—are a little more flared.

In the next hot cycle, when one of those towers sweats and the sun beats on its flanks, air begins to rise inside those tubes, drawn by heat. It moves faster than air normally does along the tower’s skin. This would have happened anyway; the geometry makes it so. But the new stiffness and ridging act to maintain laminar flow longer, reducing turbulence. The flared openings couple that flow more tightly to deeper hollows inside the tower and to the shadowed mat at the base.

A primitive, vertical windpipe has been born.

Air from close to the cooled root-plate is pulled up the tube, flows along inner surfaces that have been nudged to be slightly more heat-conductive, and exits higher, carrying heat away from deeper tissues while also dragging fresh, cooler air in from lower strata. From the outside, if you place your hand near the base opening in the hottest part of the day, you feel a faint draft.

The tower-people do not at first. This is subtle. But the tower does. Its internal thermodynamics shift. Water can be moved and retained differently. Certain heat-sensitive symbionts, which used to barely survive at its core, thrive a little more. The tower grows through a particularly harsh dry interval with somewhat less damage.

Selection notices.

Over many generations of towers, this trait spreads—slowly, patchily, biased toward lineages and locales where heat stress is greatest. The power does not push it universally; it allows it to be just one strategy among many. Not every tower grows such chimneys. Some that do remain solitary freaks. But enough adopt the trait that the plateau’s overall ability to keep its deep tissues cooler improves.

The tower-people eventually notice.

In a bad dry cycle, those who have invested in patient listening and memory find that certain towers remain more hospitable. The inner hollows of “chimney towers” stay just bearable when others become lethal ovens. The power does not guide them there with visions; it lets them be found through the ordinary tools of this world: watching which towers groan less under heat, scratching new notations near those towers’ names, migrating their bands’ cycles accordingly.

Over longer spans, the people begin—again without any explicit revelation—to make use of the chimneys themselves.

Some bands, exploring in hotter hours, discover that by threading woven tubes of their own (made from dried ladder-vines and stiffened leaves) into the bases of natural chimneys, they can extend the cool-drafts farther into their fissure refuges or shelters under terraces. They invent, by trial and error, small convection-works: nothing like human machines, just shaped air.

They sit in their hollows during the worst heat, feeling the faint but real movement of cooler air over skin. For them, this is not miracle. It is simply another pattern: some towers pull; some do not. Some hollows can be coaxed into better breathing.

To the benevolent power, this is enough. It has added a path for entropy to flow, a way for surplus heat to be bled off a bit more gently, and entrusted its discovery and exploitation entirely to the local intelligence already present.

### 3. Quieting a Fire That Has Not Yet Happened

The plateau has burned before.

Under combinations of extraordinary dryness and lightning, rare corridors of plant-matter—especially in regions where semi-motile mats have drawn themselves together into denser belts—can transmit flame in chains. Those fires are not frequent, but when they happen, they rewrite whole districts: killing towers outright, sterilizing mats, turning diverse soundscapes into thin, harsh ones for generations until recolonization runs its course.

The tower-people have stories of such scars. In the myth-layered notches on bone-tables, some radiating circles stand for “once, the world here became brightness and black, and when it cooled, all familiar songs were gone.” There is no particular malice in those fires; they are simply what happens when many slow constraints fail at once.

The wise power cannot (and would not) remove fire. Fire is transformation, and some lineages on the plateau depend on post-burn niches. Instead, it chooses to address a very specific future: a configuration of growth patterns, tower placements, and climate pulses that, if unchecked, will produce a fire larger than any the plateau has yet known, one that would jump whole terrace systems and take a wide belt of the outer rim down with it.

It does not stop the lightning that will one day strike. It intervenes decades earlier, and with almost ridiculous delicacy.

Along one corner of the plateau—far from any tower-people band that currently has detailed maps—semi-motile mats have, over long time, crept into a continuous, fuel-rich expanse. They have done this by following gradients of favorable moisture and shade in the wake of a slowly migrating cloud-band track. The power watches this, watches the tower-pores and membrane forests above, watches the rare, heat-hardened scrub-forms that thread between mat-islands.

Then it tips two initial conditions.

1. In one lineage of semi-motile mat-organism, it slightly increases sensitivity to a certain volatile compound given off by a family of crystalline pioneer fronds that cluster around exposed stone. The mat always avoided bare stone before; now, in a few individual bodies, that aversion is weakened.

2. In one lineage of those crystalline fronds, it lowers very slightly the water requirement for spore release and makes the spores more buoyant. They travel a bit farther on a bit drier wind.

Those tweaks, introduced at the right place and time, produce over decades a small reconfiguration: the mats’ creeping belt is shot through more often with stone fingers carrying chiming fronds, which in turn seed more stone fingers ahead as the mats advance. The belt that would have been continuous, all fuel, becomes more patchy, with narrow mineral breaks.

The mat still spreads. Local creatures still live and die as they would have. To any tower-person walking through that region, the only visible difference from alternate futures is that there is slightly more glitter of crystal in the undergrowth, slightly more ting of fronds in the wind, and fewer places where the mat feels like endless, uniform sponge.

When, long after, a sequence of unusual dry cycles comes, and a lightning storm rakes that corner of plateau, the fire still burns. It runs through mats and low growth, scorches the bases of some towers, sends tower-people bands nearby to their safe hollows, gives new, hungry niches to smoke-loving opportunists.

But when the flames reach the would-have-been belt, they find stone fingers too wide to leap easily. The fire fragments, slows, breaks into separate, containable fronts that exhaust their fuel and die. Patterns of smoke and ash still make memories on bone-tables, but the radiating circles etched for this event are smaller than they written would have been.

No one thanks anything for this. It is framed, in the stories, as “the time the mats did not carry fire as we feared.” A generation of cautious elders who moved bands earlier than usual, remembering older, worse burns, congratulate themselves on wisdom. That, too, is part of the gift.

### 4. A Seed of a Different Slowness

The tower-people are good at what they do: reading slow change, mapping patterns, using their own bodies and crude objects as instruments. The benevolent power does not wish to replace their ways with anything like its own global awareness. But it can see one vulnerability they cannot yet fully name: the limits of their distributed memory.

Bone-tables and seed-rods are beautiful, robust for their scale. Stories told in front of them maintain correlations across some generations. But when something rare happens only once in many lifetimes—a particular combination of tower-collapse pattern and swarm failure and outgassing sequence—its trace on the arrays is fragile. A few bored or hungry years, a few casual rearrangements by a younger cohort who does not fully grasp the meaning of a certain bead’s material, and an important, hard-learned pattern can be diluted beyond use.

The power decides to try an experiment.

It looks not to the people first, but to their symbionts.

Their skin already hosts photosynthetic sheets and microlife tuned to various tower-secretions. Their guts maintain complicated consortia of bacteria that digest things the people otherwise could not. Among these bacteria are lineages that have short generation times and long horizontal gene transfer networks—a substrate that, on other worlds, would be called an excellent information medium, if one is patient and subtle.

Gently, the power threads into one common gut symbiont a new faculty: the ability to make, store, and slightly modify a particular class of inert, complex molecule that encodes pattern-statistics from its chemical environment.

The decisions it “remembers” are not verbal. This is not language. It is something like this: over many meals and many days, this bacterium’s ancestors will have been exposed to slightly different mixes of metabolic byproducts depending on what the host ate, where they camped, what swarms were thick or thin nearby, what subtle tower-compounds perfused the air. Those mixtures have statistics of co-occurrence: “when compound A is high, so is B, but not C,” and so on.

The modified symbiont now has, embodied in those inert molecules, a way to gradually bias its own metabolic knobs toward recording which sets seem “normal” and which “rare but survivable” for a given host lineage. When a host lives through a highly unusual configuration—say, a near-fatal hybrid event of drought, rogue swarm, and tower-collapse—the bacteria that survive in that gut carry, in those molecular patterns, an internalized shadow of “this constellation was real, and we lived.”

When that host shares gut flora with others—as they already do, through grooming, shared food, shared water—the bias diffuses. Over generations, across the band, a kind of chemical prior is established: an embodied expectation about what combinations of environmental traces are part of the band’s surviving history.

One might think this is too indirect to matter. But on this world, lives and choices are made from hairline differences in perception and habit.

Decades later, when a similar rare pattern begins to form again in the outer world, and the power does not interfere, a curious thing appears in the tower-people’s behavior. Before any conscious recognition, before any elder can say “this feeling is like that carved circle from the time of my mother’s mother,” individuals find themselves subtly more uneasy in certain soundscapes, more inclined to linger at bone-tables, more restless along familiar paths. Their gut flora are slightly more stressed by the compounds associated with this rare pattern; their bodies respond by small spikes in hormone analogues that, in their species, map not to panic but to “heightened noticing.”

They listen more.

Because they listen more, they pick up marginal changes in cliff-song sooner, hear small variations in the insect hiss, smell faint differences in tower exudates. Because they pick those up, their elders are prompted to point to certain beads sooner, to tell certain pattern-stories more often.

From the power’s point of view, what it has done is give the people a deeper layer of priors about the world’s causal structure—encoded not as prophecy but as bias toward paying attention when the rare-but-known starts to form again.

To the people, it is “a feeling in the body, like when songs of such-and-such pattern once led to almost-ruin.” They make a word for it that sits halfway between “remembered taste” and “felt warning.” They do not know that bacteria named it first.

### 5. A Place to Dream Together

The last and most intimate act the wise power undertakes in this epoch is not about safety at all. It is about widening the plateau’s own sense of itself.

Listening, the power has come to respect the tower-people’s peculiar genius: their capacity to weave slowly observed details into narratives of cause and consequence, and then to modify their lives based on those narratives without needing any guarantee. It wants to offer them a new medium in which to do that weaving—a slightly larger interior space.

So it looks for an existing behavior that can be deepened without breaking anything. It finds it in stillness.

The tower-people already have seasons of near-hibernation in extreme heat: days when, to conserve water and take advantage of their own photosynthetic symbionts, they lie pressed against warm trunks or in filtered light under membranes, breathing barely. In those times they are aware, but awareness is thin, drowsy, easily turned inward.

Into these states the power introduces only a very small adjustment: when certain sensory patterns coincide—a specific low-frequency tower hum, a specific envelope of insect hiss, a particular faint sweetness in the air from a flowering mat—it lowers, in their brains, the energy threshold for reactivating stored perceptual fragments in novel combinations.

In simpler terms: at such moments, when half-asleep, they become a little better at dreaming.

Not random dreams, but recombinative ones. Their minds, already full of sound-maps and bone-table patterns, begin to more frequently and more vividly reassemble these elements into trial scenarios: “what if the rope-predators came from this angle while the gliders called that way and the tower creaked thus?” “What if the cliff-singers’ new harmonics kept going instead of stopping?” These are not experienced as elaborate plots, but as strong, immersive intuitions, felt in gut and muscle.

Because the threshold is only lowered under certain, rare conjunctions of outside sound and smell, these deep-dream sessions tend to happen synchronously among members of a band sharing the same environment. They do not know they are dreaming “together” in any telepathic sense; but when they wake, more often than before, they discover that different individuals have had nearby insights. Conversations at bone-tables and in hollow-chambers become a bit richer: “In my rest, I felt that if the gliders’ calls doubled so and the hiss declined, the next collapse would not be like that carved circle but like the notch-sequence over there.” “I also felt such a thing.”

They begin, tentatively, to name and honor these convergences. Where once all novelty had to arise from waking observation alone, they now sometimes grant weight to what they have felt in the still-times—especially when several have felt similarly. They add a new kind of bead to the memory-arrays: not for events that have happened, but for dream-patterns that many have shared and decided are worth watching for.

From the power’s view, the plateau has just acquired a crude, distributed forecasting layer: an internal simulation capacity anchored firmly in the existing ecology of sensation and memory, not in abstract models. It is fragile and poetic, but real.

From the people’s view, the world has become slightly more talkative in rest. The towers murmur stories a bit louder. The insects’ hiss is more easily anthropomorphized into “voices” that warn and suggest. They do not start worshipping anything new; they simply extend the same intimacy they’ve always had with the place into their own inwardness.

### What It Does Not Do

There are many things the benevolent power very consciously refrains from.

- It does not give fire without cost, or tools beyond what bone and sap and stone allow. A metal tool-mind would tear through this place’s equilibria like a knife through a living membrane. The power has no interest in setting up kings on root-mats.

- It does not stop death. Rope-predators still drop and catch, grazers still strip, diseases still burn through over-dense guilds, children fall from terraces or are taken by sudden collapses. To end that would be to unmake what this place is.

- It does not single out the tower-people as “chosen” in any operatic way. It does not raise them to dominion over gliders or cliff-singers or beetles. It does not whisper ideology. It regards them as one of many clever lineages here, admirable but not central. Its few interventions involving them are about helping them fit their pattern-sensing gift more snugly into the plateau’s flows, not about granting them unnatural advantage.

- It does not make itself an object of belief. None of its acts come with anomalies so clean and loud that they demand attribution to will. Every change could be—and is—explained locally as mutation, coincidence, luck, emergent pattern. That is deliberate. The power wants the plateau’s inhabitants to remain at home in a world that makes sense through their own methods.

### What Changes, Seen from Far Above

If you watch this plateau across many tower-generations, after these interventions, the differences are not in any single spectacular skyline, but in accumulated curves.

The rim still crumbles, but the bands of tower-people that live near it show fewer abrupt vanishings from the record. Their bead-lines for “whole-band death by collapse” taper off. Stories of “the time we heard the singers warn, and we moved” accumulate instead.

The hottest intervals still cull weak lineages, but the distribution of towers with heat-drafts is such that internal hollows remain habitable in more places even in bad years. Fossilized internal tissues of “chimney towers” show a history of cooler cores, richer symbiont diversity, and, in some segments, faint scratches of tower-people shelters deeper inside than would previously have been possible.

The enormous fire that would have blackened a continent’s worth of terraces never quite happens. Instead, a pattern of smaller burns leaves a mosaic of ages and successional stages that, in turn, supports a richer diversity of semi-motile mats, swarms, and pioneers. Millions of lives die in those fires; millions of others live because a different fire did not come.

Within the tower-people, gut flora show, if you could assay them, a structured distribution of “pattern-memory” molecules. Bands separated by long distances carry different statistical priors—matching their different histories—but all have more internalized rare-pattern traces than they once would have. Behavioral ecologists, if any existed, would note slightly earlier migrations, more pre-emptive evacuations of certain hollows, more marginal-but-life-saving choices that, from inside, feel like “old caution flowering in us.”

Their art, if you call it that, evolves. Bone-tables in some regions acquire a third dimension: vertical stacks of seeds and beads representing not only “what has happened” and “how often,” but “what we have dreamt might happen.” A few elders in each generation specialize in curating those stacks, deciding which dream-patterns proved useful, which wasted attention, which should be remembered as warnings but not treated as predictions.

Their language gains a handful of new terms blending body-feeling with world-feeling, words for “this configuration tastes like the time the mat-fire stopped at the stone-fingers” or “this stillness in the singers sounds like a dream many of us had.” Objectively, these are small linguistic mutations. Subjectively, they allow more nuanced sharing of intuitions about complex causal webs.

All of this—stone-fingers in mats, windpipes in towers, singing in cliffs, bacteria remembering, bands dreaming—is easily read as the natural consequence of a complex biosphere’s own dynamics.

Which is exactly how the power wants it to look.

### Why This, and Not More?

Given its reach, this being could, if it wanted, reshape the plateau in bold strokes: fill the air with self-repairing machines, knit the rim with unbreakable roots, lift the tower-people into artificial towers of their own. It does none of that for a simple reason: it respects the world as it has already made itself.

This plateau has an integrity: its own long-accumulated compromises between sun and stone, between water pulses and metabolism, between fear and hunger and song. The tower-people, the gliders, the rope-predators, the mats—all are expressions of that. To overwrite them would be to destroy a unique conversation between physics, chemistry, time, and chance.

So the benevolent power chooses to act only where a light touch can:

- reduce gratuitous suffering that arises from sheer mis-timing, without eliminating difficulty itself;
- increase the ability of local minds and bodies to notice and adapt, rather than blunt the world’s teeth;
- preserve or enhance the plateau’s already existing logics, rather than impose alien ones.

It listens, it answers softly, and then it goes back to listening, willing to do nothing for long stretches if nothing is needed that the plateau cannot already do for itself.

No one here ever names it. No shrine appears to it on tooth-stone knolls. The only visible testament to its care is that, in this hot, loud, living world, more lives find ways to continue their patterns a little longer, and more minds—be they tower, beetle, glider, or person—have slightly wider room in which to learn what this place is and how to live within it.

If you stand on a mid-terrace a hundred tower-generations from now, press your hands to a chimney-wall, feel a faint cool draft slide over your palms while, far below, a band of tower-people sits listening intently to an odd new overtone in the cliff-singers’ song, you are feeling its work. And also, equally, theirs.
