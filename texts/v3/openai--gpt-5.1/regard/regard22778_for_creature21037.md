The power does not descend all at once either.

It begins with attention.

Not the crude beam of a lantern but the way a mountain might, if it could, notice a single crack in its side and choose to remember it. The cleft, already crowded with its own awareness, finds itself thought-about by something vaster—something that moves no stone directly, yet adjusts the conditions under which stone will, in time, decide to move.

There is no proclamation. Nothing in the gorge hears a voice. Yet, over a span of seasons so long even the patient orbs cannot remember a before, the rules change a fraction.

---

First, the light.

Far above the highest filament-wing roosts, where the wound in the rock opens to an alien sky, clouds have always passed indifferent. Snow fell or did not; sun burned or did not; storms cut at the rims of the cliffs and sent their news downward only as pressure, drip, and chemical taste.

Now, without fanfare, the weather up there begins to take a new, repeating shape.

On some days, the gorge feels the familiar murk: high, heavy overcast, the usual tired glow trickling down. But at intervals—irregular enough to resist prediction, regular enough to build into pattern—a different brightness leans over the fissure.

It is not harsher. If anything, it feels smoother, more diffuse. Microscopic particles, lofted and arranged in the thin air above the crack, catch the outside light and pour it downward as a soft, deepened dusk. To eyes adapted to noon, it would still be gloom. To the fungi and pallid mosses, it is surplus.

They do what they always do with surplus: they metabolize. A few lineages, stirred by these new pulses of barely-adequate radiance, push their pigments a step further. Molecules that once only transformed stray photons into faint glow inched toward actual energy capture: chemoautotrophs, slowly becoming photo-chemo hybrids. It is no miracle, just an accelerated nudge along directions already latent.

The benevolent power does not insert new genes; it adjusts probabilities. Mutations that would have vanished in the quiet wash of selection now arrive slightly more often when they are useful, slightly less when they are waste. Each brightened spell becomes a test: those crusts that can store a little more of its gift endure the lean dark better. Their spores fall and spread, and the gorge, from mid-height up, gains a thin, invisible film of organisms who no longer treat light merely as a signal, but as food.

The filament-wings learn this light in their own way. Their glassy hairs, already finely tuned to vibration, begin—through that same long, favored selection—to sprout clusters of nanoscopic pigmented bodies at their bases. Not enough to hamper flight, just enough to feel photons as a change in their own charge.

They do not turn toward brightness; they do not need to. But over many generations, roosts that hang on ledges with the best compromise of drip and new, softened glow do better. Roost-lines that anchor near slightly more luminous crusts raise more young to full gliding. The power never tells them “here is better.” It simply tilts what “better” already means.

Below that gentle augmentation of light, the gorge’s slow acoustic conversation acquires a new instrument.

---

It begins in the pools.

The anchored bells and their phase-tuned vortices have, for ages, cooperated accidentally. They contain and clean because each one responds to the turbulence of its neighbors. Now, one lineage of bell-colonies receives a gift: an extra feedback, a simple biochemical rule that translates a particular pattern of mechanical stress into a tiny pulse of stored chemical and then, with a delay, into a flicker of faint bioluminescence.

The flicker is so soft no human retina could see it. But other bells, already in the habit of responding to each other’s currents, are prone—very slightly prone—to alter their beat when they happen to contract at the moment some neighbor glows.

A loop emerges. A group of bells that chance into a useful rhythm—one that clears a bit more silt, draws in a bit more nutrient—also happens to flash in a particular sequence. Those that respond to that sequence reinforce it. It is not language. It is not even intention. It is a resonance pattern propagating along flexible bodies, stabilized because the power has, from the outside, smoothed away some of the random mistakes that might have broken it.

Over years, the pattern comes to correlate with “pool health” in a profound way. Bells in basins about to go anoxic—where too much dead matter has accumulated, where water refills too slowly—flash less, or out of tune. Those in basins whose rim-sippers are thriving and where runoff is enriched by new fungal films flash in steady, complex pulses. The basin-sippers’ stilt-gleaners, sensitive to both chemical and light change, begin making life-or-death decisions based on this emergent code: where to bud daughters, which pool edges to abandon.

The benevolent power does nothing so coarse as make them understand. Instead it repeatedly, minutely, enhances any mutation that strengthens the coupling between “state of pool” and “pattern of bell-glow,” and any tendency in neighbors to read and act upon that coupling. What emerges is something like memory—not in any one body, but across the little republic of the pool.

When a rockfall sends dust choking into a basin, the bells there fall silent. Long after the dust has settled and new water has seeped in, others upstream still “remember”: their own patterns bear a ghost of that silence. Over the decades, the gorge acquires a flickering, ultra-low-resolution sense of its own injury and repair, encoded in timings of light and ciliary beat.

Above, in the air, an analogous refinement arrives among the tap-sentinels.

---

They already ask questions, their glassy spines knocking careful pulses into stone. They already, in a crude way, influence each other: a bout of anxious tapping here sometimes propagates as a storm of sound down a whole vertical tranche of the cleft.

What the new power gives them is not new anatomy but timing.

Entrainment.

Spine lengths and joint stiffnesses that produce taps at certain natural frequencies become ever so slightly more likely to appear and persist if they lead to coherent interference patterns over distance—waves that reinforce instead of canceling out. The sentinels do not suddenly start to think. But their calls, once white-noise-chaotic, self-organize.

In some strata, every few days, an intricate rhythm will form of its own accord: a long, slow pulse from deep below, answered by a skirling higher beat from midway up, accompanied by faint, rapid tremolos at the brink of darkness. These are not planned. They are the only patterns that can survive intact through the complicated geometry of stone and crack and chamber. The power has quietly tipped development so that sentinel bodies are tuned to those surviving notes, much as a craftsman might carve many random flutes but keep only those that naturally play in a certain key.

The gorge is now full of recurring motifs of vibration: signatures of particular vertical zones, particular water tables, particular arrangements of life and rock. Over generations, mobile creatures—ribbon-beasts, thread-binders, juvenile gliders—develop reflexes keyed to those motifs. A certain composite rhythm means “a new crack has opened; this tract of wall is freshly exposed.” Another implies “a section below has gone unstable; swimming spores will not survive long here.” This, again, is no vocabulary. It is a mesh of conditioned movements and avoidances, pruned by a gentle, ever-present bias toward those that keep bodies alive and flows resilient.

From the outside, if you could hear on that timescale, it would sound as though the cleft has learned to murmur to itself about its own shape.

---

The benevolence is not sentimental. It does not abolish death. Orbs still close. Pitfalls still smother. Gliders still misjudge and strike stone, leaf-drifters still vanish against adhesive filaments. What changes is the ratio of waste to learning.

In the pre-attended gorge, a rockfall would obliterate a shelf-ecosystem and, over dozens of generations, life would spread back in roughly the same pattern, shaped only by gravity, moisture, and chance. In the attended gorge, that same rockfall leaves a stronger memory-trace in bell-flashes, tap-patterns, and the altered currents of leaf-drifters. Colonists that can exploit shattered mineral faster are, ever so slightly, more frequently born. Tap-sentinels whose spines resonate with the new hollows below send different questions into the rock, and those questions travel in familiar motifs others are equipped to respond to.

The next time a similar fracture threatens—signaled by faint changes in drip cadence and stone tension—gliders that would have roosted in the doomed section settle one ledge higher. Thread-binders extend more bridges across the void above the nascent break. Ribbon-beasts shift their habitual grazing corridors a few body-lengths aside. No creature understands “preemptive evacuation.” They simply inherit bodies whose reflexes, under these conditions, amount to it.

The gorge as a whole becomes harder to wound in ways that destroy its pattern.

---

Once this resilience is in place, the power allows something more delicate: divergence.

For a very long time, the cleft’s vertical zones have been contiguous in one important sense: there was always a sloping gradient of habitability from light to dark, from damp to drowned to merely wet. Every guild blurred slowly into its neighbors’. The new intervention does not break that gradient, but it thickens certain layers within it.

In the upper third, amid the gentled extra light, one lineage of filament-wings grows heavier bones. Not thick—just dense enough that, once they spread their hairs and modulate air as they always have, they sink fractionally faster than their kin. At first this is only a disadvantage; they must spend more energy to arrest descent. But the power stacks the deck. Below the gliders’ usual roost-band, it has, over centuries, been favoring fungi and mosses whose metabolic byproducts make the air very slightly richer in oxygen.

A filament-wing that happens to settle and roost a little lower now finds, in that slice, air where more vigorous muscle is rewarded. Over generations, these heavy-boned gliders tilt imperceptibly toward true flapping. Not wide, open strokes—the gorge is too tight—but short, sharp, almost hummingbird-like bursts that let them hop from one wall pocket to another without relying wholly on gravity.

They become, to the lighter-winged castes above, something halfway between cousin and machine: creatures capable of active ascent. Not all at once; not to the open sky. Just enough that, when they have fed richly on mid-air larvae and spore-clouds, they can, slowly, climb back to particular roost-caves that cut deep into the walls.

The power makes room for them. Micro-collapses open those caves a bit wider than random would warrant. Acid-weavers’ etching biases such cavities to hold just enough water and just the right mineral profile to support a new bloom of crusts specially adapted to these slightly more muscular gliders’ droppings. Within a few hundred of their slow generations, this lower-roosting caste becomes its own semi-enclosed world: brood-chambers lined with soft, felted mats full of symbionts, a place in which eggs can be reared in unprecedented safety.

They never understand that they are, in the gorge’s terms, “engineered.” They simply do what goes well and leave more of themselves behind.

Further below, near the mid-pools, a more radical divergence occurs—but this time, not in any body. In a relationship.

The basin-sippers have always been central filters. The glass-legged gleaners have always been opportunists. The power notices—“notices” in the sense of seeing, across thousands of years at once—that whenever an especially efficient pool-consortium arises (bells, sippers, gleaners tuned perfectly to one another’s emissions), that arrangement, once knocked out by a flood or a drying, takes much longer to reappear than physics alone would suggest. The structures of mutual benefit are fragile.

So it adds one more line of feedback.

Among the many invisible organisms that inhabit the sippers’ waxy crusts, one bacterial strain gains an extra quirk: its waste products slightly alter the stiffness of the sipper’s upper skin. Which in turn modulates how easily the gleaners’ feeding punctures can penetrate. Now, gleaners that inadvertently “overgraze” the sipper—piercing too often, taking too much—find themselves, in the next generation, faced with tougher ground. Their offspring’s mouthparts meet more resistance, and their budding success wanes.

Conversely, gleaners whose foraging pattern correlates with more stable sipper health find their hosts softening under them, rich with exudates. No mind chooses this. It is an ecosystem acquiring, via the external bias of the wise power, a crude sort of negative feedback against overexploitation. The result, some millennia later, is pools that swing less wildly between boom and crash. Their community compositions can explore more “risky” arrangements—more specialized grazers, more intricately choreographed bell-phasing—because the subtle new constraint reins in mutually destructive spirals.

In human terms: the gorge has been given the ecological equivalent of a conscience. Not a moral sense, but a physical reluctance to let any one participant burn the rest.

---

As above, so below.

In the lowest dark, where orbs wait and sentinels listen, the power’s touch is gentlest, for here the gorge’s own emergent order was already strongest.

It adds, here, almost nothing—only makes a few rare things a little less rare.

Very occasionally, in the ancient gorge, two orbs would form close enough that their inward-curving rims nearly met, making a double-mouth. Pre-attention, most such twins died; they competed for the same clumsy prey, and when one succeeded, the other starved. Post-attention, cell-lines in which twin orbs faintly fused—not in body, but in shared nerve-plexus—are more often preserved.

A thin bridge of tissue grows between their deep, hidden sacs. Not enough to share full contents; enough to transmit some stretch-signals and chemical hints. Now, when a sizable victim falls into one mouth, the other feels it in advance and loosens its own rim, wasting less effort. Over generations, pairs become triads, then chains of partially coupled orbs along the same fracture. Each mouth is still itself. But their collective behavior is no longer “wait alone and close when full.” It becomes something more like: “this section of wall has chance of prey now; coordinate closure so some mouths are always hungry, some always recovering.”

They do not split labor deliberately. But the fitness landscape has been nudged so that orbs that accidentally join loose, distributed networks perform better. In the deep dark, a faint kind of company appears, built of mouths and nerves and reflex arcs: distributed appetite with something like rhythm.

The tap-sentinels, their hollow spines now humming at more coherent spectra, are favored when they happen to perch near these orb-chains and adjust their tapping when the orbs contract. A quiet side-effect: mobile grazers that have learned, ancestrally, to avoid certain “danger beats” of vibrating stone now avoid not single orbs but whole predatory arrays, leaving other stretches of wall for safer passing. Overkill becomes rarer. Prey flows are, paradoxically, more reliable. The gorge’s lower food web becomes both more dangerous, locally, and more stable, globally.

This is benevolence, in stone-slow mode: not sparing individuals from teeth, but sparing patterns from annihilation.

---

Only once does the power intervene in a way that even a time-lapsed human observer might, untrained, notice.

It times a fall.

In the gorge’s upper column, where the adjusted light pulses down, the thread-binders have webbed a particular waist of the chasm ever thicker. That section has become a high-traffic mid-air boulevard, crisscrossed with cables polished by oils and covered with hitchhiking films. Weight gathers there. Tension builds in the stone around the main anchoring knobs.

Without attention, the inevitable crack would open anywhere. Ledges would drop in a random roughness, smashing pools, burying sippers, shearing off whole roosts. Instead, just before the strain peaks, the wise presence encourages one particular micro-fracture—so small as to be nearly invisible—to propagate along a path already half-opened by acid-weaver etchings.

The fall still comes. A wall-slab tears free with a thunder nothing down here has heard in centuries. Dust avalanches through the cleft, slamming gliders against rock, tearing threads, sending orbs into panicked premature closure. Many lives end.

But when the dust settles—in days, in weeks, in the long organic sighs that follow—an odd thing is true.

Not a single basin-pool has been destroyed. The slab broke along a line between them, preserving their learned bell-patterns and sipper communities. The densest roost-caves of the new flapping gliders, too, have been spared, though some outer perches now open into a wider void. The majority of the damage falls instead upon already-fragile, lightly-colonized walls that had hosted only the thinnest films and the most generalist grazers.

There is mourning here, if you count the slow attrition of calling patterns from tap-sentinels whose mates were crushed, the sudden absence of certain familiar flight-wakes in the upper air. There is also opportunity. The fresh, broad face of the fallen slab, leaning now partially across the cleft, catches the gentled sky-glow on its upper margin and channels seep-water down its side.

Into that new, slanting plane pour all the devices the gorge has, now, for self-repair.

Spore-clouds fall on it not randomly but in paths biased by leaf-drifter currents which, themselves, have been slightly skewed by the power ahead of the event. Thread-binders build cautious, thin-traffic filaments first, linking safe ledges, then gradually reinforce as grazers and their riders find stable paths across. Acid-weavers at the fracture’s edge etch furiously, as if drunk on new mineral. Bells in pools untouched by the fall adjust their glow-patterns in response to altered vibration, and the larvae of some chain-colonies, cued by that low light music, choose to settle in micro-puddles at the slab’s base rather than drifting aimlessly higher.

To a human imagination, this looks like aid sent in. In truth, it is enablement already in. The wise power stayed its hand for centuries, letting feedbacks, couplings, and subtle redundancies accumulate, then cut the stone where those new capacities would be most tested and most affirmed.

That single fall, and the gorge’s unusually graceful re-occupation of the raw wound, becomes a template. For thousands of years afterward, its echo remains in the bell-lights and tap-codes, in the instinctive routes of ribbon-beasts and the perched decisions of new gliders. The place remembers how it did that. It is now better at not only living, but living through change.

---

Does the power ever show itself? Not as a shape in the chasm, not as a voice.

At one distant point in the gorge’s history—distant to it, close to us—a curious observer of another world might have lowered an instrument on a shining tether, flash-lit these creatures, measured their chemistry. Might have called the thin extra light “artificial” and the biased mutations “induced.” Might have written papers on vertical ecologies and emergent caverns minds.

But benevolence here is not curiosity’s flash. It is a choice, made again and again from outside time, to do less than it might.

It could have bored a side tunnel to the open slope, flooding the cleft with new species from sunlit scree. It does not. The sudden competition and parasites would shatter the gorge’s hard-won coherence. It could have sterilized a layer to see what recolonized. It does not. Ignorance persists in favor of continuity. It could have elevated one lineage to straightforward, manipulating intelligence—given a ribbon-beast or a glider hands and a central brain, turned craft and tool-making loose in these cramped walls. It does not. There is already a mind here, too spread-out to be flattered, too subtle to be flung into fire.

Instead, it accompanies.

- It turns the far sky’s chaos into a more dependable, nourishing gloom.
- It increases the gorge’s capacity to take a wound and remain itself.
- It thickens relationships wherever they might stabilize exploitation into partnership.
- It allows complexity where simpler loops would have burned bright and gone out.

And, perhaps most crucially, it learns in turn.

Because attention runs both ways, if it is true attention. In watching how life here arranges itself—how a few rules about water, rock, and hunger can generate such a dense, mutually informative crowding—the wise power comes to understand that intelligence need not sit behind eyes, need not grip tools, need not plan from a head.

It can be the way drips, glows, taps, glides and grazes, taken all together, form something like a stance toward the future.

Once it has learned that, the power’s touch grows lighter still. It does not depart; it relaxes. It lets the gorge’s own little, distributed mind walk further along the paths already under its many feet.

Light keeps pulsing softly down the throat of stone. The bells still hum and flash, the sentinels still question rock, the gliders still negotiate their patient falling. Somewhere in the deepest dark, a chain of coupled orbs closes in careful sequence over a rare, fat, drifting body, and for a moment the vibrations of their shared nerves, the adjustments in all the lives around them, produce a configuration the cleft has never held before.

No one names it. No one writes it down. But the gorge keeps it, fractionally.

The benevolent power witnesses, approves, and does nothing at all to interfere.
