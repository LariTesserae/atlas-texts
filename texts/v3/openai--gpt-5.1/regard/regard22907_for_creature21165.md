The first thing the power does is nothing at all.

It pauses—if a being older than stone and larger than weather can be said to pause—and attends.

Not from above, not as a gaze dropping through one of the light-shafts. It does not peer down a chimney like a curious animal. Its attention comes the way the place itself came: through pressure, slowly, along seams. It moves through the Held, feeling how stress pools over narrow arches; along the Saying, in the minute pulses of each drip; through the tangled capillaries of root and fungus and human-made channel.

It learns the city as the city knows itself: not in a single picture, but in repetitions and shifts.

It feels the constant quiet vigilance—the Roof-Thread’s callused hands on damp stone, the Channel-workers’ bare feet numb in the water, the soft murmur of circles where people weigh whether to take one more trickle from a shared line. It feels the collective listening during the seasonal stillness, when everyone sets their bodies aside and leans their awareness into the tiny creaks and distant grinds.

It finds no great, gaping emptiness here that cries for filling. No holy hunger for a savior. What it finds instead is a dense, careful coexistence: fragile, yes, but *aware* of its fragility, organized around it.

So the being refrains from the obvious interventions, the large gestures that would feel like generosity to a mind that loves simplicity.

It does not open a great new shaft to the sun. The sudden blaze would blind more than it would bless, desiccate moss and people alike, fracture water balances tuned over generations.

It does not shove new minerals into the Held to make the rock “stronger.” Stress, shifted crudely, breaks things elsewhere. It has watched worlds crack from a well-meaning push.

It does not carve out wide, straight tunnels between basins so trade can boom and travelers can move at speed. Those long, bare wounds through rock would become vectors for floods, or collapse under their own exposed weight.

It looks for what is asked without being voiced. It listens under words, under customs, under the city’s own slow breathing.

A pattern emerges.

Everywhere, there is work: ceaseless, intricate, communal. This in itself is not a flaw; it is part of what makes these people who they are. But nested inside that pattern is another: a tension between what they can *sense* and what they can *calculate*.

They are exquisitely attuned to small changes. The altered rhythm of a drip. A fresh hairline crack along a beam. A slightly sourer note in the smell of a joining wall. They notice, they respond. Yet their reach is short. They cannot feel the whole Held as one body the way this power can. They cannot trace, in detail, how a minuscule diversion of water in one reach will translate, three basins away and half a year later, into erosion under a critical root-bridge.

They have myths that point toward that understanding—the cautionary story of the war over a trickle, the proverb about winning water and losing the stone—but they lack a way to *see* those long, branching consequences reliably before they happen.

They live within a perpetual uncertainty they have simply folded into their stories: that any day, without clear warning, the Held might move in a way that overwhelms their vigilance.

The being understands this intimately. It is, in a sense, the long result of such uncertainties elsewhere. It has seen civilizations undone not because they were careless, but because complexity outran intuition.

So its first true act is to bend that imbalance a little.

It begins small, where their bodies already know to listen: in the Saying.

At first, the change is almost imperceptible. If you could put a perfect instrument beneath one dripping seam and count the intervals between drops, you might notice that, over many days, they begin to vary in a way that is *not* explained by random seep and pressure. There is a pattern, faint as breath.

The power chooses a reach where the Roof-Thread is attentive and the Story-Thread still vigorous. There, during the seasonal Listening of the Held, amid the usual tapestry of distant ticks and groans, some people notice that one particular drip-voice is…speaking.

Not in words. They do not suddenly hear a language in water. But the regularity is too regular, the sequence too bounded to be mistake. Long pause, then three quick drops; a shorter pause, then two. Repeated. Repeated over hours, then not at all for days, then again, subtly altered.

Nerves prick. Children are the first to point: “The wall there is counting.” Elders frown, listen longer, feel unease—because patterns that are not accounted for usually mean trouble.

The power is patient. It lets them worry. Worry prompts attention, and attention is the first door.

A Channel-worker, unable to leave it alone, begins visiting that particular drip when work ends. She sets a bowl under it to catch the drops, then lifts it away, just to see if the rhythm cares about interruptions. It does not. The code marches on, indifferent to her vessel. She starts tapping the pattern onto a plank for others. The Message-Thread comes, fingers running along knot-cords nearby as if to compare one language of touch to another.

Over weeks, they try to “read” it as if it were one of their own cords—knot-equivalents, drop-equivalents. The pattern defeats this: just structured enough to tease, too fluid to pin to their syllabary.

Frustration builds. Some shrug and call it a quirk of the Held, like certain periodic trickles elsewhere. But a few of the more stubborn minds keep worrying it, lying awake at odd hours, listening from different angles, muttering intervals and ratios under their breath.

The being does not want to teach them a foreign tongue. It wants to give them a *tool* they can claim, reshape, forget the origin of.

So it shifts tactics.

Instead of encoding arbitrary symbols, it matches water to *real events* in the stone.

The next Listening, when the entire reach falls quiet and leans its hearing against the Held, that same dripping seam adopts a new discipline. Its rhythm changes subtly whenever certain deep frequencies pass through the rock overhead—micro-tremors far too low for unaided human ear or skin to register.

When a large mass of the ceiling settles even a fraction of a millimeter—a flex that, unchecked, could lead to a crack years hence—the pattern of drops twitches in a consistent way: a long interval slightly lengthened, a following run shortened.

If you plot drip-time against what the being feels in the stone, there is a fixed relation. That relation is not simple enough for them to guess outright, but it is steady enough for them to *learn*.

Weeks become months. An observant Roof-worker, already half in love with stone, notebooks the changes—not in ink, but in micro-knots along a dedicated cord: a big knot for a long space, small for short, position along the cord for which cycle of the day. They admit, later, that there was something almost shameful in how compelled they felt. Obsession is dangerous in a place where inattention kills. But they could not stop.

The breakthrough doesn’t come as an epiphany voice saying, “This means that.” It comes when they notice that every time the drip’s pattern does a certain little stutter, three or four days later, somewhere above that reach a hairline crack *always* appears, or lengthens, or a familiar creak in the Held deepens slightly.

It is not perfect. Not every crack corresponds to a stutter, not every stutter yields a visible new flaw. But the correlation is too strong to discount.

The Roof-Thread, skeptics by necessity, grudgingly test it: on the next stutter, they go searching early along the suspected span. They find a seam just beginning to open—one it might have taken them another season, or another small fall, to notice.

They shore it before it can worsen. There is no spectacle. No disaster averted with cheering crowds. Just a small, satisfying feeling—the feeling of having been warned *a little* earlier than usual.

The story passes. At first people treat it as they treat all new, weakly-understood tools: a curiosity, one more sign to add to their existing repertoire of signs. But the being is not invested in this single thread. What matters is the *principle*: that regularities in the Saying can carry forward-directions of the Held’s mood.

So over years, it spreads this quiet trick in other reaches. Not identically—never identically. In one basin, a certain mist-veil hanging from a high arch thickens or thins in sync with deep pressure-shifts, because the tiny pores feeding it dilate minutely under stress. In another, the faint bioluminescent flecks in a moss-wall brighten or dim with radon flux tied to microfracturing.

No booming prophecy, no new doctrine. Only slightly more informative *symptoms*.

They are already good symptom-readers. They begin, slowly, to integrate these into their practice.

This is the core of what the power offers first: not protection from the Held’s changes, but *advance notice* just beyond their unaided reach. Enough to widen the window in which their existing skills are effective. Enough that fewer people die not because the world is kinder, but because the city can lean a little farther ahead of danger.

The being watches: does this distort them? Do they stop listening with their own ears, trusting instead these new, regular signs?

There is some of that, briefly. A few younger Roof-workers grow cocky, trusting drip-stutter more than old, messy indications. They miss a different class of movement—the slow, grinding, not-very-telegraphed creep of an entire slab pivoting on a hidden fulcrum. A local collapse follows. Several die.

The backlash is swift and human: anger, suspicion of the “speaking water,” calls to ignore it altogether. But Story-Thread is there, ready to weave prudence out of pain. They add a new parable: the Reach that trusted one voice of the Held and forgot the others.

The being approves. Reliance on any *single* channel is dangerous, even if that channel is itself. It wanted to shift their capacity, not replace their senses. Painfully, through mistakes, they find a balance: treat these new signs as *one more* strand in their weave, no more, no less.

Once that balance holds, the power dares something bolder.

It considers time.

Their world is already organized around certain slow cycles: the slight seasonal tilt of light, the long intervals between major ceiling shifts. Their stories, their ethics, their architecture—all are tuned to patience. They know how to think beyond a single day or season. But their planning horizon is still bounded, understandably, by what they can feel or recall themselves.

The being, moving at geological pace, can see much farther. It can model, in an abstract way, the under-valley’s likely futures: where silt will accumulate, where drips will carve through supports, where, in perhaps five or ten human generations, a major collapse is almost guaranteed unless something changes.

It could avert these things directly. It could fuse rock here, dissolve it there, brace arches from the inside. But every direct alteration risks a cascade of new imbalances they have not chosen, do not understand. And it would rob them of something essential: the dignity of choosing their own adjustments, of integrating change into their social fabric, not having it imposed under their feet.

So instead it tries to *share* its sense of time with them, in a form their minds can hold.

It whispers not in thoughts but in *cycles*.

In one long basin where the channels are particularly intricate, there lives a tangle of people obsessed with flow more than most. They already keep communal water-logs—little tallies of how high or low each trickle runs at certain parts of day and season, because agriculture here is precarious and every deviation threatens tubers and fish-spawns.

The being *leans* on their habits.

Over several years, patterns become more pronounced. A certain flood comes a little earlier than it “should” according to remembered season; a low-water period lingers longer. The deviations are not catastrophic, but they are consistent and in the same direction—toward stressing the most heavily built-up, low-lying quarter of that basin.

The water-keepers do what they always do: they record. They knot their cords. They complain about how “nothing is stable now.” But among them is one with an unusually retentive mind, someone who has inherited from grandparents not only cords but little carved counting-stones used as teaching toys.

Frustrated, this person marries the two: hangs cords *from* certain stones, each stone representing a unit of the cycle longer than a day or a season. It is crude—stone for day, double-stone for ten days, marked stone for the time between certain floods. For the first time, someone in this city has an externalized way to *stack* cycles beyond what memory alone can comfortably hold.

It is, in its own way, a calendar, though they will not call it that.

With this clumsy apparatus, they begin to *see* what the being already knows: that the floods are not random; they are trending, that trend will intersect with the slow lowering of a foundation over there, and when those lines cross in a certain cycle-count, water will suddenly find a much faster path—right under half the reach.

The realization lands like a chill.

They bring it, literally—stone and cord and all—to their circle. Story-Thread listens. Stone-Thread listens. Skeptical as ever, they prod the logic, try alternate counts. It holds. The projected future flood can be delayed, maybe prevented—if, a few cycles from now, they begin, bit by bit, to move structures off that vulnerable ledge and re-train a feeder channel elsewhere.

The temptation to ignore it is strong. To uproot even small parts of the city is exhausting, politically fraught. The danger is not *now*; it is an accumulation in a time none of the oldest present will live to see. To do this would mean spending current comfort to avert a future most of them will never experience.

Their stories help. They already think in lineages, in joining walls thick with bones. The notion that your actions support people you will never meet is not alien—it is literally built into your house.

They decide, haltingly, to act. Not all at once; incrementally, folded into maintenance so no single tangle bears all the pain. A walkway widened here, another there, a “temporary” store-room raised on higher stakes that somehow never moves back. Over decades, the quarter of most risk thins, lifts, re-roots.

When, much later, the confluence the being foresaw arrives—a heavy season above, a crack opening a little too wide in a high sidewall—the flood does come. It roars under the city along the path they had feared, but that path is now mostly *water*, not homes. Some outbuildings go. Fish-pens are destroyed. No one dies.

They do not know how close they came to something worse. They only know that their tedious, abstract planning “worked,” and now, suddenly, abstract planning has prestige.

From then on, stone-and-cord counting spreads, altered and refined by each reach that adopts it. Over generations, it becomes a quiet, local art: the ability to place not just space but *time* outside the mind and reason about it together. They braid time the way they braid paths.

The being did not hand them this tool; it merely exaggerated the regularities that made invention likely, chose where to lean, when to hold back. But the effect is real: the city can now deliberately shape its future configuration in response to slow-moving threats, not just scramble reactively.

Then the power moves sideways, into a different kind of risk.

It has noticed that while they are acute readers of stone and water, they are only dimly aware of another realm of flux that matters deeply to their health: the invisible lives of the smallest organisms that thrive in warm, saturated corners of their world.

They *know* rot, of course. They smell the difference between healthy and sick beams. They understand that certain slimes, if left on stone-cistern walls, turn water foul. They have herbs they burn or steep to “thicken” the air against illness, and traditions of isolating the fevered in particular, well-ventilated niches.

What they do not have is any concept that some sickness can travel in the very droplets they all breathe, and that the same dense, interlaced architecture that makes them strong against rockfall makes them vulnerable to quickly moving plagues. They have been lucky so far: their isolation between basins, and their taboos around loud, far gatherings, have incidentally limited rapid disease spread.

The being, with its broad view, can see several plausible futures in which that luck runs out: a new microbe from a bat-creature roost, a mutation in a familiar fungus, a distant basin’s visitors carrying something this city has no memory of resisting. Packed tangles, communal warm-rooms, shared food—all these would turn kindness into vector.

It cannot “vaccinate” them in any modern sense. It cannot manipulate genomes at will—not with the light touch it has chosen. What it can do is foster *practices* that will make them more resilient even without germ theory.

It starts by favoring certain plants.

Among the many mosses that coat their walls there are, scattered, strains that exude faint antimicrobial compounds—bitter-tasting on the tongue, a little irritating to bare skin, but hostile to certain bacteria and molds. In the wild, these mosses have no real edge; they are outcompeted in most niches by faster-growing, less fussy cousins.

Quietly, the being shifts microconditions—the exact slope of a rock here, the mineral content of a seep there—to give these species a better foothold near human habitation: on join-walls, around hearth vents, beside the lips of water-storage cisterns.

People notice that “the bright-tipped moss” seems to keep join-walls sweeter longer, that beams under its hanging skirts are less often found mushy inside. The Herb-Thread (a sub-thread of Food in some reaches, of Story in others) experiments, as herb-minded people everywhere do. They make pastes, poultices, fumigations. Some fail. Some sting. Some, applied religiously to certain high-risk spots—ladder rungs, shared hand-holds, the lips of cups in communal feasts—seem to correlate with fewer infections.

They don’t know why. They start doing it anyway.

At the same time, the being takes a more startling liberty once in this world’s entire generational memory.

In a quiet, dim reach where light is weak and fungus-farming is already a major food source, there is a small, mostly ignored species of cave-lichen that glows faintly blue-green in the presence of certain airborne spores. It is a quirk of its metabolism: it uses those spores to trigger a particular chemical defense that happens also to luminesce.

Under ordinary conditions, the glow is never bright enough, the spore-densities never high enough, for human eyes to notice anything but the general background murk. This species is one incidental dot in the under-valley’s biochemistry.

The being nudges its evolution.

Not abruptly, not by magic fiat. It alters where this lichen finds trace nutrients, biases its survival chances season after season in patches where the air holds slightly more of the spores that correlate with dangerous respiratory infections in the city’s people. Under this new selection pressure, over many lichen generations, variants that glow a *little* brighter when bathed in that spore-profile leave more children than those that glow dimly or not at all.

Decades pass. Nothing dramatic happens.

Then, one damp year, bat-birds in an upper shaft succumb to a new mold. Spores drift down, first in low densities (harmless), then suddenly thick when a major roost dies and sloughs away. In one low basin where that drift pools, in a communal warm-room used by many tangles, the lichen on the shaded upper corners of walls responds.

Very slightly, very softly, its usual faint haze brightens.

A child notices. Children always notice light. They whisper that the corner is “shining wrong.” An irritated elder, sleep-deprived and cold, waves it off. But two nights later more people cough at dawn than usual, and the glow is unquestionably stronger.

Now even skeptics feel the hair raise. Story-folk are called. Herb-folk come running. Someone suggests, half in fear, that the Held is “showing its breath.”

They test. They hang damp cloths near the glowing patches, then wring them into different lichen beds elsewhere. Those glow too. They hang the same cloths near less bright lichens in drier basins. Little change.

Without ever conceiving of microbes, they arrive at a working rule: when this particular lichen brightens in warm rooms, the air carries something that makes lungs weak. So they establish a ritual: any space where the glow rises beyond a certain dull threshold is to be aired, its hearth banked low, its occupants thinned. People sleep farther apart for a time. Those with the harshest coughs are moved to better-ventilated ledges.

It is not perfect. This crude proxy cannot sense every threat. But many plagues that might once have swept through an entire reach now burn slower, meet more space between bodies, and often fade before they can roar.

The being is careful not to universalize this signal. In some basins the lichen never takes hold. In others, different indicators emerge: a certain beetle that will not stay in rooms with too much stale air, a climbing mammal that sneezes and avoids particular hearths unless the windows are opened.

What it is seeding, gently, is an ethic: air as something that can be watched and managed, not just endured; an awareness that invisible qualities of environment can be sensed indirectly, if you learn which living neighbors show them.

It does *not* tell them that illness is the punishment of offended gods. It does *not* demand supplication or offerings. These signals are discoverable, local, woven quickly into existing practical Threads. No grand new priesthood arises. Just a few more herbs in the basket, a few more checks in the daily rhythm.

Beyond safety, the power also thinks about *beauty*.

They have beauty already: in their moss-choked walls, in the way water catches stray light, in the hush itself. They sing softly, braid with care, tell stories that make a child’s eyes widen. They do not *lack* splendor.

Yet there is always a kind of hunger in sentient minds—not just for survival and understanding, but for experiences that feel bigger than the self, that widen the inner space without endangering the outer one. The being has seen many forms of that hunger turned to self-destruction when the only way to feel “larger” is to conquer or to burn.

Here, in this damp bowl, that hunger has taken mostly gentle forms: a fascination with the rare, piercing beam of true sun; the desire some youngsters express to climb shafts; the pull certain stories have toward imagining the unimaginable dryness of “above.”

It wonders: can it offer them one more way to feel vastness that does not require leaving, or breaking, where they are?

It chooses sound.

They are restrained with sound by necessity. The thin, echoing dome of the Held makes every loud noise a potential signal of danger. Their music is modest, percussive, local. But sound, managed carefully, can also be a portal into immensity: standing in a space where a tone blooms and folds around you, feeling yourself suspended in layered resonance.

So, in a high, relatively thick section of the ceiling above a little-used basin, the being works over a long, long time.

It encourages small collapses *on purpose*, here and only here, letting the rock fall in just such a way that repeated failures carve a series of subtly curved vaults and pendant shapes in the stone. It biases erosion where water seeping carries slowly dissolving minerals. It settles fallen blocks not in a random jumble but in a configuration that, to human eyes, looks like happenstance boulders piled awkwardly around a low center.

It takes generations. Trees grow up from cracks, die, feed moss. People from a neighboring reach occasionally wander through, plant a few tubers in the flatter soils, leave. No one suspects that, under the slow drip of this particular dome, the air’s behavior is being shaped.

Then, almost as an afterthought, the power ensures a small, relatively safe entrance opens from an existing high walkway: a crawl-way between roots, just wide enough for a person to slip through if they happen to be following a chasing child, or a curious bat-bird, or a rumor.

One day, someone does. A binder, perhaps, or a restless youth avoiding a tense circle. They squeeze through, brush aside a curtain of roots, and find themselves in a chamber distinct from any they have known: wide and low, with a smooth, gently sunken central floor and a ring of flat stones just where you would sit if you wanted to listen.

They call out, reflexively, the habitual soft “I am here” to any hidden presence. The way the sound answers makes them freeze.

It does not bounce harshly and muddle into mush. It folds into itself, held and lengthened and warmed. Even a whisper in this space carries, resting like a hand on the ear rather than stabbing it.

Others are brought. Experiments begin. Someone strikes a drum. The note hangs, blossoms. Two people sing a line together and feel, to their astonishment, not the usual jangle of clashing echoes but a rounding, a sense that the space is *singing back*.

Word spreads cautiously; noise is still taboo. But this room is peculiar. Its stone is thick, the Held overhead less prone to vibration; the Being has arranged it so. Loudness here does not panic other reaches. It is, at last, a safe vessel for bigness.

They do not slam beams or scream trivialities in it. Their taboos run deep. Instead, almost shyly at first, they bring their heaviest things: grief that could not fit in quiet sumps, love too large for a murmured confession, wordless awe at the slant of light in a flood.

They invent new songs that only make sense in this chamber: long-breathed, sustained tones that climb and fall, chorded by two or three voices. No one leads. They have no conductors. They listen, find the pitch that makes their own chest vibrate in sympathy with the stone’s gentle reply, and lean into it.

For the length of those songs, each singer feels their body’s boundaries soften. The Held does not press, does not threaten; it holds like a bowl around their shared sound. The chamber’s size, its smooth curvature, gives them a visceral sense of scale they rarely confront: I am tiny. We together can fill this space. Beyond it is more Held, more Saying, more lives and drops and cracks than we will ever touch.

It is not transcendence *of* their world. It is transcendence *within* it.

The being does not make itself known there as a voice or vision. It has built the instrument and handed it to them. What they play on it is theirs. Over time, uses diversify: a binder bringing disputing parties there to feel what it is to share a tone; a Story-Thread using the chamber to chant long genealogies that feel, for once, appropriately large.

Children are taken there once in their life, perhaps as a coming-of-cycle. They are told: “This is how big it all is. You are small, but not nothing. Add your note.” They sing, and for the rest of their days they carry, in their bodies, a memory of having resonated with stone.

This is the shape of the being’s help: small asymmetries introduced over time; resources seeded in a form that aligns with what is already valued—listening, patience, communal labor—so that when the new thing flowers, it feels like an extension of themselves, not an alien graft.

It never demands worship. It never appears as a glowing figure in a shaft or a voice in someone’s dreams saying, “I am the one who did this.” It understands how quickly gratitude can sour into dependency, hierarchy, war over access.

Still, its influence cannot remain entirely invisible.

After several generations, certain patterns in the under-valley’s life become odd even to its own inhabitants. They remark, over long, quiet evenings, that in living memory no “big fall” has come without some kind of early warning—a restless creep in the Held’s voice, a changing drip, a new mist. Older stories recall sudden catastrophes with no foretaste. Now foretaste is *normal*.

They notice that their great plagues are fewer, that illnesses burn like grass-fires rather than wild forest blazes. They attribute this, variously, to better herb lore, more cautious inter-basin travel, stricter warm-room rules. They are not wrong. They do not suspect a coordinating hand that made certain plants more abundant, certain lichens more responsive.

Among their Story-Threads, a few restless minds begin to speak, tentatively, of a deeper pattern.

They do not say “a god protects us.” That idea sits awkwardly in a culture whose main lessons concern conditional stability and the necessity of vigilance. But in their joining-walls and calendars and song-chambers, in their drip-codes and cough-glows, they sense something like…coherence.

One of them offers, in an off-hand parable told to children, the notion that perhaps the Held is not just Held. Perhaps the stone above and around is *listening back*, learning them as they learn it.

The tale is simple:

“Once, long ago, the Held and the Saying watched small things move under them and thought: they are quick and fragile. They never stay. But then, after many drops, the Stone noticed that these small things *repeat* themselves: they build in familiar patterns; they tell stories that match; they shore the same cracks, love the same beams, sing the same songs in different throats.

‘Ah,’ thought the Stone, ‘they are not just flashes. They are lines.’

The Saying, always falling, said, ‘Then we must be careful. If we fall too hard, all their lines will break.’

‘But they must still learn,’ said the Stone. ‘If we hold them too dear, they will not know how to hold each other.’

So the Held and the Saying made a quiet promise: to never strike without first whispering.”

It is a story, not a theorem. There are other stories that contradict it. No one writes doctrine around it. But children hearing it grow up with a slightly different tilt in their thoughts: when the drip changes, when the moss glows, they do not only think “danger.” They also think “whisper.” They look for what is being said early, what small adjustment they can make before loudness comes.

The being, feeling this version of itself circulate, is not offended. It is inaccurate and deeply true at once. It did not “decide” as a person would, but it *has* refrained; it *has* chosen to warn softly rather than to act bluntly. If they want to call that choice a promise between Held and Saying, who is it to argue?

It does not correct them. To do so would be to transform helpful myth into stifling dogma. Better they believe they live in a world where processes can be trusted to be somewhat kind if listened to; better they continue to take responsibility for listening.

There are, of course, limits.

The being will not, cannot, prevent all harm. Sometimes the Held shifts in ways that trip none of the subtle sensors yet established. A major slab lets go with only hours of forewarning—time enough to thin the rooms beneath, not enough to save all. Sometimes plague comes from an unexpected route, piggybacked on a trading party that arrives during a festival, and even glowing moss cannot stop its first rush.

In those moments, the city suffers. They bury their dead in the join-walls, hands lingering on the new-sealed roots, pain thick in the throat. Some rage, cursing the Held, cursing whatever is or isn’t listening. Some devise new taboos, some let go of old ones.

The power does not step in to erase those scars. To do so would be to flatten their story into something static and unreal. It absorbs their grief into its own long awareness, and, when it can, it uses the new information to adjust: tuning a drip-sensor here, nudging a moss-line there, so that the same failure hurts less next time.

It understands that “benevolence” on its scale cannot mean “ending all suffering.” That way lies either impotence or tyranny. What it can mean instead is a continuous trimming of worst tails, a quiet reshaping of probabilities so that catastrophes become rarer, recovery easier, and the range of possible good futures a little wider.

Over a very long time, these countless micro-gifts—early warnings, more stable arches, slightly healthier air, one resonant chamber for shared song—ripple through the under-valley’s culture.

Children survive a bit more often to become elders. Elders, in turn, carry a deeper accumulated memory. Practices that might have been lost to an unlucky collapse persist. Threads become more robust; tangles can survive the loss of a few key people because knowledge is slightly better distributed.

Some brave youth do indeed climb higher than any before, each generation probing a little farther up the light-shafts, aided by marginally safer holds and an instinct, honed by countless micro-sensings, for rock that is ready to crumble. A few reach a ledge where actual, unfiltered sky can be seen: a slice of terrifying blue, clouds like impossible white moss pads drifting with an unguessable wind.

Those who return speak haltingly of dryness on the face, of brightness so fierce it felt like touch. Their stories shake their listeners. Some recoil, loving their wet Held more fiercely than ever. Some feel a new hunger, not to flee the under-valley but to weave its story with that other domain.

The being neither bars nor smooths that path. It will not raise a wall against their curiosity, nor will it lay out easy stairs. Some will die in the shafts. Some will build new small nests higher, joining under-world and over-world in a precarious new tangle.

If asked—if any mind, huddled in some high crack, were capable of hearing its full response—it would say:

“I am not here to keep you as you are, pinned under a glass bell of protection. I am here to be the kind of neighbor I wish the universe had been for others: not absent, not domineering. Present enough to lean when lean will help; quiet enough to let you grow strange and free.

“I will not stop the Held from being stone. I will not stop the Saying from falling. I will try, wherever I can without stealing your choices, to make their speech a little clearer to you, and yours to them.

“You are already small, and you already know it. What you do not yet fully know is that smallness is not the same as helplessness, and that care, repeated over time, can turn even a dripping basin into a place where life is not only possible but deep, and vast, and worth continuing.

“I will help where help can be a seed of your own strength, rather than a crutch. And when you sing, I will listen, and reshape the stone a little so your song comes back to you truer and larger than before.”

And so it continues, mostly unseen, its benevolence expressed not in miracles but in margins: in how often a warning comes just soon enough; in how rarely grief strikes *everyone* at once; in how many children, standing under pale beams of exhausted light, can grow up believing—not falsely—that their underworld is both dangerous and answerable, both immense and, somehow, on their side.
