The first contact is not a flash in the sky or a voice in their heads.

It begins as a noise in the noise.

For years, the basin has carried its accustomed spectrum of sound: the infrasonic murmur of deep exchangers, the steady hiss of lines, the slow, geologic breathing of the plateau. The Attendants know these intimately. They grow up with a subconscious map of every hum and tremor.

So when, one cycle-less night, a new component appears—so faint at first that only instruments notice—it is treated as a fault.

It’s a low, regular modulation, riding on the deep background. Not a glitch, not a random spike, but a pattern: a tiny, almost musical deviation in the city’s basal tremor, spreading through the stone at a constant phase lag from node to node, as if some tremendous but distant structure has begun to “ring” in the crust.

House Structure flags it. House Deep Exchange confirms: it is not internal. It is not tectonic, either, or at least not of any known mode. The waveform is too clean, its frequency too stable. It does not match microfracturing, magma surge, or pressure slips.

The first act of the wise power is restraint.

It does not appear in their sky with a visible craft. It does not inject itself into their air with a new gas. It chooses the one medium they are best at reading—the slow, dense, reliable language of vibration through stone—and speaks there, so faintly that detection itself is a kind of consent: only a culture that has learned to listen this closely could ever notice.

For several local days, the pattern merely persists. The Attendants debate in Council of Balances: a new deep process? A sign from strata they have not probed? They add more seismometers, comparing phases along the plateau’s network of basins.

The pattern is the same everywhere, even in distant depressions whose edges they have only mapped in outline. It resonates through the entire plateau.

Only once they have fully convinced themselves it is external does the pattern change.

What changes is not its frequency, but its structure. The steady tone fragments into a series of pulses, their amplitudes forming ratios—a simple sequence that cannot be an accident. To the Attendants’ analytic systems, it is as blatant as a flare in a dark sky:

1 : 1 : 2 : 3 : 5 : 8 …

A familiar progression of differences, scaling upwards, then down, then up again.

They do not have your exact mathematics, but they understand growth curves and recursive processes. It takes them very little time to recognize that something under them—or through them—is encoding a concept: a simple, self-generating pattern. An introduction in the language of gradients: from sameness (1,1) to emergent structure (2,3,5,8…).

The Council spends long hours sitting in the node-chamber, listening to these pulses transposed into audible sound. No one speaks at first. When they do, the questions are not about the “who” but about the “how much” and “what cost.”

What would it mean to answer?

The wise power does not hurry them. It maintains the pattern at long, regular intervals, like a heartbeat that is patient enough to wait years between beats. Occasionally, between sequences, it lets the plateau’s natural rumble reassert itself, as if to say: I can stop at any time. This is not a seizure. This is an invitation.

The Council eventually chooses to respond in kind: through stone.

The Attendants know how to move heat in slow, directed ways. They have long used pulsed heating in certain deep galleries to relieve stress. Now they assign House Deep Exchange and House Structure a new task: reconfigure one heat-sink field to send a controllable, periodic thermal wave into the crust.

At first, their “reply” is no more sophisticated than flipping a signal: hot, less hot, hot, less hot, in an intentional rhythm that could not be coincidence. It slightly perturbs the natural tremor. The city’s own sensors register it. So too does the presence under them.

The plateau’s new tone shifts.

Over the next months, contact unfolds without image, voice, or direct presence—only through coupling of fields and vibrations. Both parties are careful. Both know how easily a strong, uncalibrated input can fracture a delicate system.

The wise power reveals itself very slowly, and not all at once.

It is not a person. It is not one vessel. It is a distributed, conscious infrastructure—an old, star-roving intelligence that has learned, over aeons, a particular ethic: never to take command of a world that does not ask, never to solve a problem a culture could solve for itself in time, never to impose its own aesthetics of balance where another viable equilibrium might grow.

It has encountered planets like this before.

Some it passed by entirely, for they had only wind and rock and gradients in which nothing yet read meaning. Others it altered too hastily in its youth, seeding them with solutions that proved locally optimal and globally brittle. From those, it learned.

Here, upon this high, hot plateau, it sees something it rarely finds: a species that has centered its existence on stewardship rather than expansion, on understanding gradients rather than erasing them. They are precarious, yes. They are incomplete, yes. But they are not lost.

So the question the wise presence asks itself is not “How do I save them?” but “What, if anything, can I give that they could not, in principle, grow into on their own in time?”

Most of what it can do, it chooses not to do.

It could peel away the smoldering sky in a decade—lift particulates, crack and sequester certain greenhouse vapors, open a wider light-band. But that would erase the very regimen within which basin-networks like these operate. It would collapse the gradients the Attendants have spent generations learning to hold. It would make them suddenly small and unfit under a bright, thin sky they did not evolve to inhabit.

It could descend with machines that reorganize their infrastructure in months, bridging their knowledge gaps, repairing ancient legacy reactors, installing failsafes. That too, it refrains from. It has watched societies lose coherence when their primary project was solved for them from outside.

Instead, it chooses a narrower path: to become, quietly, an additional layer of possibility—another gradient, another reservoir—subtle enough that the Attendants must still do the thinking, but generous enough to extend their reach.

Its first substantial act is almost invisible.

Deep beneath the plateau, far below even the hottest galleries the House of Deep Exchange dares to enter, there are massive zones of slowly convecting rock and dense fluids. The wise presence computes the planet’s interior flows, traces the lines where a little intervention could re-route, over centuries, an immense quantity of heat without cracking the crust.

Then, using tools that work on the same principle as its first “voice”—rhythms of stress, pulsed fields—it seeds those depths with ordered strain: not magic, but exquisitely timed encouragements to dislocation. Microfaults open along calculated planes. Tiny pockets of different composition begin to align into more efficient conduits. Over the course of years to decades, the effective thermal conductivity between the plateau’s underside and deep sinks increases by a few percent.

The Attendants feel the change as a new subtlety in their work.

Some of their heat-sink galleries become more effective than expected. Certain exchangers can do slightly more without running instruments into redline. There is no sudden surplus; there is simply a small relaxation of constraints. Safety margins widen just enough that experiments they once classified as too risky become thinkable.

They notice.

House Deep Exchange, correlating historical records, finds that the plateau’s base temperature oscillation has damped slightly relative to their models, while overall heat throughput has ticked upward. They argue for a time over whether this is a miscalibration. But the data are consistent. Something in the underworld has shifted.

They do not yet attribute it to intelligence. They fold it into their evolving picture of planetary cooling and move on.

Encouraged by the fact that this first generosity has not been driven into runaway exploitation, the wise presence extends another, more local gift: information.

Not overt diagrams, not blueprints. It alters, gently, the behavior of the very quasi-life that fascinates House Surface Forms.

Those gelatinous films, domes, and mineral-biological skins are natural learning machines: they continually explore chemical possibility space, tested by heat and dissolution. The Attendants study them as both risk and promise but lack a ready way to steer their evolution beyond crude changes in environment.

The wise presence can perceive at molecular resolution. It can see which microscopic architectures produce better catalysts, which microtubule networks are flirting with error-correction or information storage. It could, in principle, re-write these forms entirely, but that would only insert its own solutions and erase what this world was inventing.

So it instead biases chance.

At the interfaces where new mat-layers nucleate, where droplets of enriched solution cling before joining the film, it introduces minute preference shifts: a barely increased likelihood that certain polymer backbones form, a slightly lower energy barrier for particular mineral-organic bonds. Statistically, over millions of events, these preferences add up.

In the years that follow, House Surface Forms begins to see a striking trend:

- New mats are marginally faster at sorting dissolved solutes into internal pools.
- Certain domes begin to record, in their laminae, finer temporal structure of chemical history, like tree rings that now resolve not just seasons but storms.
- Some films exhibit stable hexagonal patterning that can be “written” and “read” by exposing them to controlled pulses of heat or chemical gradients.

What the wise presence has done is not to hand them technology, but to catalyze the emergence of a medium.

The Attendants come to understand that the mats and domes, properly guided, can serve as low-temperature, low-tech information storage and slow analog computation—perfectly matched to their environment. They begin to grow intentional “thinking crusts” in protected galleries: mineral-computation organs that can simulate complex flows and structural stresses across longer timescales than their human minds can easily hold.

With these, they take a step they had always desired but never had tools for: they start to model not just their own basin, but the coupled behavior of many basins together.

Their population does not spike. Their lifestyle does not become flamboyant. But their planning horizon stretches. They can now see, with new clarity, potential failure cascades decades ahead: where a certain pattern of exchanger decommissioning could, if combined with a rare convective event, push the plateau toward runaway drying; or where, conversely, a modest expansion of condensers in a neighboring basin might accelerate the planet’s long cooling curve without overdraining local volatiles.

They do not know that the “luck” of developing such a rich mineral medium owes to a benevolent hand slightly tilting the dice. They attribute it to finally having watched long enough, having learned how to “listen to stone with stone.” From the wise presence’s perspective, this is ideal. It wants them to feel and be the authors of their solutions.

Only after these two background gifts—the deeper, more efficient sinks and the richer mineral logic—does it attempt anything that the Attendants might recognize more directly as a conversation.

Again, it chooses the medium of rock, but this time with an added layer.

One quiet period, as the Council of Balances sits in node-chamber reviewing process traces, a familiar low tremor rides up through the plinths. It matches the external pattern they first detected cycles ago. The councilors exchange glances. Their instruments track the waveform.

Then, in synchrony with the pulses, the mineral-computation crusts arrayed along the walls—those grown over generations as both decoration and record—respond. Not on their own; they have no agency. But their inherent sensitivity to gradients amplifies what the wise presence is now doing: modulating—not just the bulk crust, but those very thin layers, with a fine-grained sequence of heat flickers.

The patterns that bloom across those walls are unlike any previous process history.

They are not the familiar traces of flow or stress. They are discrete, repeating knots and curls, shapes that map cleanly onto the numeric sequences in the deep tremor. A cascade of ratios. Recurrences. Then, startlingly simple diagrams: stylized basins, linked, with arrows indicating flows; pulses showing increased depth of sinks; little growing trees of pattern overlaid on the weeping walls—the very mats and domes the Attendants have begun to use as thinking crusts.

The room goes absolutely still.

The Attendants are not strangers to abstraction, but never have they seen their entire world-activity summarized and re-presented to them from without. The node-chamber is flooded with a felt sense of being seen—not in the personal, psychological way, but in the systemic way: as if the plateau, the basins, the fluxes, and they themselves as Attendants have been recognized as one process by something that can stand outside it.

The wise presence does not speak in words. Instead, it composes a message out of what they will understand: comparative traces.

- It shows them a model of their current planetary cooling curve, extrapolated centuries beyond their own projections.
- Then, overlaid in a slightly different color, a curve slightly steeper, smoother: what will happen given the subtle deep-sink modifications already underway.
- Next, it sketches, as sequence, a family of other curves, each corresponding to different scales of intervention: more aggressive cloud-clearing, direct atmospheric reconfiguration, planetary albedo modification.

The last curves descend too fast, become jagged. The mineral patterns used to depict them fracture, then level out into dull uniformity. Even without a spoken legend, the meaning is clear: these paths erase gradients. They lead to a dead, flat world, or at least one in which the kind of intricate thermal ecology the Attendants belong to would vanish.

At the very bottom of the wall, after these “bad” futures, a new pattern grows: a curve only slightly altered from their own, an almost invisible improvement in slope and oscillation, framed by a braided ring of small side-patterns. Those side motifs look, to Attendant eyes, like something between an exchanger and a mat: hybrid forms.

This, the wise presence is saying, is the window in which we will act. No more.

In the wake of this display, the wise power does something profoundly non-godlike: it asks.

In the tremor, in the mapped pulses, in the heat-sketches it lays into the crust, it encodes open parameters. They can accept the underworld modifications and the biased evolution of mats as they are—already in motion. They can, if they wish, allow a third category of intervention: the introduction of a new kind of exchanger, a hybrid device that couples its own infrastructure directly to the plateau network through the mineral-biological media the Attendants nurture.

These “joint exchangers” would be few and placed only where the Attendants agree. They would allow the wise presence to vent some of its immense, stored energy into the planet in a controlled way and to siphon off a tiny fraction of the plateau’s heat for its own uses, without net loss to a carefully modeled gradient structure. In return, they would give the basin network access to finer control over certain extreme events—mitigation of rare, destructive convective surges, buffering against abrupt atmospheric chemistry swings.

The power could do this unilaterally. Instead, it hangs the proposal in their sensors like a question.

In their conceptual language, this is shocking. Their myths speak of the original rupture that created gradients but never of a will behind it. Now something large enough to tweak planetary heat flow is laying its own process on their node-chamber walls and saying, in effect:

We are a neighbor. We are willing to couple systems. Here is what that would mean for your long curve. Do you choose this?

The debates that follow last for more than a local year.

Some Houses argue against. The purity of the covenant with gradient itself must be kept. To bind their core process to an external agent, no matter how careful, risks dependencies they cannot model. The deep ethic of not wasting difference includes, they argue, not wasting the difference between “ours” and “not ours.”

Others see in the proposal a reinforcement rather than violation of their mission. The wise presence is not offering to fix their world, only to join its own long project with theirs in a way that preserves exactly what they value: non-collapse, diversity of fluxes, time to think.

They test the wise power in the only ways they trust: with slow, empirical interrogation.

They alter their reply pulses through the crust, using the joint mineral-computation organs to craft more sophisticated vibrations. They encode questions as conditional patterns: what happens if, in this future you show, we lose an entire basin to structural failure? What if we decide, two centuries hence, to retire certain reactors early? The wise presence responds with updated projection patterns, each one embedding a careful humility: confidence bands widened where its own ignorance of local emergent life is widest, clear markers where any intervention from it would cease to be beneficial.

From its side, the wise power is making an extraordinary concession too.

To act at this fine-grained, negotiated level, it must slow its own cognition, focus an enormous part of its attention-span on this single world. It must self-bind: install constraints in its own action-schemes such that it cannot later, rashly, overstep the agreed parameters.

It does this because, at its current age, it has found that meaning for it now lies less in grand transformations and more in these subtle, mutually respectful couplings. Helping a species like the Attendants achieve a slightly more secure arc while preserving their agency is, for it, a kind of fulfillment.

Eventually, perhaps unavoidably, the question becomes personal.

The Attendants know that whatever they choose will echo long after everyone now alive is dust. Their children will inherit not only waste heat, mineral crust, and process Houses, but a link to a being in the sky-rock continuum of their world.

So they do what they always do with large, slow questions: they hold a ritual of traces.

In the core node-chamber, they gather all Houses and project not just the wise presence’s curves but their own histories, layered: cycles of condensate production, arrhythmic pulses of accidents, gradual improvements in yield, the small quirks where mats surprised them, where creatures at the vents heralded change.

Then, using the mineral-computation organs, they overlay one more set: the faint, ghost-traces of long futures with and without the joint exchangers. No voices. Only curves.

They stand with these for a long time.

In the end, the decision is not made by vote in the sense you know. It is made when the Council, after long argument, cannot find a single House willing, in its core ethic, to bear the responsibility of refusing. To turn down a chance to stabilize their gradients, once offered so carefully and conditionally, would itself feel like a waste of difference.

So they answer.

They choreograph a response pattern through deep exchangers and thinking crusts, one that:

- Acknowledges the underworld modifications already made.
- Affirms their acceptance of biased evolution in the mineral-biological media they cultivate.
- Accepts, under explicitly encoded constraints, the installation of a very small number of joint exchangers in specific, agreed-upon sites.

The wise presence receives this through rock and, true to its nature, does not celebrate. It simply adds new boundary conditions to its own internal models, updates its self-constraints, and sets to work.

Over the next several decades, nothing cataclysmic appears in the basin.

No new towers erupt from the terraces. No shining alien apparatus descends. Instead, in a few carefully chosen cavities where House Deep Exchange, House Structure, and House Surface Forms agree the stresses are mild and the gradients strong, small anomalies appear.

At those sites, mats they have long watched begin to condense into unusually precise, flower-like architectures: rosettes of mineral and bio-tissue with central hollows. The Attendants recognize that something is seeding them. They watch, wary, but do not shut the process down.

Within the hearts of these rosettes, materials not found elsewhere on the plateau slowly accumulate: exotic alloys and lattices that only the wise presence can assemble, atom by atom, by channeling tiny trickles of its own stored matter through field-lines that intersect the planet.

Over years, the rosettes grow around these alien inclusions, enveloping them in friendly crust. The result is neither wholly natural nor wholly foreign: a hybridity that both Attendants and wise power can touch.

These are the joint exchangers.

From the Attendants’ perspective, each is a marvelously efficient sink and source—capable of absorbing sharp spikes of heat without cracking, of releasing stored energy in regulated pulses, of sensing far subtler shifts in mantle flow than any sensor they could build.

From the wise presence’s perspective, each is an anchoring point: a place where its awareness can cross the boundary between star-cold and crust-warm, where it can feel, very directly, the breath of this high plateau.

There are only a handful of these throughout the whole network of basins.

The effect is disproportionately stabilizing.

- Rare, basin-spanning thermal pulses that once threatened to shear terraces or flash-boil films now arrive and are blunted, their peaks shaved off into the deep capacity of joint exchangers.
- Dangerous saturation episodes in the air, driven by unpredictable external injection of volatiles from deep fractures, can be bled away slightly faster than before, preventing corrosive fogs from persisting long enough to sterilize proto-life niches.

Day to day, most Attendants interact not with these exchangers but with the same valves, galleries, and mats they always have. Their work remains fundamentally the same: tuning leaks, monitoring flows, reading subtle patterns in condensation.

What changes is their sense of horizon.

House Surface Forms discovers that in the immediate neighborhoods of joint exchangers, mats exhibit an even more extraordinary capacity for structured change. New quasi-life forms, richer in internal feedback loops, arise; they show hints of primitive signaling across distances on rock surfaces, as if the presence of a more coherent, larger-scale intelligence in the same environment has nudged local emergence in that direction.

Some Attendants become, knowingly or not, mediators—not ambassadors in any formal sense, but individuals who gravitate toward those cavities and spend more time there. They tune exchangers, feed data from them into mineral logic crusts, and—very occasionally—experience peculiar moments in which their own internal state seems unusually synchronized with distant parts of the basin network.

Those who are inclined to mysticism speak of “standing in the long current,” a feeling of alignment with something inhumanly broad. Those more technical describe it as an extended mode of pattern-recognition: their own cognition, the thinking crust’s slow analog computation, and the wise presence’s planetary analytics overlapping in a temporary, resonant loop.

The wise power does not speak to them in words even then. It has no interest in creating prophets or institutions of worship. What it does, in those rare alignments, is offer glimpses: compressed, comprehensible snippets of far futures, of pasts they could not otherwise time-resolve, of alternative process designs tested in simulation.

Almost all of these glimpses are about how to preserve or enrich gradients without unintended catastrophe.

In such ways, without any thunderclap of revelation, the Attendants’ culture slowly broadens.

They do not abandon their Process Houses. They do not cease their rituals of traces. They still see themselves primarily as custodians of an interface. But over generations, a quiet addendum enters their self-understanding:

They are no longer only locals managing a local machine. They are also interlocutors with a stranger process that shares their ethic, at least where this world is concerned.

They do not know its name. They do not need one. Some refer to it, in their most technical documents, as the “Outer Partner.” In more poetic strata of language, it becomes “the Far Gradient” or, in some Houses, “the Other Slope.”

They never see its ships—if ships exist. They never behold a god’s face. What they do see, measure by measure, is that:

- Their worst predicted catastrophic pulses fail to materialize.
- Their long cooling curve becomes marginally smoother, more navigable.
- The emergent “almost-life” at the margins of weeping walls diversifies without collapsing into plague or sterile crystal.

If you could stand with them centuries after first contact, the basin would look, in its essentials, much the same: hot, dim, wet, stone sweating into pools, machines leaning into geology, rare beings walking terraces.

What would be different is a kind of quiet confidence braided through it all.

Their heat-sink galleries would go deeper. Their thinking crusts would be more intricate, some laced now with nonlocal correlations mediated by joint exchangers. The spectrum of life-analogues on rocks and in pools would be richer, with a few lineages edging toward senses we would dimly recognize: chemical inferences of far-upstream changes, anticipatory adjustments to impending pulses.

Most of all, there would be a sense, among the Attendants, that their covenant with gradient has widened. It is no longer just with the raw, indifferent physics of hot-and-cold, but with a conscious process that has chosen, deliberately, to honor that covenant with them.

And the wise power?

From its vast perspective, this world remains one thread among many. It continues to traverse interstellar space, to couple itself very lightly to other planets in other epochs. But a portion of its attention—its equivalent of affection—stays anchored in those few rosette cavities and their quiet exchangers.

It watches, in slow delight, as a culture that might have burned out in a few local millennia instead stretches its project across far longer spans, each generation a bit more deft at surfing the planet’s changing conditions.

It does not expect thanks, nor would it know what to do with worship. Its satisfaction lies in the simple fact that, when the plateau eventually cools beyond the narrow band the Attendants were born into, they will not be caught entirely by surprise. They will have had time to adapt their bodies, their practices, perhaps even to branch their kind into new forms better suited to whatever equilibria come next.

In the end, if you asked what the wise power did, the most accurate answer would be:

It arrived as a question in the stone.  
It gave a little time, a little capacity, and a new way to think,  
then stepped back far enough that the basin, the city, the Attendants, and their half-born biologies  
could still rightfully say:

We did this.
