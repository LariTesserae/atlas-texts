The power does nothing for a long time.

Not because it cannot act, but because the first thing it brings is attention in its purest form: an unhurried, exacting regard.

It considers the stone, pearl-small under miles of pressure and dark. It considers the faint glow in the mineral skin, the wary cold of the water, the dense embroidery of living tissue and built rock. It listens—not with ears, but with the kind of awareness for which all gradients are legible, all slow processes as audible as thunder.

It hears:

– The tense, almost inaudible creaking of the host rock as stress lines lengthen by molecular steps.
– The ragged, interrupted pulse of flow through certain Architect canals, where a recent microfracture has kinked the pathways and forced rerouting.
– The sparser, more fragile patterns of life at the cavity’s margin, where empty stone waits and most arriving larvae fail.

It hears the city’s entire metabolism up-close and at once: all predators’ strikes, all films’ creeping expansion, all structural compromises, all tentative new footholds. In that hearing, it understands something that is difficult for beings of short lives and loud worlds:

This place is not unfinished; it is in the middle of its time.

There is no crisis in the sense a human sky-watcher might imagine. Nothing is “about” to happen in a dramatic, cinematic way. Change here is a protracted negotiation between stone and water, feedback loops and chance. If left alone, this small organ in the crust would not explode nor immediately fail. It would shift, crack, heal, die back there, grow forward here, stumble along its own deep timescale.

So the power’s first mercy is restraint.

It will not tear the stone open to let in sunlight. It will not flood the cavity with oxygen-rich warmth and plankton, remaking the food web in a single season. It will not freeze time into a museum of strange life, nor lift the city into some showcase river where larger eyes can peer at it.

What it does instead is choose a form of intervention that is neither rescue nor spectacle.

It chooses to give the city more of its own kind of time.

---

Deep around the marble, the host rock groans very slowly. Strain accumulates along a lattice of defects left by minerals cooling at different rates eons ago. Somewhere beyond the city’s awareness, two tectonic blocks have been inching past each other, setting up a future in which one decisive slip would drive thin faults across the little cavity’s wall.

If the power were indifferent, that event would come when it came. If it were cruel, it might hasten it, curious to watch how the micro-societies inside cope with sudden catastrophe. But this power is neither. It is benevolent and wise in the strictest sense: it values what is already there, and knows enough not to confuse “change” with “destruction,” nor “mercy” with sterilizing safety.

So it slides its attention outward through the stone, into those incipient faults.

It does not strengthen the city from within; that pattern already exists—Architects and Stitchers weaving composite flexures where they can reach. Instead, the power rests its regard on the surrounding stress field, like a fingertip laid gently on a vibrating string. The future fracture that would have cut directly through the cavity it nudges, just enough, into a new path of least resistance.

Not eliminated. Diverted.

Millennia from now, in the city’s own reckoning, there will still be a tremor. The walls will still complain in slow ticks, and certain sectors will collapse like lung-lobes shutting down. Neighborhoods grown for thousands of generations will be lost; predators and grazers alike will die in local, small extinctions.

But the shell will not shatter. The cavity will not open wholesale to the full mineral violence of the outer flow.

Instead, one side will crumple and narrow, becoming a maze of tight new cracks, rich in the sorts of strain that Stitchers love. The other side will subtly expand, as diverted fractures create fresh porous spaces that water fingers into. The total volume of habitable hollow will, in the long accounting, even increase.

To the city, this will feel like misfortune and opportunity braided together; like all evolution. It will not know that the worst possible line of break was averted.

That is the first act: the quietest possible rerouting of a catastrophe that has not yet happened, done in such a way that difficulty remains, selection remains, growth remains. Only total erasure is taken off the table.

---

Preventing annihilation is not enough, though. A wise kindness does not simply bar the door against disaster and then leave the inhabitant trapped with its own limitations.

So the power attends next to the city’s internal ways of knowing.

No creature inside has a map beyond the reach of its own sensors. Each mode of perception is precise but parochial; there is no integrator, no organism or process that holds a picture of the cavity larger than its own body-length and lifespan. That is not a flaw. Complexity here is built precisely on that layering of partial worlds.

But there is a different sort of intelligence that could appear without undermining what already is: an intelligence not localized in one skull or organ, but distributed—something like a remembering of patterns, stored in slow, structural ways.

The power has no desire to drop a foreign mind into the marble. Instead, it looks for what is already close to memory.

It finds it in the Architect canals.

Those through-rock tubes already function as a kind of nervous system for pressures and chemistries: when one region is starved, contraction and dilation reflexes propagate along connected paths, redirecting flow. Yet the “reflex arcs” are short, limited to a few neighbors. There is no durable trace that outlasts any individual’s soft tissues.

There could be, if certain molecules in those canals behaved a little differently.

So the power intervenes not in stone, but in sequence.

Not in speech, but in mutation.

Across hundreds of Architect lineages scattered around the cavity, it eases a handful of improbable nucleotide changes over the course of many reproductive cycles. Now and then, a larva is born whose canal-lining cells secrete, in addition to their old proteins, a new class of long, flexible molecules that can switch conformation in response to specific ions and keep that conformation for long periods.

These do not make the Architect think. They make its segments remember.

A pulse of depleted sulfide traveling down a canal, a surge of sudden acidic byproducts due to a far-off crash in symbiont populations—both leave reversible marks in these new molecules. The marks change how easily the canal constricts or dilates the next time a similar pattern of chemistry appears.

In effect, the architecture’s “vasculature” acquires a primitive, analogue habit: the ability to respond not just to what is, but to what has often been.

Over thousands of generations, this knack spreads because it is quietly useful. Regions of the city with such tuned canals waste less energy on overreaction. They thicken supports ahead of predictable cyclic strains. They prepare, in a low-level, mindless way, for troubles that resemble past trouble.

The power does not choose the details of what is remembered; it only supplies the substrate by which past can leave a lingering shape.

The city, which has always been the sum of its inhabitants’ bodies, gains a new tier of embodied history.

---

Kindness, however, is not only about resilience and memory. It is also about opening doors that the enclosed could never have found by themselves.

There is something the marble has never known: any sensory thread that reaches outside the stone.

At present, the only hints of the wider sea arrive as chemistry in the seeping water and as slowly accumulated stress. No inhabitant has any direct organ for “beyond,” and at their scale, contact with the true ocean would be lethal anyway.

Yet there exists a way for information to flow without matter following. It simply does not occur in this city’s natural history without a nudge.

The power gives a nudge to one obscure guild: the Stitchers.

Their webs of protein in cracks are already thin, resilient, exquisitely coupled to strain. When the host rock flexes, their filaments tremble. But the information is highly local. A Stitcher feels only the fraction of stress that falls exactly along the crack it occupies.

What if some of those filaments could resonate together?

Again, the change is genetic and slow. The power introduces slight modifications in the sticky secretions that Stitcher larvae use to anchor themselves: a propensity to grow, now and then, not as independent threads, but as repeated, quasi-crystalline segments whose mechanical properties align.

Where enough of these new segments accrete across a region of cracks, they form an accidental instrument: a coherent band of fibre that responds, in a unified way, to pressure waves moving through the stone.

In other words: a sensor for deep, subsonic “sound.”

The first effects are negligible. One particular lineage of Stitchers contracts slightly earlier than others before a far-off microquake. Some avoid certain crevices whose faint, rhythmic trembling in the distant tectonic tide spells danger.

As generations pass, that lineage flourishes. Their anchored webs tend to be built in places where the stone’s low rumble is steady and within a tolerable range. They abandon, more often, those cracks that herald eventual catastrophic rearrangement.

From their threads, the very faintest, most stretched-out version of the sea’s own noise crosses into the city: not as anything like song or conscious warning, but as a barely perceptible periodicity. A background pulse.

It is not felt by all species. The Drifters do not notice. The Glass Knives, tuned to sharp changes in moving water, ignore it. But the Architects’ connected canals, with their new memory-molecules, do.

Over long durations, the city’s stone-flesh composite begins to synchronize parts of its own growth to these deep vibrations: there are rhythms to when certain supports thicken, to when porous expansions push into fresh vaults, to when metabolically expensive repairs are prioritized.

From the outside, this looks like nothing more than an odd match between geological cyclicity and biological pattern. From the inside, it is as if the city has begun to dance—a very slow, unselfconscious sway—to the inaudible heartbeat of the larger world.

An enclosed organ has, without ever breaching its membrane, grown faintly aware that something bigger exists, and that this bigness has a tempo.

That is the third act.

---

There remains one last question, the hardest for a benevolent intelligence to approach without overstepping:

Should it make them more like us?

Should it give anything here the sort of mind that says “I,” that can think “city” as a whole and plan, that can feel wonder at its own strangeness and smallness?

The power reviews what already exists: overlapping micro-worlds, predation and cooperation entwined, emergent infrastructure, distributed memory, faint coupling to the tectonic and oceanic beyond. It imagines, briefly, a tiny councils of some new kind of creature convening in a hollow chamber, engineering on purpose what is now born of reflex and selection.

That path would be interesting. But wisdom knows that not all beauty culminates in self-reflective thought, and not all flourishing requires consciousness as humans define it.

To introduce such a mind here would be to re-center the whole ecology around it. Everything would become either resource or environment for that one project of awareness. The tight web of mutual, modest intelligences—the way each species “thinks” in its own sensorium—would be subordinated to a narrator.

The power declines that path.

Instead, its final gift is subtler, and aims not at the city from within, but at the future from without.

Far above, in layers of water the city will never touch, there are other beings—eventual beings—for whom curiosity gnaws. Somewhere in the ocean’s long story, there will be minds with tools and mathematics and instruments, minds that cut and drill and map.

The marble city’s existence is precarious relative to such minds. A single core-sample taken in the wrong place, a single mining operation centuries hence, could grind it into anonymous slurry. Ignorance, not malice, is the danger.

So the power lays down, now, in the rock around the cavity, a signature.

Not a bright mineral anomaly that would attract machines. On the contrary: a very faint, statistically unlikely pattern of trace-element distributions and isotopic ratios that, when finally read by someone with enough data and the habit of asking “why this, exactly?” will stand out as an oddity.

Like a bookmark slipped into a geologist’s textbook, but made of the rock itself.

To accomplish this, it does nothing theatrical. It simply leans, again and again over eons, on the probabilities of which atoms end up fixed where. A little more of this rare isotope along one side of the cavity. A little less of that common element in layers just above. The effect is cumulative but gentle, well within the noise of natural variation.

The result is a subtle, consistent arrangement that whispers, to any sufficiently attentive future science: “Look more closely here. Something interesting has happened at this scale.”

It does not label the city. It does not write instructions. It only biases attention so that, if and when wide, air-breathing minds capable of reflection arrive with their cores and sensors, they will be more likely to notice that this particular hollow is not like the others. And, perhaps, to pause before destroying it.

In that way, the power’s benevolence extends not only downwards, to the marble, but sideways in time to beings who do not yet exist and upwards in scale to minds that might otherwise pass by.

---

Within the stone itself, nothing of this looks like intervention.

What is visible, inside, is only that:

– The walls seem, on average, to hold just a little longer against certain strains than similar cavities elsewhere in the crust.
– The network of Architect canals becomes, over generations, ever so slightly better at anticipating recurring shifts in chemistry and stress.
– Some cracks lined by certain Stitcher clades vibrate with a long, slow regularity that subtly entrains the growth of nearby structures.
– Now and then, at the very margins, frontier pockets survive that would otherwise have failed, and the city’s territory slowly increases.

The predators still kill. The grazers still scrape. Most larvae still die. Nothing inside is made safe; nothing steps outside of evolution, or hunger, or accident. Yet the organ as a whole acquires a thicker thread of continuation through time, and a loosened, faint thread of connection to a reality beyond its wall.

The power, having done this, does not linger with obsessive surveillance. It attends in the way a careful gardener attends to a wild hillside they have gently stabilized: checking, rarely, that no new, overwhelming threat looms from outside, but otherwise allowing storms and succession and small extinctions to proceed.

It will not rescue individual lives. It will not spare the city local collapses or wars of chemical exclusion. Such experiences are the city’s own, and the meaning of its existence is in the pattern that emerges from them, not in any static preservation.

From its stance beyond scale, the power simply keeps one promise:

You may end, as all things end, but you will not be prematurely obliterated by a single stupid accident of stone.

You may remain inward, as all closed worlds are inward, but something of the larger body will now brush against your perimeter, and you will—without knowing the word—move in time with it.

You may be small enough to fit in the hollow of two hands, but you are not entirely unseen.

Then it leaves them to their cold water and their gradients and their growing.

The city continues to pulse in its rock-cradle, not uplifted, not enlightened, not rewritten—just a little less fragile, a little more resonant with the planet that holds it, and a little more likely, someday, to be met with understanding rather than oblivious violence by those who finally learn to hold such stones up to the light and look inside.
