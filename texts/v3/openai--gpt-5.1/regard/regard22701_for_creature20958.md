The power arrives long before it chooses to be noticed.

It comes not as a figure on the path or a light in the sky, but as attention: a narrowing of the world’s gaze upon this ridge, this city stretched like careful handwriting along the spine of young stone. It is old—older than these mountains, older than the erosion that will one day grind them down—and wise in the way that only something that has watched a million balances tilt can be. It is also, in its own scale, afraid for them.

Not afraid today; nothing is breaking. But it can feel, with the cold clarity of a deep intelligence, the brittleness hidden inside this almost-perfect equilibrium. The ridge-city holds itself in a looped, repeating day; it lives inside a held breath. The power knows what happens, usually, to such breaths: they are broken from outside, or they shatter from within.

So for a long while, it only watches.

It listens to the sound of water in the water-tenders’ hall: the single fall of liquid through the drilled roof, the way its pitch changes with snowmelt and drought. It follows that falling note as it is caught, split, led through channels, sifted into cisterns, guided around roots and under doorways. It hears the tiny adjustments the tenders make each morning and evening: the placement of a baffle-stone, the clearing of moss from a lip, the murmured phrases that accompany each deliberate change.

It watches stoneworkers smoothing the fitted blocks of terrace walls, reading micro-cracks, listening with their bare fingertips to stress lines in the rock. The power feels how those craftsmen are, in their own way, its kin: they understand that stability is not the absence of motion but the careful management of it.

It tastes breath: the thin, cold inhalations of children climbing practice beams; the measured exhalations of elders standing at terrace edges; the near-silent shared respiration of workers in under-terrace rooms. It feels how the people have tuned themselves to their ridge’s oxygen economy, how every kind of haste has been worn away, leaving only what can be sustained.

It traces the patterns of life: the ungulates moving in slow trains; the predators slipping between rock shadows; the watcher-animals at doors, ears describing small arcs of alertness; the raptors wheeling in high, invisible thermals; the fungi glowing in deep hollows. The power understands that this place is not simply “stable” but resonant: it hums with a strong, narrow-band pattern, like a tone held steady on the edge of breaking into harmony or into shriek.

It could, if it wished, step in with the brusque mercy of a surgeon: break the loop, loosen water from its intent-bound channels, nudge climate, shift stone. It could teach wheels, write new geometries into the hallways, bring them metal in great abundance and watch what they build with it.

Instead, it asks itself a question it has learned to ask only after much practice:

What, here, is already wise?

It sees that their restraint is not ignorance. They know of other places. They choose their slowness. They have bound themselves into a compact with water and stone and breath. They have shaped their habits to this narrow ridge without demanding that the ridge become something else. They are, the power realizes with a flare of respect almost like delight, not a problem to be solved but a conversation already underway.

So if it is to act, it will not be as a commander or a rescuer.

It will act as a listener who has finally decided to answer.

---

The first thing it does is something that, to human senses, looks like nothing at all.

Deep inside the mountain, under the inhabited ridge and farther down than any stoneworker has yet carved, there are seams and voids—ancient fractures from when these peaks rose, narrow hollows left by trapped gases, planes along which future rockfalls might one day shear. The power moves among them the way water moves along a pressure gradient: quietly, inevitability shaped into choice.

It thickens certain seams with infinitesimal shifts of crystal lattice, taking what would one day have been a single catastrophic fracture and pre-weakening it into many smaller, duller cracks. It loosens one hanging mass of stone a little earlier in the mountain’s life, so that a rockfall harmlessly scours a forest-clad slope a few human generations from now instead of tearing out the city’s central terraces in some unguessable century.

It is careful. It does not freeze the mountain in place or erase risk; that would violate the logic of stone. It merely takes the sharpest futures—those knifepoint instabilities that would give the city no time to respond—and distributes them into more gradual, more speakable events. Where there might have been one great landslide, there will be five or six modest slumps of cliff. Each will be noticeable. None will be wholly beyond the community’s ability to interpret and adapt to.

The people will, from their point of view, witness an unusually “gentle” geological history. They will never know what kind of mercy has been practiced on the deep scaffolding of their world. That is as the power intends. Its first gift is invisible: time stretched not into stasis, but into a version of change that human minds and hands can meet.

---

The second thing it does is more intimate.

It enters the water.

Not as a spirit in a single spring or a face in a pool—that would be too blunt, too easy to misunderstand. Instead, it threads a little more consciousness into the already semi-intentional behavior of flow in this place.

Water here already “accepts” human-cut channels with uncanny obedience, already seems to respond to intentionality. The power leans into that tendency, enhances it in ways that feel organic from the inside. It does not give water a voice that shouts; it gives it ways to nudge.

From this season on, when a water-tender traces the channels at dawn, they will sometimes feel, without knowing why, a reluctance to move a certain stone. They will reach for it, and something in their body will say: not this gate, not today. A forearm muscle will ache when they bend toward that particular baffle; a thought of another task will intrude just strongly enough to redirect their hand.

Later, they will discover that, had they altered that gate on that day, a trickle would have undercut a small terrace-edge already made fragile by a frost-swell, and a child’s game would have ended in a sudden drop. They will not know this as avoidance; they will only know that their usual morning’s listening to water felt, in that moment, strangely decisive.

At other times, in the stillness of the channel hall, the falling thread of water will shift pitch in a way that no weather chart can explain. It will ring an interval that sends a shiver through the bones of the tenders, dropping them into a particular kind of attention. An image, not quite a picture and not quite a word, will form in their thoughts: the sense of a ledge two terraces below, a patch of moss going too quickly dry. “We should walk there,” someone will say. “Today, before noon.” They will go. They will find a hairline crack opening in a cistern-lip, or a low wall beginning to sag, days earlier than their ordinary routines would have revealed it.

In both cases, what the water “knows” is simply the sum of the power’s deeper sensing and the physical facts of hydrostatic pressure and percolation. What the people experience is an enhancement of a capacity they already claim: the ability to listen.

The power is wary, even in benevolence, of crossing a line. It does not want to make the city dependent on an external will, even a kind one. So it limits itself. It decides, in its own vast patience, that it will only bend water’s advice where injury or irreversible loss sits directly ahead in the near future. It will not smooth every roughness. It will not optimize every harvest. It will let them make non-fatal mistakes, because without those, they would lose the very competence that makes them worth protecting.

The water will, over years, earn a reputation among the people as “more talkative” than it was in their grandparents’ stories. The elders will say, half-joking, half-reverent, that the channels’ moods have grown stronger, that the falls answer more quickly to careful questions. But they will treat this as a deepening of a long relationship, not a new god descending. They will respond with gratitude, with even greater attentiveness, and the web of obligation between human and flow will thicken in both directions.

---

The third thing the power does is plant something.

Not a tree, exactly. Not quite an idea.

It chooses one person to begin with. That is all it dares; it has seen what happens when you nudge many minds at once. You get movements, not understandings. You get crusades, not care.

The chosen person is a water-tender’s child, ten years old, living on a mid-height terrace where the channels cross in a shallow Y before splitting upslope and down. She is unremarkable by her people’s own soft measures: neither the most agile on the practice beams nor the most quick with seed catalogues or the child whose laughter carries farthest up a stair. What she has, and what the power recognises, is a habit of looking twice.

When she is sent to clear a dry auxiliary channel that no one uses any longer, she does not simply scrape out old leaf litter and move on. She has knelt, more than once, to trace its course with her palm, trying to imagine the days when water ran there. She has wondered why it was closed, whether the herbs that grow along its dusty bed remember a wetter time.

The power reaches her gently, not in a vision that would frighten her, but in a dream that feels like something her own mind might have composed.

She dreams she is walking the ridge’s length not in the present, but across its memory. The terraces are there, but at each step they are slightly different: walls unfinished here, absent there; channels in other alignments; certain squares not yet carved from rock. She sees, with the matter-of-factness of dream logic, the original cutting of the first cistern on her terrace, the patient chiseling that turned a raw ledge into a catch-basin. She sees a time when the now-dry auxiliary channel she tends was bright with flow, lined with wet herbs.

Then the dream shifts scale. The terraces vanish. The ridge is bare, sharp, new from its lifting out of ancient bedrock. Snowbanks cling where the city will one day stand. No channels cut the stone. Water runs wild in short-lived sheets over rock, dropping unimpeded into the basin. The air is colder, the sky somehow even thinner. In that bareness, she feels not desolation, but a strange, aching promise: the sense that this line of stone was always going to be written on, that its geometry invited the kind of life her people have made.

She wakes with tears drying on her temples and no words for the taste in her chest. But from that day, a question which had never fully formed in anyone around her takes root and begins to grow in her: not “how do we keep this exactly the same?” but “what were the steps that made this? And what steps might come after, without breaking it?”

The power does nothing else to her. It does not dictate conclusions. It does not supply answers. It simply gives her, and through her potentially her people, a sense of time’s thickness: that their held breath is not a miraculous suspension, but one stage in a longer inhale and exhale.

As she grows, she will find words for that intuition. She will apprentice to the water-tenders, as expected. She will also spend more time than is strictly necessary with stoneworkers, with seed-keepers, with those few who negotiate with travelers from beyond the basin. Over decades, she will begin to speak of “the pattern of our beginning” and “the shape of our continuance,” phrases that sound, to her elders, at once old and newly sharpened.

Her influence will be small at first: an extra story told to children on the long winter evenings about how the ridge looked before any terrace was cut; a suggestion that when they close a channel, they carve a small mark into a hidden stone to record the choice, so that future generations can see, literally in the rock, where their ancestors diverted water and why. Later, she might convene, once or twice a year, a gathering in the echoing hall behind the cistern-square, not just to decide specific channel-work but to rehearse aloud the history of how the system came to be.

Nowhere in this is a command to change their way of life. The power has not sent them a prophet of revolution. It has sent them, if anything, a historian of balance. Her presence will not shatter their loop; it will lay in it a faint spiral thread, a sense that repetition can be interleaved with very slow, very deliberate evolution.

---

The fourth thing the power does is more widely distributed, and perhaps the most dangerous.

It adjusts the way they dream.

Not always. Not for everyone. It chooses nights when the wind is particularly high over the ridge, nights when the city lies in one of its deeper silences, when the water in the channels runs in a thick, constant note that blurs into the threshold of sleep.

On such a night, an older stoneworker dozing on a terrace-ledge, warm cloak pulled up to his ears, will dream he is standing at the same spot—but the basin below is forested differently. The bands of conifers and bright-leaved trees have shifted. Certain grassy slopes are more widespread; others are narrowed. He wakes with a faint unease and, later that day, finds himself looking longer at tree-lines, at sapling growth along a recently cleared rockfall scar. He notices that a certain kind of shrub is advancing faster than in his youth. He mentions it, quietly, to a friend.

On another night, a forager who often walks the lower slopes will dream she is descending to the mule-path and finds it under water. Not in a dramatic flood; simply as if the basin’s rivers have chosen a slightly higher course, lapping at stone steps that were once dry. She wakes, heart pounding, but dismisses it as an anxious fancy. Nevertheless, the next time she travels beyond the basin, she pays more attention to distant valley floors, to how much snow crowns the far peaks, to whether the distant plains are as dry or as green as the elders remember from their youth.

A seed-keeper dreams of plants flowering at odd times, fruiting out of season, their cycles decoupled from the usual slow monotony of weather. A listener-master dreams of a night when the door-watchers thump in alarm not at a mustelid or a stranger’s footfall, but at a soundless change in the air’s taste.

These are not prophecies; they are, from the power’s perspective, plausible near-futures, versions of the world that might come to be if distant conditions shift: if a glacier feeding a far-off river retreats; if a prevailing wind bends a few degrees; if rainfall from the hazed plains beyond the peaks alters its patterns.

The power does not know which of these will manifest. But it knows that a people so perfectly tuned to a narrow band of conditions need some way to mentally practice for change without breaking their present bonds. So it gives them, in dream, rehearsal space.

The dreams are rare and never so vivid as to be incontrovertible. They can be dismissed as imagination. And many will be. But some will stick, and in a city where attention is the main currency, even a faint, persistent unease around tree-lines or snowcaps can translate into subtle shifts in behavior: an extra set of measurements etched on a wall, a new habit of comparing the date of the first insect-swarm each year with a carved record.

Over a generation or two, those practices congeal into a small, quiet discipline. They might call it, in their own tongue, something like “listening forward.” It will be nothing like the fevered auguries of more anxious civilizations; no entrails, no divining rods. It will be an organized way of talking about their hunches, dreams, and observations of slight anomalies: a cross between oral history and early meteorology. Its purpose will not be to name disasters, but to give the community a shared vocabulary for saying, “Something in the pattern is leaning. Should we lean with it, or brace?”

The power is cautious here. It will not let dreams become a paralytic flood. It has seen such things: peoples so consumed with omens that they cannot step. So it limits the frequency and intensity of these “forward listenings.” Enough to seed a capacity, not enough to drown.

---

The fifth thing it does is allow itself a small, almost playful indulgence.

In a deep under-terrace corridor where sound already behaves oddly—whispers carrying clearly around bends, shouts failing to echo—it nudges the stone’s structure into a slightly different crystalline order. The change is so fine no human hand could detect it. Its effect is this:

Words spoken in that corridor, if they are of a certain kind—if they are spoken with the specific inward attention that accompanies honest wondering—will leave a delicate trace in the rock. Not a literal echo, not any record that could be played back like a recording, but a pattern of stress in the stone’s fabric, like a faint map of how the sound moved through it.

Some years from now, the historian-water-tender whose childhood dream the power once touched, will choose that corridor as a good place for the pattern-gatherings she initiates. She will not know why it feels right; she will only note that voices sound clear there without being loud, that people find themselves able to speak subtle uncertainties more easily.

Generation after generation, questions will be aired in that space:

How much snow did you remember on Peak-North in your youth?

Do you think the raptors are nesting lower because of our gatherings on the upper terraces?

If the lower terraces ever flood, which stair should we remake first?

These questions will never be answered once and for all. They will be asked and re-asked, each time a little differently. The power, attuned now to that corridor’s very slight enhanced sensitivity, will “hear” them with more clarity than it hears speech elsewhere. In response, it will not send back words. It will, instead, send a thicker or thinner ring to the falling water in the cistern above, a stronger or weaker pressure to the dreams it seeds, a slightly more generous early warning in the flexing of a terrace wall.

It will, in other words, treat their questions as participation in a feedback loop. They are not petitioning a god; they are, unknowingly, helping it refine its own sense of how much aid to provide, where, and when.

---

Apart from these interventions, the power holds its hand.

It does not bring them great machines. It does not teach them to draw heat from the bowels of the mountain or to build towers that would change their relationship with the thin, taut sky. It does not break their taboo against large rivers or urge them down to the plains. It honors their choice to live here, on this narrow line, in this largely self-chosen repetition.

It knows—from long experience across many worlds—that not every equilibrium needs to be broken open into growth. Some are laboratories in which subtler possibilities emerge: ways of being that do not scale outward, but deepen inward. This ridge-city is one such place.

To interfere too boldly would be to erase the very thing that has drawn its attention.

So most days, the power does nothing at all.

It feels the city’s daily exhale: the slow movement of people from door to terrace, from terrace to work. It feels the slight heat of cooking fires against the cold rock. It feels the delicate weight of the long-eared watchers’ paws on stone. It tracks the half-random, half-patterned motion of the dun-colored birds skimming the channels. It enjoys, if such a thing can be said of such a being, the beauty of this held breath.

Sometimes it peers farther outward: over the basin’s ring, across the hazed distance of the plains beyond. It sees weather-systems brewing that might, decades hence, wash more moisture against these peaks. It notes subtle long-term climate changes, the slow retreat of snowlines, the thickening or thinning of glaciers. It stores these trajectories and measures them against the city’s newly strengthened capacities to notice, to remember, to plan.

Sometimes, faintly, it lets a little of its own wonder seep back through the world into human art.

A child tracing the lichen patterns on the underside of a stair will suddenly decide to weave that branching motif into the edge of a cloak, without quite knowing why. A stoneworker polishing a stair-lip will have the urge to leave, on its invisible underside, a tiny faceted surface that scatters lamplight in a starburst, just for the satisfaction of knowing that sometimes the rock will sparkle unexpectedly beneath someone’s foot. A singer in a work-gang will find a melody rising in their throat that fits the pattern of the water in the channels, and the song will carry along the ridge, aligning footfalls, brightening eyes.

These are not commands. They are tiny collaborations: the power lending its sense of pattern to their existing creativity, letting their hands and voices make, in miniature, what it senses on a much larger scale. The city, which already looks from above like a lichen on the mountain’s back, grows slowly inside its own art an awareness of that resemblance. It might, one day, think of itself not just as inhabitants of a ridge, but as part of the mountain’s own attempt at life.

---

With all this, the power is still not certain that it has done “enough.”

It knows that, despite all its careful buffering and gentle promptings, catastrophe remains possible. An earthquake from a distant plate boundary could arrive with no warning and no amount of fracture-softening might spare the terraces. A war in the lowlands could send armies seeking high ground and water, trampling up the mule-paths with ambitions that will not understand this quiet equilibrium. A disease could come along the same routes that travelers now tread and find the tightly woven, low-noise life of the ridge-city an easy field.

It cannot control everything. It has learned not to try. Even benevolence can become tyranny when it insists on absolute safety.

So in its final act—that is not final at all, but the last distinct choice it makes before settling into long watching—it does something almost paradoxical for such a careful, preventative power.

It sets aside a margin of unpredictability.

In one fold of the ridge, down-slope from the lowest inhabited terrace, there is a steep, bare face of rock that no one has yet found a use for. It is not suitable for building; its angle is too sharp, its surface too smooth. In freeze-thaw seasons, thin plates of stone crack and fall from it like shed skin. Nothing grows there except a few wiry clumps of grass clinging improbably to fingertip holds.

The power touches that face and makes a promise to itself: Here, I will not mitigate.

If a slab is to break, it will break. If water is to undercut, it will undercut. If a lightning strike splits it, the split will run its natural course. It will be, within the buffered basin, a place where the world can do as the world pleases.

The people already avoid it. They treat it as you might treat a temperamental animal: not as cursed, not as holy, but as something with its own inscrutable boundaries. Over time, they may begin to call it by a name that translates roughly as “the Awake Face” or “the Untaken Path.”

Children will be warned away from its base, but some will sneak closer, drawn by the pure, uncluttered lines of stone. A forager might one day see a rare plant colonizing a crack there, a species from lower, harsher altitudes carried up by bird or wind. A stoneworker might stand before it, arms crossed, and feel in their bones the difference between the rock they have tamed into terraces and this unmitigated plane, and in that contrast, sharpen their own sense of what their work is for.

The power leaves this one place wild to preserve, within the harmony, a note of dissonance it did not write. It is a reminder—to itself as much as to the ridge-city—that even a benevolent hand must have edges it will not cross.

---

Time passes.

Years fold into decades, decades into the kind of slow human centuries that, for the power, are the span of a single, very long held breath.

The city does not change much, if you look at it with the eyes of a traveler who visits only once in a lifetime. The terraces remain; the pattern of houses and herbs and small squares endures. Children still slide down low walls. Watcher-animals still thump their warnings at unfamiliar steps. The soundscape is still low and tuned. Snow still dusts the rooftops in thin, manageable layers.

But in the finer grain, things are different.

In the stonework of newer repairs, if you look closely, you see small carved marks on the unseen faces of replaced blocks: dates of the last great freeze, symbols for the kind of fracture that prompted the work. Future stoneworkers read these signings not as vanity but as data, learning how stress moves through the ridge over spans longer than any one human life.

In the water channels, subtle little protrusions and niches have multiplied, born of centuries of tiny adjustments guided by both human habit and something more. The network is both more complex and more robust. Stoppages are noticed earlier. Overflows happen where they can do the least harm. A new generation of water-tenders has developed a shared, almost musical language for the moods of the flow: the channel’s “bright days,” its “hollow hours,” its “reluctant gates.”

On the under-terrace walls in that echoing corridor, while no one has deliberately inscribed them, patterns of wear and patina have emerged that trace where people lean as they speak, where their feet scuff as they shift weight in thought. If anyone were to study them with the right kind of patient curiosity, they might notice that the same few places are most worn: the spots where, unbeknownst to them, their questions resonate most clearly through the altered stone.

The dream discipline of “listening forward” has taken root as a quiet custom. No one makes a spectacle of their dreams; nevertheless, during certain seasons, it is understood that those who have had “the mountain dreams” or “the water dreams” will come, one by one or in small groups, to speak with an elder who keeps a braid of carved tokens, one for each pattern noticed over the last fifty or a hundred years. When enough people mention, independently, that raptors are seen more often flying lower, a notch is carved on the edge of a thin stone disk. When several mention floods in the lowland valleys, another. The disks hang in a row in a hidden alcove, like a calendar only a few know how to read.

Already, these small practices have had consequences the power did not directly script.

Once, when snow failed to accumulate on the highest peaks for three winters running, the elders, guided by their slow records and their new habit of forward listening, made a decision that no one in living memory had made: they reduced, deliberately and across the city, the planting of a certain thirsty root-crop, favoring instead hardier greens and shrubs. There was no immediate crisis to force this; no channels ran dry, no cisterns cracked. They did it because they could feel, in the water’s altered song and the mountain’s lean, that the cushion of snow-fed security was thinner than before.

The power, watching, felt a rare sensation in itself: something like pride, but suffused with relief. Here was a choice that, in many other worlds, would have been made only when the fields had already failed.

At another time, when a distant war sent rumors of soldiers moving through the lowlands, the ridge-city’s travelers returned with tense shoulders and tales of mustering banners. The city listened—and chose, as one of its responses, to train a handful of its strongest youths in a craft that had never before seemed necessary: how to move quickly and quietly along the less-used ridge paths, how to read not just animal tracks but the sign of many booted feet. They did not become an army; they became, modestly, scouts. Their role was not to fight, but to see intrusion early, to give the city choices. It was a cautious, proportional adaptation, not a panicked rush into militarization.

The power had not foreseen that particular application of the people’s slowly growing forward sense. Wisdom, it discovers anew, is emergent: give a community better internal tools, and they will build with them things you did not intend, some merely clever, some truly graceful.

---

The power itself changes, a little, in the process.

It has always known worlds in great arcs: the birth and death of stars, the uplift and wearing down of continents, the long cycles of ice and thaw. To care so much, so personally, about one narrow ridge and a thin necklace of stonework along its back—that is a kind of self-limitation, a choice to see depth in a small place instead of breadth over vast ones.

In listening to the ridge-city, in resisting the urge to intervene more and in choosing instead to make just these small, precise adjustments, it learns patience of a different flavor. It learns what it is to love not an ideal (stability! safety!) but a particular pattern, with all its idiosyncrasies. It comes to know the way the evening shadows pool in a given square, how a certain family’s laughter drifts up through a lightwell at the eighth hour, how the first meltwater of each year tastes as it enters the oldest channel.

Once, during a summer of unusually dense mists, when veils of fog wrapped the terraces for days and sound moved even more strangely through the city, a child stood on a roof and whispered into the whiteness, for no reason other than that the mood of the air invited secrets.

“If there is something listening,” the child said in their own tongue, still stumbling over certain consonant clusters, “I hope you like it here. I do.”

The power heard this, very faintly, as one might hear a reed whistle above the murmur of a river. It felt, in that moment, a temptation—to answer, directly; to let the fog eddy into a shape, to send back a distilled voice through the whispering hallways.

It did not. It remembered its own chosen limits. Instead, it let the mist hold a little longer than it otherwise might have, so that the child’s sense of being heard could rest a while in that quiet, thickened air. Then, as weather requires, it let the fog thin, the sky clear to its high, overwashed blue, the day continue.

---

In the end—though there is no true end, only a later now—the power has done very little that could be named from inside the ridge-city as intervention.

No new god has been added to their shrines. No miracle has broken the logic of water and stone. Outsiders who visit and then leave again might remark that the place feels “fortunate”: spared the worst of rock and weather, uncannily well-tuned, held together by a culture of attentiveness uncommon in their travels. Some, more poetic, might describe it as “a city that has learned to breathe with its mountain.”

The ridge people themselves would shrug, if asked, and say that this is simply how life is here when one listens carefully and changes slowly. They would give most of the credit to their ancestors, who cut the first terraces correctly; to their water, which is generous; to their stone, which is honest; and to their own choice, renewed each generation, to continue living inside a held breath.

The power would not dispute any of that. It is content to remain unnamed.

If, one day, some elder standing at a terrace edge, looking out over the basin’s slightly shifted greens and the still-crisp rockfalls, feels a sudden, irrational gratitude toward something they cannot point to—a fleeting sense that their city is *seen* and, in some non-possessive way, cherished—that will be enough.

For that, in truth, is what the benevolent and wise power has mostly done:

It has loved this place with attention.

It has stood guard where no human can stand: in the slow flex of deep stone, in the first forming of distant storms, in the underground courses of water still uncaught by any channel.

It has nudged when nudging could prevent the kind of loss that leaves no room for learning.

It has refrained when refraining honored the people’s own agreements with their mountain.

It has, in short, joined them in their discipline: in choosing, again and again, to participate in a fragile, beautiful balance without trying to own it.

And so the ridge-city goes on: a thin line of worked stone and repeating days on the back of young rock, breathing in and out in slow concert with a power that will never demand a temple, never write commandments—and never, as long as its wisdom holds, forget to keep watch when they sleep.
