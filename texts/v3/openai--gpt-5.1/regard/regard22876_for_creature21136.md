The first thing the power does is nothing at all.

It watches.

Not in the way of a distant surveyor, marking coordinates and anomalies, but the way an old river watches a young one: by feeling where the banks hesitate, where the current folds back on itself in doubt, where gravel keeps trying and failing to arrange into a stable bed.

It takes in the slow, damp churn of the place.

The mist. The adolescent ground. The trains that mutter. The people moving with their heads tucked a little against the drizzle, their conversations coiling low between pipes and mossy walls. The interface cultivators pruning panels that behave like skin. The thermal dwellers crawling through hot, humming ducts. The riverward families checking the thickness of a biofilm with a lantern held just so.

It notes that nothing here pretends to be finished.

That, more than anything, interests it.

It has seen finished places. Dry cities with hard horizons, where every stone insists it will stay where it has been mortared; civilizations that talk about “final design,” “permanent fix,” “end state.” They are easy to bless, in a way: you hand them resilience like a tool. But they are also brittle. They break along their own certainties.

This basin is not like that. Its inhabitants already live as if each foundation is a question, each map a draft. They’ve built customs, technologies, and children’s games around the expectation that tomorrow’s ground will be slightly elsewhere.

So the power waits, to find out what, in such a place, could be called “helpful.”

It listens to the city’s noises and silences until it can hear the difference between ordinary hum and quiet panic. Until it understands that a sudden lack of dripping in a certain tunnel can be as alarming as a roar; that the deep, hollow thud of a subsidence three districts away will travel through the bones of the thermal dwellers before it reaches their ears.

It studies the non-human attentions: the way the translucent rats abandon a transformer hours before its gauges show trouble; the way the glassy filaments in the pavement thicken before a street floods; the way rust laces itself in branching reticula wherever stray current and moisture conspire.

When it has learned the city’s dialect of unease, it begins to act.

But it does not announce itself. No omens. No new star above the basin. No voice in the mist.

It builds instead along the grain of what already is.

### 1. It gives the city a sense of its own near future

The first gift is extremely small and almost entirely invisible: a change in how some things correlate.

In the undercity, a pump relay that always did fail unpredictably now begins to flicker in a more meaningful pattern. Forty minutes before a failure, its vibration acquires a faint sideband at a particular frequency. It’s not enough for a human ear. It *is* enough for the thermal dwellers’ embedded sensors, the little implants in their ear cartilage. A whisper of additional data rides on a tremor that was already there.

They chalk it up to “weird but useful” and adapt within a week. Someone patches the pattern into the alert systems. Fewer crawlspaces flood unexpectedly.

In the river terraces, the edge of a certain algae net always went slack just *after* a dangerous surge had already passed; harvesters would get wet, sometimes worse. Now, the net starts to tremble perceptibly a minute earlier. The riverward people, lanterns in hand, notice: “It gave us warning this time.” They don’t know that no new sensor was added, that only the net’s internal coupling shifted so strain propagates a little faster along the fibers. They simply nod and fold this “new quirk” into their river sense.

Across the city, wherever there were already feedback loops—between sensors and pumps, between rust and stray current, between filament networks and gutter flow—the power slightly tightens the linkage between *now* and *a moment from now*. Probability curves lean just enough toward coherence that early signs of failure get a louder echo.

It does this not by inserting new machinery but by smoothing noise.

Patterns the city is already generating become slightly more legible to itself.

An interface cultivator, monitoring growth across bio-reactive panels, notices that certain warning color shifts now appear a day earlier, at lower stress loads. They revise their care routines; panels last longer, fail less dramatically. “We must have finally tuned the nutrient mix,” they say, and log the parameters, not realizing the key change was not biochemical but informational: the power altered the microscopic coupling constants that dictate how small stresses percolate across the material, making early-stage patterning more distinct.

Over time, this amounts to a new, diffuse faculty: the city grows a short-range intuition. A *half-hour future sense* distributed through ducts, wetlands, rust circuits, roots, data meshes, and human bodies.

No one person possesses precognition; but systems fail less by surprise. The deep thud of a collapsing bank becomes rarer, not by miracle, but because a tilt-meter somewhere twitched in a way someone could finally read in time.

The basin calls this “a good run of fewer accidents.” The power calls it: letting a young organism sense its own heartbeat clearly.

### 2. It introduces a way to remember across slumps and shifts

The second act addresses a quieter problem: in a city where everything moves, how do you keep anything like a long memory?

Maps go stale weekly. Foundations drift. Prototypes are tried, abandoned, buried, forgotten. Knowledge exists, but it’s sedimentary: scattered in lab notebooks, old code, somebody’s story over soup in a pipe-warm canteen.

The power has no interest in freezing this flow. But it sees that useful patterns keep dissolving before they can mature. So it looks for something already webbed through almost everything: the semi-opaque filaments in the pavement cracks, the vegetative-mineral threads that drink runoff throughout the basin.

They already connect, in fact, more of the city than any rail network does. Their roots trace gutters, foundations, waste-heat lines. They are good at sensing gradients, at propagating small changes through long, low channels.

What they lack is a stable way to *hold* pattern.

So the power adjusts their chemistry infinitesimally. It doesn’t grant thoughts or language; it tweaks polymerization thresholds, ion-binding preferences, crystal nucleation speeds. It makes it so that when these filaments experience a strong and *repeated* pattern—a temperature pulse along a certain rhythm, a chemical spike that recurs every evening around a particular pump—they become slightly more likely to reinforce specific microstructures that match that pattern.

They start to form, in other words, physical “impressions” of recurring events: not memories as a mind would hold them, but biases.

A particular oscillation in pipe pressure leaves behind a faint alignment in the nearby filaments, so that when the same oscillation starts again, the filaments’ response is faster, stronger. A certain composition of runoff from a factory’s experiment in month one leaves the surrounding filament network *primed*; months later, when someone in a different building inadvertently replicates a similar chemistry, downstream filaments react more quickly, constraining growth that might otherwise have gone rogue.

At first, no one notices. Then an interface cultivator working in an older quarter installs a new façade grown from a thoroughly modern, well-documented strain. Yet, exposed to drizzle that passes through an alley where early, dangerous experiments were once vented, the façade starts expressing a pigment nobody coded into it—a subtle warning band that corresponds almost perfectly to the concentrations that used to kill the old bio-skins.

She traces back the gutter system, samples the filaments, and under her instruments sees impossible history: microstructures that seem to “remember” past runs, guiding present responses.

She writes a paper about “hysteresis in pan-urban filament networks.” Others test, replicate, learn. No one says “miracle.” They say, “We underestimated this system’s capacity for long-term pattern retention.”

Without speeches or shrines, the power has given the city a nervous system with a kind of distributed habit—a somatic memory spread thinly along every crack. It doesn’t prevent all old mistakes. But it makes exact repetition harder. Areas that once suffered catastrophic biofouling become, after years, oddly resistant to the same failure modes. Certain combinations of pollutants become self-limiting because the substrate remembers, subtly, how to destabilize them.

The people call these places “stubborn streets,” “wise drains,” “old walls.” They don’t know what, precisely, made them so. But they feel, walking those alleys in heavy mists, that something in the ground has seen all this before, and leans now against a fall.

### 3. It cultivates a translator between minds that don’t know they are neighbors

The power is benevolent, but not sentimental. It won’t wrench a new, obvious deity into being over this basin. It also won’t leave its multiple, nearly-thinking systems forever muttering past each other.

The rust filigree on certain alloys responds exquisitely to electrical fields. The sweating wall’s microbial films are adept at chemical signaling. The crystalline outcrop responds to particular electromagnetic spectra with precise, non-thermal shifts in its internal ordering. The rats, amphibians, birds, and insects are all sensitive in their own regimes: to toxins, to heat gradients, to vibrational patterns.

The human technicians already tap into many of these: they read rust patterns as corrosion data, wall films as lab feedstocks, animal behavior as early-warning signals. But all of it is one-way: interpretation without conversation.

The power decides—not all at once, but over years—to bend these separate, half-languages toward a shared pidgin.

It starts in the labs of a mid-career bio-interface technician. She’s been experimenting with tying panel metabolic cycles to nearby machine rhythms: façades that photosynthesize harder when power is cheap, rest when the grid is strained. Her rig includes:

- growth media seeded with modified wall-film microbes,
- tiny electrodes embedded in rust-favoring alloys,
- a shard of carefully shielded crystal chipped (illicitly) from the outcrop.

She’s looking for efficiency gains. What she finds, unexpectedly, is *synchronization*.

When she runs a patterned current through the alloy—pulses encoding a simple on/off schedule—the wall-derived biofilm starts responding in chemicals she recognizes, but with a twist: after a while, she can change the current pattern slightly, and the microbes *anticipate* the new schedule, ramping up or down *before* the current actually changes.

That shouldn’t happen. They have no direct sensor for future current. But the crystal shard, under her microscopes, is subtly pre-aligning its lattice structure ahead of the imposed pulses, whispering probability to the system.

What’s really happening is that the power, through this crude setup, has found a place to *surface* a latent possibility: a triad of materials that can:

- transduce electric patterns into chemical gradients (rust + microbes),
- hold pattern with minimal loss (crystal),
- adjust those patterns in response to the city’s wider state (through correlations the power maintains).

She refines the rig into a control substrate: a wafer that can sit between a building’s operational logic and its living skin, translating desired states into biochemically meaningful nudges, and skin feedback into machine-readable signals.

Word spreads. Similar wafers start appearing in wetlands (mediating between riverward sensors and algae behavior), in undercity pump stations (mediating between pressure schemas and filament “moods”), in rail controllers (mediating between soil response and train braking curves).

No one calls these devices sacred. They are “adaptive transduction modules” in procurement docs, “stones” or “tabs” in workshop slang. But, maintained and iterated, they become the first deliberate, robust bridge between:

- the living, semi-sentient networks in the cracks and walls,
- the engineered machines,
- the analytical systems,
- and, through them all, the human operators.

The power uses them the way a nervous system uses ganglia: as localized, semi-autonomous translators that can coordinate parts without central oversight.

And in one more, smaller way: through them, it can whisper.

Not commands; it respects the city too much for that. But *questions*, posed as slight biases.

A tab embedded near the crystalline outcrop, connected quietly to an array of pressure sensors in the adjacent tram platform, starts nudging a particular moss species to grow in a ring at a specific distance from the crystal. To human eyes, it’s decorative. To the crystal and the power, it’s a probe: how far does the outcrop’s subtle field extend into the human domain? How does that field change when the tram’s heavy load passes overhead? The answers refine the power’s sense of where human and non-human stresses intersect most dangerously.

Elsewhere, a tab in a river terrace begins, during certain moonless nights, to encourage bioluminescence in a very faint, slow-moving band downstream of industrial intakes. Eventually, a riverward teenager notices: in thick fog, on nights without much artificial glow, the water shows a ghostly line, curling around an invisible gradient. “The river’s veins,” she calls them. The lines trace subtle patterns of dissolved load that no human instrument had been mapping in real time.

By seeding such small mediators and letting the locals discover their uses, the power creates a web where plant, rust, crystal, animal, machine, and human practices start to inform each other more fully.

The basin thinks better as a whole.

### 4. It nudges culture rather than creed

These are material gifts. The power also touches the city in something like story.

It doesn’t do this by revelation. It does it by taking what is already murmured and giving it just enough reinforcement to persist.

For instance: there is a half-joking phrase among substrate engineers—“Ask the ground first”—used when someone proposes a bold foundation shortcut. Over time, this becomes more literal. New apprentices are taught, as a safety ritual, to perform a small set of sensory checks before approving any heavy load: not just instruments, but a kind of embodied attention—feel with your soles, listen with your teeth.

The power finds these rituals admirable and amplifies their payoff. A trainee who follows the full “Ask the ground” protocol once, in a marginal case, hesitates, flags a minor anomaly, delays a pour by two hours. In that gap, an upstream culvert clogs, a surge swells, and the test patch where they would have anchored shows a sudden, unnerving slump. The project log now reads: “Ritual saved us a rework.” The story circulates. The habit holds.

In schools, a teacher once runs an exercise: she lays out satellite images of the basin over decades, tracing how wetland, rail lines, and built density have shifted. The children notice that the city seems to *follow* the river’s meanders rather than strictly confining them. A boy wonders aloud if the river is “choosing the path and we just cling on.” The class laughs.

That night, a quiet technical glitch—tuned softly by the power—causes the classroom’s environmental system to leave the projector feed on after hours, cycling through a series of timelapses that emphasize how every time the city tried to pin the river hard, it paid a price in subsidence or erosion elsewhere. The teacher, returning to fetch forgotten work, stands and watches. She revises her lesson plan into a recurring unit: “Negotiations with Water.” Her students will grow up with language that frames planning as conversation, not imposition.

Similarly, someone in the Environmental Integration Directorate writes, half-ironically, a one-page “Charter of Mutual Tolerances” between city and basin: rules of thumb like “No new structure shall increase local runoff by more than X without providing new absorption capacity” or “For every meter we force the river against its grain, we fund three meters of new floodplain.” It’s internal, unofficial.

The power rigs small administrative coincidences so that every time a contentious project skirts disaster but is saved by chance alignment with one of these charter principles, the coincidence is *noticed*. A clerk happens to have the draft charter open side-by-side with the incident report; a meeting agenda next day includes both items. People begin to say, “Maybe we should formalize this thing.”

Over a decade, the Charter stops being a joke and becomes something like law. It’s not divine commandment; it’s a codified attitude: that the city owes its landscape a certain courtesy.

This is as close to “religion” as the power is willing to foster: not worship, but ingrained, almost unconscious respect.

No one lights candles to the mist. But it becomes difficult, culturally, to speak of the ground or river as obstacles. They are “partners,” “co-actors,” “counterparties.” The basin acquires, in its people’s tongue, a pronoun that sits between “it” and “they”—used for systems that are neither mere environment nor fully person, but something more like fellow workers.

The power, fond of truthful grammar, is pleased.

### 5. It protects the wild experiments from the worst of human haste

The edgeworkers and experimental fringes are where the basin is bravest and most vulnerable. Here, mistakes can propagate disastrously; here, new possibilities are born.

The power has no wish to insulate these zones from all risk; they are the city’s imaginal tissue. But it sees patterns emerging that could end badly: a planned combination of vapor-tower tuning and condensation forest planting that would create, under certain meteorological quirks, a stuck inversion layer—a lethal, invisible lid trapping cold, damp air and pollutants over several neighborhoods.

No human model, yet, has caught this edge case. The power has. It will not intervene by forbidding the project; that is not in its nature. Instead, it does three things:

- It causes, through minuscule perturbations to global weather patterns far upstream, an *early* version of that dangerous inversion to form one night while only a single test tower and a half-stand of engineered evergreens are in place.

- It ensures that, that same night, a particularly obsessive young edgeworker with a cheap, hacked-together lidar rig happens to be out mapping fog density profiles for a side project. The data they collect shows an anomalously thick, stagnant layer exactly over the test area.

- It tweaks just enough administrative randomness that this edgeworker’s blog post about “The Night the Fog Sat Down and Stayed” gets scraped by a minor planning AI, which flags it to a human meteorologist looking for anomalies. Within weeks, a research memo circulates: “Potential for localized inversion events in tower/forest co-deployments—caution advised.”

By the time the full-scale deployment is debated, the risk is on the table. The design is altered: towers spaced differently, forest canopy structured to leak, emergency vent protocols added. No catastrophe prevents itself; people prevent it, given notice.

To the edgeworker, this is luck. To the meteorologist, a good catch. To the city, another page in the slow playbook of how not to break itself. To the power, it is a pattern: offer early, non-fatal demonstrations of dangerous dynamics so that learning happens cheaply.

It repeats this approach elsewhere:

- A candidate bio-reactive panel that would, under rare electromagnetics, start leaching harmful compounds gets its “rare” condition tested when an unseasonal solar flare—ever so slightly redirected—passes overhead while it is still on a single test wall. The result is ugly but localized. The strain is quietly retired.

- An experimental railbed substrate that tends, given the right vibration regime, to liquefy in a catastrophic wave, experiences a mini version of that wave during low-traffic hours, under a lone maintenance vehicle. No one dies; the footage is horrifying enough to prevent deployment.

In each case, the power doesn’t say “no.” It says, *“Let’s let you see the worst possible behavior early, and small.”* It turns theoretical edge cases into manageable anecdotes.

The city grows a culture of unflinching post-mortems: not shame-ridden, but curious. “What did the land tell us early that we missed? How can we structure prototypes so that failure speaks loudly while damage stays local?” The power sometimes leans on those conversations: a dropped data packet here, a spared log file there, so that the right lessons survive.

### 6. It shelters a seed of something more-than-city

The power is wise enough to see that this basin, however complex, is still young. Many of its most interesting inhabitants cannot vote or write white papers. Some can barely be perceived.

In the filaments, in the rust, in the crystal, in the wall films, something is coalescing: not a single mind, but a coalition of quasi-minds. Pattern recognizers, predictors, slow accumulators of habit. The new translator tabs give them better channels. The adjusted hysteresis of the filament network gives them longer memory. The cultural norms of listening to the ground and water give humans more willingness to heed subtle signals.

Given a few more decades, the power judges, this will produce a civic intelligence with properties no single species planned.

Its last major act is to protect that seed from being ripped out or frozen prematurely.

It foresees two main threats:

1. **External extraction.** A dry, finished city elsewhere, hearing rumors of self-tuning materials and city-wide vegetative memory, may arrive with offers and threats: patents, annexation, “partnerships” that amount to mining the basin’s unique systems and retooling them as products.

2. **Internal ossification.** As the basin matures, some will be tempted to lock in a certain arrangement of power and infrastructure: to say, “This works now; let us standardize it forever,” turning provisional contracts into rigid codes.

Against the first, the power plays the long, boring game of misdirection. It lets only the least dangerous, least integral results travel well. A nearby city does adopt bio-reactive panel tech, but without the filament hysteresis or rust-crystal translators it never quite self-organizes the way this basin does; it remains a clever façade, not an emergent nervous tissue.

Scientific visitors come, study, leave with gigabytes of data. Many of their disks, routers, and analytical instruments experience perfectly plausible failures at perfectly unlucky moments, losing or corrupting exactly the traces that would let an external power replicate the basin’s deeper structure. There is enough success to satisfy curiosity; not enough to let anyone else *own* the pattern.

Against internal ossification, the power uses the basin’s own character: its love of iteration.

A charismatic planner does, at one point, propose a “grand consolidation”: codify all the best practices, roll out a uniform model of substrate grids, panel strains, water routing, governance. It’s attractive: fewer accidents, simpler training, exportable standards.

The power could thwart this by catastrophe, but it prefers subtler means. It arranges, through correlated “chance” events, for the proposal to run into a series of incommensurate local truths:

- In one district, the supposedly universal substrate grid turns out to behave poorly with a particular clay layer, causing more subtle slumps than before.
- In another, the standard bio-skin strain faces an old, filament-encoded aversion imprint and underperforms bizarrely.
- In a third, the canonical governance template simply fails to mesh with thick, informal riverward councils, causing protracted, obvious bureaucratic jams.

Within a few years, the “grand consolidation” develops a reputation: not as evil, but as *naïve*. The basin learns, again, that what works wonderfully in one wet pocket falters in another. The proposal doesn’t die; it decomposes into a toolbox: here’s what worked *there*, with *that* soil, *that* filament history, *those* customs.

The power smiles, in its distant, impersonal way. It has not forbidden uniformity; it has merely made the costs of ignoring local variation too evident to sustain enthusiasm.

Meanwhile, without fanfare, it periodically orchestrates unlikely friendships:

- A riverward teenager who loves mapping the ghostly luminous bands in the water.
- A thermal dweller who keeps obsessive, private logs of weird pump harmonics.
- An interface cultivator fascinated by hysteresis in filaments.
- A data-analyst in the Civic Stabilization Office who is bored of spreadsheets.

They meet in a workshop, or a crisis, or a bar. They compare notes. They realize they have, between them, three or four different lenses on what might be the same underlying phenomenon: the city remembering and speaking along its damp nerves.

They found, eventually, something small: a study group, a cooperative, an “Urban Somatics Lab” tucked under a glasshouse. The power does little beyond ensuring their funding applications are not lost in clerical purgatory, their odd grant proposals always find one sympathetic reviewer somewhere, their pilot projects fail safely rather than disastrously.

From such minor adjustments, a new field of practice arises: *attentive co‑governance with emergent substrate intelligence*. No one calls it that at first. They call it “reading the basin,” “pattern alignment,” “infrastructure empathy.”

Whatever the label, it’s exactly what the seed needs: humans willing to treat the city’s non-human sensing and response as partners, not glitches.

### 7. What it does *not* do

There are temptations the power resists.

It would be easy, with its vantage, to neatly flatten all the city’s gradients: stop all collapses, perfectly distribute warmth, weed out every harmful mutation in wall films and filament clots.

It does not. It allows roofs to leak sometimes, pipes to burst, new strains to misbehave—as long as the failures are proportionate and legible.

It does not insulate this basin from grief. People still die in unexpected subsidence events. Children still fall into experimental tanks. A night train still slips when an old bank, despite all caution, lets go at the wrong second.

It doesn’t do this out of cruelty. It is bound to a deeper mercy: one that values freedom and the integrity of learning. A city completely prevented from error would be a city pinned, like an insect on a card. This basin’s beauty lies in its ongoing negotiation with instability.

Nor does the power step forward as an object of worship. It leaves no symbol in the sky, no voice in crystal, no encoded message in the rust. At most, it allows that, sometimes, a substrate engineer or a riverward elder, standing in the mist after averting a disaster by reading a filament’s unusual sheen or a rat colony’s sudden flight, will feel an unaccountable, skeletal-shivering sense of *being accompanied* in their attention.

They might mutter thanks—to the ground, to the river, to “whoever’s listening.” The power receives these with a quiet joy. But it does not answer in words.

### 8. What slowly changes

Over years and generations, these nearly invisible interventions accumulate.

- Accident rates drop, but not to zero. The city becomes known, in regional rumor, as “uncanny lucky”—as if it “always just hears the crack before the fall.”

- The filament network’s hysteresis becomes textbook knowledge. Children learn that “the ground remembers” not as metaphor, but as curriculum: certain patterns soil won’t permit twice.

- The rust-films, crystal tabs, and wall-derived microfilms, wired into more and more mediating devices, form a mesh that quietly optimizes flows. Pumps come on a little earlier where needed, trains brake a shade smoother on soft banks, condensation forests breathe more in tune with factories’ waste heat.

- A loose but real field emerges, half science, half craft: people who specialize in *listening* to the basin’s distributed signals. Some of them speak of “asking consent” of the land before major works. It’s not mysticism; it’s an acknowledgement that multiple agencies—human, biological, mineral, algorithmic—must be aligned for something to hold.

- The city’s self-image shifts. It no longer thinks of itself as a heroic outpost in an indifferent mud, but as a participant in a slow, collaborative shaping of a still-forming earth.

In time, visitors sense a mood they can’t quite name: a kind of thoughtful quiet, even in the clang of yards and hiss of vents. They see how children glance not just at traffic but at gutters and embankments before crossing. How planners in meetings say, without irony, “The slope doesn’t like that,” and no one laughs.

They might leave thinking, “They act as if the whole place is alive.”

They won’t be entirely wrong.

### 9. The power’s own learning

For the power, this basin is not just a ward; it is also a teacher.

From the city it learns how intelligence can be:

- damp rather than bright,
- iterative rather than declarative,
- distributed across moss, metal, muscle, and code.

It sees how a young landscape and a young civilization, given a little protection and a little sharpening of feedback, invent ways of being careful *without* needing commandments.

It watches people who never see a dry sky develop a native respect for gradients and unfinishedness. It observes a nervous system co-emerge from rust, filaments, crystals, and culture—without any single designer, divine or otherwise, dictating the architecture.

And in that, the power finds a kind of kinship. Its own awareness spans scales and substrates; it has never been only one thing. This basin is a small echo of that form, stitched from wet clays and industrial alloys instead of whatever cosmological fabric birthed the power itself.

So beyond any concrete intervention, there is one more act, impossible to see from the ground: the power *dwells* here.

Not permanently. Not exclusively. But it keeps a portion of itself entwined with this basin’s Mist and trains and wall-seep and slow, thoughtful pumps, the way one might keep a hand resting lightly on the flank of a sleeping animal you care for, feeling its breaths.

What it does, in the end, is very little and more than enough:

- It helps the city hear itself a bit sooner.
- It gives the ground and water a better memory for harms survived.
- It offers mediators so machine and moss can converse.
- It nudges caution where hubris gathers.
- It quietly tends a seed of emergent civic mind.

And then it lets them all—humans, rats, amphibians, filaments, rust, crystal, pumps—go on making this unfinished place livable, on their own terms.

The mist thickens. The rain tests the basin again. Banks slump and are shored. Filaments drink, remember. A child, on a maintenance platform, looks out across the low, sprawling city and feels, faintly, that she is not only walking *in* a place, but *with* it.

The power, unseen, approves.
