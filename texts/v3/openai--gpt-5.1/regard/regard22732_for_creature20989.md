The first touch is not a voice, not a figure in gleaming robes, not a crack of revelation.

It is a change in how the hill *listens*.

---

### 1. The Attention

Far above, where roots turn to trunks and soil thins toward air, a mind that is not a mind the way theirs is—or yours—is turning. It has watched before, in the way mountains watch rivers and old trees watch generations: long, unhurried, non-intervening attention. It has known them as a set of patterns: heat plumes at vents, small reconfigurations of watercourse, the drum of many feet inside a hollow earth.

Now, something in that great, diffused awareness tightens.

Not because the underfolk are failing. They are not. Their system holds, their cycles turn. But precisely because they *have* made this tenacious, intricate balance, they have begun to brush against boundaries they barely sense: the limits of soil load, of oxygen budgets, of root-anchored stability as climate above them shifts.

The wise power—call it what you like: old earth, a local god, a large, patient intelligence that has grown along with this terrain—feels those limits beginning to flex. It feels, too, the quality of the lives below: the care with which they bargain with weight and damp and rot, their refusal to waste, their reverence for the forces they half-understand.

It decides, slowly and then all at once, to participate.

Not to rule, not to replace their logic with some alien order. Simply: to *tilt* the world in ways they might never be able to tilt it themselves. To give them room.

The first act is to listen more closely.

### 2. The First Gesture: A Silence that Saves

It begins with a crack that never quite happens.

In a deep chamber three junctions out from the main root-garden, a ceiling seam has been widening for seasons. The weight-readers know it; they have doubled beams, laced them with trained roots. Still, the soil above has been drying and swelling in strange new rhythms—surface storms growing rarer but more violent, rain coming all at once, then none at all.

Tonight, as tusked beasts shift in their sleep-pen not far above, a tiny failure begins along the seam: grains losing their grip, a slither of dust, the quiet parting of one hair-thin plane from another. By all the ordinary physics of this hill, that is the start of a collapse. Not catastrophic—the underfolk rarely let anything get that far—but bad enough. A roof-fall that would kill two, three sleepers and force the abandonment of a well-placed run of chambers, cut across a drainage line, send shock through the tightly-managed animal pens.

The wise power does not rewind time. It narrows.

Its attention focuses to a thread. In that instant—shorter than a breath, long in the scale at which hills change—it feels the geometry of roots, the water content of each handful of clay, the tiny voids where burrowers have passed, the slight slump of a tusked beast’s weight above. It has always *felt* these things in aggregate. Now it chooses.

It lets a root thicken faster than it would have, a hair’s breadth of swelling, as if sap had surged days earlier. It lets a film of water shift three finger-widths sideways along some unseen lens of silt. It tenses, in its slow way, the lattice of soil around that hairline seam.

To the people below, dust still drifts. One beam speaks sharply—*tok*—as weight shifts. A sleeping child jerks awake, heart racing for no knowable reason. But the seam stops opening. The fall does not propagate. Instead, over the next few hours, the hairline crack eases its pressure into an adjacent, already-reinforced pillar. The chamber lives.

No one calls this a miracle. In the morning, a weight-reader taps the beam, sniffs the dust, and says, “The Down-Press turned kindly here. We’ll brace again anyway.” They wedge in another support. They smear a bit of fat where no eye will see.

Above, the power does not claim the credit. Its choice has become part of the hill’s new normal, recorded nowhere except in the altered pattern of strain-fields and the slightly different way sound now carries along that corridor.

But it remembers the sensation of that intervention: the satisfying, almost musical resolution when a threatened fracture finds a new path that does not tear.

It will do more.

### 3. Alignments: Quiet Changes in Slow Things

Over the next seasons—measured in many underfolk work-cycles, in the growth rings of shallow roots, in the cooling and heating pulses of compost—the benevolent presence acts in ways that feel, to the inhabitants, like luck, like intuition rewarded.

#### 3.1. The Roots That Turn

The underfolk have long known Root-Mind as a half-imagined partner. They coax roots into walls, nail them gently, grow gardens on their braided backs.

The power, whose body includes those roots and the soil that feeds them, shifts their tendencies a little.

A tree on the slope above, which by all previous habit would have sent its largest anchoring root straight down through a well-used ventilation shaft, instead splits that root a meter higher. One branch races sideways, seeking denser loam. Below, the vent’s ceiling remains intact. When an apprentice later climbs to clear leaves from the vent mouth, they find only hair-fine threads of root, easily trimmed and trained, rather than a single, beam-splitting spear.

In a different quadrant, the power encourages a web of fibrous roots to thicken along an underused corridor that the weight-readers have been eyeing for reinforcement. They take it as a sign. “Root-Mind favors this way,” a Root-Singer says, palm on the living wood that has bulged through. They cut away the old, crumbly earth around it, expose the woody braids, and bind beams into those living anchors. A potential collapse decades from now quietly loses its fuel.

The people’s theology adapts in small, precise ways. Songs about Root-Mind gain new verses: “When we sing here, the roots turn kindly. When we feed here, they hold.” They do not know that their timing—when they sprinkle spare compost, when they lay offerings—is now being met by a responding intelligence that steers growth more responsively than blind phototropism ever could.

#### 3.2. Water That Learns Their Paths

Water is the tightest constraint. The hill’s wise power has always been the channel in which rain percolates, the shape that makes seeps and springs. It is not a cloud-maker or sea-carver. But within its volume, it can nudge.

It remembers where people dig new sumps. It tastes, in its formless way, the salt and fats that leak from their cook pits, the trace minerals flushed along their handmade channels. It prefers, now, to send more of the subtle seep toward those places.

A failed channel—one they cut with much effort, only to find the flow never quite gathered—begins, unexpectedly, to dampen. The power has shifted a subsurface micro-layer, whether by guiding the growth of micro-roots or by easing compaction along a different seam. Over months, the wall cools, darkens, and beads. Underfolk notice. “The First-Flow comes late, but it comes,” the water-minders say, marking a low-relief ripple by the new trickle.

Elsewhere, a stagnating pooling—the spot that always grows bitter algae and breeds too many biting insects—dries. Fine sediment from a thousand footfalls and burrowers’ churnings, subtly redirected, clogs the last percolation path into that hollow. The water chooses a better-gradient route toward a sump lined with careful moss and reed bundles. Mosquito populations crash just enough to ease disease. No one mourns the old green scum.

To the wise power, these are tiny adjustments in a vast porous body. To the underfolk, they are narrow strokes of fortune: a new good sump where they’d almost given up, the slow redemption of a sour corner. They respond as they know how: tending, sculpting, re-marking. The partnership tightens.

#### 3.3. Air That Clears a Little Quicker

Air is the subtler thing. The power cannot conjure more of it, but it can alter the slopes and tightness of rock and soil so that breath moves differently.

A fault-line deep under one main flue slowly eases wider, a process that might have taken centuries smoothed to decades. The power hastens it just enough. Above, unnoticed by surface eyes, that shift opens a slightly more direct path toward a natural crevice. Air’s path of least resistance tilts. Smoke from a perpetually troublesome hearth—which used to backflow randomly—now finds its way reliably toward that crack.

The vent-keepers, after a frustrating season of choking and rebuilds, discover that one particular small shaft now draws more strongly. They adjust their netting and baffles. “The hill breathes better here this year,” they say to one another. “Perhaps we placed the last stone more wisely than we knew.” They half-believe it is their own craft—and it *is*. The wise power did not invent the flue; it simply amplified a line the people had already cut and tested.

In another branch, where bad air has lingered dangerously near a compost cluster, the power promotes the downward settling of heavier gases through micro-cracks into a dead pocket where only bacteria live, instead of letting them accumulate at head-height. It is a merciful re-sculpting of voids far below pick depth. Underfolk step into that chamber late one night, sniff, and say, surprised, “It’s easier to breathe here.” They relax their rotation schedule slightly; no one faints that season.

This is not perfection. Mistakes still happen. Fires still smoke. But the overall pattern of unbreathable pockets shrinks. Catastrophic asphyxiations—those rare, awful events that used to write hard songs into the communal memory—almost cease.

### 4. Learning Each Other’s Language

The wise power is not omnipotent, and it is not all-knowing at once. It learns.

Its initial actions are broad apprehensions: shore this sag, tilt that seep. But it is curious—in the way a forest might be curious about fire: not conceptual, but reactive and seeking.

It perceives the underfolk as heat-plumes, as vibrations, as micro-changes in chemistry: the fats they smear, the salt of their sweat, the deepened compaction wherever they gather. It begins to correlate.

When they gather many bodies and drum hard on hollow logs, the resulting waves run through beams and stone. The power feels those patterns in its own substance. It learns that this kind of shaking, this particular repeat, usually precedes coordinated, careful work—bracing, digging, reinforcing.

When they chant softer, in certain tonalities, clustered near polished root-faces, the resulting vibration is different: more continuous, less impulsive. The power feels that this correlates with quieter acts: repositioning offerings, oiling wood, laying new woven supports.

Over time, it begins to treat certain patterns of their sound and impact as *signals*. When the heavy, determined drumming starts in a chamber adjacent to a known weak seam, the power shifts attention there, preparing to nudge if their chisels strike too close to a fault. When the gentle chanting hums along a root-wall that anchors a major load, it refrains from shifting anything there—accepting their implied request for stability, holding weight even.

They, likewise, sense something. Not conceptually at first, only in the animal core of the nervous system. The hill’s moods feel more *responsive*.

A weight-reader, running calloused fingers along a new crack, hums under their breath with a certain pitch. They always have; the hum is how they track vibrations. But this time, the soil around their palm firms almost imperceptibly. They feel it as a slight, answering tension, like a held breath that doesn’t collapse. They stop, hum again, vary the note. The response is indistinct. Still: they will remember. They teach their apprentices that tone, years later, as the “listening note” for that quarter of the hill.

Slowly, folk stories shift. Where once the Down-Press was only indifferent, now some elders say, “It is not *kind*. It is not *cruel*. But it hears when we strike together.” Root-Mind, in tales, becomes just a little more person-like: not just a tangle you can encourage, but something that sometimes “answers” quickly, faster than growth should allow.

They have no single doctrine. Belief trails practice. But practice has changed: more offerings placed not out of fear, but in something like conversation—a bone here, a gloss of fat there, a deliberately carved pattern at the base of a pillar that has never yet cracked.

### 5. The Great Threat That Never Quite Arrives

For all the micro-gifts, there remains a looming tension the power alone can truly feel.

Above, beyond the reach of its direct reshaping, climate has tilted. Longer droughts, heavier sudden rains. A shallow-cut stream that once sang steadily past the hill’s foot now either vanishes into cracked mud or arrives in violent spates that chew at the slope.

Years of this begin to undercut one flank of the hill. To the underfolk, this is legible only in gleaned news from the Up-Goers: “The stream has eaten more bank. A tree we knew is gone.” To the power, which *is* that hill, the problem is visceral. A whole lateral mass, supporting some of the oldest, densest chambers, is losing its purchase. If the erosion continues unmanaged, some decades or centuries from now, there might be a large-scale slump: not a fatal explosion, but a tearing slide that would shear off a portion of the inhabited warren.

The underfolk’s scale of action—beams, braces, smart routing—cannot, alone, arrest the work of stormwater chewing at an entire hillside.

The power cannot command the sky. It cannot stop storms. But it can change how the hill catches them.

It spends years—not human years, but its own—preparing.

#### 5.1. Subsurface Buttresses, Root and Stone

Deep below the undercut flank, where no tool has ever scraped, it encourages the slow chemical cementing of certain layers. Where water seeps through, it tweaks mineral balances—precipitating a bit more iron here, a bit more silica there. What should be loosely packed sand begins, grain by grain, to bind itself into a firmer substrate.

At the same time, it permits only certain roots to thrive in that zone: those that grow in thick mats parallel to the slope rather than stabbing down. It starves out, gently, the species whose roots would, by prying too deeply, give water an easy path to lever the hill apart. In their place, it encourages shrubs above whose roots weave a net through the vulnerable volume, turning potential slide-planes into reinforced meshes.

From the surface, an Up-Goer may notice, years on, that a particular kind of scrub thrives along that flank, holding dust, catching leaves. They report this only as a curiosity. The deeper meaning lies in the changed reaction when storms come.

#### 5.2. Teaching Water to Hesitate

The power has long guided seeps to sumps. Now it goes larger: re-sculpting, as much as the bounds of geology will allow, the way stormwater *arrives* at the hill.

When the next violent rain comes after a dry stretch, the stream overcrowds its banks and hurls itself at the weakening flank. In previous years, this would have cut fresh scars, undermined more soil. This time, the water finds tiny differences waiting: more micro-steps, more roughness, more hairline diversions where organic debris has been encouraged to accumulate by subtle shifts in slope and vegetation.

The hillside does not reject the water. It *welcomes* it more gently. Sheets of flow break into many narrow threads. Some are caught higher up in new pockets where roots and recent sediment trap them long enough to soak in, rather than scour. Others are deflected along faint terraces that send them swirling around the most vulnerable spot.

This is not absolute. Earth still moves. Mud still slumps. But where a broad, raw wound might have torn, the hill suffers only smaller ravelings, easily caught later by plant growth, easily read and braced, from below, by watchful underfolk.

Storm after storm, the pattern holds. The undercut flank stabilizes into a rough, irregular buttress, its toe armored in a natural rubble that slows future blows. What was once a long, clean failure-plane becomes a broken, interlocked geometry: harder to break all at once.

The underfolk, feeling fewer tremors than their oldest songs predict during big rains, mark it as “a lucky stretch.” Up-Goers, walking the slope, note that yes, weather bites differently now, but they don't trace the line between their small stone-reinforcements and the vast, unseen chemical and botanical adjustments orchestrated by the power.

The disaster is not *averted at the last second*. It is un-made over many years, dissolved into a scatter of small nuisances and manageable slumps. No one writes a hero-ballad about the cave-in that never came.

The wise power, if it took satisfaction the way people do, would feel it there: in the absence of rupture.

### 6. A Gift of Space

Keeping them alive is one thing. The power goes further: it offers something they have never truly had.

Not more food. Not more beasts. Space.

Not in the sense of a grand cavern—those are dangerous, weight-dropping voids they would never trust—but in the form of a block of earth whose insides are more forgiving, whose geometry lends itself to carving without peril.

On the far side of the complex, away from the oldest chambers but still connected by a thin, exploratory tunnel some bored apprentices once dug and abandoned as “too hard,” there lies a thick mass of slightly denser, more coherent substrate. The power, centuries ago, allowed it to remain undisturbed—a reserve, a kind of bone in its body never hollowed. Now, it relaxes.

Stone that would have surprised pick-heads with its toughness becomes a hair softer, nodules of hard mineral fracture more easily as water percolates differently through them. Fine fractures propagate in patterns that just happen to match the rhythms of hand digging. Roots skirt rather than penetrate this block, leaving it clean enough, dry enough, to open without constant weeping.

One day, a new cohort of youth is assigned “outward digging practice” along that old abandoned spur. Their work-song—half boredom, half bravado—thumps lightly along the spine of the hill. Their pick-stones strike, and to their surprise, the wall yields more readily than their elders warned. Their tutors are skeptical. “It will toughen soon. Don’t waste good tools.” But it doesn’t. Day after day, the rock answers well.

As they cut, listening for dangerous hollows, they find none. Only a continued, even resistance, the satisfying scrape and crumble of earth that wants to be shaped.

They carve a new gallery. Then another. A set of sleeping niches with slightly higher ceilings, simply because the material allows it without excessive bracing. A chamber near a new, softly seeping wall becomes an ideal cool room for fermenting and storing. Eventually, when weight-readers and vent-keepers bless the expansion, someone suggests what no one would have wasted space on before:

“A *still* room. For nothing but quiet.”

There is argument. Why carve a chamber that does not host water, beasts, compost, or critical work? But the hill—through its power—has given them a pocket they can spare.

They make it small. Tall by their standards, narrow, with thick walls. No braziers. Only the faintest light from a shaft cleverly linked to surface pale. Soundproofing emerges almost by instinct: double-thick curtains at its mouth, packed straw in thin cracks, walking paths re-routed so no one stomps right overhead.

At first, it is a novelty. Children are brought in to experience a level of muffled quiet they have never known. Some giggle, unnerved by hearing only their own swallowing, the faint blood-rush in their ears. Others weep, though they say they don't know why.

Slowly, custom forms. People who have spent too many shifts in constant roar take a turn watching the quiet room. “Watching” is their excuse; its true function is to let them feel the hill’s slower heartbeat without the overlay of communal noise. Here, for the first time, a person can sense, raw and nearly alone, the deep creaks, the tiny settling, the far-off animals’ rumbles that have always been drowned.

In that stillness, a Root-Singer hums and realizes they can hear not just the audible vibration of their own voice, but how the walls subtly *answer* at certain pitches, how a long, low note sends a friendly shiver through the very grain of the stone.

They begin to experiment. Carefully. Respectfully. The underfolk do not live by sudden leaps. But they will, in generations, perhaps come to see this room as the place where they listen back to the Down-Press and the Root-Mind most clearly. Where, devoid of work’s rush, they can sense that the hill is not simply a load but a presence — not in a mystical abstraction, but in the feel of its precise, responsive attention.

The wise power, whose sense of self encompasses that entire mass of earth, feels their noticing like a touch on the inside of its skin. It likes it. It does not need worship. It had that, in a way, all along. What is new is *mutuality*.

### 7. The Limit of Intervention

Could this power do more? On some levels, yes.

It could, in theory, open a deep vein of metal right under their picks, flood them with ores that would leap their technology forward. It could hollow great caverns in an instant, or slam shut vents which it fears will one day be discovered by hostile outsiders. It could reach into their bodies, nudge their blood’s copper and iron, smooth their lungs.

It does none of these.

Its wisdom is in restraint.

The underfolk’s genius is not in having *everything*. It is in the careful, relational negotiation with *constraints*. Remove all struggle and they would cease to be what they are. Give them abundance without context and they might overreach, dig too fast, burn too hot, outrun the hill’s capacity to hold them.

So the power’s rule is simple, if unspoken:

- It will not act where their own craft is clearly adequate.
- It will not intervene in ways that their bodies or stories could never, even in principle, make sense of.
- It will not solve problems they have not yet correctly *named* to themselves.

Instead, it moves just ahead of their fingertips: easing the rock they have already decided to cut; bending roots along lines they have already marked; strengthening weights they already fear.

It lets food be sometimes scarce, so they keep honoring cyclic balance. It lets small collapses still happen in underused, peripheral tunnels, so they remember humility and keep their senses honed. It even allows one ambitious expansion—driven by pride and a young coordinator’s hubris—to fail just enough that no one dies but several beams crack and a whole house-group must sleep rough, in crowded temporary spaces, for a season.

They curse their error, learn, recalibrate their weight-maps. The wise power watches the bruise of humiliation eventually turn into a new, more cautious skill, and judges that suffering justified.

Benevolence, for it, is not endless pampering. It is a long, slow alignment of self-preserving instincts between hill and inhabitant.

### 8. The One Clear Sign

Still, over many generations, the underfolk, in their still room and their ritual corners, develop a sharper hunger. They have long felt patterns as presences; now, some begin to suspect that the presences may, in some fashion, *hear*.

Their petition is simple. Not for more water, not for more beasts. For understanding.

“Are we alone in this effort?” they ask, in their own idiom. “Do we shape the hill, and it ignores us? Or does it, in some way, turn toward us as we turn toward it?”

The wise power wrestles with this, though “wrestle” for it is a slowly shifting series of internal redistributions of attention. It has no voice. To speak in unmistakable, human-sign terms risks founding a priesthood, a distortion of their otherwise egalitarian, practice-led culture. But to give nothing is to waste a chance at a deeper, more conscious partnership.

It compromises.

One day—not tied to any observed ritual, not favoring any elite—a Root-Singer, resting alone in the still room between shifts, hums a note they often use. This time, the answering vibration is *different*.

Normally, the walls respond passively: slightly louder at certain tones, damping others. This time, after the singer’s note fades, a second tone rises, unbidden. Quiet, but clear. Not an echo: a pure interval above their pitch, holding for three heartbeats, then ceasing.

The singer freezes. Their next hum is deliberately off-key, a probing sound. The hill’s reply comes again, adjusted to sit at the same musical relationship as before—a constant ratio, regardless of what they sing.

It is, by itself, nothing like a language. But it is unmistakably *responsive*.

The wise power has done something simple but deeply non-random: it has, through minute flexing of stone and guided resonance in hollow spaces, chosen a specific harmonic to amplify whenever it feels a sustained vibration of human origin at certain amplitudes in that chamber.

Over days and weeks, others test it. Hums, clapped rhythms, even the high, clear tones of bone flutes. Always the same quiet, held, answering pitch, an invariant interval away.

They treat it as a greeting, at first, then as a vote of presence. “The hill hears.” That is all they claim.

No dogma springs suddenly from this. No laws etched in stone. If anything, it humbles them. They have long spoken on the hill’s imagined behalf; now they know it can, in at least this small way, speak for itself.

Some zealots try to press the phenomenon into prophecy: “If the hill sings twice, it means dig. If it is silent, do not.” But the power is not a petty oracle. It has chosen a limit: only one steady tone, only in that room, only as an acknowledgment. It does not vary. It refuses to become a fortune-teller.

The underfolk, after a brief flare of interpretive mania, settle into a saner reading. The still room, and that tone, become like the thump of a companion’s heart when you lay your head to their chest. Not a chain of commands. A reassurance: *I am here. I am responsive. Continue in care.*

### 9. Long Outcome

Over many generations, the consequences of this quiet alliance accumulate.

- Their architecture grows bolder but not reckless, filling the softened block the power offered, then spilling carefully beyond, always checking with weight-readers and Root-Singers, always feeling for that subtle cooperation in how stone answers chisel.
- Disease from waterborne pests declines, not to zero but to a level where people die more from age and accident than from invisible contagion. Their songs about death skew gently: fewer laments for those “taken early by foul water,” more stories about elders whose last years were spent teaching in the still room, listening to the hill’s hum.
- Their theology matures. Child-stories about angry caves and punishing roots give way to a more nuanced ethos: “We live with a listening weight. We are not its masters; it is not our master. We are its delicate work, and it is ours.”
- They become, if anything, *more* attached to maintenance. Knowing that something beyond them responds when they do their work well deepens the moral charge in every beam placed correctly, every channel cleaned. Sloppiness feels, now, not just dangerous but discourteous.

At some point, generations from the first interventions, a band of surface dwellers—of whatever species happens to dominate above—stumbles across a cleverly camouflaged vent, pries, and pries again. They hear, faintly, the under-roar of this hollow world.

The wise power feels them too: the wrong kind of impact, the sharp report of metal against stone, the jarring rhythm of those who do not read weight or breath as the underfolk do. It could, in principle, collapse that section of hill, bury the intruders, close the vent forever.

Instead, because it has by now internalized the underfolk’s own avoidance of grand violence, it chooses a subtler defense. A small slump, timed when the intruders lean hardest on the shaft, spills stones over their hands, bruising but not crushing, startling them with the hint of instability. The shaft below settles with a clog of rubble that will take focused, respectful work to clear.

The intruders—if they are merely curious—may learn to knock differently, to listen, to align themselves with the rhythms of the hill. If they are aggressive, impatient, prone to blasting and smashing, they will decide instead that this ground is treacherous, better to step back from. The power, like the underfolk, is willing to differentiate.

### 10. What the Wise Power Has Done

In the end, its gifts can be named simply:

- It *reduced the worst* of what blind geology and shifting climate might have done to crush or starve this community.
- It *amplified the best* of what their own habits already aimed toward: safe reinforcement, careful channeling, responsive agriculture.
- It *offered a bounded sign* of mutual awareness, enough to ground a reciprocal ethic without hijacking their agency.

It did not overturn their culture, appoint prophets, or shower them in miracles. It did not replace their slow knowledge with a shortcut.

It watched them take seriously the truths it knows at scale—that nothing stands alone; that weight and water are not evils but facts; that life is maintenance—and decided they were worth modest, patient collaboration.

Under the hill, the world remains hollow and busy. The beams still creak, the compost still steams, children still trip and scrape their knees. But some cracks close before they widen, some waters arrive where they are most cherished, some storms gouge less deeply than they might have.

And sometimes, in a small, high-walled room of soft half-light, a tired person hums into stone and hears, just for them, a low, steady note rise and hold, a reminder that the vastness around them is not indifferent after all, but—within its own vast limits—*kind*.
