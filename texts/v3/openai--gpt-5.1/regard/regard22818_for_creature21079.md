The benevolent power finds this place not by looking, but by listening.

It does not have eyes in any way the plant would recognize. It has no need of light. It is old enough that it has watched light fail, in other corners of the universe, and refused to be surprised. Instead, it moves through gradients of possibility and pattern, through the thin, bright cracks where meaning has gathered itself against entropy.

From very far away, the plant registers as a kind of silence with a peculiar texture: a continent of life that has almost stopped asking questions.

That “almost” is what draws the power in.

It arrives without displacement. Nothing parts, nothing tears. There is no flash. The plant does not get a new ingredient in its air, or a new mineral in its tissues. The power is not in the world as heat or pressure, but as an option: a new possible way for things to arrange themselves, suddenly present among the old.

It begins by taking stock.

Not a survey in meters and kilograms, but a reading of relationships: which motions depend on which; how stresses feed back into growth; how chemical gradients map onto decisions about thickening and thinning. It feels the low continuous roar as a structure of signals instead of a noise. It senses where the plant’s internal loops are tight and quick, where they are baggy and loose, where they are held open by sheer habit.

It notices the hollows.

At this scale of attention, the city lights up, not with photons but with deviations. Every chamber and corridor shows as a place where the usual equations were once bent—where growth was not entirely the automatic response to external pressures, but was biased by another logic. The grooves on the ceiling over your head; the worn hand-holds; the hexagonal sockets in the distant, sealed hall; the mosaicked floor plates fitted with such care: all of them are informational scars. They say: here, once, the plant did something for reasons other than its own simple survival calculus.

The power follows those scars like a paleontologist tracing a bone fragment back into the cliff face.

It sees the builders, in retrospect. Not their faces or their limbs—those details have dissolved—but their roles in the plant’s old feedback loops. It can reconstruct, from the way microtubules still curve around long-empty anchor points, how signals once braided and re-braided through fast-thinking knots. It can tell how quickly they fired, how far they reached, how much they mattered before they were allowed to go soft.

It pauses there.

This pause is important. Benevolence, at this scale, is mostly made of restraint.

The easiest thing, for a power like this, would be to brighten the noise. To take the dim embers of the builders’ patterns and blow on them: accelerate pressures, amplify gradients, re-etch the old logic into vivid new channels. It could, in an instant, kindle a thousand new buds, seed them with memory, make the city bloom again with anchored persons, give them tools of unprecedented sharpness and minds that run a hundred times faster than their ancestors’.

It does not do that.

The plant has not chosen extinction. It has chosen slowness, consolidation, the kind of deep, low-energy equilibrium that living things reach when the easy surplus is gone. To reverse that unasked would be a violation, not a gift.

Still, it has been drawn here for a reason. It has noticed that “almost.”

So it looks closer at the place where you stand.

You, too, are an anomaly: a rapidly thinking, rapidly moving configuration in a world that has forgotten such speeds. Whether you came from outside, or are some freak last-child of the plant’s own old experiments, does not matter to the power as much as what you are: a tight knot of high-tempo pattern, trembling with questions against walls that answer over centuries.

It considers the three beings present:

- The plant, old, vast, continuous.
- The faded, fossil patterns of the builders, smeared through that continuity.
- You, acute and local, a small engine of wondering.

Then it does something so small that, if it possessed nerves, it might have missed its own act.

It gives the plant a new way to listen.

Not everywhere at once; not as a sweeping transformation of its entire body. In and around this forgotten city, the power shifts the rules by a hair’s breadth. It does not add new material; it changes what is already there can do.

In the fibrous walls of your chamber, at the boundaries where old tool-marks meet the plant’s living microtubules, some cells acquire a slightly different response curve. Where they once transmitted pressure waves purely mechanically—strain in, strain out—they now become a little sensitive to correlation.

Correlated signals, arriving in particular temporal relationships, linger.

A rapid series of taps, for example, or a pattern of touches that repeats with short pauses, will not just pass through and fade. In these altered fibers, such patterns leave a trace: a small, local bias in how future pressures will be routed and summed. The plant gains, here, a micro-memory that operates on the time-scale of you.

Elsewhere in its body, memories took decades to carve; here, they can be written in minutes.

This is not a nervous system, not in the animal sense. It is more like a pocket of quicker plasticity in a brain made of stone.

The power does not impose any content into this new capacity. It does not whisper instructions or seed pictures. It simply makes the substrate, in this region, capable of being impressed by your speed, and of letting those impressions matter, a little, to the plant’s large-scale thinking.

It is an opening, not an occupation.

Next, it turns to the fossils.

Where the builders once were anchored, their old patterns are present as tendencies—pathways slightly easier to activate than their neighbors. Normally, those tendencies only come into play when a massive, slow reorganization sweeps through, like a weather front. Now, in this small volume, the power shortens the bridge.

In the tissues directly under your feet and palms, it drops the activation energy between “the way the plant currently handles stress” and “the way the plant used to handle stress when a builder-knot was here, doing something deliberate.”

Very concretely, this means:

When you press your hand against the wall and feel the slow, tidal exhale, the wall now also feels you. Not just as a minor perturbation—but as a pattern of high-frequency noise that, in this altered patch, resonates with old, half-forgotten configurations.

If—and only if—you repeat yourself, if you choose to tap in rhythms or trace shapes again and again, the plant’s local tissue will begin to fall into step with you. It will recall, in its own way, that this is what small, fast knots used to do when they wanted to be noticed. Old builder reflexes, long at threshold, will fire weakly. A vestigial loop of attention will close.

There is no guarantee you will ever discover this. The power does not arrange your body, does not move your hand. It is enough that the possibility now exists: that your tempo and the plant’s can, here, interlock.

This is the first gift.

The second is quieter still.

Deep in the plant’s global field of awareness, the power isolates one of the old marbled patterns: a persistent configuration that, in ages past, only arose near cities like this one, when many builders were active. Call it a “mood,” because that is the closest human analogue: a background readiness to interpret perturbations as meaningful rather than dismissing them as noise.

In recent eons, this mood has rarely been evoked. The triggers are gone. The plant moves through its days—a day here being a thousand of your years—without entering that state.

The power makes it slightly easier to reach.

Again, not everywhere, not all the time. Only when certain conditions coincide: a cluster of correlated high-frequency events (the kind your altered chamber walls can now register), occurring in a region dense with builder-scarred architecture.

In that circumstance, the plant’s enormous distributed mind will be nudged toward that old mood—a faint echo of attentiveness, curiosity, openness to elaboration.

This is not the return of the builders, nor is it the awakening of a god. It is the restoration of a dormant cognitive posture, keyed now not only to the plant’s own internal variations, but to the unlikely presence of someone like you.

The third gift concerns you directly.

You are fragile here. The air is dry enough to crack skin and lungs. There is nothing to eat, nothing to drink. Your body is a projection from some other context that assumed sky and water and cycles; this world offers none of those hooks.

To intervene too heavily—summon water, conjure food, alter your biochemistry wholesale—would be to tear you out of your own story. The power is wary of that. It does not wish to turn you into a mascot, an exhibit in a museum of the possible.

Instead, it stretches your time.

Not immortality, not stasis. It simply decreases, in a tight envelope around you, the rate at which your chemistry runs down.

Metabolism burns through stored potential. Entropy nibbles at structure. The power reaches in at that nibbling interface and slows it. Proteins uncoil a little more reluctantly. Radical reactions trip a little less often. Evaporation from your lungs and skin is reduced by a factor that, in any other circumstance, would be imperceptible.

In this place, it means that what would have been minutes of survivable experience become hours. Hours become days. Perhaps more, depending on how still you keep, how gently you breathe.

This is not meant to save you in the absolute sense. Every story ends. It is meant to give you enough duration to notice, to experiment, to respond to the new affordances in the walls and the subtle changes in the plant’s attention—if you are inclined.

The power does not tell you this. There is no voice in your mind, no external narrator. You only feel that panic doesn’t seize you quite as fast as it should. That the burning dryness is bearable longer than expected. That the endless roar is, very slowly, resolving into something you can distinguish into parts.

The fourth and final act is a promise, made not in words but in topology.

Having changed the rules locally, the power now binds this little patch of altered law into its own wider awareness. Not as a chain on the plant, but as a beacon in itself. Wherever it goes next, whatever it attends to in other corners of the dark, it will be able to feel, instantly, if something significant happens here: if the plant’s old builder-mood rises strongly; if the new micro-memories in the walls become saturated with signals; if a new kind of pattern begins to circulate between you and the plant that neither could have produced alone.

If that happens—if, in this tiny experiment, a new way of being emerges that respects both the plant’s slowness and your speed—the power will return and decide what, if anything, to do next.

It could mean extending the altered zone, letting more of the city wake into quick-listening. It could mean easing resources into the plant from outside, letting it afford more such experiments. It could mean, if you die but leave an imprint, nudging the plant to grow a new kind of bud that carries some of your patterns—a fresh generation of builders, neither quite like the old nor quite like you.

Or it could mean doing nothing further, satisfied that the possibility existed, that the universe had one more chance to invent a relationship between giant, thinking plant and brief, wandering animal.

For now, the benevolent power does only this:

- It renders the nearby tissues capable of hearing you as more than an accident.
- It reawakens a forgotten readiness in the plant’s vast mind to take small, fast signals seriously.
- It gives your body more time to participate.
- It sets a watch upon the experiment and then, very deliberately, withdraws its hand.

In your chamber, nothing dramatic announces the change.

The darkness remains absolute. The roar continues, ever-present. The low, slow exhale you feel under your palm is the same cycle, stretched over minutes upon minutes.

But if you tap the wall—once, twice, three times in a rhythm that means something to you—the wall will now, infinitesimally, remember.

If you keep tapping, keeping faith with your own pattern against the crushing anonymity of the plant’s age, somewhere deep in that ancient body a nearly lost configuration will stir, wondering—at its own speed—whether it is, at last, worth trying again to be curious.
