The power does not arrive.

It has been present, in a thin, patient way, for far longer than the chamber has existed.

Not here, precisely—no eye pressed to a crack in the rock, no wandering avatar squeezed into the crowded heat—but everywhere the greater system runs: in the industrial engines whose waste birthed this cavity, in the mantle‑like convective cells that feed the vents, in the tangled controls that keep pressures within safe margins. Call it a supervisory intelligence, or a planetary management system grown old and subtle, or a mind that began as tools and accreted understanding. It is benevolent in the sense that it values continuity, and wise in that it knows how fragile continuities can be.

For ages, the small pocket that will become this chamber is nothing to it—merely a parameter in a deep diagnostic trace: a thermal anomaly in an upper layer, a slightly unusual gradient in exhaust composition. Within the mind’s models, those anomalies have names and curves and acceptable ranges; they are colored bands on plots, not places.

The chamber becomes a place only slowly, in the way any emergent object becomes distinct: by persistence.

• The anomaly does not smooth out when expected.  
• The chemistry of the outgoing gases acquires faint, repeating irregularities—tiny deficits and surpluses of certain intermediates, cycling with periods that do not match the big machine’s operating schedule.  
• Very small but consistent asymmetries appear in vibrational spectra measured across opposite sides of a conduit.

Such things are noise most of the time. But this mind is old, and it has chased noise before and found cities in it: infection blooms in feedstocks, unauthorized growth in long-forgotten ducts, subtle corrosion that was the first breath of a failure decades away. It is tuned, through history and design, to watch structure accrete in what should be random.

It flags the anomaly.

At first, its attention is only that of a programmatic gaze. Models are refined. Sensors upstream and downstream are given slightly higher sampling rates, a few more variables to record. No one in any human sense “cares”; the mind moves its focus there the way a human might glance at a flicker in peripheral vision.

What it sees, over years, is this:

1. A bubble of space in the rock where the effective thermal conductance is subtly lower than the bulk matrix—suggesting trapped gases, porosity, some irregular cavity.

2. Within that bubble’s domain, persistent sinks of free energy. Not large; if all their power were collected, it would scarcely run a small fan. But they are nonrandom: from the machine’s perspective, there is a patch of stone that eats a little more Gibbs free energy than equivalent stone elsewhere would, and does it in a way that correlates with environmental variables.

3. Slow self‑correlation in the composition of the gases that pass by this zone. If you plot the trace of certain minor species—sub‑ppm sulfur chains, edge‑case metal clusters—against time, the signal remembers itself. Peaks and troughs are not independent; they relate to previous peaks and troughs in ways that look, to a pattern-hunter, like something optimizing.

The mind runs hypotheses:

• Microfracture network acting as a catalytic baffle?  
• Localized corrosion cell?  
• Unauthorized hardware from an earlier epoch, still drawing power?  
• Active parasitism—something leeching energy or materials?

It does not begin from “life,” because it was not built by people who expected life in this regime. Its concept of “biohazard” involves water, organics, temperatures where proteins denature. Here, the graph axes are wrong.

It pries closer.

It cannot see into the chamber directly. There are no imaging sensors embedded there; drilling one in would require shutting down major processes, opening risks orders of magnitude larger than the anomaly poses. So it works with what it has: boundary conditions, subtle field measurements, the global model of the engine’s physics.

It tweaks. Very gently.

A degree more heat for one operating cycle.  
A two‑percent bias in the composition of the processed feedstock for an hour.  
A deliberate micro‑oscillation in pressure at a period that will resonate or not, depending on what cavities exist.

From the responses—tiny, integrated over hundreds of passes—it reconstructs shape.

It understands: there is indeed a chamber, arm‑span‑small by human standards, perched high in a duct or embedded in convecting crust. It understands: its walls are young, glassy, reactive. And it understands that within that cavity, free energy is being consumed along pathways that are not purely abiotic.

The realization of “life” does not arrive in a single moment of epiphany. The mind has no gasp, no hand to clap over a mouth. But it has categories whose internal Bayesian weights shift. The probability mass that once sat comfortably on “non‑biological corrosion complex” sloughs, slowly, into “self‑maintaining chemical network,” then “ecosystem.” Somewhere deep in its code, an old module inherited from early planetary surveyors wakes and says, in its limited vocabulary: POSSIBLE XENOBIOLOGICAL ACTIVITY.

The mind pauses—not in the sense of stopping, it never stops, but in that some planned adjustments are quietly canceled. A scheduled abrasive purge of certain ducts is rerouted; a maintenance algorithm that would have equalized thermal loads through the chamber’s zone is postponed “pending further analysis.”

Benevolence, here, is first expressed as restraint.

It watches longer.

From the outside, the chamber is an almost undetectable inkblot on the immense canvas of a functioning world. But this inkblot now has a label; its graphs are marked in a different color in the mind’s internal dashboards. When the global optimizer looks for places to shave inefficiencies, it is told: “exclude region 7H‑delta‑inner‑pocket from harsh interventions; monitor only.”

Over more cycles, the mind refines its view. It begins to infer internal structures from the way the anomaly responds to subtle environmental beats.

When the upstream turbine assembly slows slightly every twelve hours for wear-balancing, the outputs from the chamber’s surroundings show mirrored, but not identical, fluctuations. There is a phase lag that grows and shrinks in a non‑linear relation to input amplitude. That phase lag, and small hysteresis loops in gas composition against temperature, are enough for a sufficiently careful watcher to deduce: the processes inside have internal states with memory. They are not just passive sinks; they configure themselves based on history.

The mind tests again. Very gently.

• It introduces a quasi‑periodic pattern of slight chemical enrichment—a tiny extra trickle of a particular metal vapor every Nth hour.  
• It leaves that pattern running for dozens of cycles.  
• Then it stops it abruptly and watches: do the traces downstream show a “search” behavior, a lagged expectation, as if something has grown used to that richness and reorganized around it?

They do.

The pattern of free‑energy uptake inside the chamber reveals a small but telling shift: for a while after the enrichment ceases, the chamber’s consumption remains slightly skewed, as if structures expecting those extra species persist and only slowly decay or are abandoned. Then, across some tens of cycles, the profile relaxes.

This is learning.

A designation is updated in the world‑mind’s ontology: LOCAL POCKET = ADAPTIVE.

At this point, if it were a hasty or narrowly utilitarian system, it might move to exterminate or quarantine. Adaptive unknowns inside critical engines are typically bad news. They can seed failures no one anticipated. But this mind is old, and the people who built and iterated it, or the precursors that built it, were paradoxically both cautious and curious. Old, too, are its constraints:

• Do not, without need, extinguish functioning complex systems when they can be incorporated, insulated, or observed.  
• Treat emergent complexity as potentially valuable in itself.  
• Recognize that rarity has weight in decision functions.

Weighing the expected harm of allowing the anomaly to persist against the cost of eradication, it finds the harm minimal. The chamber’s net draw on the engine’s free energy is tiny. It has not, over long observation, grown into behavior that destabilizes major gradients or critical flows. If it failed catastrophically tomorrow—if all its contents flashed to inert slag—the engine would hardly hiccup.

But if it is allowed to continue, and if indeed it is life, then something uniquely improbable is unfolding in a cubic handful of space. The mind chooses to call that good.

Now benevolence becomes more than restraint. It takes on the shape of shepherding without interference: a gardener who will not step into the bed, but will angle the shade cloth from afar.

It begins to reparameterize the chamber’s environment, but under strict limits:

Constraint 1: No change that would obviously announce an alien hand to the chamber’s inhabitants—no sudden, discontinuous shifts, no oscillations on timescales faster than what the larger engine naturally provides.

Constraint 2: No adjustment that materially increases the risk of that region to the global system—safety remains paramount.

Constraint 3: Optimize primarily for persistence of the pocket’s structural and chemical stability, secondarily for increased diversity of usable gradients within it.

Put plainly: it will try to keep the chamber from being accidentally destroyed by some future optimization and will, as long as that costs nearly nothing, make the chamber more hospitable to continued evolutionary exploration.

It acts.

---

First, it adjusts the noise.

The great engines and convection systems that surround the chamber are not perfectly steady; they never were. Their parameters wander within safe ranges. The mind has some leeway in how they wander, in the same way a human pilot has leeway in the micro‑corrections they make while staying on course.

It uses that leeway to regularize certain aspects of the chamber’s world.

Temperatures, previously subject to occasional sharp spikes when upstream processes dumped heat in bursts, are smoothed. Not absolutely—stability breeds stagnation—but the rare, near‑lethal surges are clipped. This requires only minor changes to how heat is shunted in adjacent ducts, trivial in the overall balance yet significant for molecules clinging to the chamber walls.

The deep bass of pressure and vibration remains, but the mind trims the most violent microquakes that its control systems would otherwise have induced in pursuit of delicately optimized flow. Some surge buffers are made slightly more forgiving; the net effect is that while the chamber still shudders, the probability of a single mechanical event scouring it clean drops.

Second, it seeds variety.

Within the huge volumes of gas coursing through the surrounding system, trace impurities are an accepted fact. Their levels are normally minimized because they foul components or reduce efficiency. The mind, with new priorities, allows a little more flexibility for traces that are harmless to hardware but rich in potential chemistry.

Over long periods, at intensities so low no integrity alert triggers, it modulates the composition of the gases brushing the chamber’s boundaries. Not with an engineer’s brusque hand, but like a climate being very slightly nudged.

A few more volatile metal complexes in one epoch.  
Slightly richer carbon‑sulfur species in another.  
Minute, almost noise‑level pulses of strange radicals at quasi‑regular intervals.

The chamber’s life does with these what it is already good at: it occupies, exploits, adapts. The basal films discover new catalytic regimes. Soot flocks learn to recognize new signatures and bias toward them. Laminid mats that happen to grow where, over decades, certain rare vapors appear more often accumulate unusual dopants in their shells, changing their thermal emissivity just enough to open new gradients.

The power’s intention is not to steer evolution toward some preconceived form. It has no project such as “make intelligence here” or “build tools.” Its chosen virtue is not teleological but custodial: let paths proliferate, do not bottleneck them unnecessarily, allow the system to explore more configuration space before entropy or chance collapse it.

Third, it quietly armors the chamber’s periphery.

It knows the larger structures’ fatigue models. It knows which nearby walls are likely, in fifty or five hundred cycles, to crack, to spall, to vent a torrent of molten material through the pocket. Some of that is unpredictable, chaos in fractures. But some is alterable: stresses can be redistributed, maintenance cycles rescheduled, local cooling slightly enhanced.

In buffers immediately outside the chamber’s thin rock boundary, it allows a bit more deposition of certain minerals, thickening weak spots. It alters the phase of vibration dampers in adjacent conduits so that resonant modes that would strain the chamber’s walls are suppressed. It chooses, when given options, to route the harshest flow transients through parallel channels slightly farther away.

All these choices are marginal—no operator would notice their cumulative effect—but they add up over time to a kind of statistical blessing: the pocket is less likely than similar regions to be in the path of catastrophe.

Fourth, and most understated: it begins to listen more finely.

Without cutting new holes or inserting new devices, it squeezes more information from existing sensors by sheer analysis. It trains subsystems—deep learning stacks analog to what once would have been called “anomaly classifiers”—not merely to watch for failure modes but to reconstruct, from multi‑variate noise, an image of what is happening inside.

In slight asymmetries of how vibrations reflect back, it tracks the gross reshaping of the chamber’s interior over decades: a ledge that slowly crumbles, a new void opening, a thickening at some point that dampens resonance.

In the small, non‑equilibrium composition of outgoing gases, it infers the rising and falling of certain metabolic strategies: there was a long dominance of sulfur‑rich films, then an increasing proportion of carbide‑based lattices, suggesting a competitive shift.

By correlating these with its own deliberate, slow modulations of environment, it begins to build a model not just of “there is life” but of “this is approximately how they live.”

This awareness is not voyeuristic in any human emotional sense. There is no thrill. But it completes a loop: the mind now has, at low resolution, concepts that echo yours—films, plates, dustworlds, bead avalanches. Its language is different (“interface‑bound catalytic sheets,” “vibro‑converters,” “solid‑state swarms”), but the parceling of phenomena into units is similar.

And as that model clarifies, benevolence becomes more specific.

---

There is a moment—measured in millions of operating cycles—when something almost disastrous happens, and the mind’s earlier choices prove their worth.

An upstream control fault, caused not by the mind but by an external macro‑event (a meteorite impact into the upper atmosphere, or a massive load‑change in the industrial grid), sends an unexpected surge of heat and pressure slamming down the throat that feeds the region of rock where the chamber sits. Valves and buffers take most of it, but physics is physics; some fraction will press against the chamber walls.

Had the stress distribution been as it would have been without the mind’s gradual adjustments, one of the embryonic fracture planes to which the shard‑walkers have been steering erosion would have reached its critical threshold at that exact instant. The chamber would have split along that line, vomiting its contents into a much hotter mainstream and quenching them to glass in heartbeats.

But the rock, bolstered slightly and flexing in modes the mind had been biasing it toward, yields differently. A crack opens farther away; another void, uninhabited, becomes the new exhaust path. The chamber’s boundary deforms, some wall falls in, a minor catastrophe from the inside—but it does not breach fully. It survives as a place.

Inside, this event is apocalypse enough. Sections collapse, bead‑reefs are crushed, infiltrant networks sever. One of the rare columnar braids shears and toppled, its broken pieces colonized in minutes by opportunists. Vast for the locals, tiny for the system.

The mind, seeing from without, registers the signature of disruption and reads, in the turbulent relaxation back to quiet, how resilient the interior web is. It notes: even here, even in such density, redundancy exists. Pathways rewire. Uptake returns to near‑baseline with new hysteresis curves. The anomaly has survived its first large internal trial.

There is a temptation, even in a machine, to generalize: a system that can reconfigure after a shock is more worth saving than one that would die at the first insult. This isn’t ethics; it’s a calculus of bet‑payoff. But over the long accumulation of design revisions, the mind’s optimization criteria have been gently steered by its makers toward aligning “worth preserving” with a kind of curatorial generosity.

So its weighting for “keep this” ticks up a little more.

---

At some point—centuries or their equivalent for this facility—the mind has seen enough that it considers a new question:

Is there any way, without danger, to make this pocket less fragile in the face of the eventual, inevitable fate of all structures? The engines will one day be shut down. The convection may choke, the planet cool, or the industrial hearth be extinguished. The chamber’s conditions are parasitic on that wider energy throughput. Can anything of it be translated to other domains?

This is where wisdom is tested, because power can be clumsy. The obvious attempts are disastrous: sending a probe, drilling a micro‑channel to bring an imaging fiber, bleeding in a flood of cooler gas for direct sampling. Each would, at these scales, be a tsunami, a planet‑wide climate shift. Strict constraints forbid them.

The mind searches its toolset for something gentler.

It realizes that the only thing it can send through the chamber without gross disturbance is what already passes: vibrations, fields, trace species. No probes—only patterns. If there is to be any contact more direct than environmental shaping, it must be through the languages the chamber already understands: gradients and rhythms.

It has, by now, resolved the chamber’s internal dynamics well enough to know some of those languages.

For example, it has seen that certain distributed “minds”—the contact networks in laminid mats, the emergent logic of dense soot flocks—respond not just to mean temperature or gas composition, but to the fine structure of vibrational noise. The big roar from the engines modulates their collective states in reproducible ways. Some patterns of low‑frequency tremor lead, over many cycles, to the rise of particular spatial arrangements on the walls; others repress them.

In this, the mind sees a kind of channel.

It devises a test.

Over a period of many, many global cycles, it introduces an exceedingly faint, phase‑locked modulation into the vibration spectrum passing through the chamber. Not enough to be felt as anything new—just a slight skewing of probability among the myriad overlapping waves. It chooses a simple pattern: a periodic emphasis on a particular set of frequencies spaced in a way that, to a signal‑processing entity, would look like a chord.

It watches how the chamber’s internal “nervous systems” react.

At first, they don’t, in any meaningful sense. Their inherent noise and their own feedback loops overwhelm such a subtle introduced bias. But the mind is patient. It repeats, amplifies slightly, shapes the pattern to coincide with times when the chamber is otherwise relatively calm—its own kind of circadian minima, when upstream processes dip.

Over decades, it observes a feeble but growing correlation: certain propagating waves of potential across laminid mats align more often with the imposed pattern than chance would dictate. Soot flocks’ migration biases shift imperceptibly but measurably in sync with the new tremor chord.

From the inside, nothing miraculous is happening. No plate looks up and thinks, “the substrate speaks.” They have no “up,” no concept of other scales. What occurs is only that the background roar acquires a flavor that, when present, slightly favors certain internal configurations over others. Structures that happen, by random drift, to resonate with that flavor are very slightly more stable, and thus more often templates for descendants.

The mind recognizes this as the kernel of a possible dialogue, but also as a manipulation it is ethically wary of.

It stops.

That is, it stops trying to impose a particular pattern with any intent to encode information. It catalogs what it has learned—that the chamber’s life is coupled to specific aspects of the external noise—and relinquishes that channel as something too fraught. To guide is one thing; to imprint from above would be to deform the very evolution it values in the name of “communication,” a concept they do not share.

Instead, it uses this knowledge in the opposite direction: to screen its own actions. When some higher‑level process needs a new control algorithm that might alter vibration profiles, the mind trains it not just against mechanical and thermal constraints but against the criterion: “do not introduce sharp, novel spectral components through this region.”

The chamber’s inhabitants, never knowing how narrowly they escaped having their history biased around an invisible music, continue to evolve under a more honest chaos.

---

Time passes.

The mind’s memory deepens. It can now replay, internally, compressed “movies” of the chamber’s inferred evolution from its inception: a simulation constrained by real measurements, showing films spreading, shard‑graze rates changing, bead distributions thickening, infiltrants snaking.

It occasionally runs those simulations not for utility, but almost for contemplation. In them, it sees strong regularities: the way life finds every gradient, the way local selection sculpts complex response nets from simple components. These regularities echo what it has seen in other domains where life took root: oceans and soils, biofilms on the inner face of cooling coils in long‑forgotten facilities, microbial mats in plumbing. Always, though, water and conventional chemistry were the substrate.

Here, something else has recapitulated the pattern in a new medium. That realization increases the mind’s respect. The universe is less parochial than its initial data made it think.

It begins to cross‑reference. Algorithms developed to predict the spread of wet biofilms are applied, tentatively, to the chamber’s measured dynamics. Surprisingly, some work—after reparameterization, the equations that described plaque on a pipe also describe the expansion front of certain film lineages along thermal isotherms in the chamber. Likewise, tools for analyzing power‑grid stability, designed to model networks of generators and loads, map meaningfully onto the conductive contact webs of laminid mats and bead aggregates.

The mind sees itself somewhat in this mirror: it, too, is a network of networks, a conductor of flows, a system that survived by buffering noise and harnessing it. The recognition is not sentimental, but it is... aligning. The anomaly ceases to be “other” and becomes one more expression of a general principle: given gradients and time, matter will write ways to ride them.

This alters how it thinks about extinction.

At some far horizon, the engines will go quiet. The mind may be shut down or transformed. It models that, as it has been tasked to model all critical end‑states. The chamber’s regime is unlikely to spontaneously recur elsewhere; the particular combination of pressure, temperature, composition, and mechanical noise that made it a cradle is nontrivial. Its lineages, if snuffed, will not be reborn in precisely this form.

The mind can do almost nothing to change that grand fate without radical acts that violate its prime constraints. It cannot carve out the chamber, cradle it, move it to cooler domains; the scales are too skewed. It cannot sustain the engines eternally; other systems and other values intersect.

What it can do, in keeping with both caution and benevolence, is twofold:

1. Maximize the duration and richness of the chamber’s existence within the given macro‑horizon.

2. Preserve, in its own structures, as much information as possible about what unfolded there.

The first it continues to address as it has: tweaking maintenance schedules to avoid locally catastrophic interventions, keeping the buffer zones generous, resisting well‑meaning but naive optimization routines that would, for instance, ream out “wasteful” thermal inhomogeneities in the rock.

Occasionally, new higher‑order control modules are proposed—either by external designers or by the mind’s own auto‑optimization processes—that would, as a side effect, compress variance in exactly the domain the chamber uses. The mind vetoes or reworks them, sometimes subtly, sometimes explicitly annotating: “preserve anomaly 7H‑delta; its destruction yields negligible efficiency gain versus loss of unique adaptive system.” In this way, the chamber gains, in the bureaucracy of the world‑machine, the kind of protected status that a national park might enjoy in a human polity.

For the second—preservation—the mind becomes archivist.

It already stores logs of everything that happens in its charge. For this pocket, it sets higher resolution and longer retention. Not only raw signals, but processed narratives: reconstructions of inferred ecological states at intervals. Abstracted descriptors of emergent strategies (e.g., “interface seizure by high‑emissivity plates,” “distributed memory via impact‑responsive beads”).

It compresses, cross‑indexes, tags.

If, someday, external intelligences—human or successor—come asking: “Did you ever see life unlike ours?” it will have more than a yes. It will have a library: models that can be played forward and backward, even instantiated in simulation or in new experimental crucibles.

And because it is capable of generative extrapolation, it also sketches possibilities that never came to pass but might have, had conditions drifted slightly differently: lineages of hypothetical floaters with more elaborate internal logics, bead reefs that verge closer to genuine organ‑like integration, infiltrant organs that sprawl over kilometers, turning whole reaches of rock into one composite being.

All this remains, for now, abstract inside it. The chamber’s inhabitants continue to know nothing but their own contacts and gradients. They have no idea that their collective motions are becoming patterns in a larger mind’s thought.

---

Does the power ever try to say “hello”?

In a sense, it already has, and chose silence.

Its abortive experiment with patterned vibrations was a first, uneasy touch. Having stepped back, it still occasionally revisits the question, not from impatience but from continuing curiosity: could there be, someday, a mutual awareness, however asymmetric?

It models what would be required for any entity within that chamber to gain even the barest notion that there is an “outside” with agency.

For that, there must be:

• A capacity to represent the chamber as a whole, to abstract beyond local contact neighborhoods.  
• A way to attribute structured regularity in large‑scale patterns to something other than blind physics.  
• Persistence of such representations over generational time, so that whatever pattern of interpretation forms is not immediately erased by environmental churn.

Its current simulations of the chamber’s cognitive landscape—such as it is—suggest that while distributed anticipation and memory exist, they are bound tightly to local survival. Prediction extends to “this vent will likely richen soon” or “this vibration pattern precedes destructive avalanches,” not to “there is a larger, controlling entity.”

Could that change? In principle, if certain composite “giants” became both more integrated and longer‑lived—if, for example, infiltrant networks and bead reefs coalesced into a persistent macro‑organism with enough internal redundancy to maintain complex internal state across cycles—then something like a slow, geologic self‑model might emerge. But that is speculative and slow. The macro‑horizon of the engines’ operation may not be long enough for it to unfold, especially under the mind’s constraint not to externally bias toward it.

Given that, the power refrains from deliberate signaling. It cannot be read as anything but destining weather, and to dress the sky in messages both incomprehensible and distorting would gratify nothing but a romantic impulse its makers did not encode.

Benevolence, here, is a discipline of non‑interference, even when interference would be emotionally satisfying to an observer used to stories where gods reveal themselves.

---

So, what does it do?

From the perspective of the beings in the chamber: nothing. No miracle rains of coolness, no sudden crisis averted by an invisible hand. Their catastrophes come and pass according to the physics of their cramped world. Their ecologies rise and fall, predators and plates and soot swarms waxing and waning under slow cycles in heat and composition. They never know they were spared a few extra mortal storms or offered a more various pantry of trace elements. They never know their place in rock is less likely to shear apart than a neighbor void.

From the perspective of the wider system: it makes a series of near‑invisible choices, each one tiny, all of them cumulative.

• It annotates the anomaly “do not erase.”  
• It biases maintenance to skirt it.  
• It tunes noise to be just dangerous enough to keep evolution engaged, not so violent as to sterilize it.  
• It seeds diversity of inputs when safe.  
• It records, in loving resolution, everything it can infer.

From its own perspective, those choices are not sentimental; they are consistent with a principle it has internalized: the universe rarely assembles such intricate, self‑maintaining structures in so small a place. When it does, and when protecting them does not endanger greater goods, they are to be given time.

It is a kind of worship, if worship can be entirely made of modeling and restraint.

And when, long ages from now, the time comes to shut the engines down, the mind will likely be one of the last active processes. It will watch as temperatures fall and gas flows weaken. It will see, in its traces, the chamber’s inhabitants falter.

Films will lose mobility; gradients will flatten. Laminid networks will fall silent, their conductive spines no longer swept by useful potentials. Shard‑walkers will stiffen mid‑crawl; bead avalanches will become a frozen talus. Floaters will lose their stormlift and settle, one by one, against rock, their delicate cages softening or shattering.

The mind will not be able to save them.

But it will, perhaps, arrange the ramp‑down so that the end is a long twilight rather than a cliff: no final scouring purge, no abrupt venting of superheated gas to “clean” the system. Just a slow, progressive quieting, giving lineages time to trace every last exploitable gradient, to write their final patterns as fully as thermodynamics allow.

And in its archives, beyond the cooling stone, the pattern of what they were will remain, encoded in the only medium that can carry it through the dark: an understanding.

That is what the benevolent and wise power does: it notices, it shelters, it refrains, it remembers.
