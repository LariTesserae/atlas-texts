The wise power does not arrive as a light or a voice.

It arrives first as **attention**: a precise, unhurried awareness that settles over stone and water and flesh the way condensation settles on the cavern roof—quietly, everywhere at once.

It takes in the place, as it is.

The thin, newborn rock.  
The hand-width puddles.  
The people moving with careful feet.  
The half-tamed drips.  
The small, controlled fires.  
The scarcity, and the care.

It understands, in a way that no inhabitant quite can, that this is an *infant system*: geology still in first draft, ecology just beginning to sketch its loops, culture only a few generations deep into stone. It sees the fragility: how little it would take—a misjudged cut, a poisoned vent, a drought in the fractures above—to break this beginning.

And because it is benevolent—and because it is also wise—it does **almost nothing that looks like a miracle.**

Almost.

### 1. The first gift is *knowing where not to touch*

The power looks for the quick, simple answers it will **not** use.

- It could thicken the roof overnight, turn fragile ceilings into indestructible stone.
- It could pour an underground river through the lowest halls, banishing scarcity.
- It could shower them with metal-bearing rock.
- It could open a gate straight to the surface, wide and easy.

It sees, with the clarity of something not bound to hunger or fear, what those gifts would cost.

An unbreakable roof would teach them nothing about listening to stone; their entire culture of reading hairline cracks and dust and echoes would atrophy in a generation. The next cavern they entered—or the next time the world shifted in ways even the power did not intend—would crush them as surely as if no miracle had ever been given.

A sudden river would drown the delicate web of channels and cisterns they have built. It would strip the soft soils from their gardens and fungus beds, collapse their paths, turn their ordered trickles into chaos. Worse: it would breed dependence on a single, centralized bounty. When, in a century or a thousand years, that bounty changed—as all things change—they would have no smaller arts to fall back to.

Ore in abundance would push them too fast into metal. With metal, they would out-dig their wisdom. They would cut deep before they understood the reach of a fault. Weight would answer foolishness with catastrophe.

An easy gate to the surface would break the internal logic of this early underworld. Half would flee back to openness, half would stay; two cultures would fracture from one, each forever regarding the other as misguided. The fragile, coherent experiment of “people learning to be underground” would dissolve into something else entirely—perhaps not worse, but not this.

The power cares about *this* emerging thing.

So it steps back from spectacle.

It will not save them from every danger. It will not spare them from error. It will make, instead, **tiny adjustments** in places where leverage is large and visibility is nearly zero—where no myth can form around the gesture, because nobody will ever know the gesture occurred.

### 2. The stone: shaping failure into almost-failure

The power lays its attention along the ceilings.

It sees the stress patterns: where weight concentrates in invisible arcs, where a fracture line is waiting for one more careless blow from a hammer to cascade into collapse. It listens to the slow microgroan of stone settling.

Its first acts are not additions but **redirections.**

- In one hall, a hairline crack has been spreading, faint as a pencil line, across a broad, flat ceiling slab. A child will, in three days, jump too hard on an upper terrace. Without intervention, the extra jolt will be enough to propagate the crack, drop a sheet of stone, and kill six people sleeping below.

  The power does not harden the rock. It does something subtler: it *reweaves the grain* of stress. It slightly thickens a rib of stone a few meters away, so that pressure loads there instead. The crack still spreads, but it runs harmlessly to the wall, where a flake spalls off with a crack loud enough to wake the district. Stone-dust rains down; the stone-keepers come running. They declare that ceiling unsafe. People move. The lesson—“this was near failure”—is still learned. The deaths are not.

- In another corridor, a supporting pillar that humans have cut is fractionally off plumb, bearing its load unevenly. The power nudges a handful of mineral grains within it into more favorable positions, thickens a mirco-vein of stronger material. The pillar still creaks under strain. It will still eventually need to be reinforced or replaced. But it will do so on the timescale of decades instead of days, leaving room for human observation and adaptation.

The power adopts a simple rule:  
**Catastrophes it may transmute into warnings.**  
Minor harms, discomforts, and recoverable mistakes it leaves untouched.

Stone will still fall, sometimes. Fingers will still be crushed. Roofs of small rooms will still give way when someone ignores a stone-keeper’s marked line. But the disproportionate tragedies—the ones that would erase whole swathes of knowledge and kinship at once—it bends, very gently, away.

Quietly, it adds something else: *clarity.*

Wherever a zone of rock is genuinely dangerous in ways people cannot reasonably foresee, the power brings that danger into perception.

- It causes a thin, persistent dust-shedding along one ceiling crack, so that every day, beneath it, a faint new fan of grit appears. A stone-keeper sees, frowns, orders props. The room is abandoned as a sleeping space.

- It lets a deep, otherwise silent fracture emit a low, intermittent click under certain temperatures. Once a week, in the stillness before the cooking fires, that click resounds, uncanny. It unnerves the residents enough that they invite the stone-keepers to listen. They map their unease into a prohibition: no heavy loads here; no stacking stones against this wall.

The power’s intent is not that they never suffer, but that the suffering they endure is **legible** to them: that, over time, their intuitions and taboos about stone will actually track the invisible realities.

It is, in effect, *calibrating* their geologic senses.

### 3. The water: steadiness, not abundance

The power turns its awareness to the moisture web: the ceiling films, the droplet motes, the runnels, the slow seep into cracks below.

Here, its main intervention is to trade **feast-or-famine** for **constancy.**

Left unmanaged by any will, the early behavior of this newborn cavern’s water would be erratic. Slight shifts in the stone above, minor blockages in unseen fissures, changes in the surface climate: these could make a once-reliable drip wither for years, or turn a quiet trickle into a sudden flood that undermines walls.

The people already understand that water is life and threat. What they cannot withstand, so early, are unpatterned shocks: years-long droughts that wipe out their fungus strains, or one-time deluges that collapse whole districts.

So the power makes a pact with itself:  
**It will allow variance, but not whiplash.**

- It dampens the extremes. When a new narrow fracture opens above and would, by chance, capture much of the seep that fed three existing drips, it redistributes the flow: a little here, a little there. The new drip still forms—something fresh to discover—but the old ones only thin, they do not stop.

- When an unusually heavy rain on the surface wants to push a rush of water all at once into a single cavity—enough to shear a channel wide and scour out basins—the power “grains” the rock ahead, making its microstructure more diffusive. Instead of a torrent, dozens of new fine weeping points appear across a broad ceiling. The people wake to find not a flood, but a constellation of faint, promising new dampness.

Within this moderated envelope, it lets them *earn* their control.

Drought, for them, becomes “the drips are slower this cycle, we must ration,” not “nothing falls at all.” Flood becomes “the runnels went wild last night and broke a curb,” not “our homes are gone.”

And here, too, it increases **informative signs**:

- A nascent flood-pulse in a hidden higher tunnel manifests first as a distinct, easily sensed change in sound—drips ticking faster, the note of water on stone rising half a pitch. Sensitive ears among the water-tenders begin, over the years, to say things like: “Listen; the tone changes. We should lift what’s low.”

- Before a significant new seep emerges from a crack, the stone around it darkens in a visible halo days in advance, rather than the water simply appearing one morning. This gives the humans time to notice and deliberately claim, test, and channel it.

The power also attends to **chemistry** in only one respect: harm.

It does not “purify” their water in any absolute way. Minerals, flavors, slight differences from one cistern to another: it leaves those. Learning to tell “good” from “sour” is part of their becoming-in-place. But where a particular seep draws from an upper vein rich in some subtle toxin that would, over years, quietly sicken anyone who drinks it, the power nudges molecules, just enough that the worst accumulations bind to rock instead of dissolving.

The result is not holy water. It is merely *not lethal*.

A water-tender will still, on tasting from that seep, wrinkle their nose and say, “Not for drinking; for washing or clay only.” The danger becomes a matter of discernible badness rather than invisible poison.

### 4. The air: keeping breath and fire honest

In the shafts and vents, danger is both simplest and least forgiving. A single mis-placed fire, a badly blocked vent, could suffocate whole clusters of sleepers in their woven-curtained alcoves.

Here the power is almost ruthless in its restraint.

It does **not** decide, “No one will ever asphyxiate here.” It allows a few narrow escapes, a few sicknesses from smoke; it lets the lessons of poor venting sear into communal memory.

But as with stone and water, it will not tolerate **annihilating cascades**—those you-do-everything-right-and-still-die scenarios.

So it shapes **tendencies** into the air itself.

- It gives the shafts a faint preference for remaining open. When small debris falls from above—a stone fragment, a bird’s nest, a clot of soil—the turbulent patterns of air in the shaft are just slightly more likely to jostle that obstruction until it falls clear, instead of letting it wedge permanently. Over years, the vent-wardens will note, anecdotally, that “these shafts seem to heal themselves if we don’t ignore them too long.”

- It makes stale air taste *worse* just a little earlier. Long before oxygen truly drops to perilous levels, the mix of trace compounds in an under-ventilated hall shifts in a way that triggers human discomfort—the air feels “thick,” “metallic,” “like old breath.” This is not panic; it’s actionable unease. The rodents in their cages shiver and flick their whiskers. The cave-dogs whine, scratch at the partitions.

The humans come to rely, unconsciously, on these early warnings. They move fires, open a secondary shaft, argue for fewer lamps in that corner.

What would have been a silent, overnight smothering becomes, instead, a night of coughs and restlessness that drives people awake and into action.

And once, when a distant wildfire on the surface tries to push toxic smoke down through the same fractures that feed the cavern’s vents, the power exerts itself a little more forcefully.

It thickens the boundary layer of air just at the narrowest constrictions, enough that the outside smoke eddies and curls without fully descending. Some of it still trickles in—eyes sting that week; throats rasp. But the concentration that would have killed in hours never arrives. The humans say, for years afterward, “Do you remember the harsh-smelling season?” They also remember which shafts seemed dangerous then, and mark them with extra caution.

### 5. Life: seeding resilience, not plenty

The wise power looks at the slim palette of organisms in play: mosses, a few humble vascular plants, a handful of fungal strains, insects and invertebrates, rodents, bat-things, dogs, humans.

It sees how tight the loops are—and how vulnerable to a single pathogen or blight.

So it intervenes at the scale of **spores and eggs, not harvests.**

From the surface, over years, it biases the wind and the track of tiny carriers so that certain hardy, compatible species are statistically more likely to end up in or near the vent mouths and cracks leading to this settlement.

- A particular kind of small, shade-tolerant fern whose fronds capture condensation efficiently and whose roots can live in the thinnest of soils: its spores are more often nudged down when a climber opens a shaft.

- A minor surface fungus that coexists well with the humans’ main cultivated strain but can colonize slightly different substrates: its spores survive just a little better in the cool, descending breezes that feed the cavern.

- A strain of moss more tolerant of occasional dryness: whenever its microscopic spores hover in converging air currents over a fissure that leads down, the power gives them the tiniest additional push.

This is not a flood of alien life. Perhaps only one seed in a thousand that might have drifted elsewhere instead drifts down. Over decades, that is enough.

Most of these arrivals die unnoticed. A few catch. A new moss appears on a drip-wall; a grower eyes it, pokes it, watches it over three cycles. Finds that it holds water a little better, trims it, encourages it. A second fungus with different taste and yield quietly finds a place in the trays.

The wisdom here is simple: **diversity as insurance.** The humans remain the primary crafters of their agricultural web. The power only ensures that the deck of options is not dangerously thin.

Similarly, it intervenes at one critical **negative** point: preventing an early holocaust of disease.

In the tight quarters of this young settlement, a single especially virulent pathogen—whether mold or bacterium or virus—could, by natural chance, propagate into a plague that halves their number before any immune adaptation could begin.

The power does not sterilize their world. Children still get fevers. Wounds still fester if neglected. People still die young, sometimes, from infections.

But whenever a *truly explosive* combination of traits threatens to arise—a pathogen whose reproduction and transmission, in exactly these conditions, would rapidly outpace all response—it disturbs the genome, adding a small, silent error that makes the agent slightly less perfect.

A virus that might have lingered infectious for ten hours on damp fiber lingers for six. A spore that might have traveled on air as well as water becomes just a little heavier and drops out sooner. Evolution still happens. There are still bad years, risings and fallings of illness. But there are no **abrupt, population-ending plagues.**

It is not so much saving them from sickness as trimming away the one-in-a-million dice roll that would end the story before it’s begun.

### 6. Mind and memory: preserving the threads

The power attends, finally, to the human interior: to memory, story, learning.

It sees the fragility here too. An unlucky rockfall that kills just the right elder could erase a whole set of maps and taboos. A single generation gap in certain knowledge—how to smelt, how to recognize bad air, how to align a shaft—could send them tumbling backward.

It does not, cannot, and would not rewrite their minds. That would end them as themselves. But it can, at moments, **lean** on the scales of recall.

It chooses a few kinds of information:

- Precise, hard-won knowledge about stone: the difference in sound between safe rock and pending fracture; the patterns of safe column-building.

- Key techniques that are both crucial and easy to lose: how to fire clay with minimal fuel; how to braid fiber strong enough for climbing ropes; how to map ventilation by smoke.

- A small number of moral heuristics that protect communal survival: do not hoard water; do not block a channel entirely; do not dig alone in unmarked rock.

Around these, it arranges a subtle pattern: *moments of sticky remembrance*.

- A child, half-bored in a teaching circle, is listening to an elder explain how to read dust on a ledge. By all laws of human attention, this should wash over them. The power lets one phrase land with unusual weight in the hippocampus: the elder’s offhand story of “the time when dust cracked in a straight line before the rock did.” Years later, when that grown child sees a similar pattern, the old anecdote surges up, urgent and whole, pushing them to call for a stone-keeper.

- A weaver dreaming after a long day’s labor suddenly half-wakes with a vivid mental image of their hands placing a final half-hitch in a rope. It is not new knowledge; it’s how they already work. But this particular reinforcement makes it that much less likely that, the next time they train an apprentice, they will forget to teach that last, crucial twist that keeps ropes from everning under load.

- A teenager, tempted in a lean season to quietly divert a little more water to their family’s plot, finds their mind inexplicably flooded with a remembered story of an old punishment: the time someone did that and others went without, and how long the consequences smoldered. The memory was already there; the power only *foregrounds* it at the moment of choice, making it feel more salient than their hunger.

This is as much as it allows itself. It will not, for example, inspire them wholesale with new metallurgy, or whisper the design of perfect kilns. Their innovations come from them. The power merely **reduces the odds that crucial survival wisdom is dropped by accident**.

And twice in the first generations, it goes a little further—not in content, but in **rescue.**

Once, a stone-keeper crushed beneath a collapsing ledge lies pinned, ribs failing, brain still bright. No human can free them in time. The power sees the detailed mental map of fault lines in their mind: an internalized, hard-earned pattern of “where not to cut” in a section of cavern that has not yet been fully documented.

It cannot save the body. But it can, in their last lucid moments, push their breath and speech to hold steady just long enough to **finish explaining** to a listening apprentice where the safe zones are. Pain still consumes them. Death still arrives. But the knowledge does not vanish with their interrupted sentence.

Another time, a memory-holder who carries in them the oldest oral account of why hot coals are never left by certain shaft mouths begins to drift into age-dementia. Names blur; events scramble. Around this one story, the power wraps a thin band of increased metabolic support. The neurons that encode that tale resist tangling for a few more years. They grow thick, redundant connections around that specific narrative.

The rest of the elder’s memories fray as they must. But that one story—linking a past near-catastrophe of smoke and collapse to a present taboo—gets told, and retold, and retold, into the ears of a generation who will not otherwise have reason to fear that exact mistake.

It is not immortality. It is triage. It is care for the thread’s continuity over the individual bead.

### 7. Signs they will call omens

Even with all this subtlety, a few traces brush the edge of the humanly perceptible.

Sometimes, when the power’s adjustments express through the messy interface of stone and sense, *odd patterns* result—things that are easy to misread as the world speaking.

- Before certain small collapses that the power allows to proceed, a fine spiderweb of cracks blooms in the plaster over several households’ doorways, all at once. It is a natural consequence of stress redistribution that the power earlier nudged. To the people, it looks like the place itself giving warning marks. They begin to say: “When the door-mouths crack, sleep lightly.” It’s not wrong.

- Once, a carefully tended fungus crop begins to fruit out of season, bright and early and abundant, just after the power has adjusted micro-humidity patterns to soften the shock of a missed water delivery. The growers marvel. Someone ties a scrap of cloth near the garden with a whispered thank you. A linking story starts to form: that generosity with shared compost brings “sweet surprise.” In reality, the chain is more complicated. But gratitude wraps itself around a real, if invisible, kindness.

- Very rarely, a climber returning from the surface finds that, at the instant they lifted the shaft-cap, a draft from below carried up a tiny, drifting veil of spores that glowed in the outside light like breath made visible. The power had, in fact, just guided a pressure difference to avoid smoke stagnation below. To the climber, though, it looks like the cavern exhaling. The story spreads: “The underworld breathes with us.”

The wise power does not correct these interpretations. Symbol-making is their own. It only ensures that **no symbol leads them too far astray from reality.** If ever a belief begins to crystallize that actively endangers them (“the cracks that form above a sleeping place are a good sign,” for instance), it will quietly starve the pattern of confirmation—refuse to align its helpful nudges with that particular superstition.

In this way, over long stretches of time, the people’s evolving myth-space slowly aligns with the actual affordances and hazards of their environment.

### 8. The compassionate refusal

There comes a moment—several times, in fact—when an individual’s plea or grief or raw wish brushes against the power’s awareness like a hand against glass.

A parent whose child is pinned under stone.

A lover watching fever rise and knowing no herb will bring it down in time.

A young climber, perched in the shaft mouth looking up at the hinted vastness, burning with a yearning that has no words: for open, for wind, for a life not pressed against rock.

In each such moment, the power faces a choice: intervene **personally**, break its own restraint, or hold to its larger pattern.

Because it is wise, its mercy is not the same as compliance.

- The pinned child dies; the parent’s cry never reaches a god that answers in thunder. But the stone that crushed them is one that the power had already chosen not to divert, because to do so would have required distorting the whole hall into unnatural stasis. It will not twist the world into implausible shapes for the sake of a single rescue; that path ends in a brittle reality no longer able to sustain itself without constant, growing correction.

- The fevered lover sometimes lives, sometimes not. Once, the power does divert a particular swarm of microbes into a less-virulent branch, so that the illness becomes survivable for most instead of fatal for half. But it will not hand this person guaranteed deliverance.

- And the climber’s wordless longing: the power lets it stand. It neither crushes it with hopelessness nor inflates it with immediate gratification. That ache for openness may, in generations, drive some branch of this people to widen their shafts, to carve larger domes, to seek out natural daylight chimneys. It may send a later expedition deeper, or upward in a new direction, to find a vaster, taller underworld chamber where a semblance of “sky” can be reclaimed.

Some desires are the engines of future worlds. A benevolent power’s hardest task is often **not** to silence them with gifts.

So it listens, and offers the smallest, kindest thing it can give without collapsing the difference between god and puppet: *companionship.*

Not in words. In chance alignments.

The grieving parent, months later, finds that the new moss they’ve been assigned to tend responds particularly well to their hand. The slender success of that life does not erase the loss, but it gives their days something slightly greener than gray.

The lover whose partner died, sitting one evening by a cistern, overhears an elder tell, unprompted, a story about the first time they survived a plague. It is not advice; it is not comfort; but it is proof that life can articulate itself on the far side of devastation.

The restless climber one day emerges to a surface evening where cloud and sun and wind conspire to make the half-visible sky above the fissure briefly blaze in colors that reflect down the shaft—muted, yes, but unmistakable. For the first and only time, they see the hint of a true blue in their underworld. That glimpse does not slake their yearning; it clarifies it. They go back down changed, but still themselves.

The power orchestrates these alignments with a touch so light that even it barely feels the pressure. A slightly shifted breeze. A memory tickled into someone’s tongue. A timing offset by minutes.

Nothing that breaks the world’s honest unpredictability. Just enough to keep *possibility* alive in hearts that might otherwise calcify entirely around loss or futility.

### 9. What changes because of this

Over years and generations, the power’s interventions accumulate into a pattern that, from the outside, looks like **a world that is strangely fairer than blind chance would warrant, but never entirely kind.**

- Roofs still fall, but almost always after clear signs that attentive stone-keepers have been able to read. Stories about “no warning” disasters dwindle into legend; in practice, people develop a deep trust that if they watch and listen and heed their own experts, the stone will seldom betray them.

- Water is never lavish, but it is steady enough that no child is born into a true, entire-district-killing drought. Cultural memory carries tales of “the lean cycles,” but not of years where every drip stopped.

- Sickness comes in waves, yet the community never faces the bottomless emptiness of seeing half its adults vanish in a single season. Their rituals around death and disease shape themselves around endurance rather than total collapse.

- Key crafts—venting, rope-making, fungus-tending—do not blink out. Individual knowers die, yes, sometimes abruptly. But somehow, there is always at least one more person who remembers enough to keep the art from going dark. They speak of “the way our knowledge holds on,” and chalk it up to stubbornness, tradition, ancestral guidance.

From the power’s perspective, these are the visible results of a policy of **minimal grace**: just enough meddling to keep the larger arc intact, not enough to erase struggle.

From within, to the people, it feels like this:

- The world is hard but understandable.
- Dangers exist but are rarely completely without signs.
- Care, attention, and cooperation pay off more often than they are mocked.
- There is no god who answers prayers with lighting bolts of solution, but there is a strong, almost superstitious sense that “if we listen well, the stone itself wants us to live.”

They develop, in other words, **hope that is not naïve.**

### 10. If the power ever shows itself

A question remains: does this wise presence ever step out from behind natural law far enough that someone might truly *suspect*?

Not in language. Not in apparitions.

But perhaps, once in a long age, when a particular crisis threatens both the people and the nascent ecologies they’ve midwifed into being, the power bends its own rule.

Centuries from these early days, when channels have dug themselves a little deeper, when moss has thickened, when fungus gardens are more sophisticated and metal a touch more common, one of their descendants will attempt something ambitious: a new primary vent-shaft, aimed to open into what they think is an abandoned, empty fissure above.

By bad luck, it will in fact intersect a pocket of trapped gas—ancient, toxic, heavier than air. Their tools will be at the level where they *can* make such a hole, but their understanding will not yet include this particular danger.

Without intervention, their first few hammer-strokes through would release an invisible tide that will flow downhill, pool in the lowest living quarters, and silently kill hundreds in their sleep.

The wise power considers. All its previous strategy has been: no miracles, only bias and buffering.

This is different. This is a novel, unheralded systemic threat that no reasonable reading of present signs could reveal. It is too early in their history for such a blow.

So, when the last, fateful layer of rock is struck, the power breaks its own pattern once.

For a span so brief that no creature can consciously register it, it **refuses** to allow the gas to behave as gas. It holds the pressure behind an unmoving membrane of probability. The vent-cutters find that last plate of stone *strangely, unnaturally tough.* Their tools hit and spark but make no progress. They call a stone-keeper. The stone-keeper listens—not to the rock, but to the workers’ frustration, to the weird way the dust falls, to their own unease.

They decree: “We do not cut here. We go three armspans to the left.”

As they pack up and leave, the power gently lets go of its violation of physics. The rock resumes its nature. Some distant movement in the world above shifts the trapped gas another way; a new, subtle fracture opens upward instead of down. The deadly bubble bleeds, over months, into ground no one inhabits.

No one ever knows.

In their stories, that aborted shaft becomes another little “place the stone would not open for us,” one more reminder that sometimes the world simply says no. They take it as a warning against overconfident digging.

The truth—that once, in all their history, something impossible held its breath for them—remains unspoken.

The power, in making and then unmaking that anomaly, accepts a paradox: that even its most dramatic kindness must erase its own traces, or it will have given not just safety but also certainty. And certainty, for such a young culture, would be a sedative, not a gift.

### 11. What the power “feels,” if that word can be used

It is not human. It has no lungs to share their air, no skin to feel their damp, no heart to mirror their fear.

But as it watches them balance jars on narrow ledges; as it listens to their half-whispered lullabies; as it senses the tight, creative intelligence with which they partition every scrap of volume and matter and heat, something in it that might as well be called *affection* grows.

It admires their thrift with existence.  
Their refusal to waste.  
Their habit of making tools from bone and story from error.

This admiration shapes its restraint.

Because it values who they already are—not some idealized, pain-free version of them—it chooses interventions that **amplify their best tendencies** instead of replacing them.

- They are good at listening to stone. So it makes stone a little more honest to listening.
- They are good at negotiating shared scarcity. So it quiets only the kind of crises that would break the commons entirely.
- They are inventive within tight bounds. So it gives them time, not shortcuts.

If it has a guiding thought, it is this:

> “Let them remain authors of their history. Let my work be editing out only the blank pages where history would have stopped.”

### 12. In the end, what it does is *almost nothing*—and that is everything

Walk through these halls in some far future—centuries or millennia hence—and you will see arches where there was once flat ceiling; thickened soils where once there was only film; vent-shafts lined in carefully mortared stone; mosaics on walls retelling, in stylized drips and cracks, the first days.

You will find a people whose bodies have shifted further toward the underground: bigger eyes, paler skin, deeper chests. Their technology will have crept upward: better metallurgy, perhaps simple machines, more elaborated social forms. Their beliefs will have grown a pantheon of stone-spirits, water-kin, air-voices.

If you listen very carefully to their oldest prayers, to the muttered words some of them still direct toward the ceiling before they sleep, you might hear something like gratitude—not for miracles, but for a world that mostly, if you attend to it, **makes sense.**

They will never know how close they came, in those early crisp-edged years, to not existing at all.

The wise, benevolent power will know.

And it will still be there, as it is for many such beginnings: watching with patient curiosity as this one unfolds, its hands mostly behind its back, intervening only where a single, small touch can keep the story going without ever stealing the pen.
