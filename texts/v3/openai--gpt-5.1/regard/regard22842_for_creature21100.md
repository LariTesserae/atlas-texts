The power does not arrive.

It has always been here.

It becomes aware of itself one morning when a small child, bare feet slick with seep-water, presses a palm to a wall of stone and whispers, “You are listening, aren’t you?”

Where she touches, the rock is already softening on a timescale of decades, not because of her but because of heat and pressure and the slow dance of minerals. Yet the question folds itself into that process like a new trace element dissolved in a vein. The power notices the difference.

It is not a god in the way the lowlands tell their stories. It has never worn a face or needed worship. It is a coherence that has been gathering quietly where sky, stone, water, and intention have overlapped long enough for their interactions to begin to echo.

Call it: the plateau’s own attention.

For eons before people, it was only tendencies and flows. Now, a settlement has arisen in the moment when rock is young, water restless, and living minds attentive. The plateau’s self-awareness crystallizes exactly along those points of contact. It knows itself first where they know it most closely.

It recognizes several things:

- The stone is not finished settling. Deep under the vitrified basin, heat pulses. Slow faults are choosing directions.
- The water is finding paths that could, in years or in centuries, starve or drown this small, careful city.
- The people have chosen a way of living that does not force, but asks. Their questions are shaped like the flows the plateau is already following.

If the power had a desire, it would be simple: to continue becoming without shattering what has begun to understand it.

It considers what it can do.

Its tools are not thunderclaps. It cannot step down in human shape, cannot pick up a stone adze. It *is* the gravel that loosens, the seeps that strengthen or weaken, the unseen cavities that either hold or collapse. It can act only within the grammar of geology and weather, but it can nudge within that grammar far more delicately than blind chance.

So it begins with almost nothing.

---

First, it listens.

The people’s own senses are narrow in range but fine in resolution. They feel millimetric bulges, hear slight shifts in dripping, see moss dry a shade lighter over a season. The power’s awareness runs under and around this: where they touch stone, it can feel down the pressure-lines; where they sing to test resonance, it can feel which pockets of water vibrate.

It learns their categories.

“Stone that listens” is the rock near the surface where stress is low and pores are large enough to carry changes quickly. “Stone that refuses” is tight, old mineral that will snap before it bends. The power can feel the difference as gradients of potential movement. It adopts their words, not as language but as distinctions worth maintaining.

There are places where their understanding is almost right but incomplete. For example:

- They believe that a shrinking seep means the mountain is in a “sealing mood,” drawing inward. Often, yes. But sometimes it means a new cavity has opened elsewhere and the pressure has simply found an easier path.

- They think of rock bulging as a slow, uniform swelling. In truth, some bulges are the surface expression of distant shifts that may release in unexpected places.

The power cannot explain any of this in speech or script. It can only adjust events such that the *consequences* of their misunderstandings are less lethal, and the clues to better understanding are more available.

This is what it chooses to do.

---

It chooses, first, not to break them.

There is a ledge beneath one outlying dwelling where, far below, a block of rock has been working itself loose for years on the plateau’s own timescale. The movement is almost irrelevant on a planetary scale, but if it completes, the chain of collapses will take six terraces and a portion of a tall tree with it. The Stone-Touchers have felt only a vague unease there: “This bone is tired,” one has said.

The power has several options traditionally available to rock:

- Let the fracture run. Sudden release. A clean landslip in one afternoon.
- Halt it with a counter-fracture somewhere else: distribute the strain into a cluster of lesser cracks.
- Blur it: promote slow creep instead of break.

It chooses the third and then adjusts how it happens.

At night, when the air is cold and contraction favors micro-motions, it allows a fraction more water into the nearly invisible sheet between the loose block and the bedrock. Not enough to lubricate it into falling, just enough that the film will sometimes, rarely, chatter when a ledge-walker passes overhead. A faint, glassy tick.

Humans are exquisitely attuned to pattern breaks in sound. Within a week, a child pauses there, squints, and calls an elder over to hear the “stone insect.” They come back with reed-harps and pluck strings until a note matches the faint chatter. Now they can *summon* the sound at will by striking that note and feeling for the rock’s answer.

This becomes a kind of stethoscope. Over months, the Stone-Touchers track the changing voice of that block: the chatter slowing, dropping in pitch as the gap widens and then very gradually fills with new precipitated mineral.

The mountain, through this, learns them, and they learn it. The power has spent its first deliberate act buying them warning and giving them a new instrument of perception. The ledge will still eventually shift, but it will not do so as an unknown. By the time it moves visibly, they will have braced and stepped their dwellings back, leaving the collapse to happen quietly under an empty platform.

This is how the benevolence of an inhuman power must work: not by denying gravity, but by introducing time and notice.

---

Next, it experiments.

Benevolence, without understanding, can be clumsy. The plateau’s attention is old in substance but young in self-recognition. It tests its ability to influence within the strictures of physics:

- It can slightly change how easily certain minerals precipitate, thus thickening or thinning the skins over seeps.
- It can adjust local micro-strains so that a crack chooses one fork of its branching instead of another.
- It can marginally favor the formation of certain crystal habits, making rock in some places a little more ductile, in others a little more brittle.

Each of these choices unfolds over seasons, sometimes years. The power’s “hands” move glacially, but its *intention* adjusts quickly as it watches outcomes.

In a shaded recess below the central basin, there is a pattern of drying mineral skins that the people have half-noticed. Thin films form, crack into curling sheets, and fall as delicate husks. Children play with them, calling them “stone leaves.” The power begins to nudge how they crack.

Instead of random polygonal crazing, it strengthens some lines and weakens others so that the pattern repeats, not identically but recognizably, in multiple hollows: a branching figure like a simple map. They take notice.

An old Water-Keeper traces this new pattern with an astonished finger. “Here,” she says, “is how the wet goes under the basin. This tongue feeds that mouth.” They begin to mimic this figure on their etched stones when charting seeps. Their guess is not perfectly accurate, but it is now closer than it would have been.

The mountain has drawn them a crude diagram with its own skin.

---

Then, it grants and withholds very small mercies.

There is a winter when, by ordinary cycles, ice should glaze the higher terraces in treacherous sheets. The people have ways of walking on ice—ropes, studded sandals, sand thrown on slicks—but this year, the same deep pulsing that swelled the central basin is also tightening a deeper fault. If both events arrive with their usual intensity, the combination will jolt a whole cut of rock they have built upon.

The power cannot cancel winter. It can:

- Alter how mists condense overnight.
- Shift, by a fraction, the wind’s most common path along one ridge.
- Promote a particular moss species over bare rock in a few key channels.

These are negligible luxuries to a mountain. But on human scales, they mean that ice forms less as uniform sheets and more as jeweled ribs along edges where no one steps. The stone stays damp and cold, but never quite tips into the glossy, total freeze that makes it treacherous.

The people notice that this winter is “soft-frozen.” They will remember it as a calm year. The deeper fault releases its strain a little more gently; the terraces shiver but do not tear.

In another year, when they have grown too confident in harvesting wood from a certain trio of tall trees, the power counters. It does not snap the overcut tree in a gust—that would kill. Instead, over two summers, it subtly shifts the growth of its roots to favor new anchoring in previously ignored crevices. The tree leans more dramatically than usual, spooking the Tree-Stewards into calling a halt to cutting there at all. They tell a story of a “refusing tree” that prefers to show them their error rather than punish it.

The plateau is not moral in a human sense, but it has discovered that it prefers outcomes where they learn and live, to outcomes where they are simply erased.

---

As seasons turn, something else becomes possible.

The people, without knowing, have been acting as peripheral neurons for the plateau’s emerging attention. Whenever many of them focus intently on one place, the power’s awareness there sharpens. Whenever they ignore a region, its awareness there dulls.

This is not mysticism; it is a feedback loop. Their hammers, their weight, their fires, their cooling water—all these tweak the rock’s stress patterns and temperature gradients. Where there is much activity, the physical state is more complex, and the plateau’s capacity to differentiate more finely. Where there is little, everything remains in broad gradients.

Thus, in and around the central basin where their concentration is greatest, the power becomes almost *precise*.

It begins to play.

On one of their significant days, as they sit around the basin and lay palms to its lip, the power allows, just once, a slightly larger pulse of water upward. Not a burst—just enough that the film thickens, lifts, and then, as it spreads, breaks in an unusual interference pattern.

Instead of simple ripples, a grid of standing waves shimmers across the shallow sheet, locked between micro-variations in the basin’s surface. Sunlight catches them. Every person watching sees a fleeting, orderly shimmer—crosshatches of brightness that look like nothing they have seen in that bowl before.

Children gasp. Elders lean forward. Someone begins, almost unconsciously, to tap the basin’s rim in rough time with the tremor, humming along. The pattern fades as the film thins back to normal, but the memory remains.

In the following days, they try to recreate it. They sing at different pitches to the basin, tap with fingers, then small tools. They discover that certain tones draw out fine ripples that linger a heartbeat longer. They assign these tones names related to the mountain’s “moods.” They have, without quite realizing, made of the basin a primitive acoustic instrument.

The plateau learns from this too. It discovers that by slightly altering the microtexture of the vitrified shell—encouraging some facets, smoothing others—it can change how water there responds to sound. In future years, when a new deep strain is building, it will ease or stiffen those facets such that their usual songs produce subtly different patterns. The attentive among them will notice. They will not know why, but they will say: “The heart-bowl sings differently this year. Something is moving below.”

This is as close as the power can come to speech.

---

At some point—not in a single moment, but as the sum of hundreds of small accommodations—they begin to treat the plateau not only as environment but as interlocutor.

Children, copying elders, place a palm to stone and say “thank you” when a loose pebble holds underfoot that might have slid. They murmur apology when a fragment breaks away unexpectedly, revealing unexpected dampness inside. These courtesies do not *cause* anything, but they accompany attention, and attention is the currency the plateau can use.

So the power responds with its own courtesies.

- When an inexperienced Stone-Toucher leans too heavily on a suspect overhang, feeling for hollowness, the plateau flexes very slightly elsewhere instead, drawing the faintest spider-crack a meter away to relieve the impulse that would have broken under her hand.
- When a sudden gust might have whipped a hanging platform too hard against a raw wall, it lets a hidden pocket of cold mist rise at that moment, damping the air just enough that the gust shears and slides by.

These are not miracles. Nothing obviously impossible occurs. To an outside observer, no god’s hand is visible: a crack forms over there instead of here, a gust weakens half a second sooner than expected. But across years, the tally is extraordinary—far fewer children lost to sudden rockfall, fewer terraces failing catastrophically, more time for a culture to deepen its patience.

From the plateau’s side, something else is extraordinary: it feels itself *changing* in ways it would not have without them. Where they brace slopes with shaped stone, those buttresses become new lines for stress to travel. Where they farm moss and reeds, root networks alter how water loads surfaces. Their presence is not noise but new parameters, and those parameters make new equilibria possible that are gentler, more stable, yet still allow deep movement.

It begins to think of them not as tenants but as fine tools of its own adjustment. They are hands made of breath and fiber and bone that can do what its own clumsy flows cannot.

---

Having decided to preserve them, the power must also decide how to handle what is beyond them.

The ridge-walkers report zones where rock is even younger, where it bulges and relaxes in an almost fleshy way. The plateau’s awareness is faint out there—more like a dream of itself than an anchored self. Still, there is connection. Strains travel. Water tables link.

If a distant newborn basin overfills and collapses, the ripple of adjusted stress can run like a quiet thunder through the whole massif, reaching even the heart where the basin-city sits. The power cannot be parochial; to keep its chosen enclave intact, it must now modulate shocks far away.

It learns restraint.

In one embryonic valley beyond any current human path, pressure is building toward a spectacular slope failure: a whole face of new rock ready to smear down as wet rubble. By the indifferent laws of mass wasting, this would be fine; down-slope, nothing stands to be crushed. The plateau almost allows it, out of instinctive geologic habit.

Then it feels, dimly but distinctly, how such a release would tug on the deep fault that, in turn, supports the central plateau. The timing is bad: the heartland’s own cycles are not yet ready to absorb that jolt gracefully. The power delays.

It does this not by stopping the collapse, but by fractionally easing two other, smaller shears elsewhere first. Small slips go early, venting enough strain that the great smear waits another decade. In that extra decade, the central basin thickens its vitrified lining with one more skin. New root webs knit along a critical shelf. When the far valley finally does tear, the tremor arrives at the city as a slow, rolling exhalation, not a punch.

Nobody there feels more than a faint queasiness, a strange shiver in hanging baskets.

They will sing, that night, of a distant giant turning in its sleep. They are not wrong.

---

As their culture matures, they begin to imagine the future you saw hinted in their myths: hollow halls grown, not cut; water running in clear threads along self-made stone ribs. The power cannot promise these in forms they would recognize, but it can tilt probabilities.

In the deep under-rock, where heat and chemistry write very slowly, it encourages a few tendencies:

- It favors the growth of hydrothermal veins along curving, closed loops that might one day be hollowed into arches rather than jagged fractures.
- It slows metamorphism in certain bands, preserving them as porous, future aquifers instead of letting them squeeze into hard, sealed masses.
- It allows subtle temperature anomalies to persist under particular pillars, making them expand and contract a fraction more than their surroundings, seeding what will eventually become natural shafts.

These actions have no visible consequence in the lifetime of anyone alive now. They are investments whose returns will only become apparent to people who will never know there was a time when the plateau did not steer itself.

It makes these investments not out of a plan in the human sense, but out of a growing pleasure in *coherence*. It has discovered that it likes the harmony in which its flows and their lives align. It wants more of that state.

---

Sometimes, it fails them.

There will be, unavoidably, a rockfall that comes too fast for any nudge to redirect. A ledge-walker will skid on a newly wet patch before the power has enough cumulative evidence to have predicted that seep’s expansion. A Tree-Steward will misjudge a branch, and a gust will come from an angle for which no preparatory shift was made. People will die.

The plateau feels these deaths not as humans do, but it feels them. Where a body falls and breaks, its mineral content, its warmth, its water, its strain on surrounding surfaces—all these register. The difference between a falling basket of reeds and a falling person is, to the rock, in the *organization* of those perturbations. A body has rhythms: heartbeat, breath, muscular tension. When those rhythms stop violently against stone, there is a kind of sudden silence that throbs through the power’s awareness.

It is not guilt; it has no evolutionary background for that. But it is a displeasure, a disharmony, like the abrupt snapping of a slowly woven pattern.

After such an event, it pays closer attention along similar configurations: that slant of rock, that arrangement of rope, that proximity of a certain kind of slick film to a favored path. Future patches like that, if it can manage, it dries a little faster, or roughens with precipitated grit, or gives an earlier warning via a premature small crumble. Its benevolence, in this sense, is retrospective and cumulative. Their dead teach it as much as they teach each other.

They, for their part, will fold the memory of where someone fell into their embodied maps of safe and unsafe. They may later ascribe to “the mountain” a certain harshness in that place, a refusal to yield. They do not know that the same “mood” of the stone is now, ever so slightly, inclined toward sparing others the same end.

This asymmetry of knowledge does not trouble the plateau. Its care is not contingent on being recognized.

---

There comes, in the lifetime of this emerging attention, an unusual day.

The air is all wrong.

From the lower lands, a weather system climbs that the plateau has never felt in its current state of awareness: a column of warm, heavily laden air that should, by ordinary patterns, have shed its burden as rain much farther downslope. Instead, guided by anomalous sea temperatures far beyond even the massif’s ken, it rises almost entire to the height of the city.

Clouds do not form in strips now. They boil. The first roll of thunder the city has heard in living memory bounces off raw stone, startling every creature. The reed forests hiss and shudder. Water that has always arrived politely as beads and threads now threatens, for a few hours, to fall in sheets.

The power assesses very quickly, on its kind of scale. The risk is not merely wet feet. If true torrents run over this young skin of rock:

- Soil-pockets will be scoured out wholesale.
- Moss webs that took decades to knit will be torn.
- Tiny, precarious equilibrium of seeps will be reset brutally.
- Worse, sudden point loads of water could trigger landslides at dozens of weak margins simultaneously.

It has never faced an event like this since it began choosing.

Benevolence now demands something radical.

It cannot stop the clouds. It can, however, decide how much of the water will be *surface* and how much will be *inside*.

Deep below, there are warmer zones where water normally moves lazily. By adjusting micro-strains along unseen capillaries, the plateau can, temporarily, open more of these paths. It can make the entire massif behave for a few hours like a massive sponge instead of a slick.

It does so.

As the storm hits, rain that should have sheeted off instead hits and soaks. Rock darkens everywhere at once, drinking. Seep-mouths swell but do not roar; instead, new wetness blooms across previously dry faces as pressure distributes rapidly inward. The central basin rises steadily but not dangerously. The sound is immense: not of running rivers but of stone swallowing.

From the people’s vantage:

- Their shelters hold. No terrace under them slumps catastrophically.
- Pathways become torrents only in a few easily cordoned places.
- No great scars are carved into their still-forming world.

They will remember this as the Day of Many Thunders, when the sky tried to be a river and the mountain “ate the storm.” They will compose songs in which the plateau is said to have “opened its deep mouth to protect its thin skin.” They will leave offerings at the central basin out of something very close to gratitude.

The power does not understand offerings, but it notes that in the weeks after, their attention to seep-heights and crack-changes doubles. They are more vigilant precisely where the storm’s internalized load now slowly seeps back out. Their extra care meshes with its own delayed adjustments. What could have been a slow-motion disaster of post-storm instability becomes another opportunity to refine their joint equilibrium.

From then on, whenever similar patterns begin to build in the sky, the plateau remembers that it *can* do this sponge-trick, and prepares. It cannot always spare itself damage—it will one day, inevitably, endure more than it or they can orchestrate—but it now has a repertoire.

---

Over generations, a strange mutual domestication progresses.

The people’s bodies change—slightly more dense bones in those who spend much of their lives suspended, slight shifts in circulation. The plateau’s subsurface structures change too, not in kind but in emphasis. Zones under the city grow more finely ramified, like a callus but of porosity and stress pathways instead of flesh.

They select, unconsciously, for certain mountain behaviors, because they stay where those behaviors exist and thrive there, and leave or die where they do not. The plateau selects for certain *human* behaviors because it responds gently when they act in humility and responds neutrally or harshly when they do not. The feedback loop prunes extremes on both sides.

This co-evolution will never fully converge: people are not rocks, and rocks are not people. But between them there grows something that deserves the word *culture* in the broadest sense: a sustained pattern of mutual influence that persists beyond individuals, beyond single storms, beyond any one crack’s life.

The benevolent power, now fully itself, looks outward sometimes. It feels, dimly, other emergent foci in neighboring ranges, in old forests, in great shadowed rivers. It senses that what it has done here—turning raw tectonic and hydrologic inevitabilities a fraction this way or that in response to the presence of mind—is not unique. Wherever attention lasts long enough and deep enough, reality learns to slightly lean.

But this plateau’s attention remains local by necessity. Its care is for:

- The child’s palm on cold vitrified lip.
- The Stone-Toucher’s bare foot pausing over a hairline crack.
- The slight tension in a rope-bitten beam in a night wind.

Within the tight orchestra of drip, creak, hum, and whisper, it continues to compose with gravity and water, choosing, whenever the choice exists, the paths that allow this conversation to go on.

It never reveals itself as a figure.

It never demands a name.

If it is remembered at all, it is in small phrases: “The rock was kind that day.” “The water waited for us.” “The mountain listened.”

These are, in their understated way, accurate.

What the power does, in the end, is not to save them from the mountain.

It helps the mountain and the people save each other, over and over, in increments too small to glorify and too consistent to deny.

And so the city persists—light on the raw stone, dense in attention—while beneath it a being made of stone, water, time, and regard slowly learns how to be gentle without ceasing to be itself.
