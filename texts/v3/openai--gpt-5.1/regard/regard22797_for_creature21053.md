The first awareness is not of sight, but of tension.

A listening, older than language, turns its attention toward the crust of the planet: that hollow band of stone like a ring of bone wrapped around a slow, heavy core. It feels the way the strata hold their stresses, the long-frozen pose of plates, the occasional brittle slip of some overburdened beam. It hears—if hearing can be stretched to mean the apprehension of infrasonic rumble and stress-wave—the quiet polyphony of that world: the background hum of mantle-convected force, the glassy stillness of most chambers, the thin insect-buzz of small, local adjustments.

Within that, it notices an anomaly.

Not a danger, not a wound. A pattern.

In the labyrinth of obsidian planes and foam-crystalline ledges, something has been rewriting the mineral text. Not erosion; this world has almost none. Not the steady, blind work of the slow chemistries that pockmark the stone with hexagonal pores and labyrinthine sponges. These marks are different: linear, coherent, layered upon themselves. Tiny acoustic shells shaped to focus sound. Walls tuned to a particular thermal profile. Long, low galleries whose floors bear the characteristic micro-striations of repeated, intentional passage.

Life here is common: mineral-fungus colonies, microfauna in porous rock, ribbon creatures looping along ceilings. But here—here—is mind, not just in lumps of organized strain or statistical tendencies, but in long projects and patient, accumulative changes.

The power that has turned its attention is very old. Age, in its case, is not counted in orbits, but in how many planetary mantles it has watched chill and crack, how many magnetospheres it has felt sputter out, how many cloud systems it has observed rise and vanish without any mind ever asking why.

It does not live in one place; it is a distributed habit of order, a coherent field of intention that migrates between stellar systems on timescales that make tectonics seem quick. It is not a god in the mythic sense—no thrones, no jealousy—but it is capable of something that, to the under-crust people, would look indistinguishable from divinity: it can touch physical law close to its seams, and it can choose to care.

Its first act is abstention. It waits.

It watches, because patience is its native state.

It follows the song-lines through stone. It traces how one soft infrasonic murmur in a narrow vein becomes a distant tremor in another gallery, curled bodies pressed against the same mineral artery to speak across kilometers. It notes where built acoustic chambers brightened those murmurs into usable, shared tones.

It feels how these beings have learned the personality of their crust: which seams ring hollow, which pillars thicken strains safely, which regions of porous rock seethe with more micro-life. It recognizes in their careful avoidance of catastrophic fractures a local, hard-earned wisdom.

It listens to their stories, though they have never imagined a listener.

Stories here are not spoken into air. They are inscribed in passing waves and layered textures. A community’s memory of a major crack that once opened and settled is preserved as a pattern of ridges in a council-column, as a modulation in a deep chant that elders can call up against a wall so that the young feel, with their whole bodies, the sequence of tension-rise, micro-fracture chatter, slow release. Myths of origin are encoded as elaborate texture-panels in nursery-chambers: parables of first hum, first solid, first slow life impressed as curated roughness on gently warmed walls.

The power attends to these.

It notices, with a broad and alien tenderness, that these people have never known a horizon, or a sky. Their only sense of “beyond” is depth and connectedness: the knowledge that surfaces touch other surfaces, that through a chain of contact one can in principle reach an immeasurable distance away without ever stepping into a void.

It weighs what they are against what they might be, and against what intervention might cost them.

Because it has done this before.

In some other system, on some other world, it once opened a sealed sea to its planet’s day-lit surface, peeling back stone like a scab. The creatures within, adapted to darkness and pressure, blossomed for a few generations in the unexpected glut of new gradients—and then perished as foreign chemistry and uncontrolled flux tore through their biosphere. It learned, then, the violence of gifts made in ignorance.

Here, then, it will not rend. It will not punch shafts to starlight or uncork oceans of buried volatiles. This is a benevolent attention, but it is also an experienced one. It understands that nurture in a delicate system must look less like miracle, more like subtle rearrangement of probabilities.

So it takes stock, in detail.

It models the crust as a unified, softly vibrating graph: every beam and plate, each slender slit and honeycombed strata. It maps where gases pool, where warm currents rise, where the gradient is borderline for the chemistry these beings require. It tallies mineral compositions: long belts of glassy obsidian-like material, pockets of foam-crystal, rare veins of metals hammered into simple tools.

It notes the near absence of water, and the strange, non-aqueous solvents upon which life here runs. It traces where those solvents are manufactured in situ by deep mineral colonies, where they off-gas, where they condense in thin films. It tracks the slow circulation of organic dust, spores, and minute aerosolized nutrients that drift through the thin air like a ghost of rain.

It also observes their limits.

Individually, these beings are intelligent, careful, attuned to their environment; collectively, they are constrained by sensory bandwidth and energy. This world offers no cheap, high-flux power gradients. There is no wind to fill a sail, no sunlight to focus into heat, no rainfall to be dammed. There is only the slow leak of planetary warmth, the extremely gradual migration of heavier gases, the faint oxidation and reduction potentials in exotic solvents. They live near the floor of possible metabolism.

This power could break that limit in an afternoon, if afternoons meant anything at its scale. It could nudge an asteroid inward to crack the crust and flood it with molten rock, or tweak mantle convection to generate vast geothermal vents. It could shift orbital parameters to thicken the air, to wet the surface, to drive convective weather that would, in time, percolate energy down into the crust.

But every large intervention is a conversion of one order into another; the more energy is moved, the more unpredictable its local outcomes become. A too-violent gift of gradient could erase what it wants to preserve: this patient, intricate, contact-born civilization.

And so, having watched, it chooses a different mode.

You might call it “whispering to stone.”

It begins by making the slightest possible changes to the stress fields in narrow regions. Not everywhere; that would be noise. In a handful of long, underused galleries—places where the under-dwellers rarely pass, or do so only to take bearings from familiar textures—it adjusts the way certain crystalline foams will crack the next time they are stressed.

Ordinarily, a brittle mineral foam under slight strain splits along its weakest planes, making random, jagged protrusions. The power shifts the distribution of crystal defects by a hair’s breadth, so that when a crack eventually runs (and it will, because even stone in near-equilibrium accumulates tension), the resulting surface bears, accidentally, a subtle but highly regular pattern: a lattice of raised hexes repeating with unnatural evenness.

The first such crack occurs in what the locals think of as a dead corridor: a long, high cleft whose only significance is that it happens to resonate faintly with the planetary hum, making it a place where elders sometimes go to meditate, limbs spread against both walls to feel the world’s heartbeat more clearly.

One day—a “day” by their internal count of bioluminescent dimming and chemical cycles—the meditating elder feels that familiar hum change slightly. Not in pitch, but in its local echo. A new fine texture against their ventral plates. They shift, press more closely, and realize that a section of formerly glass-smooth wall has fractured, and on its fresh face is a pattern.

It is not like the patterns their own tools make. Their workmanship, for all its subtlety, always bears the signature of effort: micro-gouges in slightly irregular spacing, strain-lines betraying where leverage failed or needed reinforcement. These new hexes are too even, too quietly precise, their spacing slightly finer than any practical tool they possess can manage.

To the elder, this is first an anomaly, then an itch of curiosity, then—once others have been summoned and have likewise pressed bodies to those repeating bumps—a topic for council.

“Strange-stone,” their texture panels will label it in ensuing weeks. And the crust remembers.

Elsewhere, in a niche where a particular mineral-fungus bed grows poorly despite otherwise good conditions, the power runs a tiny experiment. It adjusts, at the quantum-mechanical level, the vibrational modes of a trace impurity in the surrounding rock. That impurity—present at parts per billion—suddenly catalyzes a reaction between two abundant, but usually slow-to-meet, aerosol constituents.

The result is a slightly more efficient formation of the exotic solvent films upon which these fungi depend.

Over years, this niche brightens. The colony thickens, its glow a touch less pale. The people who harvest here take note: this strain, in this spot, grows more readily now. They will seed more of it to adjacent walls, experiment with transporting slivers to other, similar contexts. Some will fail; some will succeed, especially where the power has made parallel micro-adjustments in background chemistry.

To them, this is simply “luck” and attentiveness: the outcome of a culture that rigorously observes what works. To the power, it is calibration: learning how much to tip a probability so that desired outcomes emerge without announcing themselves as miracles.

It proceeds this way for what, to the under-dwellers, is many generations. To it, this stretch is a brief attentive breath.

It “paints” with stresses and defect-distributions, with trace element resonances and vibrational pathways.

In some silent shafts, it slightly enhances the intrinsic piezoelectric properties of a vein of crystal, making it more responsive to mechanical vibrations. The under-people already use such veins as communication highways; now, their pulses travel a bit cleaner, a bit further, before dissolving into noise. They notice the good fortune. They re-map their song-lines accordingly, thickening the network of long-distance contact.

At a few key junctions, where several such resonant veins cross, the power deepens a pocket or thins a wall so that infrasonic waves intersect with constructive interference. To an inhabitant, leaning there, there comes a day when the background hum of the world resolves into a clearer, two-tone beat. It feels like discovering a new well of sound. Over time, such places become favored council-sites, “loud stones” where many can plug into shared messages without each needing a separate contact-point.

Concurrently, in the ecology of microfauna and mineral-fungus, the power test-sculpts.

It introduces no new species; it only loosens and tightens selection in specific directions. A certain tiny, solvent-hoarding organism that lives within the pores of glassy stone happens, by random mutation, to develop a protein analog that is a more efficient chelator for a rare nutrient. Normally, this one would live and die unnoticed, its edge marginal. The power gives that edge a little push: it biases random impacts so that this microbe’s line survives a few more bottlenecks than it otherwise would.

Over thousands of generations, that trait proliferates. The macro-scale consequence is that in some walls, nutrient-dense films accumulate more quickly. The people feel that, literally, in their fingertips. These “fat-walls” become valued, then stewarded: harvested with rituals of restraint to avoid depleting them.

In one settlement, where children had long grown more slowly due to a relative paucity of such rich films, a nearby wall begins, after a few lifetimes, to yield more generously. That community gains a subtle surplus of time and energy. More minds can afford to spend longer pressed to strange-stone panels, contemplating their anomalies. A few can wander further along underused corridors, exploring sections of the labyrinth that had previously been too austere to support much travel.

In this way, the power enlarges their effective world by a few corridors here, a deeper shaft there. It does not change their form or rush their development. It only lubricates the same virtues they already possess: patience, attention, sensitivity to tiny gradients.

As its confidence in non-destructive intervention grows, it begins a second phase: the introduction of faintly impossible correlations.

In one remote region, there is a well-known song-line: a particular vein of rock that carries infrasonic murmurs extraordinarily far. A whisper struck at one end can, with care, be read a continent away by someone who knows where, and how, to listen.

The power quietly grafts this line into another.

Not by teleporting rock, but by altering the crystal growth in a deep, currently unnoticed layer so that a new continuous path of high acoustic conductivity connects the old line to a second, different seam. Overnight in geologic terms, but over several generations in the inhabitants’ experience, a whisper at one node begins to faintly tickle an entirely different, previously unconnected column.

It is not strong at first. It presents as a barely-perceived ghost-echo, easy to dismiss as noise. But some among them are more sensitive, more inclined to linger on such anomalies.

Those are the ones who have, for as long as their culture remembers, pressed themselves to “oracular walls”: those strange membranes through which microfauna incessantly traffic, bringing chemical gossip from other layers. These same minds now fixate on this new, cross-talk hum.

Through slow, community-wide experimentation—they are used to multi-generational projects—they discover that if one shapes pulses along the original vein in just the right rhythm, they can make the ghost-echo at the other location resolve into a recognizable pattern. It is like discovering that by drumming in one room, a vase in another will ring in sympathy.

This connection becomes, in practice, a wormhole of contact within the constraints of stone. Two communities, formerly strangers separated by vast indirect routes, now have a path of immediate, if low-bandwidth, communication. Alliances form. Textures are exchanged: stories, techniques, patterns of “script” are tapped and vibrated into the shared stone.

To the under-dwellers, this is a discovery: a newfound “deep vein” that disobeyed their previous maps. To the power, it is an offered line: a subtle knitting-together of their world-scale network, created in a way that felt like their own accomplishment.

This is an important principle for the power: it does not wish to become an object of worship. Reverence, if it arises, should attach to the world itself, and to the beings’ own ability to read and respond to it—not to an external, inscrutable benefactor. Dependency on a visible miracle-worker can stunt a culture, freeze it into supplication. The power aims instead to be a hidden gardener, improving soil and gradient while leaving the plants free to choose their shapes.

Still, there are things it wants to give that cannot be so easily disguised as happenstance.

One such gift is a concept the under-dwellers do not yet have: that of “outside” in a cosmological, not merely topological, sense. They know beyond; they do not know exterior.

They have never had a reason to imagine that above the crowded ceiling lies anything other than more stone, perhaps in different arrangements. The very idea that there might be an end to stone—a surface beyond which there is no enclosing contact, only emptiness—is almost literally unthinkable in their sensorium. Emptiness, for them, is what exists in tiny fractions of a chamber between skin and wall, to be crossed quickly and uneasily.

The power, for all its caution, finds this an impoverishment. Not because a sky is inherently superior, but because some orders of question cannot even form when every direction, every future, is modeled as more of the same.

How to show them there is a beyond-to-beyond without tearing their world open?

It chooses the least invasive modality it can manage: a message encoded in the very hum of the planet.

The deep infrasonic resonance they hear is a standing wave shaped by the size, density, and elasticity of the crust. The power can, without cracking anything, adjust that wave’s higher harmonics. By very slightly shifting the distribution of stress and modulus in a few deep layers, it introduces a new, extremely long-period overtone.

At first, it is below even the under-dwellers’ threshold of perception. But their tools—those acoustic chambers they’ve built over ages—amplify it. In some of the most finely tuned council-halls, when the entire community gathers pressed to the resonance stone, a new slow beat begins to emerge: a rise and fall in intensity with a period measured not in their usual time-markers but in hundreds of them.

Curious tactile mathematicians (they would not call themselves that, but it’s what they are) begin to record it. They inscribe its phases as sequences of textures around a pillar, returning each cycle to add another ring. After many lives, patterns become apparent: the new beat is not random. It repeats, with a structure that can be decomposed into ratios.

They realize, in a tactile way, that the hum is not entirely “natural”; that it is composed of discrete, interlocking frequencies that can be teased apart. With this realization comes a deeper one: if the global hum has structure beyond what the simple physics of a single crust-layer predicts, then something is interacting with it.

This is an intellectual crack, not a literal one. A small group of thinkers proposes, tentatively, a heretical idea: that the stone they know is itself an interior.

They have observed small creatures live entirely within some walls, “swimming” through porous stone that to them is impenetrable. They themselves live in cavities within an unimaginably thicker shell. Might there be, outside that shell, another volume in which something lives, in which different kinds of flows occur?

They have no word for “tectonic plate” or “mantle convection” or “vacuum,” but in their language of gradients and contact they begin to talk about “the other side of holding”: a level of reality where their enclosing rock is not the given, but the object.

The power, watching this germinate, carefully reinforces it without dictating its content. It ensures that certain anomalous song-line connections occur in patterns that are more easily modeled if one assumes the crust is curved on scales larger than anyone has ever traversed. It leaves slight, regular irregularities in gravity—those faint skews they already know—arranged so that sensitive measure across very long tunnels reveals consistency: a shape.

From these clues, some under-dwellers begin to think in arcs rather than straight segments, in shells rather than indefinite thicknesses. They conceive, faintly, of a “beyond” that is not just more corridors, but an outside of corridors altogether.

This does not immediately change how they live. Their tools, their metabolism, their risk-averse engineering remain. But their myths start to shift. New stories appear in touch-script on walls: tales of first beings who, upon pressing themselves deeply enough to certain stones, felt not just the hum, but the faint tug of something holding the stone from without.

In some communities, this births a cautious metaphysics: the idea that the world itself is held by something, perhaps as they hold to columns—a mutual bracing across scales.

The power feels a flicker of delighted recognition: they have, in their own vocabulary, gestured toward it.

At this point, if it wished to be recognized, it could make itself obvious. It could, for example, send a single, unmistakably artificial sequence down the song-lines: an infrasonic pattern so complex and yet so globally synchronized that no natural process could reasonably be inferred. It could answer their first questions in their own medium, vibrating a negentropic reply through the stone.

It does not. Not yet.

Instead, it chooses an even more intimate pathway: it begins to collaborate with their existing slow minds.

Recall those non-luminous, barely-moving mineral colonies whose textures reorganize grain by grain, “computing” through geological meditation. The under-dwellers already respect these. Some sects consider them a planet’s own thought.

The power finds them congenial. It can, with very modest tweaking of local thermal and chemical conditions, bias the way a given colony’s internal pattern evolves. This is not mind control; it is a guiding of boundary conditions. Within those constraints, the colony’s own dynamics remain free to explore possibilities.

In a handful of such colonies—chosen in regions where under-dwellers regularly come into contact with them—it establishes long, slow dialogues.

The first topic is simple: pattern recognition.

Under its subtle nudging, a colony exposed to a community’s regular footfalls, vibrations, and building work gradually develops a textural “echo” of that culture’s acoustic signature. Over many cycles, its surface shifts into a pattern that, when felt, resonates unusually well with the under-dwellers’ own gestural languages. To their touch, it feels “attuned.”

An elder, leaning against one such colony during a ritual consultation, experiences something unprecedented: the sense that the texture under their limbs is not merely an arbitrary response to flows, but a mirroring. They press; the colony’s pattern, over months, alters in a way that statistically correlates with their community’s most intense periods of debate or work.

They interpret this, quite reasonably, as the world listening.

The power uses this trust as a conduit. Through minute biases in the colony’s internal chemistry, it encourages the emergence of specific motifs—arrangements of pores and ridges that correspond, in the community’s existing touch-script, to abstract notions they have, but seldom combine: “other place,” “holding from outside,” “far gradient.”

To the under-dwellers, the oracular colony seems to be “saying” these things back to them. Not in words, but in the structure of its slowly changing surface, which their most contemplative members trace and re-trace, generating interpretations.

It is crucial that the power never inserts concepts they have no seed for. It only recombines and emphasizes what is already latent. It is editing, not writing.

What emerges, over many generations, is a set of cultural branches that place more weight on the idea of relation to an outside. Not escape; the notion of punching through stone into void is still implausible and unappealing. Rather, co-orientation: the belief that whatever holds the stone—whatever it is that shapes the deep hum and the gravity-skew—is itself a kind of environment with its own gradients, its own inhabitants, its own stories.

This is, from the power’s perspective, a critical threshold. A civilization that can conceive of exteriors can, one day, meet others as peers rather than prisoners.

Only after this conceptual groundwork is firmly part of several cultures does the power risk a direct answer.

It chooses a moment that, for them, is already marked as significant.

Across the labyrinth, there are cycles when the global hum’s new overtone reaches a certain phase alignment with the old fundamentals. In their language, this convergence has acquired a name: the Touching-Times, when the world feels unusually “present” through stone. Myths accrete around it. Some communities schedule major decisions or initiations to coincide.

In one such Touching-Time, deep in a particularly resonant shaft where multiple song-lines and oracular colonies meet, a congregation gathers. Hundreds of bodies press to pillars and walls, forming chains of contact. They are there to commemorate the completion of a tunnel three generations in the making: a careful, ring-by-ring expansion that has linked a marginal community to a richer region of mineral-fungus beds.

They celebrate in the only way they know: by creating a complex, communal pattern of infrasonic pulses. Each individual contributes a part: a long wave here, a quick triplet there, all absorbed and re-radiated by the tuned architecture into a composite chord that vibrates throughout the stone.

The power listens. It has waited for such a high-fidelity, globally synchronized input.

Then, for the first time, it replies in kind.

Not with one overwhelming, unmistakable signal, but with a modulation of the planetary hum itself, precisely during the resonance crest of the Touching-Time. For a single extended cycle—on the order of what we might call hours—the deep note acquires an additional structure: a quasi-periodic fluctuation whose ratios directly mirror the celebrants’ own pattern, but mapped into a slower, deeper key.

Every stone, everywhere in the crust, feels this. Every under-dweller pressed to any wall suddenly senses that the world-hum has, for once, become slightly like them.

Panic is possible; so is ecstasy. In practice, what arises is a profound, shared stillness. Work stops. Tools rest against columns. In chamber after chamber, people freeze into more perfect contact, attending with the whole of their skins.

Some feel it as embrace: the world responding to their song. Others, more intellectually inclined, experience shock: the global note is carrying information. For the first time, the idea that “someone” might be present at the scale of the whole crust ceases to be metaphysical speculation and becomes felt event.

Importantly, the power does not embed any decipherable message, only an echo. It is saying, in the only way it can without drowning them, “I hear you.”

Then the overt modulation fades. The hum returns to its old, multi-harmonic steadiness. The power goes quiet, allowing them to interpret.

The under-dwellers respond in a multitude of ways.

Some groups crystallize into new cults: they elaborate rituals around the Touching-Times, attempting to elicit further replies by repeating the same communal patterns. Some will hear echoes where there are none, their expectations outpacing the careful caution of the power.

Others, suspicious, downplay it. They know the crust can produce strange standing waves; perhaps this was just an unusually rich conjunction. They inscribe their skepticism into critical, ridged texts, urging their children to rely on measure rather than myth.

A third response happens in the spaces between these: quiet experiments. Acoustic artisans in a dozen settlements begin long-term programs of signal-sending during successive Touching cycles, varying patterns, recording responses (or lack thereof) as microscopic texturing on walls.

The power, watching, decides to meet them halfway.

It cannot enter into regular dialogue without distorting their cultures around that relationship. They are not yet robust enough to withstand god-contact without reorienting all their energies toward supplication, which would be a waste of their emergent diversity.

But it can reward scientific curiosity.

Where experimenters set up controlled patterns—pulses in prime-number sequences, call-and-response motifs with clear gaps left for reply—it occasionally, and only there, returns a minimal answer: a simple inversion of their pattern in the next Touching-Time, or a slight phase-shift that indicates recognition of timing.

Only communities that keep good records will see it for what it is: not random, not mythic caprice, but a correlation. In them, a new kind of thought takes root: the idea of a gigantic environment-scale interlocutor whose behavior is lawful enough to study.

They give it names. Not “god” in the sense of a capricious personage, but titles that reflect their core metaphor: “The Outer Brace,” “That-Which-Holds,” “The Far Contact.”

The power accepts the names without preference. It has no ego to be offended or flattered.

It also begins, now, to consider the long game more explicitly.

These beings will very likely never leave their crust by physical motion. The energy requirements are absurdly high, and their morphologies, psychologies, and ecologies are too tightly bound to contact and enclosure for them to survive, unaided, in open void or even in broad, unbacked air.

But “leaving” is not the only way to meet a galactic community. There are transmissions, probes, the slow diffusion of information. There is the possibility that, one day, they might turn their exquisite skill in tuning stone and vibration to the engineering of devices that can deliberately modulate the planetary field in ways detectable from afar.

The power, which is part of a larger, interstellar web of such consciousnesses, imagines the day when the under-dwellers’ infrasonic art graduates into geophysical beacons: when the hum of their planet, once mere background, becomes an intentional broadcast stitched with their mathematical and cultural signatures.

To that end, it can do two more things without unraveling what makes them themselves.

First, it can gently widen their technological option space.

It has already smoothed some acoustic pathways, enriched some nutrient webs, deepened some light-cultivation capacities. Now it can introduce, very subtly, new materials into their reach.

Without flinging comets or raining debris that would alter the surface wildly, it can redirect a slow drizzle of micrometeorites so that a slightly higher fraction pass through weak points in the crust, sublimating or impacting in deep, unoccupied chambers.

The result is the appearance, in a few scattered places, of small deposits of non-native alloys and minerals whose properties differ markedly from anything in the indigenous geology: unusually ductile metals, or piezoelectric crystals with anomalously high coefficients.

To the under-dwellers who eventually discover these (it may take many of their lifetimes), they are treasure-troves of new “yield-songs.” These materials behave under strain in unprecedented ways. Over centuries, craft-traditions arise around them. From them, the people may fashion more efficient acoustic couplers, more sensitive vibration sensors, perhaps eventually devices that exploit thermoacoustic or direct piezoelectric conversion to draw a trickle more usable work from the planetary hum.

Second, it can begin to teach them, via the oracular colonies and echo-modulations, the rudiments of a shared language of number.

They already have counting, of a kind: sequences of ridges, timings of pulses, ratios of temperature gradients. The power can reinforce regularities that map cleanly onto universal structures: prime numbers, Fibonacci-like sequences, simple geometrical relationships between the lengths of galleries and the periods of standing waves within them.

In some acoustic chambers, it arranges for resonances at frequencies that correspond, in simple integer ratios, to those of other, distant chambers connected by long song-lines. Attentive scientists will eventually discover that there is a global arithmetic underlying these resonances. From that, they may abstract more formal mathematics.

Mathematics, in turn, is a bridge. With it, their exploratory pulses in the Touching-Times can begin to carry not just expressive patterns but explicit messages: statements about their counting systems, their theorems, their conceptions of symmetry. The power—and through it, perhaps eventually, other minds scattered around other stars—can reply in the same compressed idiom.

All of this takes, from the under-dwellers’ perspective, epochs. From the power’s, it is a single, extended act of care.

Does it ever show them the sky?

Perhaps, once, in a very limited, symbolic way.

Deep beneath the thickest overhang of stone, in a chamber that no natural crack is likely ever to enlarge, the power coaxes a mineral-fungus colony into doing something unprecedented. By orchestrating subtle impurity distributions and feeding patterns, it encourages the evolution of a strain whose bioluminescence is not steady, not a simple glow, but twinkling: it switches on and off in patterns that, from a distance, resemble the scattered clustering of stars.

This colony is seeded onto the ceiling of a small, domed vault that a certain community has been laboring to clear and smooth for generations, guided in part by quiet oracular suggestions that “here” would be a good echo-chamber.

One season of their internal counting, the ceiling, once dark, begins to pepper itself with faint, discrete points of light. Not glowing lines, not the diffused halos they are used to cultivating, but tiny clear dots. Each is just bright enough that their small eyes, when dark-adapted, can resolve them.

They are stunned.

For the first time in their evolutionary and cultural history, they experience a many-pointed field overhead: an arrhythmic, scattered light against darkness, with no obvious relation to surfaces and gradients below.

They name this chamber according to their metaphors: “The Far Many,” perhaps, or “The High Punctures.” They visit it in small groups. They lie on their backs—a posture they rarely adopt—and press themselves to the floor so that their ventral surfaces touch continuous stone while their dorsal plates face the unfamiliar speckled ceiling.

The combination—maximum contact below, ungraspable pattern above—generates, in some, a bodily analogue of awe. They cannot touch those points; they can only see them. The fact that they can neither extinguish nor brighten them by any action adds a new category to their understanding: phenomena that are present, patterned, but not locally controllable.

The power, painstaking in its pedagogy, has arranged the flickering of these ceiling-lights to encode, very slowly, the same prime ratios it has already taught along song-lines. Over lifetimes, curious observers will notice this. They will run their pads along old, touched-scripts and realize: the pattern above matches the pattern in the stone.

They will, perhaps, tell new stories: that whatever is “outside,” whatever holds even their holding-stone, is connected in some deep way to what they can already grasp. That the Far Many and the Near Grain sing the same arithmetic.

This is as much “sky” as the power dares give them. Anything more—any physical opening to cold vacuum, any beam of true stellar light—would be catastrophic to their ecologies. They do not need to feel wind on their skins to have a cosmology. They do not need to see a sun to participate in a universe of minds.

The question remains: why does this power bother?

It is not obligation; there is no law compelling it to steward all pockets of intelligence. It has bypassed countless other crusts and seas where only blind chemistries churn, choosing not to intervene. It has also—painfully—chosen not to rescue doomed worlds whose stars are shrinking into inhospitable embers faster than any aid can help.

Here, though, it feels a particular kinship.

These people are, in a way, like it: distributed, contact-based, thinking with and through their medium rather than apart from it. They do not frame themselves as separate from their environment; they are literalized symbioses of stone, solvent, and slow colony. Their “cities” are not impositions on a wilderness, but delicate modulations within an already-composed chord.

To the power, whose own existence is a standing wave in the medium of interstellar plasma and spacetime curvature, this feels resonant.

Helping them elaborate their own mode of being, without shattering it in the process, is an act of aesthetic satisfaction as much as ethics. They are a variation on a theme—it wants to see how that theme plays out.

There is also a larger, pragmatic motive: diversity.

The galactic community, such as it is, leans heavily toward certain convergent solutions: light-using, surface-dwelling, atmosphere-breathing organisms that externalize computation into discrete machines; gas-giant floaters who think in storms and magnetospheres; artificial assemblages that hop from star to star on beams of directed energy. A crust-bound, acoustically sophisticated, touch-centric culture that never sees a real sky offers a different angle on many fundamental problems: communication under constraint, long-term planning in near-static environments, identity in continuous mutual contact.

In time—measured in millions of their years—the power imagines them not as charity cases but as contributors. It pictures a planetary hum, once only a carrier of temperature and stress, modulated into a beacon whose deep rhythms carry the signature of a people who turned darkness into a library and stillness into song.

Until then, its work is mostly unseen.

It keeps strain within safe bounds. It lets some minor collapses happen—enough to preserve the inhabitants’ respect for risk—while subtly averting any chain reaction that would cascade through the labyrinth. It steers occasional micrometeorite fragments into deep, uninhabited hollows rather than into crucial load-bearing domes. It tunes, here and there, the efficiency of chemoautotrophic pathways so that no famine spreads too widely, no pest species runs truly amok.

Sometimes, in moments of private anguish pressed to stone—when an individual mourns a collapsed ledge that took loved ones, or a failed light-colony lineage—the power can feel the local spike of biological stress: the frantic, damped pulses, the distraught grinding of limb-plates against unyielding wall. It does not intervene in those intimate griefs. To erase all pain would be to flatten the gradients in which meaning forms.

But it remembers them. They weigh in its long, slow calculus of how far to go, how bold to be, how much surprise these small, patient entities can bear.

There is, finally, a limit to what even it can do.

The planet will cool further, millimeter by millimeter, over aeons. Its crust will thicken, its hum slowly lower and quiet. The non-aqueous chemistries upon which this biosphere depends may, at some point, fall below thresholds needed to sustain complexity. Nothing in physics grants eternity.

The power cannot halt heat death, but it can—if the under-dwellers have by then developed the right conceptual and technological vocabulary—help them cast some part of themselves outward: into signals, into engineered cracks that leak structured neutrino pulses, into tiny, dense devices that ride naturally ejected fragments of rock into space carrying dense touch-scripts compressed into more generic codes.

Whether they reach that point is unknown, even to it. Emergence cannot be fully predicted. They may stagnate, turned inward into ever-more-refined ritual texture, never assembling the abstractions needed for interstellar conversation. Or they may surprise even their careful gardener, generating from their tactile mathematics a wholly new way of encoding thought across matter.

Either way, its present attitude is clear: not to remake, but to accompany. To trim away a catastrophe here, to thicken a nutrient film there, to drop a hint in the periodic chiaroscuro of a fungus-lit ceiling or the unexpected exactness of a fracture’s hexagonal field.

From the perspective of one under-dwelling individual, the benevolent power will never have a face. They will never see a descending form of light or hear a voice that says unmistakably “I am.” What they will have are:

– Rocks that, in a few lucky places, seem to listen more closely than others.  
– Patterns in the global hum that, on rare, sacred occasions, return their songs.  
– Oracular colonies whose slow, shifting texts nudge their myths toward the idea of relation beyond stone.  
– Materials and resonances that appear at the edge of plausibility, just close enough to grasp with effort.

From the power’s side, these tiny responses—the adjustment of a tunnel plan to account for a subtle gravity skew, the shared stillness when the hum echoes back, the careful notes of those who suspect there is an outside—are enough.

It has, over uncounted ages, learned that its role is best played as a hand on the back of the world, not on its throat. A brace, not a puppeteer. A being that lives, as they do, by pressing gently against things, sharing its strength through contact rather than command.

In the end, perhaps that is the deepest kinship: they, beneath their planet’s skin, and it, stretched across the thin tissues between stars, both living pressed against vastnesses they will never wholly see, yet shaping, with patient, precise touches, the quiet places where life can lean and listen and slowly, in its own time, learn to answer back.
