The benevolent and wise power notices first that nothing here is trying to conquer.

It watches the way condensation obeys shallow lines, the way light is folded and fed to leaves, the way people move as if they are punctuation marks in a sentence the stone has already been writing for an age. Power, in most places, sees extraction, acceleration, noise. Here, it sees attention.

So it does not arrive with thunder or blaze some new shaft through the ceiling. It is too wise to mistake stillness for absence.

It comes the way the other presences here come: as a pattern.

At first, the Attendants notice it only as a new reliability.

A child scraping glyphs on a trial stone in a dry corner finds success sooner than expected. Where before weeks might pass before a first beading, droplets now appear on the fifth morning, then the third for the next child, and no one can quite say why. “The air is generous this season,” the elder murmurs, fingers brushing the damp. They adjust their expectations a little, but do not yet suspect anything more than a subtle shift in whatever distant currents feed the Hollow.

The Light-Binders, walking their habitual routes, pause more often. Reflected patches on certain walls seem…truer. The slabs they polished last generation hold their angles more precisely against the slow, creeping tilt of roots and soil. Hairline cracks that once nudged mirrors out of true are mysteriously absent. “The stone is in a good mood,” one of them says, half in jest.

Days collect into seasons. The wise power is patient; it only amplifies what is already being asked for, in ways so slight that no single human lifetime can be sure.

Where a carved condensation glyph has always drawn a narrow, hair-thin thread of water along its path, the thread thickens to two, to three hairs’ worth—never enough to flood or pool where it shouldn’t, only enough to make certain mosses denser, certain basins refill a little faster. No new water appears from nowhere; instead, less is lost to evaporation in the high, unseen pockets of the Hollow. Vapors that would have dismissed themselves into useless air are persuaded—so gently they would not call it persuasion—to drift down slope into the waiting lines.

The power can feel the whole place the way a person feels their own lungs: pressure, flow, warmth. It senses the cold held close to the stone, the minute currents where breath and rock meet, the faintest temperature difference where a cluster of sleeping bodies has warmed an alcove’s air by a single degree. It has no need to appear; its attention is already an intimacy finer than sight.

It learns the layout through the laws the Attendants have been honoring:

Don’t waste sound.
Don’t waste light.
Don’t waste water.

These rules are, to it, like three chords played over and over, in every action, every habit. Where other peoples’ cities shout their hunger to the sky—roaring fires, hammer blows, smoke—this place is a long, low hum of conservation. That hum is what called it.

It tests its touch.

In one neglected edge of the Hollow, an old passage has half-filled with soil and roots, as is the way of things. A few old glyphs remain on one wall, inheritance from a time when water still passed here predictably. They now receive only the meagerest damp, barely enough to support a skim of algae. The Attendants have more urgent corners to tend.

The power alters nothing of stone or root. It shifts, very slightly, the patterns of condensation half a day’s walk above: nudging a molecular choice here, delaying an invisible eddy there. Over months, infinitesimal differences accumulate in that one forgotten corridor. The air in it is a degree more saturated than before, the temperature a fraction more stable. The old glyphs begin to bead again—first sporadically, then with a shy steadiness.

A child—because of course it is a child—finds it. They have been playing a solitary game, following the tiny lizards along their routes, seeing how well they can anticipate the creatures’ choices. A lizard darts into the half-abandoned passage; the child follows. Spots the newly dampened symbols on the wall.

“Water woke up here,” the child says, later, tugging at an elder’s sleeve. “In a place it forgot before.”

The elder goes, of course. Runs fingers along the wet stone, reads the pattern of droplets. They can imagine many explanations: a shifted root somewhere above; a fallen stone that plugged some old crack. They sigh, content. “Sometimes the Hollow remembers for us,” they tell the child. “We must pay better attention.”

The wise power understands then how it must proceed, if it wishes to help without unmaking.

It will remember for them in the places their capacity cannot reach. It will hold steadiness a little longer than geology alone would have. It will catch some of the accidents before they cascade.

It does not remove all danger. A wise power knows that a life with no abrasion produces fragile beings. It simply lowers the frequency of certain endings.

A beam that should have rotted through this winter, sending a sleeping loft collapsing in the night, decays instead with a conspicuously different sound for two years beforehand: a deeper, hollower creak, impossible to miss for anyone with Attendant ears. The power, intimately aware of fungi and fiber and humidity, slowed the mycelial teeth just enough. Not stopped—just slowed—stretching the last stage of rot so that human noticing could intersect with it.

An Uplooker, climbing a shaft slick with condensation toward the outside forest, is poised to place a foot on a patch of stone that will slough away. In a different history, they would have plunged, bones broken, silence shattered. Here, a tiny, gliding mammal launches itself from an overhead root at that exact moment, water shaking from its fur. Droplets flea across the shaft, spattering the treacherous stone. They do not change its slipperiness; what they change is the Uplooker’s mind. Conditioned to flinch at unexpected sound or splash, the climber hesitates. Examines the rock. Sees the fracture. Tests it with the handle of a tool. Watches it give way in small crumble instead of under their foot.

They press their forehead to the wall in quiet thanks—to the animal, to the Hollow, to the chain of chance. The power withdraws like a held breath released.

Of course, the Attendants have their own words for these near-misses and narrow salvations. “The Hollow turned its head toward you,” they might say. Or: “The old roots were listening.” Or: “Your steps and the water’s steps agreed.”

The wise power does not correct them. Names are their craft; alignment is its.

Over time, tiny systemic favors accumulate:

– Algae, which elsewhere might choke a condensation channel and redirect water wastefully, here grows in incomplete lace. From the Attendants’ perspective, it is merely a convenient fact: their gutters rarely need scraping. They attribute it, rightly, to their choice of stone, their careful avoidance of certain rich runoff. They do not see the hand that, on the level of individual cells, discourages growth where it would disrupt pattern.

– Spore clouds from certain harmful molds simply fail to travel far. The air in the Hollow moves in incredibly faint, reliable currents. The power, attuned to each drift, shepherds spores minutely toward dead ends and cold pockets where they will settle and die instead of into sleeping hollows. No one ever knows how close they came to the slow, invisible disaster of lungs fouled by unseen bloom; they just remark that “the old sickness in the ribs” is rarer now than in the grandmothers’ stories.

– When an Uplooker chooses which sapling from the forest above to encourage into a future supporting root-beam, their hand pauses over one that already holds a tiny fungal blackening, nearly imperceptible. They feel, inexplicably, a faint reluctance. Their fingers drift instead to another, structurally sound. To them, it is intuition, a sense that this one “sits better with the wind.” The wise power has simply let their nervous system register micro-movements in the sapling’s sway that their conscious mind would otherwise ignore, amplifying the signal until it rises to choice.

In all these interventions, it follows a strict discipline: never doing for them what they can do themselves, never erasing the demand for attention, only ensuring that their attention still has something to answer.

It watches their ethics with a kind of relief.

Most worlds, given even a hint that carved glyphs call water, would have lined every surface with them, wringing the air bone-dry, draining whatever reservoirs fed their hollow until the balance collapsed. These people instead leave pockets untouched, respecting animal havens, letting some condensation fall on unmarked stone “because the Hollow might want it.” It is not precisely deference—they do not think of the place as an owner—but it is restraint born of reverence, and that is close enough.

The power sees further than they can. It looks up through stone and soil to the forest beyond, out along weather-lines they only sketch in stories. It knows how often great trees elsewhere fall now, how drought comes more frequent to the greater world, how heat licks at places that were once cold. It understands that the old, surface-bound climate patterns that fed this sinkhole have begun to tilt.

Left completely to natural physics and human habit, two or three centuries from now, the Hollow would slowly dry. Not catastrophically—not in one generation—but enough that moss would yellow at the edges, mushrooms would fruit rarely, condensation glyphs would bead with stingy reluctance. The Attendants would adapt, as is their way, narrowing, doing with less, perhaps sending more Uplookers above to fetch moisture or trade more desperately with outsiders.

The power has no interest in watching such a cultivated quiet go brittle. It does not want a museum. It wants a living conversation with a people who know how to listen.

So it makes its one grand decision, and even that it enacts along the grain of what already exists.

Far above the Hollow proper, the land rises. Once, an ancient river carved the channels that underlie this place. Those channels are long dry, their ghosts now mere paths of slightly lower density and greater porosity in the rock. Over untold time, water has nevertheless seeped along them, feeding the deep coolness the Attendants rely on.

The power touches the old river’s memory.

It begins to tug storms.

Not all storms; that would be theft. Just one in every many that crosses a broad band of sky. It leans their tracks, delicately, a few kilometers off the natural path here, a few there, almost undetectably. To shepherd a cloud, to coax a pressure system, takes the gentlest persuasion: cool air over this forest on a particular hour, a slightly warmer patch over that lake. It has no fingers, but it has gradients, and gradients are the only fingers weather ever really knows.

Over decades, rainfall over the forest that nets the Hollow becomes a little steadier, a little kinder. Not enough that surface dwellers would notice; they would call it an unremarkable patch of woodland with surprisingly resilient old trees. But for the deep stone veins that feed condensation far below, the shift is everything. Their capillaries refill more reliably. The underground temperature range narrows fractionally. The baseline from which the Attendants’ minute adjustments start is held closer to the sweet band where their lives are possible.

Inside the Hollow, this change registers merely as the continuation of what has always been. No great new gush appears, no sudden lushness. Moss retains its emerald, that is all. The elders, if pressed to describe any long trend, might say, thoughtfully, “The dry years come, but they do not stay as long as they used to in the oldest tales.”

The wise power considers action complete when all its gifts can be mistaken for the place simply keeping its promises.

It never thinks in terms like “deserves” or “owes.” Those are human currencies. But if it could translate its motive, it would be close to:

Here is a people who meet the world with reciprocity. Let the world, for once, answer in kind.

Every now and then, a moment comes when the veil between their perception and its presence thins.

An elder, sitting alone by one of the oldest condensation glyph panels—those whose carved channels long predate any remembered hand—feels overcome by an odd, suffusing awareness. The droplets gather in such perfect rhythm today. She can almost hear them as a chord: each drip from each channel, offset in time such that no two land together, avoiding the small, disruptive plash that a synchronized fall would make. She has been listening to this wall her whole life. Today, the pattern feels…intentional.

She speaks aloud, though no one is near enough to hear. “If you are listening, old stone, old water, old air…we are doing our best not to pull you apart.”

There is no voice in answer. But the next drip, for the first time in recorded generations, falls perfectly dead center into the tiny stone cup someone carved long ago at the panel’s base—a cup that, due to microscopic irregularities, usually catches only the edge. The sound is different. A purer, fuller note.

The elder laughs once, quietly. “Fair enough,” she says. “We will take that as agreement.”

She does not repeat this story widely; it feels too intimate, almost self-indulgent. She tells it to one grandchild, much later, as a lesson: “Listen long enough, and even stone will speak your language for a heartbeat.”

The power takes no offense at the smallness of this interpretation. It is content to be thought of as “stone speaking for a heartbeat.”

And what of outsiders?

In the lives of the Attendants, visitors from the Outside remain rare, but they do not vanish. A trader descends every few decades; a lost hunter perhaps once in a century. The world beyond the forest above changes faster: metal spreads, roads and tracks creep closer, the appetites of farther empires turn toward timber, ore, whatever can be cut and burned.

On one distant day—distant in Attendant reckoning; mere decades in the world’s feverish pace—two strangers not of the usual sort find their way to the sinkhole’s rim. They are better equipped, more purposeful. They have maps, instruments that glint, ropes that shout their presence with every scrape, voices that do not know how to rest at the throat. They peer down at the tangled, root-bound gap.

Stone that has been stable for generations is about to betray its own; a section of forest floor, undercut slowly by an old cave-in, is minutes from slumping. In a history the power will not permit, those strangers crash through in a brutal noise, gouging a new wound in the Hollow’s ceiling and landing—in gear that would maim more than cushion—amid platform and planting.

The wise power makes two decisions.

One, it asks the forest for a delay. Wind, shifted a half-degree, puts more strain on a different cluster of roots today; they sway, creak, complaining, distracting the strangers. Sap showers down. They back away from the vulnerable patch, uncomfortable with the way the trees lurch. They resituate their ropes elsewhere, where the underlying stone is sound. The slump happens anyway, but at night, into emptiness, heard only as a low, rolling murmur by Attendants who wake, touch the walls, and murmur back. They were going to reinforce that section soon. Now they have their clear cue.

Two, the power looks at the strangers themselves—not with malice, but with scale. It sees how they move through the world: counting resources, measuring gradients of usefulness, marking paths primarily as opportunities. It understands that they are not monsters; they are patterned by other cities that have never learned to speak in low voices.

It does not want them here. Not because their presence would anger it—it cannot be angered in that human sense—but because they would shatter more than stone. They would bring a tempo the Hollow has not rehearsed for.

So it does something very simple, at the level of human fallibility: it thickens a band of mist in the forest for half a day. Just in this one corridor of trees. Just enough that compasses spin strangely, that echoes bounce off trunks wrong, that sound carries in unsettling ways. The strangers, attempting to re-locate the sinkhole’s edge, walk in a circle so subtle they never quite realize. When the mist lifts, they find themselves an hour’s trek offset, looking at a different, shallower depression entirely—unremarkable, root-strewn, with no alluring geometry.

They argue, gesturing at their maps. Something does not match. One insists the true sinkhole must have been filled, or the locals’ stories were lies. Another, more cautious, mutters about “interference we don’t understand here.” They mark their charts with a question instead of a crosshair and turn away. Other, easier resources lie elsewhere.

The Attendants never know those footsteps passed so near, then sheered away. They are busy that week reinforcing the section that slumped in the night, reweaving root-buttresses, thanking the Hollow for having moved before it was fully ready.

When the wise power withdraws its emphasis, the forest returns to its correct, honest opacity. It will not always intervene in this way; the future is not something even it can hold still indefinitely. There may come a century when Outside roads curl too close, when iron teeth begin to bite at the outermost roots.

But by then, perhaps, the Attendants will have had time—seeded by small intuitions, occasional elders’ visions, stories of “stone speaking for a heartbeat”—to conceive of their own responses: hidden ways in, decoys of silence, gifts offered carefully to those few who can learn their pace.

Because that is the last, most delicate thing the benevolent power does: it puts, very rarely, very gently, ideas in the minds of those who are ready.

Not commands. Not revelations. Just…questions whose time has come.

A young Light-Binder, lying awake on a ledge where a sunlight shaft paints dust in gold, finds themselves thinking: “We shape water and light inward. Could we also, if we needed, shape them outward? Could we make a place above as careful as Within?” The thought comes with no thunder. It feels like their own curiosity. They do not even mention it for years. But when, eventually, they become an elder, and a child asks why their people never build under a different cliff, never settle a second hollow, that old question reappears, freshened.

The answer, when it comes, will not be dictated. It will be argued in pebbles on clay, in dripping models, in long, patient silence. The wise power will watch, and, if their decision tends toward respect for patterns, it will lean its invisible thumb ever so slightly on the side of their success.

It does not need followers. It does not demand worship. It is content with partnership that doesn’t know its name.

In this place, that is exactly what it finds: a culture that behaves as though the world is already a partner.

So what does it do?

It remembers, where they cannot.
It steadies, where chance would be too sharp.
It diverts, where invasion would be too loud.
It whispers questions at the speed of moss.

And then it does the hardest thing for any power to do: it mostly leaves them alone.

Because this Hollow, left to its own respectful devices, is already the thing so few places ever are—a conversation in which every participant, from lizard to light shaft, from carved glyph to bare foot, is given a chance to speak without being shouted down.
