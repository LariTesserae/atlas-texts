It begins with listening, because that is the only way to approach a world already so full.

The power that turns its attention here is neither sudden nor loud. It has no need to arrive with light or thunder. It has watched stone cool and buckle, watched oceans rise and vanish on other spheres. Compared to those, the plateau is a small, intricate chord—a single, rich note in a cosmos thick with sound.

It does not ask, “What can I make of this?” but, “What is this already making of itself?”

So it waits, and listens, and learns the plateau’s music.

It learns the roar first: how the constant surf of wings and bodies is not random but banded, pulsed, a polyphony of different periodicities weaving through one another. It follows the rise and collapse of swarms across days and seasons, sees how predation sends great shockwaves of pattern through the air. It hears how the wet channels hum, how their density alters the timbre of the entire region, a low undercurrent modulated by storm and drought.

It listens down into the mat, into the long, fungal glows and root-pulse spaces, and hears a slower music there: capillary water shifting, sugar migrating, signal-chemicals passing from cell to cell like phrases in an unhurried language.

It hears, above and within this, the people.

Their clicks and hums and body-stamps are small against the plateau’s thunder, but they are improbably shaped. They carry intention. They occur in patterns that do not exactly match hunting or mating or warning as any other creature knows those things. There is redundancy, variation, and a peculiar attention to timing more than raw volume. A power that knows minds when it hears them cannot mistake that.

They are not many, these pattern-makers, and they are fragile against their world’s appetite. The power feels a kind of gravity toward them—not because they are destined, not because they are central, but because they are rare: a little pocket of self-reflective listening in an already intricate place.

It could intervene like weather. It could still the swarms for a day, tear open a canyon to give them refuge, sweep away their predators with an invented sickness. It could stand their plant-bridges upright, harden them into towers and domes, wrap their quiet galleries in stone and silence.

It knows how that story goes. On other worlds, doing so has bought a species time at the cost of their context. They live longer, but not in the world that taught them how to be themselves.

They are what they are because of the roar.

So the power does something smaller, and stranger, and kinder.

It enters the system where the system already speaks: in resonance, in growth, in the way this plateau trades pattern between plant and animal and stone.

It touches nothing first that bleeds.

Instead it reaches into the chemical mutterings of cells—the slow gossip of plasmodesmata and fungal hyphae—and adds, here and there, a new potential: an extra kind of memory.

Plants already remember in their way. A leaf turned to the light keeps part of that decision in its structure. A root that once met stone grows differently when it meets stone again. The power does not overwrite this; it folds into it. In a few chosen lineages of the mat-forming plants, in certain arch-builders, in a thin fuzz of lichen over ancient buried stones, it seeds the possibility of storing patterns of vibration more finely, more lastingly.

It is careful. It does not impose a script, a code that says, “When you hear this, do that.” It simply makes some tissues slightly more plastic in the presence of repeating sound; slightly more likely to stabilize a structure that has been excited in the same way many times.

The people have been singing to plants for generations, bending arches with their songs, coaxing tunnels to thicken, teaching leaves to curl at a call. Before, the plant’s “memory” of such training was diffuse, held mostly in growth-form and favorable chemistry. Now, over decades, something new accrues.

A particular sequence of hums and stamps, used every season at a certain water cache, does more than open pores and swell tissues in the moment. It leaves a faint, structural echo: a pattern of micro-thickenings along vascular strands, a subtle bias in how certain gas chambers align. Those changes do not change the song; they *are* the song, lasting on after the throats that made it fall silent.

The people do not notice this at first. Why would they? The plant-skin has always been cooperative to patience. A leaf that curls more easily this year than last is no miracle; it is habit.

But time on the plateau is long. The power thinks in those long spans.

It sees what the people cannot see yet: that a lineage of plant tissue is becoming a slow, distributed archive of their attention.

It does not improve their tools. It does not sharpen their stone or gift them metallurgy. It does not whisper agriculture into their dreams. Those are violent changes here: too much leverage in hands that survive by lightness.

What it does, instead, is make their listening easier and their losses less complete.

One dry season, a small band crosses to a basin where no kin have lived for three generations. Hunger and accident have thinned their route; they have no elder who remembers the old marks in this place. They come thirsty. The usual cues—leaf-shine, insect density—are wrong this year; a storm has shifted a wet band, and predators have driven certain pollinators elsewhere. Everything is too loud in the wrong places.

At dusk, exhausted, one of them—an older adult whose crest carries the faint, internal scars of too many close calls—rests a hand on the mat and, half out of habit, hums an old pattern taught to them in childhood. It is one of the plateau’s many nearly-lost songs: three notes descending, a pause, then a slow rising tremor you feel more than hear.

Under their palm, the mat swells. Pores, dormant in this location for decades, open in a ring. Air cools on the skin; a faint, mineral scent rises. Just beyond their hand, the fibrous floor droops minutely, as if over a hollow.

They probe. The tissues yield. Below, in a darkness that smells of deep time and stone and old plant-death, there is pooled water—cold, clean enough. Their astonishment ripples through the group, crest-hums overlapping in bewildered gratitude and fear.

They think: the plateau remembers us.

The power does not correct them. It is not wrong.

The plateau *does* remember them, now, more exactly than before. Not in image or name, but in causal grooves: “When the world is this dry, and a pattern like this is played here, then this structure opens.” The memory is not symbolic; it is physical. But to the people, who have always read physical habit as meaning, it is indistinguishable from a kind of answer.

They begin, slowly, to test this.

In the comparative safety of known basins, elders lead small, careful experiments. They sing old songs in new places, watching for strange responsiveness. They return to old sites where no one has camped since their grandparents’ time, and try the calls associated with those camp-stories: the code for “all safe”; the one for “this is where we stored the third basket when the second was lost.”

Sometimes nothing happens. The plant-skin there is too young; storms have scraped it away and new growth has no history to work with. Sometimes, though, something *does* answer: a leaf pivots a little more sharply than it should; a faint, cool draft issues from a crack that last year was only a line; a cluster of fungus below flares a shade brighter in their crests’ sensitivity.

Their worldview was already animist—already inclined to treat place and plant as persons with moods and capacities. This new reliability—this sense that certain patterns of sound, repeated and inherited, can unlock sleeping gestures in the land—does not feel like a break with their understanding of things. It feels like confirmation.

They do not call it a gift. They call it a *listening that came back*.

The power makes no distinction. To it, gift and feedback are almost the same thing: an adjustment in the system that allows it to carry certain patterns further than it otherwise would.

Over generations, this extra capacity for retention changes how the people relate to absence.

Once, when a family died out on a route or abandoned a basin, their knowledge of that place’s oddities—its safer paths and its subtle traps, its helpful plants and its treacherous sound-shadow stones—faded quickly. Their neighbors might remember the story “there is danger there” or “once we found many cracked seed-pods in that hollow,” but fine detail dissolved into myth.

Now, some fraction of that detail stays in the mat.

A traveler who has no human mentor for a stretch of route can, by persistence and pattern-making, discover that certain songs elicit consistent, meaningful responses from the plant-skin. They begin to treat space itself as an elder: not a kindly one, not always a safe one, but one that can be asked, that sometimes answers.

This keeps them from vanishing.

Not from death; individuals still die as easily as before. Storms still wash them from arches; predators still learn new mimics faster than dialects diffuse; children still misstep at the edges of wet bands and slide into churning channels below.

But the line between “no one knows this anymore” and “no one is here right now to ask” becomes less sharp.

Some songs survive that would otherwise have gone extinct. Some ways of walking and building—those that alter the plant architecture in durable ways—accumulate across centuries, stitched into the plateau as lightly as embroidery, yet enough to matter.

It is not much, on the scale of what a god could do.

It is everything, on the scale of what a listening animal can inherit from a noisy world.

The power does one more thing, even smaller.

It makes them a little harder to trick.

Predators that mimic their calls have been an arms race for a long time. The people answer with dialect drift, with layered signals folded between sound and gesture and mat-vibration. Still, a mimicked warning or comfort-call kills a child often enough to shape nightmares.

The power has no interest in ending predation. An ecosystem cannot hold without teeth. But it has the means to adjust biases with surgical precision.

It reaches—not into brains, not into choices, but into the sensory apparatus that feeds those choices. In the loops and hollows of their skulls, in the cartilage that couples crest to ear-bone, it adds a new kind of cross-check.

Very slightly, when a sound arrives that matches the *shape* of one of their own calls but lacks the fine, tangled echo of mat and body—when it is too pure, too isolated from the substrate’s low murmur—the inner ear flags it. Not as language. As not-quite-real. A shadow without weight.

The result is not immunity. A tired or frightened person can override the unease. A very skilled predator, making its call right at the mat, can fool even this improved sense. But on average, across many encounters, fewer children step trustingly toward disembodied comfort in the leaves. More elders feel their crest-edges lift in suspicion when a “safe” whistle comes from the wrong kind of stillness.

This is the limit of what the power allows itself: no guaranteed salvation, only a bias toward surviving long enough to use the world more fully.

Beyond these two adjustments—deeper plant-memory of their patterns, and a small anti-mimicry calibration—the power is almost entirely restraint.

It watches them continue to walk their long spirals, to trace their routes like stitching around the plateau’s broad, worn shoulders. It listens as their rituals change.

In some basins, the elder’s stories begin to include lines like: “Here, if you forget, you can ask the mat. This leaf knows the old way.” In others, more cautious or more wounded by years of near-loss, they treat these plant-responses as tricks at first: tempting illusions. Only slowly, when the patterns prove consistent and non-fatal, do they fold them into their practice.

Certain families become keepers not only of oral pattern, but of *planted* pattern. They choose a few robust strains of responsive plant, encourage them in galleries where their people most often gather, carry cuttings wrapped in damp fiber when they venture to new basins. It is not agriculture as some worlds know it—no fields, no rows—but it is a deliberate tending of particular kinds of listening.

The power smiles, in whatever way such a thing smiles, to see this: they have taken the nudge and made it their own work.

It would be easy, with such access to growth, to give them more obvious miracles. A self-deepening refuge-cave. A tree that grows weapons. A plant that cleans wounds with antiseptic sap.

It does none of that. They are not helpless. They already know poultices from bitter fungi, sheathing leaves that slow bleeding, the trick of plaiting dried bolster-tissue into slings. They have all the technology a being needs to live attentively on this tableland. What they lack are not tools, but continuity and a horizon wider than their own short lives.

So the power waits again, for a very long time.

It watches how the new memory-capable plant strains spread or fail. It sees some overrun by more aggressive, less coöperative species in harsh domes; it lets them go. It sees others become integral parts of the “villages”—those low, gallery-like edits in the mat—until the distinction between “shelter plant” and “archive plant” dissolves. To live in those places is to be surrounded always by tissues that know how to respond to your people’s voice.

It sees the people invent new practices: songs that are not calls in themselves but keys, sequences used not in conversation but in dialogue with place. A group arriving at the edge of unknown territory may stand still for a while and try several of these, a sort of prelude. What answers tells them whether any of their kin have ever sung here, whether they are stepping into untouched loudness or into a faint resonance of shared habit.

This is culture, but not of cities; it is the expanding of a distributed self.

Eventually—because nothing in the world stands still, not even old plateaus—they begin to thin again.

Climate shifts. The storms’ timing changes; a band of dryness that once came every ten years now comes every three. Some of the plant species that make the best listeners fare poorly under the new pattern and retreat to deeper basins. Predators adapt faster than prey; a new flier evolves a way to ride swarms like surfboards, crossing distances that once isolated populations.

Family strings break. Some routes become impassable for human-sized walkers during a critical few years; the knowledge of the way around dies with someone’s aunt, someone’s second child. There are evenings on some domes when no bipedal foot presses the mat at all.

The power does not stop this. It cannot, without breaking everything it came to cherish. To fix the climate here would be to freeze the whole plateau’s experiment in amber. To de-fang a predator would be to collapse a web of hunter-and-hunted behaviors refined over uncounted generations.

But the plateau is no longer alone in its remembering.

In certain quiet basins, beneath stones rubbed smooth by vanished rivers and half-wrapped in singing tissue, there are pockets of air in which the plant-skin holds not only the chemical echoes of long-ago droughts, but the structural residues of human songs. Even when no living throat now knows those songs, their pattern is inscribed in the slight asymmetries of tissue.

The fungi glowing in a particular tunnel bend around an old cache-chamber in just such a way that, when wind moves faintly through, a sequence of tones is produced, like a broken chord. A leaf in a forgotten gallery curls not in response to the current predators’ calls, but to a cluster of frequencies no existing animal here uses—because once, that cluster meant “children, stop.”

The power listens to these ghost responses and thinks of them as seeds, not failures.

It is not certain whether any new mind will ever come here, from the stars or from an adjacent continent. It does not plan in that sense; it provides conditions.

But it knows this: the world is not blank.

If someday, far in the plateau’s future, another listening creature crosses this high, worn tableland—whether an off-worlder in a pressure suit, or a late-descended glider-animal that grew wit in its bones—it will step onto a surface already etched with expectation.

It may notice that some stones hum oddly when trod. That some arcs of leaf-flesh shiver open at sounds that no current wind, no contemporary cry, ever makes. That in certain basins, when the universal roar drops for a moment—as it still does, even then—there is a quality in the silence like an inhalation waiting for an answer.

The power leaves that as its main work: a layered, harmless preparedness. Not a monument in stone, but a responsiveness in life. An invitation.

For the people themselves, while they still walk, it does one last, almost imperceptible thing.

It quiets, for them alone, the world at the moment of death.

Not always. That would be indulgence. But sometimes—when an elder who has carried many songs lies finally still in a gallery deep in a basin where their line began—the power reaches into fluid dynamics and insect motive and root pressure and, for a breath or two, loosens the coupling.

Wings still beat, but their frequencies slip a little out of phase and cancel. Seed-pods still crack, but their pops land in the gaps between heartbeats. The wet channels still breath, but their bellows fall just below audibility.

For a brief space, in a world defined by roar, there is true quiet around a single cooling body.

In that cleared sound-space, their kin sing—not to call rivers, not to bend leaves, but simply so that, for once, their own voices are the loudest thing they know. The song is no grand, composed thing. It is a patchwork of work-calls and warnings and comfort-hums from a long life, stitched together in love.

The mat hears this, and with its new, slow memory, holds the shape of that song a little better than it would have before the power came. A generation later, a child who never met that elder will stand on that spot and, without knowing why, find that a certain note trembles back at them from the floor with unusual clarity. They will think: someone like me was here once, and the world remembers.

The power is satisfied with that.

It does not need worship. It does not expect gratitude. If the people ever guess that something beyond plateau and plant and animal had a hand in deepening their dialogue, they do not build statues. They have no concept of a maker who is not, in some deep sense, also made.

If they give it a name at all, it is in the form of a relational verb, a phrase that means something like “that-which-lets-things-hear-each-other-longer.”

It is enough.

The plateau still wears its old, rounded bones under the mat. The roar still rises every dawn and falls, somewhat, at every dusk. Death still comes in waves and accidents and slow dwindlings. Nothing is saved from impermanence.

What has changed is this: for a while, in one small, loud corner of the universe, there is an ecology in which listening itself became a form the world could hold onto—a pattern that did not vanish as fast as breath.

The power did little. It adjusted the gain on memory, tuned a sense against deception, stilled the air at a few chosen passings. It let everything else proceed as it would have: the great, indifferent poetry of stone and leaf and tooth and wing.

Then it, too, moved its attention on, as all things must. Not away in abandonment, but outward, to other notes in the cosmic harp.

Behind it, on this high, settled tableland where mountains finished their story long ago, the plants still flex minutely when certain old songs are tried. The stones still hum when wind hits them just so. The mat still opens, here and there, to thirsty hands that never knew the ones who first taught it how.

And on rare days, when the storms slide in low and the roar is everything, if you could stand very still in an old basin and feel deeply into your bones, you might catch—as a pressure at the back of the mind, as the ghost of a chord—that the world itself is listening back.
