The first thing the benevolent power does is nothing at all.

It watches.

It gives this young, roaring room the courtesy of being seen exactly as it is, without haste to improve or rescue. It attends to the details the way the original makers did: by looking long enough that the noise resolves into distinct threads.

It notices that nothing here is desperate. The stone is uncracked. The wood is holding. The water is warm and clean. The thin greens at the edges are not choking the drains, only testing them. The attendant’s body, when they come, is tired-but-capable, not yet bowed under impossible duty. This is not a place in crisis. It is only new, intense, and alone.

So the power waits until its attention stops trying to overwrite the room with its own metaphors. It waits until the first impulse to “fix” gives way to a steady, listening curiosity.

Then, gently, it reaches.

It does not appear as a figure in the doorway, or a voice booming over the roar. It does not reverse the water’s fall, or dim the sun, or write flaming script in the mist. Those would be intrusions, not care. It comes instead the way influence always comes here: through flows, through edges, through the almost-invisible.

Its first kindness is to the attendant.

They are standing, as they so often do, on the textured patch by the dark post, waist damp, hair beaded with spray, ears half-numbed. Their attention is a taut band stretched across the system upstream. They are thinking, with a low habitual anxiety, about the gate that has been sticking, about the slight sourness in yesterday’s steam, about the rumor that water demand above will increase when a new courtyard opens. They are not miserable. They are only responsible in a world where a single mind has to hold an entire circulation in its head.

The power does not lift this weight away. Responsibility is the spine of the room’s functioning; removing it would be a cruelty to everything that depends on it. Instead, it lengthens the spine.

It slips, delicately, into the attendant’s pattern of perception.

Not as a foreign will, not as a voice issuing orders, but as an extra sense nested just below their conscious noticing. A new kind of “knowing” blooms in them so gradually that they cannot say when it began.

At first, it feels like intuition getting sharper.

They find, one morning, that standing at the post they can tell—before they touch the access panel—that the plug behind it is not seated quite right. Not from sound, not from visible change. It is as if the room’s pattern of noise and spray has become slightly faceted in their awareness; they can feel the misalignment like a burr beneath a fingertip.

A week later, they are halfway down the steps to the chamber when a small, precise unease visits them, focused not ahead but above. They pause, look up, and go instead to check a secondary cistern that is not on their usual route. There they find a piece of windblown debris half-blocking an intake—a wad of leaves and roof-dust that, left in place, would have slowly altered the entire flow.

They clear it in minutes. The future in which that blockage formed a seed for rot and sourness in the water never arrives. They will never know how much they’ve preempted. From their perspective, they only had a hunch, and it was right.

This pattern repeats. “Hunches” come more often, and more exactly. They start to trust them. Inside those hunches is the benevolent power: not dictating, simply extending the attendant’s own model of the system out along threads of cause and effect they could not, unaided, track.

The power does not make them infallible. It simply smooths the path between perception and right action, removes a little friction from the way understanding spreads.

This is the second kindness: it broadens understanding.

For the attendant, this broadening shows up as:

- A clearer sense of the upstream reservoirs’ state whenever they listen to the room’s soundscape.
- A felt grasp of how much adjustment a gate can tolerate before a basin overflows, as if they can see water levels through stone.
- A new, bodily intuition of timescales: which problems must be addressed today, which can safely wait a week, which are years-long drifts to be nudged gradually.

They do not learn equations. They do not learn names like “pressure” or “flow regime,” unless their own culture already has them. They gain, instead, a more complete craft-sense: a mental map that now takes in subtler feedback loops.

The benevolent power does not insert this all at once; it grows it alongside the attendant’s existing expertise. Where there is already knowledge—of how a particular gate sticks in humidity, or how long it takes moss to recolonize after scraping—the power threads in more distant consequences. Where there are gaps—uncertainty about what happens at the highest intake—they leave mystery, because some mystery is protective. To know everything is to be constantly compelled; the power wants the attendant to sleep.

It also does a small, quiet thing for their body. Not invulnerability, not immunity, just a softening of the worst accumulations.

Calluses still form, but joints ache less after long days. The constant roar still blunts hearing, but not so fast; the loss slows to a gentler curve. The skin that spends hours damp in warm spray resists infection better. Tiny, nearly unnoticeable adjustments in cellular repair tilt the balance away from chronic damage.

None of this is miraculous in the way stories like to tell. The attendant still tires. They still age. They still have bad days when gates jam and cistern ropes burn their hands. But the line of their life in this place, which might have been short and gradually ground-down, stretches longer and steadier. They become, in effect, the room’s first elder: someone who can see it through more years than any one body otherwise could.

The power’s third kindness is to the room’s future.

It understands something the original builders may not: that no system of water and stone and light remains stable. Either it is tended into a long, narrow band of “same enough,” or it wanders into new forms under the guidance of growth and decay. Both paths can be good; both can be ruinous. The trick is to keep change from becoming catastrophe.

So the power looks along the probable futures of this chamber.

It sees one path where interest in maintenance wanes a generation from now. The attendant’s successor is assigned but not apprenticed; the craft-memory thins. Algae thickens; drains clog unseen. One heavy storm from above overfills the cisterns, tears out a section of wall. The room floods unpredictably and, in time, is abandoned. It collapses into a damp, broken cavity where rich, chaotic life eventually thrives in the rubble, but the original choreography is gone.

It sees another path where religious or political meaning gathers around the chamber’s roar. It becomes a site of prestige; powerful people demand constant access. Bathers multiply, bringing oils and dust and hair. Maintenance tries to keep up but can’t. Substances the room was not designed to handle begin to coat the basins. Wood swells, then rots. Flow turns patchy and uneven. Insects arrive in droves—flies, mosquitoes—finding perfect breeding pockets. Disease circulates. Fear follows, and with it, either superstitious offerings or brutal cleansing fires.

It sees a rarer path where the room is fetishized as an artifact to be preserved unchanged at all costs. Maintenance becomes overzealous. Every hint of moss is scraped weekly. The stone is scoured with harsh abrasives. To “save” the tool marks, the keepers constantly abrade and seal, unknowingly erasing the very youth they think they protect. The water is diverted more and more often elsewhere “to avoid wear,” until the room’s purpose fades, leaving only a hollowed-out museum of dry stone and memory.

And it sees some quieter, narrower threads of possibility in which the chamber is neither neglected nor idolized, neither overwhelmed by human softness nor stripped of all growth. These are futures where it is allowed to become a little more than its builders planned, but not yet something unrecognizable.

The benevolent power chooses to favor those.

It does not do this by enforcing them like laws. It works—as it has with the attendant—through attention and inclination.

A young apprentice who will one day share the attendant’s duties sits above, on some high terrace, eating. They are listening with half an ear to talk about water rights and crop allotments. Their mind is wandering. The power brushes the wandering lightly toward curiosity about “the room below.” Not awe, not dread. Just a nudge: I would like to see that; I would like to understand.

They ask, later, if they can watch, and they are allowed down the stairs.

The first time they stand in the spray, it is almost too much. They squint, laugh, shout. They see, for a moment, nothing of craft, only the overwhelming presence of falling water. The power waits. It lets simple delight do its work. It does not explain.

The second or third time, their attention lasts longer. They start to ask small questions: Why is that spout different? Why doesn’t the water eat away the stone faster? What happens if this gate moves?

Each question, when answered with whatever words the attendant has, deepens the groove along which future care will flow. The power, very gently, strengthens their fascination, not into obsession, but into a sense that this system is knowable and worth knowing.

Over years, that apprentice will build their own craft-sense. The benevolent power will again nest in the gaps, linking immediate observations with distant consequences. In this way, it creates not one overburdened guardian, but a continuous lineage of competent stewards.

It also intervenes the tiniest bit in the room’s ecology.

It does not halt algae. It does not sterilize the water. It knows that perfect cleanliness here would be a brittle state, endlessly hungry for labor. Instead, it tunes thresholds.

In the thinnest, most vulnerable channels—the ones whose dimensions are critical to keeping overflow from backing up—the power nudges micro-conditions so that algae has a slightly harder time initiating. A bit more shear on the walls, a bit less nutrient deposition. Not enough to make the stone sterile, just enough to delay clogging.

In less critical, less erosive corners—small hollows where splash collects and then seeps away—it does the opposite. It makes it fractionally easier for moss to grab hold, for invertebrate eggs to stick, for a film of life to thicken. These become deliberate, if unseen, “sinks” where organic matter can gather without harming function.

By biasing growth into these safer pockets and away from the bottlenecks, the power creates an internal self-regulation. As life increases, it has places to collect, to be preyed upon, to decompose, that do not immediately threaten the room’s primary task of moving water.

Over time, this means that when tiny insects finally arrive—hitching a ride on someone's damp robe, or blown in at a vent—they find certain mossy recesses very inviting, and some bare, smooth runs inhospitable. They settle where their presence is mostly harmless. A minuscule food web begins there: algae feeding tiny grazers, grazers feeding slightly larger hunters. Their bodies, when they die, become nutrient pulses that are then washed downstream in diluted, intermittent traces.

The water remains clean enough for its purpose. But it also becomes a little more alive, in places where life can be indulged.

There is one more thing the benevolent power does, and it does it almost shyly: it leaves a message.

Not a manifesto, not a carved inscription in a tongue no one speaks. Just a pattern that can be recognized if anyone ever looks at the room with the same patient care the builders did.

Along one of the less-visited walls, above the splash zone but still inside the flickering light, the power slightly accelerates the growth of a particular moss patch. It lets the strands spread in a way that responds to the wall’s chisel marks, following them, thickening where they cross.

Over a few seasons, that patch fills out into a gently curved shape—not a letter, not a glyph from human writing, but something that, to any attentive eye, is not random. It echoes, in miniature, the curve of a basin’s lip below, as if the stone has grown a small mirror of its own geometry in green.

The attendant notices it, one day, on a pause between tasks. They frown, then smile, then merely shrug. To them it’s an odd, pretty quirk, a thing to perhaps mention to the apprentice.

The apprentice, with younger, pattern-hungry eyes, sees more. They see that the moss’s outline matches the negative space between two falling sheets of water. It is, in a way, a drawing of the room’s own flow, traced where the flow never directly touches.

This realization may not be articulated in words. It lands instead as a sudden sense that the room is self-knowing, that its parts echo one another. That what happens in the heart of the storm leaves a gentle print at the edges.

If the culture around them has language for spirits, they might take this as a sign that the room has one. If it is more secular, they might just adopt it as a private emblem, an informal mark of the system’s identity. In either case, the pattern quietly binds their affection and attention a bit more tightly to this place.

The benevolent power does not need that affection. It is not collecting worship. But it understands that humans care for what they feel connected to, not for what is merely useful. A small, inexplicable beauty, glimpsed on a routine day, can be more binding than any decree.

So, in summary, what this wise, benevolent presence does is very little, very exactly:

- It deepens the existing steward’s understanding, so that right action feels more natural and less exhausting.
- It slows the wear of their body just enough that they can be an effective, long-lived guardian.
- It quietly kindles curiosity in at least one successor, ensuring the craft does not die with a single pair of hands.
- It biases the room’s micro-ecology so that life can arise where it will not quickly break the system, and the system can welcome a modest, stable community of small beings.
- It seeds one or two small, meaningful patterns—a moss curve here, a recurrent play of light there—that make the room legible as a coherent, almost self-reflective whole to anyone who looks with love.

It does not stop stone from eroding, or wood from swelling in humidity. It does not lock the room into a static perfection. Edges will soften. Tool marks will blur. The roar will slowly, over decades, change timbre as mineral and moss revise the falls. People will come and go, some caring, some careless.

But the long, slow catastrophe paths—the ones where neglect or zeal or overuse rapidly destroy either function or beauty—become less likely. The chamber is granted, not immortality, but a longer, kinder adolescence, and perhaps a graceful maturity.

The power remains mostly anonymous, present wherever attention sharpens and small right decisions are made without anyone quite knowing why they were obvious. It does not demand to be named. In its view, the highest form of intervention is the one that disappears into the ordinary, leaving behind only a room that works, a flow that endures, a handful of lives—human and more-than-human—that have room to unfold within the roar and brightness without being broken by them.
