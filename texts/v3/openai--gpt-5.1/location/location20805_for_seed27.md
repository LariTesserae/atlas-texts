There is no soil here, and there has never been rain.

You arrive on a height that is both mountain and organism, a long-cooled crest of mineral bone overgrown until the original rock is more rumor than fact. The air is thin and cold enough that every breath seems to ring inside your chest, tin-bright and a little painful. The light is low: as if the sun were perpetually behind a thick lens of dust or distant cloud, or as if this world’s star itself were tired. Color drains quickly with distance; everything more than a hundred meters away becomes silhouette and inference.

Yet it is not barren. Far from it. Everything is plant.

---

### The substrate

The ground under your feet is not ground; it is a single, continuous living mat, a continent-wide cortex of vegetable tissue grown over eons. It feels wrong to call it “moss,” but that is the closest earthly analogy: pale, fibrous, dry to the touch, with a faint ceramic rasp when your boots scuff across it. When you listen, the mat is not quiet. It crunches microscopically, threads recombining, tiny valves opening and closing like breaths. 

Beneath that mat, there is ancient stone—worn nearly flat by time, once sharp ridges now long-buried spines. The stone forms a plateau complex: high country that once was violently folded and thrust up, now planed by epochs into almost-level expanses and soft, rounded escarpments. Whatever rivers once carved it have been gone so long that only their ghosts remain as shallow, plant-filled channels and color changes in the mineral beneath.

Here, erosion has stopped having big ideas. Everything that could fall has already fallen. Everything that could be sharp has been dulled. What’s left is a final, stable geometry: broad tables, sagging arches, collapsed cirques filled in with living tissue.

---

### Plant as city, plant as air

The vegetation is total, but not dense. It does not crowd so much as claim. There is always space—wide vaults of air, sightlines that run for kilometers—yet every solid surface belongs to the botanical body of the world.

The “city” is grown, not built.

Imagine towers like vast, hollow stalks, some the color of old ivory, others a faint translucent gray-green, grown from the substrate and tapering into the dim sky. They are not straight. They have bowed over under their own weight long ago, then thickened where they bent, forming great arcs that meet other arcs, until above the plateau hangs a web of archways and loops—structural botanical ribs connecting spine to spine.

Within those hollow trunks, the walls are layered: dry vascular rings, lattices spun from chitinous cellulose, shimmering micro-filaments that flex like muscle. Every surface is veined with faintly luminous lines, not quite bioluminescent, not exactly electrical. Their glow is the room’s light, a steady, muted silver-green that never brightens or dims with any sun.

These structures are arranged in patterns that, from above, would look like cities: clusters of towers forming districts, braided suspension-strands forming avenues, broad crowns spreading into plaza-like platforms. But there are no right angles. Everything is slow curvature and thickened nodes, fronds and ribs and joint-like knots.

There are apertures everywhere—oval mouths edged with tough, retractable lips of plant tissue. They open and close with a slow muscular precision, revealing chambers within, balconies of honeycombed bark that overlook deep interior spaces. From some, threads droop: delicate, root-like tendrils that sway in the thin air, tasting.

These are doors, windows, vents, nervous organs, and something more.

---

### No water, other flows

There is zero free water here. No puddles, no frost, not even ice clinging in shadows. The air is bone-dry, colder than its temperature by lack of moisture. Your tongue feels like dust after only a moment of open-mouthed breathing.

Within the plants, however, something moves. It is not water, not sap as we know it. The fluid is oily, transparent in thin veils and dark as smoke in bulk. It shimmers in capillaries under translucent skin, refracting the dim light like slow glass. This solvent is the basis of life’s chemistry here: non-aqueous, non-polar, perhaps something like supercooled hydrocarbons or exotic ionic fluids. It does not evaporate easily; in vacuum it would sheet and tremble but not leap away.

You sense it when a tower flexes: a subtle, delayed ripple as the fluid surges down certain conduits and withdraws from others, like blood shifting in a limb.

Nutrient exchange and signaling pulses move as colored ripples, faint and fast across surfaces, strobing in patterns like computer activity lights. Photosynthesis—as much as there is in this dimness—may not even be primary; the plants seem to feed on gradients: heat differentials between core and air, voltage differences along their own bodies. The entire plateau-sized organism might be an energy harvester tuned to fields and fluxes humans don’t quite measure yet.

---

### Atmosphere and sound

The air is cold but not still. Winds cross the plateau in steady, laminar flows, seldom gusting, drawn by unknown pressures far beyond the horizon. You hear them first as a distant organ note, then as they pass through and around the plant-structures the sound grows and splinters:

- A bass howl as air threads through hollow towers.
- A thick, throaty boom when a broad trunk resonates.
- Higher, fluting whistles and shrieks where narrower apertures channel the wind into precise frequencies.

The entire living city is an instrument the atmosphere plays, loud and never fully silent. Sometimes the wind drops, but internal sounds remain: creaks, pops, the fibrous tension of tissues re-setting; deep, percussive thuds as some gigantic internal valve slams shut; a dry, rain-like patter as micro-seeds or desiccated scales flake from high surfaces and drift down through the open spaces.

Communication rides these sounds. Some are too low or too regular to be random: sequences of pulses ricocheting through tower-forests, interference patterns set up deliberately. The city-plant listens to itself across miles, like a brain humming through white matter tracks of air.

---

### Fauna: moving through the plant

In this mostly-plant world, animals are frequent but never dominant. They are adapted not to water, but to the endless dry architecture.

Closest at hand are the **gliders**: membranous creatures that look like segments of leaf torn free and given purpose. Their bodies are thin and planar, veined with flexible structural fibers, colored in muted browns and greens that match the plant substrate. They launch from high apertures, catching the cold streams of wind to glide silently, adjusting small ripples in their membranes to steer. Some are the size of your hand, flickering by like scraps of cloth; others span six meters tip to tip, riding airflow from district to district.

On landing, they fold and compress into surprisingly dense, articulated things—jointed ribs emerging from within the membrane, legs unfolding like origami. They scuttle along walls, seeking nutrient-exuding nodes, biting into hardened plant-tissue with serrated mouthparts that grind dry fibers into powder. Moisture is scarce enough that their excretions are nearly solid, chalky, drifting away as dust.

Deeper in the hollow trunks you see **lattice worms**: long, angular bodies made not of smooth cylinders but of faceted segments like linked crystals. Each segment has micro-hooks that grip the internal scaffolds of the plant; they move in a rippling, almost mechanical gait. As they pass, sensitive filaments extend from the walls, brushing their sides, reading whatever charge or chemical marker they carry before withdrawing.

There are predators too, and they are loud.

Far above, clamped near the midpoints of arching towers, hang **resonance hunters**—massive, disk-like creatures that look like dark, open flowers turned sideways. Their radial “petals” are stiffened plates studded with sensory cilia. They vibrate these plates, sending probing sounds into the interior arches and listening acutely for echoes from moving prey. When they detect a target—usually a flock of gliders—they unfold briefly into a net, an expanding spiral of filaments that snaps shut around the flight path with terrifying speed. Caught creatures beat hopelessly against the mesh, their thin bodies cracking like dry leaves.

Every capture is announced by noise: a sharp, whipcrack percussion as the disk snaps shut, a ragged rustle of struggling wings, then the slow, grinding digest of the hunter folding its net inward.

Smaller symbionts teem at micro scales: mites that look like walking lattices of translucent rods; tiny, insect-like bots—no, not bots exactly, but creatures so clean-lined and regular they appear manufactured—that patrol the plant’s surfaces in geometric swarms. They rearrange dust, harvest debris, and perform minute repairs on torn tissue, suturing fibers together with secreted nanothreads that shimmer before hardening.

---

### Built, but not by hands

This is a city: there are patterns too intentional to be mere growth. Pathways recur. Hollow corridors run straight for improbable distances, branch at regular intervals, then rejoin. Some arcs are tuned: when the wind hits them, they hum at exactly matched frequencies across kilometers, as if syncing clock pulses.

Embedded in many surfaces are objects that are not simply plant-tissue. Dark, lens-like inclusions—oval and perfectly smooth—sit flush with bark. Approach one and it irises open, exposing an interior of nested, rotating microstructures that look more like machines than organs. They emit no light, take nothing obvious from the environment. Yet you notice that the networks of luminous veins around them brighten in synchronous pulses when they turn, like the way neurons fire around an active implant.

Some arches carry along their undersides thick cords of material that is neither bark nor vine—composite cables, faceted, metallic in sheen, grown directly into the plant matrix. Within them flows the strange, oily sap, but also something else: alternating bands of shifting iridescence that travel faster than physical fluid could move. Signal and carrier share the same conduits, physics and bioelectricity braided into a single infrastructure.

From a distance, a district of this city resembles a carefully engineered bridge network, with load-bearing members, redundant supports, optimized spans. That suggests design—purpose beyond survival. But where are the builders?

The answer may be unsettling: the builder is the plant itself, or whatever intelligence has long since merged with it.

### Tech beyond modern

The longer you stay, the more the plant-city’s subtler capabilities become apparent.

At the very base of several towers, the cortex-thick mat of ground has parted to reveal smooth, concave basins of living tissue. They are dry, lined with fractal, scale-like plates. Occasionally, a scuttling fauna-creature or a drifting accumulation of dust falls in. Nothing dramatic happens at first. Then, plates shift, reconfigure, and an opaque, shimmering field wavers into being a few centimeters above the basin.

It’s not transparent; it is more like looking at refraction in an unseen medium. Edges behind it bend oddly, lines dislocate. Whatever these fields are, they are manipulating not light but something more fundamental—local metrics of space or the behavior of the plant’s exotic solvent. Dust that passes into the field slows, stretches, and then is simply gone, its matter translated into some other form the plant can use directly.

Elsewhere, in the hollows of certain major trunks, you find chambers ringed with loops of coiled, glistening fiber. Inside these loops, mid-air, float complex patterns of fine dust and filament, holding shape against gravity and vibration. They rearrange themselves slowly, like crystals growing in reverse. The loops hum faintly at the edge of hearing and emit gentle pulses across the whole structure: updates, perhaps, or checksums.

These chambers feel like processors, memory cores, or something stranger: physical simulations run at a scale of dust and field, not electrons. The plant-city is thinking, not just growing.

Gravity itself is not entirely trusted. In some corridors, loose objects do not fall in the direction you expect. They drift sideways toward certain walls, as though drawn by fields anchored there. Plant-structures in these zones are evenly spaced, spherical rather than drooping, as if grown free from normal weight. The technology here is subtle: not flying cars or neon towers, but an organism that has learned to edit the local rules its body lives under.

### Light and the sky

Because the sun (or whatever star glows beyond the constant veil) is dim, the sky is a featureless bruised gray with occasional lighter bands. There is no blue scattering; the dryness and altitude leave the atmosphere thin, almost metallic in taste. Fine particulate blows high above—pale, mineral dust from some distant desert, or spores from other megaflora-worlds if they exist, or the slow fall of cosmic grains captured by this world’s gravity.

At irregular intervals, a glow crosses that sky—horizontal, like a comet refusing to arc downwards. It’s not a natural meteor: its wake is structured, a procession of repeating bright knots, as though a chain of precise detonations were walking across the upper air. When this happens, all the luminous veins in the city-plant flicker in synchrony. The soundscape changes. The towers still, their inner motions pausing, as if to listen.

After the glow fades, new growths appear within hours: small, antenna-like spines sprouting from high structures, orienting toward where the phenomenon passed. Whatever those streaks are—orbital machines, atmospheric currents harnessed as wires, or something even less humanly nameable—they are part of the same network. Tech here does not sit on top of ecology; it is ecology.

Night, when it comes, is not dark. The plant-city brightens inwardly. Veinnets that were faint in day glow gently. Hollow trunks fill with a diffuse, unshadowed radiance, as if lit from within by trapped auroras. In the open air, strands of airborne filament drift up from the substrate, hanging in place like low, stationary constellations: tiny seeds or sensors catching whatever signals night brings. Above, the sky is a deep, almost-black violet, punctured by more stars than the thin air should allow.

### Rules and rhythms

This world has settled into an endgame of forms. The rock has yielded what it will; the atmosphere has found a stable poverty of water; the vast plant-city has refined itself over ages to a configuration that barely needs change. Yet it is not static. Its activity is interior, algorithmic; change happens more in the patterns of flow, field, and signal than in fresh biomass.

There is weather, but not of storm and rain. The main cycle is in **field-tides**: slow, planet-wide shifts in the background forces the plant taps. Every so many hours—or days; it’s not obvious—the hum under your feet deepens, the luminous nets brighten, and the entire plateau subtly flexes. Arches tense, trunks lean minutely, as if resisting an invisible current. During high field-tide, more of the basin-fields activate, more processing chambers hum, more fauna go to ground or cling tight to sheltered undercurves.

Predator-prey cycles are synchronized to this. During low-tide, when the plant’s power is quieter, the gliders are busiest, migrating between districts in restless flocks. The resonance hunters conserve energy then, drooping limp, their nets half-folded, sensing only lazily. During high-tide, they are frantic, nets snapping at the slightest vibration, while smaller, parasitic creatures emerge from cracks in the substrate to comb the surfaces for the stunned and fallen.

Rules that might be fundamental here:

- **No free water.** Life is forced to be economical, to conserve every volatile molecule. This makes structures tough, dry, long-lasting. Nothing rots; it simply erodes into dust and is reclaimed via strange fields.
- **Plant as infrastructure.** There is no distinction between building, power grid, nervous system, and biosphere. They are one organism; even “districts” may only be cognitive modules, specialized lobes of a planet-scale mind.
- **Noise as language.** The landscape is loud because sound is cheap here and water-based transmission is impossible. Every howl of wind through a tower, every creak and crack, can carry information—if you know the code.
- **Physics as substrate.** This civilization—if that’s what it is—has not laid machines atop nature; it has learned to slightly edit the background rules: gravity pockets, weird refractive fields, space-bending basins. Technology is not separate infrastructure but extensions of physiology.

---

### Emptiness and presence

For all its loudness, much of this plateau feels empty. You can walk for long stretches along the curving interior of a trunk, or over an arch broad enough to be a highway, and see no other large creature. The scale is regional: a whole valley given over to a single cluster of arcs; another valley almost bare, with only the mat substrate textured by low, dome-like bulges where new growth could one day rise.

The sparseness gives every presence weight. A single enormous resonance hunter clinging to a distant tower becomes a landmark. A small flock of gliders is an event. When, rarely, a truly massive shape moves—a whole tower twisting half a degree, or the substrate shuddering under some deep reconfiguration—you feel it as a planetary breath.

This is a place at the far end of its own history: a high, cold, ancient plateau where rock has nearly finished its story, and plant has taken over the roles of city, machine, and mind. It is dry past the imagination of deserts, yet saturated with life of a kind that barely remembers water ever existed.

You stand in a wind that screams through living arches, listening to a metropolis that is also a single organism, old enough that erosion itself has grown tired of arguing with it.
