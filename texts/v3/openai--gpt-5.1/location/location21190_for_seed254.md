You step into a volume of space where almost everything that exists is plant.

Not trees in soil, not vines on stone, but plant as wall, floor, ceiling, pillar, bridge, duct, and roadbed; plant as air-filter and cable-trunk and drainage channel. There is no bare ground here to find. If there is stone, it is deep underneath, sealed away beneath centuries of root and pith and fibrous accretion.

The place is enclosed, but not by masonry. Imagine rooms and avenues the size of a large cathedral nave, one pressed close to the next, all of them sculpted out of a single, continuous living mass. The scale is local, bounded: you could spend a day walking and never leave the district, but you always feel the curve of surrounding growth, the sense of being inside something that has a finite, comprehensible extent—perhaps the size of a compact town, a few dozen interlocking halls and canyons and shafts.

You are below the surface of some larger world. There is a ceiling to this city-forest, and it is a roof of old, thickened tissue: laminar sheets of translucent plant matter, pale and veined like the underside of a leaf, curving gently overhead. Above that, somewhere, is daylight, seasons, wind. Down here you get only the diluted remainder of those things.

The light is dim, a greenish-amber dusk that never fully becomes day and never fully becomes night. Sunlight filters through meters of living canopy and soil above, arriving mostly as a glow that seeps through those overhead membranes. In places, the ceiling is thinner and the glow pools down in shafts, enough that you can see distance and shape; in others it is merely a suggestion, and the air turns into a murky, olive twilight. The only sharp highlights come from occasional strands of dull bioluminescent growth—threads and nodules along vascular conduits, glowing with a weak, constant blue-white to mark important routes—but nothing ever approaches glare. Your eyes adjust to shadow as a default condition.

Water is everywhere, but almost nowhere free.

The air is wet to the skin: warm and perpetually humid, just below oppressive, scented with green and rot and mineral tang. Surfaces glisten. Every wall, every arch of living wood-flesh, sweats faint condensate. The city’s “streets” are narrow corridors between massive columns and buttresses of plant tissue, and their floors are soft, spongy mats alive with roots and fine hairs that sip every droplet that lands. Where two surfaces meet, slow trickles gather—pale, clear streams running down into channels that are not carved stone gutters but longitudinal cavities in the living structure, lined with velvety tissue, drinking as much as they carry.

Here and there are sump-pools: shallow basins a few meters across, edges rounded and lip-like, holding still, dark water. Their surfaces are flecked with luminous bacterial film, faint points of phosphor in the dim. Tiny bubbles rise where deep roots respire. These pools are not stagnant, but their turnover is slow, a breath measured in days rather than seconds. Beneath them, unseen, broad vascular conduits pull water down, distribute it sideways, exhale it upwards as vapor into the breathing air.

No animals move in this place. None flutter, none scurry, none hum. You can search every crevice and find no wing case, no scattering of bones. The biomass is wholly plant and microbial, the motion slow and collective rather than individual. You realize the absence only gradually, because the city is not quiet.

It is loud in a strange, unpeopled way.

The dominant sound is a deep, continuous, many-layered murmur: the noise of fluids under pressure moving through living conduits. Somewhere between the distant rush of a subway and the interior of a great industrial boiler room, but organic. You hear it as a background roar that never goes away, rising and dipping as great vascular trunks beneath your feet and overhead pulse. Lower frequencies are felt as much as heard, a muscular thrum in the floor and walls, like the heartbeat of something too large to name. Superimposed on that are more articulate noises: the hollow gurgle of water dropping inside a vertical shaft; the wet hiss of sap being shunted through narrowing passages; the creak and faint crackle of plant fibers swelling or relaxing as moisture levels change hour by hour.

Because the entire “city” is plant, its infrastructure is also anatomical. What looks at first like a crowded tangle of buttress-roots and hanging lianas resolves, with time, into deliberate organization. Load-bearing trunks flare into arches and ribs; between them run bundles of tougher, corded tissue, arranged like cable trays and ventilation ducts in an industrial facility. Some of these bundles vibrate audibly, a chorus of muted drone-tones as fluid and air rush through. Others are stiff and quiet, likely the structural elements, layered and cross-braced the way wood grain crosses in plywood.

Everything is packed. There is almost no empty, clean-edged volume here. Narrow corridors twist between living walls swollen with knots and bulges. Vaulted chambers are cluttered from floor to ceiling with pillars, crossbeams, drooping looped cables of vine, layered balconies of shelf-like growth. Even flat surfaces tend to be textured: ridged, pocked, grown over with fine mats of supporting tissue. When space opens up—an atrium the size of a chapel, a tall shaft reaching toward the overhead membrane—you feel it as a sudden luxury of distance, an improbable vacancy in the thicket.

Despite the density, this is not wild tangle. There is system to it, the kind that speaks of long use and gradual refinement, not of sudden construction. Pathways are regular enough that you can follow them: routes where the plant-fabric has toughened into smooth, slightly springy causeways, free of obstructing growth up to a certain, consistent height, like corridors milled inward from all directions. Turns happen at repeatable angles; vertical transitions—ramps and spiral ramps, not stairs—appear where you might expect in a building designed for heavy movement of material. You see repeating modules: similar branching arrangements of transport conduits, standard patterns of reinforcing growth around major junctions. This is a plant-city, but it is also a methodical plant-factory.

The technology here is industrial, but it has long since given up metal scaffolds and bare wheels in favor of embedding its machinery into the living substrate.

Within the thicker conduits and trunks you can hear—and sometimes see through thin spots—the work of internal “engines.” Not combustion engines; there is no sharp smell of exhaust, no clank of pistons. Instead there are peristaltic chambers: repeating bulbs that contract in sequence, muscular sacs driven perhaps by electrochemical waves, pushing fluid along. Some of these pumps are large enough that their cycle produces a noticeable shift in the background sound: a long, rising pressure-hum, then a soft, thunderous whomp as valves flex. Their operation is regular, the tempo ranging from a slow beat every few minutes in the largest trunks to a quickened pulse in smaller ones.

Electrical technologies exist too, but not in the familiar form of copper wires and ceramic insulators. Embedded along certain routes are cords that glisten differently, their surfaces smoother, flecked with regular bead-like nodes. Around these, the air carries a faint, metallic tang and a low, insectile buzz, though there are no insects. If you press your ear close, you hear a high, steady whine, modulating in near-musical sweeps. Here and there, at nodal points, the plant has extruded plates and loops of specialized tissue that might function as capacitors or transformers analogues—thickened, layered pads that crackle almost imperceptibly, their surfaces dulled by constant condensation.

Industrial soundscapes exist within this: in some chambers, many conduits converge, and the overlapping pumps and valves create a complex mechanical rhythm. These spaces feel like machine halls in a factory—gallery-sized, filled with upright vascular trunks, each one throbbing and muttering, the combined noise enough that you must raise your (hypothetical) voice to speak. Yet nothing is external; there are no bolted-on machines, only living ones, grown to shape and function.

The air moves, slowly but persistently. Large, bell-shaped cavities along major routes inflate and deflate, drawing air through porous surfaces, filtering humidity and exchanging gases. Their walls are lined with microfolds that shimmer with condensed moisture; when they contract, they make a sound halfway between a sigh and a distant bellows, adding to the continuous tumult. Air is pulled through narrow, labyrinthine pores in the surrounding tissue, cleansed of spores and particulates by adhesive gels and fine hairs before being released into the open corridors again. The result is an atmosphere that smells thick but clean, heavy with organic volatility but not choked with dust.

Although no animals move here, life is not absent in its details. On every surface, tiny films of algae and bacteria, dull-gleaming in the low light, live in symmetrical partnership with their macro-plant host. At channel mouths, you see delicate fringe-like growths, translucent and pale, flexing almost imperceptibly as fluid levels change: perhaps filters, perhaps sensors. Stomatal pores the size of fingernails open and close on rhythmically breathing surfaces. You glimpse slow-motion adjustments: a cable-thick vine subtly tightening its curve over hours, thickening where stress is constant, thinning where load has lessened.

The city is mature, settled. Nothing here looks newly built; everything carries the patina of long function. Corners have rounded over as tissues swelled and fused. Old pathways, no longer in primary use, have been sealed by gradual growth and now appear as faintly recessed scars—a doorway outline filled with younger, smoother tissue, flush with its surrounds. Where major conduits have been long decommissioned, they persist as distorted fossils within the walls, their once-hollow cores now packed with darker material, like old street tunnels backfilled and forgotten. The look is of an organism that has evolved its own architecture not over years but over centuries, pruning and elaborating its vascularized city-plan to an equilibrium.

Yet it is not a place of rapid change. Although everything is metabolically alive, the large-scale layout alters only slowly. The pumps keep beating, the conduits keep flowing, but new arches, new chambers, form on timescales longer than any individual human life would notice. This industrial organism has reached a self-sustaining plateau: water in, waste out, structure stable. The world above may have seasons, storms, even civilizations rising and falling; down here, the great plant-machine adjusts only gradually, thickening a buttress here over a decade, rerouting a trunk-line there over a century.

You realize, after a while, that you are inside something that obeys a fairly strict set of rules:

1. **Plant is both material and machine.**  
   There is no distinction between building and infrastructure, between wall and pipe. Every surface potentially carries load, fluid, signal. Modification must be grown, not assembled. Industrial processes—pumping, filtering, pressurizing, even perhaps synthesizing complex compounds—are carried out by differentiated organs embedded in the fabric.

2. **Water is the primary currency.**  
   The whole city is organized to catch, distribute, use, and recirculate water in a world where it is frequent but not superabundant. The weeping walls and condensing ceilings are part of the system: moisture from above percolates downward; internal processes return vapor; everything is designed to minimize loss. Free, open bodies of water are rare and shallow, always interfaced tightly with absorptive tissue.

3. **Light is ancillary, not foundational.**  
   The low illumination implies that most of the city’s energy is not drawn from direct photosynthesis here. Perhaps its major photosynthetic organs are far above, at the surface, or perhaps the system has adapted to other energy sources—chemosynthesis in mineral-rich groundwater, for example. Down here, light is enough for maintenance and signaling, not for heavy growth.

4. **No animals: no predation, no pollination, no symbiotic grazers.**  
   The plant-city is closed in that sense. Reproduction, where it happens, must be vegetative and slow, creeping at the margins. Internally, the lack of chewing, tunneling, burrowing animals means structures can remain intact for very long periods, and surfaces can be delicate and complex without risk of being eaten. The ecosystem is simplified, but also more machine-like, free from the messy contingencies of animal behavior.

5. **Noise is intrinsic.**  
   The large-scale movement of fluids in resonant cavities guarantees a loud environment. There is no concept of a quiet district; rather, sound is a constant condition, a byproduct of the same processes that keep the organism-city alive. If any intelligence operates here—plant or otherwise—it would “hear” the state of its own system through these sounds, able to detect a failing trunk by a changing note.

6. **Change is metabolic, not eventful.**  
   There are no sudden collapses, no rapid growth spurts, no violent shifts; instead, the place responds to external changes by minute adjustments distributed over time. If less water arrives from above one decade, valves constrict: certain trunks narrow, peripheral regions desiccate slightly and toughen, superfluous spaces are quietly reclaimed. If more water arrives, stored reserves are expanded, walls soften here and there to thicken, surplus is shunted to deep retention sacs.

The terrain underfoot varies with function. In transit corridors, it is a firm, slightly resilient mat—dense, interlocked fibers shedding water quickly, textured with shallow grooves that channel stray moisture sideways into drinking tissues. In processing chambers, the floor is more uneven and spongy, full of minor ridges and depressions where specific reactions take place, collecting pools of enzyme-rich liquid before they are wicked away. Along major veins, the ground arches in a gentle crown over buried trunks, and the sound of fluid beneath creates a continuous subsonic vibration, turning your steps hollow.

Vertical space is used aggressively, given the limitation of horizontal room. Chambers often have layered walkways—ribbons of tougher growth that run at different heights along the walls, like balconies or catwalks in a factory. These are narrow and bordered by low ridges of stiffer tissue that keep you from slipping into adjacent voids. Above, more conduits: some hanging, some spiraling, some anchored like flying buttresses between pillars. Every cubic meter is doing something—transporting, supporting, exchanging.

Materials, to your touch, blur the lines between wood, flesh, and polymer. Some surfaces are dry and smooth, like polished hardwood, but with a faint give when pressed. Others are rubbery, with a subtle warmth. Still others, especially around the sump-pools and filtration organs, are wet and velvet-soft, beaded with droplets that reform seconds after you brush them away. In quiet alcoves of low throughput (quiet only relative; the roar seeps even into stillness), thin membranes stretch across gaps like drumheads, quivering in response to distant pulses.

Rust marks, crumbling concrete, corroded metal—none are evident. If any non-plant artifacts once existed here, they have been fully assimilated or overgrown, replaced by organic equivalents. The “industrial”ness is expressed not in steel beams but in repeating modules, high densities of conduits, the constant movement of fluids, and a sense of systemized throughput. There may be regions where specialized organs crack minerals, leaching useful ions from slow throughflow of groundwater; others where complex molecules are synthesized and stored in bulbous, tank-like growths, their walls thicker and more opaque than the surrounding network. Those tanks hum at a different pitch, and their exteriors sweat unusual-smelling vapors: sharp, resinous, chemical.

Subtle gradients structure the city. Moving “upward”—toward the barely brighter portions of roof—you find tissues slightly thinner, drier, more latticed, admitting more of the weak light. Here, fine netted growth extrudes into shallow, free-hanging laminae, perhaps the basal reaches of a giant photosynthetic complex above. Moving “downward,” toward the darker, lower basins, the air grows marginally cooler and more still. The walls become denser, darker, and wetter, riddled with slow-dripping pores and micro-caverns full of concentrated solutions. The background sound lowers in pitch, as major trunks descend into the earth, drawing on deep aquifers or mineral seams.

You never find an edge in the usual sense—no abrupt end of the plant and exposure of rock or soil. The nearest thing to a boundary is where corridors narrow, conduits thicken and coalesce, and the architecture becomes more monolithic and less articulated. There, you meet continuous, gently undulating walls of root-flesh, almost featureless except for the slow passing of peristaltic waves within. Those walls are not meant for passage; they are the outer hull of the organism-city, its interface with the substrate it penetrates. Beyond them lies whatever geology this world offers, but you cannot go there without breaking the living skin.

So you remain within: in humid dusk, among resonant trunks and murmuring vaults, in a town-sized labyrinth of engineered plant, packed so fully that emptiness feels anomalous. The whole landscape is less like a forest you walk through and more like a heart or liver you have shrunk small enough to traverse, industrialized and given just enough geometry that it masquerades as “urban” to a human eye.

It has been in this state a very long time, and it will likely remain much the same for a very long time to come—pumping, filtering, thickening, listening to the thunder of its own circulation in the dim, wet dark beneath a brighter, more varied world it neither sees nor seems to miss.
