It begins as a sound before it is a place.

A low, continuous roaring, somewhere between wind and surf and an endless crowd breathing in unison, rolls through the dark. It’s too loud for emptiness, and too resonant for any small room of air. The sound rises and falls in long, slow waves, like weather given a voice. Somewhere under it, another layer: crackle, creak, a vast, irregular grinding as living materials strain and shift.

Then your eyes adjust, or something in the dark does.

You are high. That is the first orientation that makes sense. The air is thin and biting, so cold it has texture, a fine, granular sting that slicks onto exposed surfaces as hoarfrost. Moisture here is not abundant, but it is persistent: beading as condensation along edges, icing in sheltered hollows, blooming briefly as fog when warm breath or some exhalation of the substrate meets air.

There is no sky as you know it.

Overhead, instead of a dome of air, there is a vaulted tangle of plant matter so immense and so distant it almost reads as geology. Columns, ribs, and planes of pale, fibrous tissue arc across the void, vanishing into occluding darkness. They are not stone. They flex, slowly, with the rhythm of something larger than weather: continental musculature, or an organism whose scale has made it indistinguishable from the world.

From those heights, thin curtains of condensation drift downward: mist that glows faintly of its own accord, outlining the bones of the air. The darkness is not absolute; it is saturated with dim gradients and rare, concentrated points of luminescence. Here, patches of lichenous growth clinging to a pillar emit a cold milk-blue light. There, a strand of vine bottles a brighter, acid-green glow in nodules along its length. Far away, the overlapping silhouettes of colossal fronds are rimmed in a faint silver, as if some other, brighter realm were shining through tiny seams in this one.

There is a sense of enclosure, but also of enormity. Like standing on a ledge inside the living atrium of a planet-sized cathedral.

Beneath your feet, there is no soil in the conventional sense. Everything solid is plant.

You stand (if standing is possible here) on a broad, gently convex plane of interlocking woody plates, each plate the size of a house, growing in slow spiraled patterns like immense scales. Their surfaces are ridged, cold, and slightly yielding under pressure. Between them, narrow fissures open into blackness. When the structure flexes with some distant internal motion, the cracks widen and close with a disconcerting, vertebral creak.

From those fissures spill roots and ropes of vegetative tissue, reaching downward into depths unseen. Some have bark-like skins and pulsing cores. Others are translucent, cables of gelled sap in which glittering particles stream, wanly luminescent—nutrients and signal flows moving through the planetary body.

The architecture of this place is grown, not built, yet it has the legibility of intentional design. Massive buttress-trunks, spiral ramps of braided vine, archways of fused branches. The terrain rises and falls in spreading terraces, ramped ridges, steep drops. Cliffs here are vertical growth-fronts: shelves and lips of tissue jutting out into the dark, newly extruded and still smooth. This world is young; edges are sharp, surfaces unweathered by anything but frost and the slow scratch of climbing roots.

Despite the dominant solidity of plant mass, the overall impression is of emptiness. Vast, hollow volumes lie between supports, trunks, and spans: giant nave-like spaces, open shafts plunging downward for kilometers, narrow ledge-paths skirting yawning voids. You can go long stretches without seeing anything but scaffolding-vines and the faint scatter of distant luminescent patches. When something moves in those spaces, its presence is magnified by the emptiness, every wingbeat or leap whispering through the air.

The ecosystem is all-plant, but not monotonous. Everything that occupies the category of “ground,” “rock,” and “architecture” in another world is taken here by botanical analogues, specialized into niches.

There are wide, membranous canopies stretched horizontally like sails, their upper surfaces slick with condensed moisture, their undersides a forest of dangling rootlets that comb the air. When the world-breath—those great pressure shifts far above—moves through, the canopies tilt and flex, cup and release, channeling faint rivers of condensed water down into braided gutters of tissue. Here, liquid does not fall as rain so much as migrate along guided pathways, a traveling shimmer flowing over and through plant structures, sought after by anything that moves.

Elsewhere, columns the width of mountains carry the overall load of this vegetal crust. Their surfaces are layered with strata like tree rings gone vertical. Between the ridges grow forests of smaller epiphytic forms: thick mats of tubular growths that open and close rhythmically, sucking in the thin air and exhaling faintly warmer vapor; lace-like nets of tendrils that flash with bioluminescent patterns when disturbed; solid fungal plates (if “fungal” still has clear meaning in a world where all is plantlike) layered like battlements.

In pockets sheltered from the main wind, finer ecological tapestries gather. Here, where a concavity captures drip after drip of condensate, a dangling basin has grown: a shallow bowl of resilient tissue forever slick with frigid water, its surface furred with microorganisms that glow in slow pulses. Tiny, glass-bodied grazers—fauna at a scale no larger than fingertips—skim along the meniscus, feeding, chased in turn by minute darting hunters whose translucent bodies flash sharp warnings when they dart from water to air.

Farther from these bowls, the plant surfaces dry, their textures shifting to leathery plates that crack stiffly in the cold. Frost grows there in fractal feathers each cycle, then is scraped away by the passage of larger things.

The fauna are abundant but spread through a giant volume. This is a place of three-dimensional movement; there is no singular “ground level.” Life has adapted to traversing verticality and void.

A shadow flicks past overhead, cutting off the faint light for a moment. Then another, and another: a flock of something—but “flock” may be wrong. They do not maintain formation, yet their paths echo and counterpoint each other as if choreographed by some invisible gradient in the air. Their wings are not wings, but arrays of flexible fronds that ripple like kelp blades in a current, catching air in waves, allowing the creatures to swim from thermal to thermal. They call to each other constantly, a rain of echolike clicks and long, quavering tones that bounce off the plant architecture and give them a sonar-map of the shifting voids.

Lower down, closer to your level, you might see a hunter: a loping, many-limbed thing whose limbs are naked, musculature visible under a thin translucent hide, but whose torso is thickly overgrown with vampiric symbiont-vines. The vines tighten and relax as it moves, milking its warmth, using the moving body as a pump to draw nutrient-rich sap from pierced plant surfaces it brushes past. The creature listens more than it sees: tufted sensory organs cradled in cartilage dishes, oriented constantly toward the roaring wind and the scratching, chittering, rustling of other lives. When it leaps, it launches out over horrifying drops without hesitation, confident in the tangle of load-bearing vines and flexing ramps that await its grasp.

Everything here hunts and flees in three dimensions. There are aerial pursuers that dive silently through the dark, guided by the bioelectric currents of the plants: the substrate radiates weak fields, and living movement warps them. There are wall-crawlers whose limbs terminate not in claws but in fibrous fans that knit themselves for a moment into the plant tissue, chemically bonding, then releasing with a soft peel. There are opportunistic scavengers that lurk in the cracks between plates, their bodies thin as blades, sliding out when vibrations signal a nearby kill, then flattening themselves into the substrate again, nearly invisible in the darkness.

The place is loud not only with wind and structural moans, but with voices.

Plants, at this scale, vocalize.

They do not speak in your sense, but their tension and release, their sap surges and gas exchanges, their periodic spasms as they flex to adjust load or reposition canopies, all manifest as sound. Occasionally, a deep bass thud echoes through the world as some huge support shifts slightly to accommodate growth elsewhere. A cascade of crackles follows when a cluster of smaller branches shear away and tumble into the abyss, tearing loose epiphytes whose distress-fluorescence flickers as they fall, a brief, blinking constellation extinguished in the shadows below.

Resonant cavities—chambers grown inside massive trunks or between tightly looped vines—amplify these movements into continuous organ-tones. A distant, rising hum might be the pressure of nutrient fluids accelerating through a narrowed channel; a throbbing pulse could be a cycle of contraction and dilation that moves heat through the vegetative shell of the world. These sounds have rhythms, some tied to the fast weather of this place—gust and lull, condensation and frost—others to incomprehensibly long growth-patterns, cellular tides that take years to complete a single beat.

Scattered through this living grandeur are marks that do not belong to the slow, blind purpose of the plants. The built.

You do not see cities. No grids, no wide spans of regular geometry or repeated module. The density is too low, and the effort here is too dear. Instead, you find single structures, small clusters of modified space, simple compression of intent into local form.

On a ledge overlooking a deep, organ-like shaft, a ring of fibrous stakes has been driven into the substrate, their ends charred, the wood around them burned and blackened. Between them, webbing of cured leaf-skin and animal sinew stretches, a conical shelter whose roof is patched with layered bark and stitched strips of some membranous plant. Inside, warmth glows, not orange like fire, but a steady, muted blue: a cluster of domesticated bioluminescent pods, coaxed to maximal brightness and suspended from the roof. Rough tools hang nearby: blades of hardened sap-glass with braided-fiber handles, scrapers made of dense root-nodes polished to a fine working edge, hooks grown painstakingly from bent branches and then cut and re-anchored.

These people—whoever they are—do not warp the entire world, but they learn the pliancies of its tissues. A path extends from the shelter: a series of scars and knots along the plant-surface where they have cut handholds and lashing-points. Low ropes, handwoven from the outer bark-fibers and tendon-like vines, mark safer routes along the edge of the ledge, across narrow, bowing bridges of living tissue that flex under weight.

In another place, a basin where condensed water gathers has been deepened and regularized. Its sluice-channels have been smoothed with repeated scraping, then lined with a thin, hammered layer of cold-hardened sap-metal—a kind of primitive bronze or glassy alloy derived from concentrated plant exudates and mineral dust from who-knows-where in this almost-all-plant world. Stakes around the basin hold nets woven from the tough inner fibers of flexible vines, arranged to catch the mid-sized glider-creatures that come here in thick flocks to drink. The arrangement is simple, brutal, effective: a tangle of lines where throats will catch mid-flight.

Sometimes, the built is even more subtle: a section of wall where the epiphyte layer has been selectively peeled back to reveal a pale underlayer of plant tissue that takes scratches well. There, figures and patterns have been incised with stone or bone blades. You can imagine them under hand: spirals that mirror the world’s own growth, jagged lines depicting falls and leaps between ledges, stylized silhouettes of prey-creatures, scaled to monumental size and given many, watchful eyes. The scratches catch frost and faint bioluminescent spores, glowing softly against the dark, easy to find again.

Fire is rare here, constrained by the damp cold and the nature of the substrate. When it appears, it is small and closely held: a carefully-nursed coal of dried plant pith and animal fat, coaxed to a dull red and buried in ash, or a wick made of twisted fibers soaked in slow-burning resin. More common is borrowed light: clusters of harvested glow-organs kept in woven cages; pelts of luminescent lichen scraped into thin blankets that, when warmed by body heat, emit a gentle, ghostly gleam.

This is a young world in its forms, but not necessarily in its civilization. The tech is primitive, yet its adaptation to this vertical, living geology is refined.

Weirdness lies not in any single thing but in their combination: a planet whose crust is a plant, a mountain range of living matter hollowed into caverns and shafts, high, cold, almost airless; a night that is perpetual except for bioluminescence and whatever faint, filtered radiation seeps from outside; and inside it all, a riot of active ecosystems.

Processes here do not rest. Condensation cycles are rapid, tied to pulses of internal heat and external cold. You might feel the temperature rise slightly as a deep convective flush sends warmer sap surging up through the columns, radiating warmth into the cavernous spaces. The mist thickens, droplets coalescing along every edge and hair, then, as the pulse passes, the air thins and chills again, freezing residual moisture into speckled rime.

Predator-prey dynamics shape the soundscape. Sudden crescendos in the background roar might be stampedes of mid-sized climbers tearing along a vertical ravine-wall, dislodging fragments of bark and spurting incident luminescence as they flee. Their pursuers—larger, slower, but relentless—may utter almost no sound, their arrival marked only by the abrupt silencing of smaller voices nearby, the way some hidden sense of theirs quiets the world before a strike.

At intervals, the whole structure experiences a tremor. It is not tectonic in the rock-and-mantle sense, but a global rebalancing: somewhere in the under-layers, growth exceeds support and a section of the plant crust fails, collapsing inward. A distant thunder of rending fiber and snapping trunks rolls beneath everything, reverberating upward through columns and pillars. For long minutes or hours after, debris rains through the emptiness: whole mats of vegetation, dead or dying, drifting downward like burned paper, shedding fragments and organisms that clutch desperately at any surface they brush, trying to anchor before they pass beyond recovery.

These events reconfigure the terrain. New gaps open in canopy and support, light from distant bioluminescent veins now visible through what were once solid masses. Opportunistic hijackers swarm to colonize the sudden wounds: fast-growing creepers whose seeds had waited for just such an opening, blowing their tiny, spore-like forms into the new exposure. They feed on stressed tissues, slowing bleeding sap, knitting edges into new architectures.

At the highest levels—the true “elevation” in this world—you find thinner, more brittle plant growth, struggling against even colder temperatures and thinner air. Here, enormous veil-like membranes stretch horizontally across very wide gaps, so thin they’re almost transparent, bellies sagging with accumulated frost. Gravity’s pull is constant, but subtle differences in tension give these membranes a topography of their own: shallow basins and stretched ridges. Micro-ecosystems live in the depressions, where frost persists longest as icy brine, and in the warmer microcurrents that run along the ridges, where internal heat occasionally leaks.

Out at these extremes, the built is rarer but more striking: a single bridge grown deliberately. A people have learned to “speak” slowly to the vegetative substrate, by repeated wounding and feeding, by guiding young tendrils and bending them. Over generations, they encourage an arch of living fiber to form between two otherwise-disconnected pillars, a direct route where once there was only a terrible detour along crumbling ledges. The bridge is thick, gnarly, its handrails an afterthought, but every knot and twist is a record of continuous cultural attention, of hands that came back year after year to trim, bind, and redirect growth.

Downward, in contrast, is mystery. The hollows fall out of sight, their luminescent specks dwindling to nothing. The sounds from below are lower, slower, more massive: the slow grinding of bulk, the groan of truly titanic supports. There might be greater warmth in those depths, and water more abundant in liquid form, collected in subterranean reservoirs where pressure and insulation permit it. Perhaps there are creatures the size of towns coiled around those pools, feeding directly from the deep vascular structures, never needing to climb.

You inhabit only a mid-band of the giant, living shell, where cold and thin air and intermittent moisture shape a particular style of life. Here, everything is taut, lean, adapted to scarcity and precipice. The emptiness between things—and the darkness that fills that emptiness—makes every presence sharp.

The rules of this world assert themselves gradually:

- Up is toward thinner air, colder temperatures, more brittle and translucent plant structures, more risk of membrane rupture and catastrophic falls through multiple layers.
- Down is toward warmth, heavier structures, more continuous plant mass, possible pools of liquid water, but also greater concentrations of massive life and global-moving processes.
- There is no true “outside”; the universe above the highest canopies may be near vacuum, radiation, something hostile enough that the plant shell itself is the planet’s skin. Light from outside, if any, is faint and filtered; most functional illumination is biological.
- Water does not fall so much as migrate along living channels. “Rain” is a localized event where a membrane fails or a tension-line of condensation reaches a critical droplet weight, releasing a cascade along its own architecture.
- Fire is marginal; warmth is internal or biological. Technology is shaped accordingly: no metallurgy forged in blazing furnaces, but careful exploitation of plant-derived composites, cold-worked metals or glazed organics, harnessed bioluminescence and biochemical tricks.
- Gravity is a constant presence but is not the main obstacle. Distance and void are. Traversal means thinking in webs, not planes.

Despite the strangeness, rhythms emerge. You can imagine a day—though “day” is arbitrary here—as a wave of relative activity. Periodic increases in internal flow warm the central supports; fauna that rely on those updrafts and microclimates become more active, crowding drinking basins, sweeping in migrations along known paths. Predators time their hunts to these rush hours. Between pulses, in the colder, stiller intervals, life contracts: huddled in niches, curled in cavities within the plant itself, metabolic fires banked.

In the near-void space that pervades all this, sound travels with an eerie clarity. A call from far away can arrive with barely any loss, distorted only by echoes. This allows a form of long-distance interaction that substitutes for vision in the dark. Some creatures sing not just to navigate but to claim volume, staking out ownership of entire three-dimensional parcels of space, their songs pulsing along invisible boundaries.

Somewhere, in one such dark volume, humans—or something like them—might stand on a narrow growth-rib, listening. They know the local soundscape like a map. They know which distant groan means a support is aging, which means a fresh crack, which means “avoid this route for a while.” They know the timbre of their neighbor-clan’s work-songs as they coax a tendril into a new shape, generations into a bridge that might serve a hundred more. Their tools hang at their sides, simple, worn, effective. Above them, the roar rises as another breath of the world moves through, and the plants answer with a deep, creaking chorus, and the whole immense hollow organism flexes, alive.
