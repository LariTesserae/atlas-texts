The city sits low in the world, pressed close to the level of its waters.

From a distance, it looks like a dark, sprawling lattice laid across a shallow basin: low-slung blocks, stilted walkways, rail lines, pipes arcing between structures, vents exhaling slow plumes that smear into the dim sky. Nothing rises very high; the whole place seems to have learned not to stand up too far from the ground.

The air is hot, thick, and damp—the sort of heat that does not burn so much as cling. Surfaces are slick or sweating: metal beads with moisture, stone darkens where water seeps from within, and every bit of shade holds a faint, radiant humidity, as if the ground itself is exhaling. It feels like the aftermath of a storm that never properly happened: puddles and glazed wetness everywhere, but no memory of rain.

There is almost no vegetation. No trees along roads, no weeds pushing through cracks, no algae films brightening the water. Rocks are bare, the soil where it shows is a dull, compacted mineral crust, and the city’s margins fade into stretches of flat, emptied ground—mud-ash, packed sediment, and hardpan—broken only by drainage grooves and the occasional low, eroded ridge. Whatever era of plants this world may have had is gone or never arrived; the materials here are inorganic—stone, rusted alloys, ceramic shells, composite panels—plus water and heat and the soft drift of airborne dust.

Water is everywhere but never quite gathers into grandeur. It seeps and hangs more than flows. Narrow, tannin-dark channels thread between infrastructure blocks; shallow basins lie beneath walkways, their surfaces faintly rippled by some unseen agitation below. In cuttings and sunken yards, rock faces weep, slow and steady, forming glassy curtains that feed trickling rills along the edges of slabs. Grates gleam with standing moisture, overflowing occasionally into slim runnels that stripe the streets.

Here and there are broader, shallow expanses: marshlike pans no deeper than a person’s height, spread across the basin floor. Instead of reeds or lily-pads, their surfaces are broken by arrays of pylons and sealed housings, by industrial barges permanently moored and half-grown into their berths, by gantries whose feet sink into silty substrates. The water’s surface is mostly clear, reflecting the murky sky and dull lights of the city’s lower levels, but it carries a faint, oily sheen from the industries around it, and a constant, thin mist drifts upward where warm effluent meets cooler air.

The landscape has been shaped for a very long time. The underlying basin is soft-edged, its contours worn into wide, shallow folds. Old shorelines trace faint arcs across the terrain: low, sedimentary lips where water once stood higher, now turned to banded terraces of compacted silt and salts. Any sharpness comes from human work: cut channels, embankments, retaining walls with smoothed, rounded edges from years of saturation and slow wear. The city appears not as an intrusion but as an added layer on something that settled long ago. Buildings, roads, and conduits follow the natural droop and sag of the basin rather than fighting it. Great ducts and causeways snake along the lowest lines, giving the impression that the whole place is plumbing first and habitation second.

Yet for all that built density, the place feels empty. Volumes of space yawn between structures. Streets are broad and underused. Courtyards lie open and bare, with only a scatter of low machinery or storage crates to break their breadth. Above the water pans stretch long viaducts and catwalks, but few of them carry constant flow; they are open to the sky, their railings crusted with mineral deposits, their surfaces swept clear simply because nothing grows here to take them. Even inside the industrial zones, ceilings span wide, dim halls where a few machines cluster in pools of purpose and light, leaving vast overhead volumes hollow.

The architecture is practical, heavy, and somewhat tired: concrete sleeves stained darker at their bases from perpetual damp; sheet-metal claddings patched and re-patched; skeletal frameworks of girder and truss bridging from block to block. Many structures have hollow under-levels stilted above low water, a maze of pillars and shadows and dripping pipes where the ground is not so much walked on as skirted. Upper surfaces are low terraces and shallow roofs at slightly different elevations, forming a broken second ground-plane across the city.

The built infrastructure is clearly industrial in character. Rail spurs spider out from a few central yards, serving loading docks and processing plants. Cylindrical tanks squat in clusters, ringed with catwalks and ladders. Pipe-racks stride across open tracts, carrying hot, humming flows toward distant stacks and exchangers. There are power lines, but more often power moves through insulated conduits tucked along walls and strapped beneath elevated walkways. Here and there, a slow-churning engine or turbine exhales heat into the already warm air, its enclosure rattling softly.

Despite all this machinery, the place is mostly quiet. Metal ticks and cools; water drips; distant fans turn with a soft, low whine. Somewhere far off, a horn sounds once, mullioned by distance and moisture, and then silence regathers. When something heavy moves—a crane pivoting, a cart passing along a track—the sound travels a long way, laid over the base hush of the city. Voices, if they exist, are too few or too far between to form part of the regular soundscape.

Life, however, is not absent. It has simply taken forms that do not involve leaves.

The dominant presences are animals: swarms, flocks, colonies, packs, shoals—every niche that can be dug, flooded, warmed, or shaded is occupied. In the standing waters under the walkways and in the shallow pans beyond, the surface is often deceptively still, but just beneath it something is always moving. Schools of slender, translucent-bodied swimmers flicker and twist in huge, synchronized sheets, turning in unison so that their bodies briefly catch whatever light leaks downward. Larger, darker shapes slide slowly through them, parting their numbers without urgency. At the edges, the shallows churn with smaller, frantic lives—larval forms darting, flicking to the surface for air, vanishing again.

Along the city’s damp underbellies—the shaded pillars, the undersides of platforms, the interior of culverts—clusters of pale, limblike creatures cling. Some are little more than damp sacks with articulation, filtering the hot, laden air through fringed mouths. Others reach out jointed feelers to pluck at motes drifting by. Without plants, the foundation of the ecosystem has sunk down to waterborne films and microbe-rich sediments. Most visible life here is either harvesting those invisible layers or harvesting the harvesters.

There are no birds in the usual sense, but the upper air is not empty. In the dimness, shapes wheel: gliders with wide, membranous surfaces that ride the slightest thermals off the hot roofs. They are nearly silent, their passage marked only by a soft, arrhythmic flapping when they correct their course. Their underside surfaces are mottled, giving them the look of moving stains across the sky. At intervals they swoop lower, skimming over canals to snatch something from the film of insects barely visible above the water.

Insects—or their analogues—are everywhere, though the word hardly fits. The hot, wet air between structures hosts thin-cloud swarms of tiny, multi-winged things that seem almost to hover in place, quivering in loose, shifting aggregates above vents, sumps, and warm gratings. They are pulled to heat and moisture, forming shimmering columns where vapors rise. In quiet corners, their occasional rasp or faint chiming becomes noticeable, a susurrus at the edge of hearing. In more open areas the sound is swallowed by space and distance.

On exposed stone and metal there is remarkably little growth—no moss, no lichen—just the faint smearing of mineral salts and the dried streaks of old seepage. Whatever might have lain down a vegetative crust here never found purchase, or was scrubbed away by the heat and chemistry of this place’s long-used waters. Instead, the visible textures of surfaces are artificial: weld lines, spalled concrete, the layered shine where countless damp hands or feet have passed, the scuffed arcs where heavy doors swing.

The rules of this world feel straightforward, if harsh.

Heat is a constant, but not explosive: a steady, inhabitable furnace that everything must work with. Water is near at all times—below grates, behind walls, inside pipes, seeping out of rock—but rarely in grand cascades. Gravity is gentle enough to allow long, open spans, yet strong enough that the whole city pools down here in the basin like sediment. Weather exists more as a cycle of saturation than of storms. The sky overhead remains dim: there may be a sun up there, but it is filtered by an almost perpetual overcast, a high, thick layer that turns direct light into a diffuse, directionless glow. Shadows are soft; edges blur quickly with distance.

Time has worn things into a quiet balance. Canals do not cut deeper; they have reached their equilibrium between carrying load and dropping it. The basin does not fill or dry out entirely; it holds its water at a series of long-agreed levels, maintained by ancient infrastructure. The city’s shapes are not new and sharp, but weathered and accommodated: corners rounded, drainage routes instinctive, low points known and given over to the water.

Within this stability, constant small changes unfold. Predators pass above or below, watching. Swarms surge and thin. Migratory patterns are compressed to the scale of the basin: from hotter outworks to marginally cooler central pools; from the open pans at the perimeter into the shadowed culverts beneath the densest industrial zones. Behavior follows gradients of temperature, moisture, and chemical richness more than it follows day and night, because the light never varies enough to matter profoundly.

The emptiness of space between presences is striking. You can stand on a broad concrete ledge at the edge of a shallow pan and feel utterly alone, with nothing but a faint, distant clatter from some rail spur and the low buzz of life hidden in the water below. The nearest visible movement might be a single glider crossing the distant slice of sky between two tank-farms, or the slow pivot of a crane on a far shore. Yet beneath your feet and under the water’s surface, in the hollows below, in the narrow conduits overhead, the density of animals is enormous. They have simply retreated into their chosen layers: inside the liquid, in the ducts, in the sheltered undersides of things.

The industrial systems themselves seem to have adapted around this wildlife, or perhaps the wildlife flowed into the structures long after they were built. Intake grills are layered with fine meshes and slow, deliberate pulsations to avoid mass die-offs. Warm outflows cascade into specially shaped basins that spread and cool the water before it rejoins the wider pans—a concession, perhaps, to prevent local scalding of the ecosystem. Lights are low and shielded; where illumination is necessary, it tends to be directed and constrained, leaving large volumes in gloom. It all suggests a long coexistence: not reverent, not kind, just practiced.

Sound maps this coexistence in a sparse, shifting way. Long stretches of nothing but distant machinery-hum and water-sound are punctuated by sudden bursts: a swarm disturbed into flight, making a faint, rolling rustle; a predator breaching the water with a quick, heavy slap; a vent system dumping pressure with a hollow, breath-like rush. These events echo far in the basin’s low topography, bouncing among structures, fading slowly. Between them, there is the oppressive awareness of how much space lies quiet and unoccupied by human voices or regular, busy clamor.

If you walk, the city reveals itself in layers:

At the ground’s lowest, under the stilted structures, your steps are on slats or narrow walkways just above the waterline. It smells of metal, warmed stone, and a subtle, not-quite-rot that comes from dense biological activity without the sweet overlay of plant decay. The light is dim and greenish from reflections, though there is nothing green alive to cause it—just water and the filtered sky. You see eyeshine staring back from dark corners, whiskered or tendriled forms withdrawing into holes drilled by nothing human.

Climb a little higher, to the main level of streets and yards, and the world becomes sparser, emptier. Broad pavements gleam with films of condensation; weak light presses down from a sky that feels too close and low for how far you can see laterally. Rail lines cross in patient grids. Trucks or trams might roll here, but rarely enough that a stray length of track can sit unpolished by use, filmed with a fine coating of mineral and dust. The animals at this level are mostly passing through: quick, low shapes darting from one drain to another, the occasional glider slanting low to disappear between buildings.

Up at the roofline, where service catwalks stitch one low block to the next, the heat is strongest. Exposed metal radiates warmth; vents breathe out slow plumes. From here, you can look across the basin and understand its scale—a bowl without walls, shallow enough that the horizon is just the far lip of slightly higher ground dissolving into haze. The city does not fill it completely. Between clusters of infrastructure lie open swaths of bare ground and water, lying in a settled pattern that looks older than the built environment itself.

Nothing about this world feels supernatural. Its strangeness lies in its choices: a city without greenery, a wetland whose only visible “plants” are pipes and pylons, an ecosystem built atop microbes and animals, thriving in the warm water and hot air around human machinery. The rules are the same as anywhere: heat moves; water flows; life follows gradients and opportunities. Over time, erosion rounds the sharpness off everything—stone, structure, even sound—until the place rests in a kind of heavy equilibrium.

And within that equilibrium, everything is quietly, perpetually in motion.
