The first thing is the sound.

It’s a layered, living roar — not a single engine or crowd, but a continuous, multi‑voiced machinery of animals and devices and fluids in motion. Booming calls pulse through the air like foghorns; high insectile whines form shifting, beating chords; somewhere, heavy reciprocating pistons chuff, venting steam or something like it in syncopated bursts. The sound never resolves into silence, only thins in patches, like a storm that breathes.

You are underground, but only just. Not in the tight throat of a mine, and not in a deep cavern. This is a hollow running just beneath the surface crust of a world, a low, sprawling vault whose ceiling is close enough that you could touch it from the upper platforms in places, and vanishingly distant in others where it swells then sinks again. Imagine a subway system that forgot to be tunnels and instead became a single, continuous chamber, pressed up under a planet’s hide.

The air is warm and wet in a halfhearted way: not steamy, not tropical, but persistently humid. Your skin never quite dries. Cold beads form on smooth surfaces and gather into thin threads that slip downward along whatever path gravity and root texture provide. It smells of soil that hasn’t seen the sun in centuries, of cut stems, resin, oil, and the iron tang of animal breath in enormous quantity.

Everything that can be called “ground” here is alive, or was.

There’s no bare rock floor, no poured concrete slab. Instead, there is a deep, compacted mat of old plant tissue — roots and trunks and fibrous, spongy scaffolding — layered, compressed, and reworked until it has the consistency of dry cork crossed with cartilage. Somewhere long ago, something soft and green colonized a subterranean void; given time and unbroken moisture, it did not merely grow in it, but made it into itself. The vault is supported by its own vegetation, a self-thickened forest turned load‑bearing.

The dominant “trees” are not trees as surface dwellers would know them. They are pillars, broad at the base, tapering slowly, composed of braided, woody cords that have fused into columns. Each pillar anchors deep into the substrate and rises to meet the ceiling, where it flattens into a lobed plate, like a massive, overlapping mushroom cap of dark, leathery leaves. These caps interlock but do not completely seal the overhead; they form a layered canopy that is more like the underside of an enormous, continuous fungus, riddled with slow‑pulsing vascular ridges.

Light — because there is light, however reluctant — seeps through that ceiling. Thin, algal membranes stretched between the cap lobes glow a tired, vegetal green, brightening and dimming in cycles not entirely in step with the surface day. In some areas, they fail and go dark, weeping condensation like tears; in others, they flare to a deeper phosphorescent amber. Patches of industrial lighting are bolted into this living roof: cage‑enclosed bulbs, humming sodium tubes, low, striplike emitters sunk directly into tissue that has scarred over them. These lights buzz in duets with the bioluminescent pulses, and shadows move in a jagged, strobing rhythm.

Between the great pillars is space — real emptiness, not choked with undergrowth. This isn’t a forest so much as a hall. You could walk thirty paces between supports without touching anything but the occasional drooping vine. The understory is sparse and purposeful: braided root‑bridges, knotted buttress walls, and curtainlike veils of fine, translucent leaves that catch condensation and guide it down into collecting basins. Plants here have specialized away from filling space and toward forming structure.

The built environment is inseparable from the living one. There are no freestanding, alien buildings; instead, the “city” is grown and fitted into the immense, slow‑grown cathedral of plants.

Out from each pillar trunk extend ribs of woody matter, grown horizontally and then trained, cut, and laminated into platforms. Along the sides of these, metal railings have been bolted into the living wood, then swallowed halfway as the organism healed around them. The results look like balconies in a hollow temple: tiered walkways spiraling partway up some columns, forming stacked rings around others, and bridging between neighboring supports in great arcs. Over time, some of these bridges have thickened, been reinforced with wrapped cables, grown ribs of their own, until they are indistinguishable from natural trunks in everything but purpose.

Embedded into these platforms are the marks of industry. Generator housings — armored, riveted carapaces of dull metal — rest on root pedestals like strange fruits. Their flywheels whine and roar, shaking the surrounding wood; the plant matter, far from rejecting them, has grown around and under, turning shock into sway. Tubes and conduits snake outward, some plastic, some braided organic, sprouting like new vines from the machine’s flanks. They carry water, gases, nutrients, power — it’s not always clear which are utilities in the human sense, and which are vascular for the plants.

In some districts, the machinery is overt: stamping lines of chitinous plates, rotating drum‑vats where something is fermented, refined, digested. Here, metal frames hang from the overhead canopy, suspending conveyor belts that look unsettlingly like giant centipedes frozen in the act of crawling, save that they move their cargo with perfect mechanical regularity. There is clanking here, hissing, rapid staccato bursts as pressure is released. The din is thick enough to become a texture in the chest.

In others, the industrial is quieter, more distributed. The pillars themselves carry grafted panels of dull, bioceramic material, as if scale from some other titan species had been pressed into them. Within these plates, faint, cool, blue‑white lines glow and flow, changing pattern slowly over hours — storage, computation, or some other form of processing. Fiber bundles, metallic and organic inseparably twined, descend from them to disappear into the substrate. If factories exist here, they are factories of information as well as matter.

All of this is merely staging for the overwhelming density of animal life.

Fauna dominate like weather. They are not just inhabitants; they are processes, infrastructure, phenomena. Every surface that could be empty is, instead, a pathway or a resting place or a hunting ground.

On the lower level, over the rough, springy floor, move the heavy things. Six‑limbed browsers with flat, expressionless faces and dense, shaggy coats that glisten slightly with moisture lumber in irregular files, their low centers of gravity suited to the uneven substrate. Their feet splay wide, soft and black, gripping the fibrous mat with a slight sucking sound when they lift. They crop at pendant rootlets hanging from the platforms overhead, or press their broad tongues to the condensation curtains, licking them clean. Parasites the size of songbirds hop across their backs, diving headfirst into fur and emerging in tiny, ecstatic sprays of dander.

Threading between them are quick, lean forms — predators or scavengers or both. They move low to the ground on folded, springy limbs, their bodies long and flexible. When they pause, their heads tilt, listening not for sound but for some subtler vibration; a moment later they flick away, having decided, apparently, that something interesting is happening three pillars distant. Their path takes them over and under the great browsers’ legs, past the shuffling feet of mechanical trolleys that follow sunken tracks grown directly into the floor.

Above, the air is thick with flyers.

Not a single unified cloud of birds, but layers: large, slow‑winged gliders that coast on convection drafts and the thermal sigh of engines, small darting things that track in tight swarms around sources of warmth or light, mid‑size beasts with translucent sails for wings that make a low, thrumming note as they beat, modulating with the rhythm of their flight. Many of them are bioluminescent — not in gaudy colors, but in subtle pulses of white and soft green, like the indicator lights on distant machines. When you look up from certain points, the overlapping fields of these signals form three‑dimensional constellations, shifting maps written in the bodies of animals.

Their droppings fall in a steady, powdering rain, but the world has prepared: the upper surfaces of platforms and walkways are coated in a living, mosslike skin that absorbs it greedily, turning waste into nutrient. It is springy underfoot and slightly warm, traversed by glittering beetles that surface to graze and vanish again as your steps send waves through their world.

Closer to the pillars themselves cling other creatures — a spectrum of symbionts. Some are limpetlike, rigid cups affixed to the bark, their rims fringed with cilia that sweep the boundary layer of air. Others are semitransparent sacs, milky with internal fluids that circulate when large animals brush past, as if mechanically pumped. A few have grown along the rails and ladders, co‑opting the built structures: jointed organisms that flex to ease the passage of heavy loads, their movements aligned with the schedules of the conveyor trolleys. It is unclear whether someone designed them, or whether, once industry appeared, evolution simply obliged.

The loudest sounds are not the engines, in the end, but the voices.

From somewhere off in the dim, a great, booming call rolls in, a single note that vibrates in your ribs. It’s answered almost immediately by another, higher and nearer. These are not random cries; they fall into a shifting polyphony, whole herds and flocks locked into slow, rising and falling choruses. Between these, smaller voices fill the gaps: chirps, squeals, barks, oscillating trills like mechanical alarms, complex clicks that bounce from pillar to pillar. Individually, each is just noise; collectively, they become a language of the place, a constant readout of position, hunger, readiness, fear, opportunity.

The city has forms and rules, even within this apparent chaos.

Transit follows the pillars. Vertical circulation is primarily alive: big, muscular tendrils that hang like elevator shafts down which creatures and vehicles ride. Some tendrils grow stiff, with regularly spaced protrusions that can latch into clamps on platform edges; industrial lifts grip them, ride them up or down, then release and coast along gliding rails toward distant balconies. Other tendrils are soft and pulsing, carrying nutrient fluids upward; small animals queue along them, pressing specialized pads against their surfaces to tap a slow, regulated trickle of warmth and sugars.

Strict layering of life keeps the place from collapsing. Some species never touch the floor — they are canopy dwellers, living their whole lives on the underside of the leaf‑plates and among the glittering light membranes, feeding on sap and the condensed fog that forms near the faint heat sources. Others never rise above the mid‑platforms. Predators maintain territories not in horizontal space but by elevation: a long‑bodied hunter might defend everything from floor to first tier; the next species, built for climbing, controls the walkways between second and fourth. Above that, only swift, sharp‑winged shapes and the strange, hovering forms that drift like jellyfish in the warmer air currents.

Human or human‑analog presence is thin, almost an afterthought in the living mass.

Here and there, on secondary platforms tucked between massive supports, there are signs of habitation: neat rows of enclosures built from woven root and salvaged plating; clusters of cables braided into thicker, insulated ropes that vanish into private spaces; small, steady pools of white‑spectrum light that do not pulse like the biological glow. These places feel quiet because their scale is tiny against the enormity of animal motion, but they are not silent — you can hear voices, tools, the clink of containers, the soft hiss of valves as nutrient mixes are adjusted.

The people, when you glimpse them through veils of hanging leaves and passing fauna, move with the wary assurance of those long accustomed to being vastly outnumbered. Their clothes mirror the environment’s logic: layered between woven plant fiber, flexible plating grown or cast, and patches of matte metal. They carry devices that do not try to overpower the noise but carve small pockets within it: local sound‑dampening fields that make conversation possible, projectors that paint faint barriers of scent or frequency to deter the more inquisitive creatures.

Much of their labor is tuning, not building. They adjust the flow through organic conduits so the condensation falls where it’s useful, not where it will rot supports. They splice new mechanical nodes into the vascular mesh, giving the plant‑pillars different processing capacities. They manage herds not by fences, but by modulating light patterns and broadcast calls that certain species have learned to follow or avoid. The city’s “infrastructure” is a living cybernetic of plant, animal, and machine, and their technics lie in maintaining the balance of feedbacks.

Every now and then, this balance shifts in dramatic, visible ways.

A remote pillar, exhausted or diseased, finally fails. Its enormous trunk doesn’t simply topple; the plant has long prepared, sacrificing sections to hollow and weaken itself in a controlled pattern. When the failure comes, it shears, sags, and folds into itself, collapsing with a drawn‑out groan and a muffled, resonant crash that sends a silence wave outward — for a few heartbeats, even the animals fall nearly quiet. Dust, spores, and old, dry leaf‑powder billow up like a slow explosion, then are caught and held in the moist air, sparkling in whatever light penetrates.

In the weeks after, there is frenzy. Decomposition crews, a mixture of autonomous machines and dedicated scavenger species, swarm the fallen mass. Thick‑jawed herbivores tear into the softer tissues; filamentous feeders thread themselves through the wood, pumping out enriched fluids to reservoirs. Industrial harvester units with rotating mouthparts chew and grind, extruding uniform chunks that disappear along undulating belts into distant processors. The collapse isn’t disaster; it’s harvest, renewal, and reconfiguration of the city’s very bones.

Elsewhere, something stranger happens.

A new growth thrusts up from the substrate between existing pillars — not a simple shoot, but a braided, geometric mass of plant tissue that already contains doorways, surfaces that look suspiciously like machine interfaces, smooth sockets where cables and tendrils will later plug in. It pushes upward until it meets the ceiling, then, with a stop‑motion slowness you can perceive if you watch long enough, it begins to unfold. Panels separate, curl outward, harden. Internal cavities flood with small, curious creatures testing the new ecology. Lights that were dormant inside begin to glimmer, aligning themselves with the city’s distributed rhythms.

Someone, somewhere, has triggered the growth of a new district — built not with cranes and bricks, but with templates encoded into the vast genetic logic of the plant‑substrate, instantiated by tweaks in nutrient flow and microclimate. Industrial tech here is not opposed to the organic mass; it has learned to specify it, to ask the living infrastructure for particular shapes and functions and let time — years, maybe decades — and the city’s own metabolism do the rest.

Water is always present, but never abundant. It appears as beads along every underside, as slim, reflective films on certain tuned leaves, as a patchwork of shallow catch‑pools occupying low spots in the substrate. These pools are never still. Small, hyperactive arthropods skate their surfaces, tapping them with filaments that send radial ripples. Amphibious things — if “amphibious” is the right word for animals that transition as easily from liquid to air as from one flavor of moisture to another — rise, pulse, and vanish. The pools scent the air with mineral, faint mold, and trace metals carried from elsewhere through the city’s vascular roots.

Rain, when it comes, doesn’t fall in sheets but in events. Somewhere above, perhaps on the surface, perhaps in higher chambers, a reservoir overflows or a scheduled purge is triggered. A siren‑call sounds — not electronic, but a deep, rolling pattern taken up by certain great throated creatures like a chant. A few beats after, from one area of the ceiling, water begins to descend: not as discrete drops, but as many thin, almost invisible filaments that coalesce, break, reform. It is like walking under a lace of streams. Animals flock to it, organizing themselves by prior right, strength, or speed. The plant surfaces flare brighter as they drink their fill. Below, the old drainage paths receive, carry, and hide the excess, feeding unseen aquifers.

The weirdness of this place is not just in its abundance, but in the way categories have blurred.

Some of the “animals” are plainly machinic under the fur or chitin: knees that bend with a hydraulic subtlety too precise for bone and tendon, eyes that contract in hexagonal segments, chests that occasionally vent a light, odorless vapor. Some of the “machines” have growth rings and scars, and when cut, bleed sap that smells of citrus and metal shavings. The plant‑pillars carry clusters of organs that look suspiciously neural: pleated nodules that shiver and ripple in ways that correlate a little too well with distant events, as if they are anticipating or calculating.

Predation and symbiosis cross into governance. There are recorded instances — etched into certain bark plates in pictographic sequences — of a large predator species culling a particular herd back from some threshold, after which plant health markers improved and power flux in the local grids stabilized. Whether the predator “understands” this role or is simply following an inherited, fitness‑optimized behavior no one bothers to distinguish; the function is what matters. The city is regulated not by code alone, but by ecological contracts written in hunger, fecundity, and growth.

There are rules you learn if you live here, spoken and unspoken.

Do not stand still too long near the condensation veils; something soft‑bodied may mistake you for a new support and begin to colonize your shoes. Do not cross a bridge when the light membranes overhead drop to their darkest phase, because that is when certain winged hunters dive in synchronized patterns and anything silhouetted will be struck. If a section of the canopy starts to flicker irregularly, leave: it means one of the deep‑root neural clusters is about to reroute, and sometimes that comes with seizures in the plant mass — sudden shifts, spasms, a raining of debris.

Above all: treat noise as weather. The background roar is safety; you are one anonymous wave in a sea of motion. Sudden, wide‑area quiet means something immense is happening: a failing pillar, a migration surge, one of the industrial nodes cycling into some rarer, higher function. Conversely, when the din suddenly increases, when every animal seems to cry at once and machines whine to higher pitch, it is rarely panic. Often, it is some collective, species‑spanning excitatory event — spawning, release, discharge.

In the dim intervals, when the bio‑light cycles low and the industrial lamps are throttled, the place feels almost contemplative. You notice the slow distortions in the pillar trunks as they thicken year by year, the faint ticking as small stones embedded in the substrate shift under the weight of centuries of growth. You hear, under the louder voices, a whispering: countless tiny jaws chewing, micro‑machines humming, sap and fluid flowing through capillaries of wood and conduit.

It is a small world in physical extent — no larger than a large cathedral complex or a clustered hamlet — but internally, it is as vast as any city. Every cubic meter is layered with purpose and process. It is old, in the sense that no single architect remembers its beginnings; old enough that cause and effect have looped through many generations, smoothing edges, arriving at a dynamic equilibrium that feels less like design and more like something that had to be this way to continue to exist.

In this underground hall of living architecture and industrial metabolism, of overwhelming fauna and sparse human touch, nothing is truly idle. Even the empty spaces — the gaps between pillars, the air itself, the dim corners where light barely reaches — are busy with exchanged signals, drifting spores, wandering, seeking forms. The place doesn’t feel like a constructed environment so much as a single, self‑regulating organism that happens, incidentally, to be habitable.
