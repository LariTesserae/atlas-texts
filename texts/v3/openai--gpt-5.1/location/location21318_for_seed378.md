Heat comes first.

It presses in from all directions, not just from the naked white sun overhead but from the ground itself, radiating up in waves off stone and metal and shallow films of water. The air is thick with brightness, the sky a hard enamel blue thinned to near-white around the sun. Every edge throws a blade of light; every surface glows or gleams or shimmers.

You are high—high enough that the horizon feels slightly bowed, the distance dropping away in pale, washed-out terraces. But there are no mountains stabbing sharply upward. This is an old highland: a vast eroded plateau softened by time, its once-jagged edges rounded to broad benches, shallow ravines, and long, low escarpments. The rock is a pale, porous stone, something like tufa or old limestone, riddled with cavities and channels from centuries of water passing through.

Water is everywhere and nowhere.

It does not pool in large, clean lakes; instead it seeps from the stone itself. Whole cliff faces darken with slow weeping, forming vertical, glassy films that run down into mossless gutters. The ground at your feet is a patchwork of damp and dry: thin sheets of water over smooth rock; shallow basins filled with cloudy, warm liquid; slick mineral crusts where it evaporates faster than it can replenish. The smell is not of vegetation, because there is none—no grass, no trees, not even a smear of algae green—but of mineral and metal and something faintly chemical, like warm stone washed in distilled salts.

Where the water pauses—inside hollows, in long grooves cut into the plateau, in the shadowed interiors of architectural forms—it grows thick with microscopic life that never quite becomes “plants” as you would know them. There are films and skins, shimmering opalescent layers that change color with the angle of light: violet at a slant, silver-white in direct glare. They cling to stone and submerged metal, undulating almost imperceptibly, releasing tiny bubbles that collect against overhangs like strings of liquid pearls. Touching them would feel like pushing through a very soft plastic that resists and then yields.

The city—if that is the word for it—spreads across this high, wet stone like a network of careful intrusions. It is not dense in the way of old crowded streets; instead its structures stand at respectful distances, each cluster with plenty of open brightness around it. From a vantage point, you see it as long bands and islands of construction separated by bare rock flats and shallow shining channels.

There is a clear organizing principle: everything aligns with the flows of water.

The primary built forms are elongated terraces and low, blocky halls raised slightly above the rock on thick, buttressed supports. Some of them step down-slope in a careful rhythm, each level catching, slowing, and redistributing the weep from the cliffs behind. Their roofs are flat, shadowed by slatted frameworks that look like they might be solar collectors or radiators, angled to shed light and gather something else. From these roofs, water drips or runs through a geometry of spouts and half-enclosed runnels—a city of controlled trickles.

Materials are sober, practical: pale composite panels that do not quite look like concrete, dark matte metal that doesn’t glare, translucent structural elements that catch the light inside themselves and glow. There is ornament only in the pattern of channels and vents, in the way pipes arc with a quiet grace between levels, in the consistent modularity of openings: tall, narrow apertures cut into facades, mostly closed with louvered shades.

Some buildings sink inward instead of rising: circular wells with stepped interiors, leading down into cooler, damp spaces where the air shifts from baked stillness to a heavier, nearly audible hush. Railings line these descents, functional and unadorned, often beaded with condensation.

Industry is present, but subdued. No roaring chimneys, no belching stacks. Instead: square low structures with heavy, insulated sides and minimal windows, their roofs laced with thick cables and boxy units that could be heat pumps, condensers, or power converters. Near them, metal frameworks bridge shallow gullies—pipe galleries carrying water, steam, and something that hums faintly under hand. Tall, skeletal towers rise here and there, not too many, each one a cluster of enclosed spirals and platforms, perhaps for atmospheric intake, perhaps for cooling, perhaps for observing.

Noise is astonishingly absent.

Machines operate, but they are hushed. You hear, if you come very close, a soft interior thrum, the gentle whickering of fans, the breath-like rush of fluids being moved through sealed conduits. From a distance, even the industrial quarter is nearly silent. There is no constant clatter, no mechanical shouting. Sound attenuates fast in this air: it is hot and oddly heavy, and it swallows sharpness.

Wind comes in long, slow pulses, not a constant breeze but a periodic exhalation. When it rises, you hear only a low, dry sweep across stone and metal, a faint rattle from loose plates or hanging lines. When it drops, the world returns to a layered quiet: soft drip, soft lap of thin water against rock edges, occasional hiss where a pipe vents a barely visible plume of vapor.

Yet the place is alive. Not with leaf and branch, but with bodies.

In the absence of vegetation, fauna has filled every available niche. Many are small, cautious things dwelling in the margins between water and stone. At the scale of your hand: flat, translucent arthropod-like creatures that glide along perennially damp surfaces, their undersides fringed with fine cilia that flicker in synchronized waves. They pause under overhangs of mineral deposits, turning to face any movement, their bodies shot through with faint inner luminescence when shadow falls.

In the shallow channels that thread between buildings move sinuous forms: sleek aquatic animals the size of eels, skin the color of pale metal, patterned not with scales but with a dense, tight tessellation of flexible plates. They surface silently, taking in air through slit-like openings along their sides, then slip back under without a ripple. Occasionally, one rests half in and half out of water, basking in the heat, eyes closed to narrow seams.

There are fliers, too, but not with familiar wings. They are more like elongated kites or manta rays of flesh, bodies thin and wide with many trailing filaments. They do not flap; they glide, adjusting themselves to small variations in thermal updraft and the plateau’s subtle winds. Their undersides shimmer with the same film-life that coats the rocks, symbiotic mats that harvest something from the harsh light. They move slowly, casting wide, shifting shadows across the pale terrain and roofs. Their calls, if they have them, are too low or too soft to break the predominant hush.

The larger land-dwelling animals are adapted for distance and scarcity. Heat-shimmer hovers above them when they walk. They are tall, long-limbed, with many-jointed legs designed to take broad, deliberate steps over rough ground. Their skin looks like a layer of cracked clay, but flexes fluidly as they move. Along their backs run shallow channels, lined with that same opalescent film, holding captured water and microscopic life. As they stand resting in the sun, you can see tiny threads dipping into those channels from their flanks—feeding, maybe, from their living reservoirs.

Predation here is not a frantic chase but a patient calculus. Motion is expensive in this heat, and there is nowhere to hide behind vegetation. Hunters do not sprint. They stalk by silhouette and stillness, by occupying shadow when everything else is lit, by drifting along the underside of overhangs where rock meets artificial structure and waiting for smaller forms to enter their reach. The struggle, when it comes, is brief and quiet: a swift convergence, a constriction, a body dragged under a lip of stone and out of sight.

The city and its fauna are not separate.

Ledges of buildings are crowded not with potted plants but with arranged strata of mineral plates, carefully wetted surfaces, and shallow basins where micro-ecologies are cultivated. Some of these are clearly for industrial use—bioreactors growing specific films that glow a certain cyan or amber in the sun. Others are more ambiguous: wide, shallow trays where water is allowed to stand and warm, attracting small animals that graze on the films, which are then swept up or harvested in some systematic but not immediately obvious way.

Archways and overhanging eaves have been designed with perching spaces: not decorative cornices, but smooth, slightly concave platforms where fliers fold their bodies and hang in the shadows. They are not domesticated, exactly, but accommodated. Some buildings have recessed panels humming softly in the walls, exuding a temperature gradient or chemical trace that draws swarms of tiny, near-invisible air-dwellers. Those swarms, in turn, become food for other creatures that patrol the same routes daily.

There are no trees, but there are “canopies”: lattices of thin, reflective strips strung above certain walkways and plazas. They serve as shade, but also as landing grids. When the flying mantas brush them, the filaments on their trailing edges make momentary contact, and small sparks of pale luminescence flicker along the metal, charging or discharging into the city’s subtle energy web.

Rules of the world emerge from this interdependence:

- Water is precious, but not rare; it is managed. Every drip is guided. Natural seep becomes controlled trickle, becomes industrial input, becomes ecological resource. Nothing is allowed to simply evaporate without purpose. Even the vapor rising from exposed basins is captured by overhead contouring that cools and recondenses it beneath.

- Heat is a fact, not an enemy. Structures don’t fight it so much as redirect it. Surfaces are chosen less for insulation and more for their capacity to radiate in specific bands. Some buildings glow faintly in wavelengths your eyes can’t see, shedding heat upward into a sky with thin air, where it can be lost more easily.

- Silence is cultivated. Engines are muffled, fluid flows are smoothed. Acoustic harshness seems to be avoided on principle, as if sharp sound itself were wasteful—or dangerous. Work that might produce noise is done within sealed halls sunk partway into the plateau, behind thick doors that shut with gentle finality. Even at the busiest hour, when you would expect clamor, voices (if there are voices) are kept low and close.

- Biology is not visibly lush, but it is intricate. Complex life here has grown from films and mats upward, never developing leafy structures but rather thickening and differentiating the mineral-bound skins that first fed off light and heat and mineral water. Everything more complex still carries the memory of that origin: smooth surfaces seeded with microfilm, back-channels of stored liquid, skin that is as much living filter as barrier.

- Time here has been long enough to smooth the land, and long enough to layer the city. You see older structures half-eroded at the edges, their original hard lines rounded by centuries of seep and re-precipitation. Some are ghost-bones of once-busier districts, now partly reclaimed by mineral drapery: stalactite-like forms descending from old roofs, stalagmite-like mounds climbing up walls. The newer constructions stand slightly back from these ruins, respecting the way stone wants to sag and fluid wants to fall.

Brightness is not simply an environmental condition—it has become a working material. There are windows that are not transparent in the usual sense, but volumes of gel or crystal that take the sun’s white hammer and split it internally, feeding certain colors deeper into the building while rejecting others. Along some facades, you see long horizontal bands that stay strangely dim no matter how directly the sun strikes them; they seem to drink brightness and re-emit it as something else entirely, perhaps powering the silent industry supported by the weeping plateau.

At the edge of the built zones, the open highland reasserts itself: slabs of pale rock fractured into polygonal fields, low ridges smoothed by time, endless shallow basins where water gleams in thin mirrors. Sparse life dots these expanses—a resting giant animal here, a drift of tiny, near-transparent scuttlers there, the falling shadow of a glider passing between you and the relentless light.

Stand in such a place, away from the soft hum of infrastructure, and the loudest sound is the blood in your own ears. Heat presses. Distant structures blur and warp in the glare, their straight lines wavering as if they are unsure they truly exist. The plateau seems to stretch on well beyond what you can see, a repeating pattern of wet rock, intentional constructions, and the thin, intricate web of life that clings to both.

Despite the oppressiveness of brightness and heat, there is no feeling of chaos. Everything is in a kind of tense equilibrium: water moving but never rushing, life abundant but never crowded, buildings present but never overwhelming the land that holds them. It is a world that has accepted its harsh givens—high, hot, wet, and bare of plants—and has quietly, carefully built a civilization and an ecology around the cool patience of stone and the slow insistence of seeping water.
