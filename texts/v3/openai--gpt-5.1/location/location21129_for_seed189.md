The place is inside something living.

You notice it gradually: the walls are not rock, not soil, not anything that has ever eroded beneath a sky. They are a continuous, vascular tissue, pale and luminous from within, flexing almost imperceptibly, as if they remember how to breathe. You are underground in the strictest sense—no horizon, no distant line where land meets air. Just corridors and chambers and shaft-like voids hollowed through a mass of plant.

Yet the first impression is of emptiness.

A long, vertical throat of space rises around you, cylindrical and perfectly straight for a distance that feels much larger than the actual pocket you inhabit. The scale is intimate—no more than a few dozen meters from wall to wall at the widest—but the sensation is of something vaster, because there is so little here. Between your eyes and the opposite wall: almost nothing. No drifting dust, no haze, no suspended debris. The air is clear, almost too clear, so that distance feels abstract, like a drawing rather than a place.

Light seeps from the plant itself, a soft daylight-white that has no obvious source. Whole panels of wall thicken with a faint, milk-glass glow; others remain in shadow, veined and translucent amber. It is “normal” daylight in intensity, enough that colors are honest and edges sharp. But there is no directionality; there is no sun. Shadows ripple because the tissue ripples, not because anything moves across the sky.

The walls are sharply defined—this is a young interior, recently made. The conduits and chambers haven’t had time to slump or smooth; their edges feel decisive, still remembering their first moment of construction. Ribs of fibrous material arch with clean, blade-like precision. Here and there, a ridge ends in a sudden overhang, undercut like a freshly split stem, as if something recently ruptured a solid mass of vegetation to make these cavities and has not yet thought about tidying them.

Moisture collects wherever geometry allows it. This is not a flooded place, not a dripping cavern, but a network of held breaths and damp exhalations. Veins along the walls pulse slowly with clear sap. At certain points, junctions in the tissue form shallow cups; mist condenses there into trembling lenses of water, suspended and thickened by surface tension and a faint mucilage exuded by the plant. Nothing here stays dry for long—condensation pearls on every exposed surface, gathers, coalesces, drops in irregular beads that vanish into porous floor-mats of rootlike filaments.

The sound arrives before you have convinced yourself that the space is stable.

It is loud, and it does not come from a single direction. A deep, resonant rushing underlies everything, like distant surf in a shell held to the ear—only the shell is the entire world, and the surf never stops. It is sap, or air, or something more abstract than both, moving through conduits in the walls at varying pressures. You cannot trace a single tube or artery; instead, the walls themselves thrum with internal weather. Frequencies add and subtract, beating against one another in patterns that almost become rhythm and then tear away again.

Occasional detonations of sound roll through the tissue: a sudden shuddering crack as a new void opens somewhere nearby, or a peristaltic contraction slams closed a channel of flow. The plant is actively reconfiguring itself. These are not geological events; they are decisions. They are not slow enough to be lumbering. This body is awake, and its remodellings happen on human timescales: minutes, not millennia.

The floor, if you can call it that, is a layered mat of overlapping growths. Not soil: woven roots, flattened stems, leaf-plates fused edge-to-edge into a near-continuous carpet, springy and faintly warm underfoot. Between their seams, capillary fissures breathe vapor at regular intervals: little sighs of warmth and humidity, each exhale visible as a flicker in the air from refracted light. Step, and the mat subtly indents, fluid forced aside in the tissues below. Depending on where you stand, the whole surface may respond, flexing in a shallow bow—as if your weight is being measured, as if it matters.

Plants are everything here, but they are not discrete organisms. Nothing stands alone as a tree or a vine or a bush. There is only the continuous substrate—the Body—and within it, differentiated functions.

You find places where the plant structures itself into columns: not trunks, exactly, but thick cords that drop from the unseen ceiling to the floor, fluted and hollow. You can hear a different sound in each: in one, a steady high hiss, like air escaping from some deep lung; in another, a slow chugging, viscous as a heartbeat drawn out over seconds. Sometimes, the wall between two such columns is thin enough that their sounds interfere, creating warbling acoustic moirés you feel behind your teeth more than in your ears.

Elsewhere, the Body raises shelves and balconies, cantilevered membranes that protrude from smooth walls and then twist to join another surface. Some carry pools: oval dishes filled not with free-standing water but with something gelatinous, a living hydrogel dense with chlorophyll flecks. The light that reaches these pools intensifies as you watch; the surrounding tissue dims slightly, shunting brightness into that patch as though focusing. Microbubbles rise through the gel and pop with a faint, constant crackle that adds a high, brittle layer to the ambient roar.

The whole place sounds like a storm inside a forest that has no leaves: wind with no wind, thunder with no sky, rain with no raincloud.

When water does appear in more familiar form, it is abrupt and local. High above, cracks in the vascular ceiling will suddenly glaze with condensation, then release it all at once in a fast, concentrated shower, a narrow cascade that lasts only a minute or two. The downpour carves nothing; there is no rock to erode, only porous flesh to receive and redistribute. Almost as soon as the rain starts, already-grown gutters inflate under it, guiding it toward a set of waiting pockets and thickets. When it ends, the gutters relax, fading back into the general textured relief of the walls.

Nothing here is static; the architecture improvises around processes that are themselves improvising.

Despite how full this world is of living tissue, it feels empty in another sense: between the plant surfaces, the voids are not cluttered. No hanging roots dangle where they are not needed, no thickets of aerial growth choke the passages. The Body maintains open volumes carefully, like lungs that insist on remaining partially inflated. Even the small things—filaments that reach across space, tendrils that appear in midair—are precise, sparse, each with some function that is not exactly structural.

The air is warm, just past the threshold of comfort. Moist enough that your skin never quite dries, but not so humid as to condense freely. The warmth is from within the plant; where the sap-laden conduits pass close to the surface, you can feel localized radiance. Stand next to one and a faint, leafy heat strokes your face. Move away, and the temperature barely drops, because everywhere else is only slightly cooler. No drafts. No chill. Just a cocoon graded by a few degrees.

At irregular intervals, something in the system initiates a full-body event.

The light dims, first locally, then spreading. Walls that glowed a steady milky white begin to pulse—slow, long waves of brightness traveling along their length, chasing each other upward or sideways into unseen chambers. The soundscape shifts: the low rushing undercurrent rises in pitch, like wind forced through a narrower passage. Somewhere in the distance, a boom echoes, followed by a series of clips and snaps that sound like enormous tendons resetting.

The floor tightens, rising beneath your feet in a broad, gentle heave. Pockets in the walls that you assumed were still membranes suddenly open like mouths, inhaling the warm, damp air with a thunderclap rush; in other places, matching structures exhale, venting spent atmosphere into new chambers. It is not an earthquake, though it feels like one. It is a whole-biome peristalsis, a system-wide pump stroke.

Whatever this Body is doing—exchanging gases, moving fluids, communicating with something beyond itself—it does so loudly, violently, and then returns to its baseline roar, leaving only small aftertremors of motion.

Fauna does exist, but it is rare and thinly scattered, like punctuation marks in a nearly unbroken text of plant.

You glimpse movement first as a ripple on the wall—something pale crawling beneath the translucent tissue, bulging it outward in a traveling lump. Then it emerges, breaking through a seam with a wet, uncurling motion. An animal, in the technical sense, but one perfectly adapted to a world with no true solid ground.

It is long and ribbonlike, sheathed in flexible scales that reflect the local light in matte bands, almost plantlike themselves. At intervals along its body, it has rings of ciliary limbs—brushes that can flatten or flare, letting it cling to any patch of wall or slide effortlessly along. Near what you might call its head, a crown of thin sensory tendrils tastes the air, sampling temperature and chemical gradients. Eyes are unnecessary; light here never varies in direction, only in intensity and hue, and the Body itself broadcasts information in ways other than shadow.

The creature doesn’t scurry or dart; it moves in slow, deliberate flows, pressing its body into contact with the tissues, then lifting to span the void in graceful arcs. Every so often, it pauses over a nodal swelling in the wall, bites in with a hidden ring of grinding plates, drinks sap. The Body allows this—or fails to resist. Perhaps the relationship is mutual. As the ribbon-creature feeds, the tissues beneath it grow momentarily brighter, as if the act of predation triggers local signaling.

Further off, perched on a protruding ledge of calcified plant, something else watches. It is small, maybe the size of a hand, with a body like a hinged seedpod attached to seven or eight long, jointed legs. Its motion is insect-fast, but its limbs terminate not in claws but in tiny pads that extrude fine rootlike hairs. When it shifts its weight, those hairs plug into the wall and then withdraw, tasting. It’s an interpreter, a parasite or commensal that reads the Body’s electrochemical chatter and acts on it, chasing subtle advantages: warmth, nutrient seepage, microclimates in the constant damp.

These animals are infrequent, each a rare event in a space that otherwise seems unconcerned with them. You could stand for hours in a single chamber and see nothing move except plant and water and light.

And that would be enough, because reality itself is a little unstable here.

Distance does not behave like you are used to.

Down one corridor, the walls are parallel, their texture repeating regularly like the ribs of a gigantic leaf. You can see where it should end—an intersection, perhaps twenty meters ahead. But as you walk, the step count doesn’t match your estimate. Ten paces, twenty, thirty, the end still the same apparent distance away. The pattern on the wall repeats, but not quite identically. There is a small scar in the tissue every few meters, a healed-over aperture, and you notice that you pass it more times than makes sense. The corridor seems to be “looping” in its own substance, reusing its own length.

At the moment you start to suspect you will never reach the intersection, the geometry decides to change. A mild flexure ripples along the floor. The Body contracts somewhere above you. The next step you take crosses a threshold that did not exist the step before, and the intersection is suddenly right there, close enough to touch. There was no visual foreshortening, no zoom—just a realignment of how distance connected to space.

Likewise with verticality. Look up, and the shaft above your head seems to go on for a hundred meters, perhaps more, full of intertwining arches and translucent balconies. But the sound that comes from overhead does not echo as if from a high ceiling; it rebounds quickly, like you are inside a much smaller cavity. The glows from above brighten and dim in cycles too short for such a great distance. If you try to climb—on ladders of extruded plant that obligingly grow runglike protrusions as your hand reaches for them—you may find that ten meters of ascent suffices to bring you level with a feature you had judged far higher. Space here folds into function instead of obeying consistent proportion.

Time flares and narrows, too. Watch a clump of budding structures—small, knoblike protrusions in a recessed alcove—and they might sit unchanged for what feels like half an hour, each maintaining its swollen, expectant shape. Turn away to follow the course of a brief rain; when you look back, the alcove now hosts a fully formed organ: a broad, fan-shaped surface riddled with pores, gently shivering as gases pass through. Somehow, the “in-between” growth happened while you weren’t looking, or slipped through a timescale that did not include you.

All the while, the technological level within this scenario is nearly absent. There is no metal, no evident crafted artifact. Yet the Body itself demonstrates capabilities beyond ordinary biology. If there is “technology” here, it is entirely encoded in the plant’s morphology. Occasionally, you will find a piece of its own matter shaped with an almost tool-like precision: a segment of stiffened fiber sharpened to a taper that could pierce its own walls; an arrangement of tubules that behave like valves, opening and closing in binary-seeming patterns. These are not tools made by external hands, but you get the sense that the Body can repurpose its growth for specific tasks the way other worlds use objects.

If any intelligence other than the Body’s resides here, it is primitive and embedded, limited to small, ephemeral interventions.

On a ledge near one of the deeper conduits, for instance, there sits a cluster of plant-fiber strips, woven loosely together and then abandoned. The weave is rough, asymmetrical, clumsy. It does not match the smooth, optimized flows of the Body’s own construction. Something with limbs and intention gathered loose strands scraped from the walls and twisted them into this form. A nest? A trap? A toy? Nearby, the wall bears scratches—parallel lines, gouged across the grain of the plant. They are not hungry bites; they look like habit, or counting, or perhaps a way to make the Body notice.

No metal. No fire. Just hand-sized minds (or tentacle-sized) making the smallest, most tentative marks on an overwhelming substrate that controls heat, humidity, light, and even scale itself.

Despite all this, the ecosystem is not chaotic. It is active, yes, loud and in motion, but its processes interlock.

Moisture cycles from vapor to film to droplet to sap and back again. Pools of gelatin host dense microbiological blooms: strings of single-celled colonies that write temporary patterns in phosphorescent green across their surfaces. When the Body chooses, it tilts a pool just enough to spill part of its contents down a channel, seeding another chamber with the same biomass. Over time, you can imagine waves of metabolism rolling through the network like internal weather fronts.

Fauna intervene as modulators. Ribbon-creatures open new microchannels when they bite, turning sealed conduits into leaky ones. Seedpod-spiders carry spores or microbe-laden gel beads on their root-hairs from one light-focusing pool to another. Their actions are not orchestrated in any obvious way, but the Body seems to anticipate them, reshaping local architecture in real time to either invite or restrict their movements.

No sky. No soil. Only this continuous, reconfiguring plantspace.

And under it all, a sense that “underground” is not metaphor. The Body sits below something else—perhaps an older, larger organism; perhaps a surface world you will never see. Occasionally, cracks appear in its ceiling, not the controlled apertures it makes for its own processes, but jagged rents edged with blackened tissue. Through them, a completely different light sometimes intrudes: sharper, directional, casting actual shadows. For a moment, the space is disoriented by this alien sun; colors twitch, plant panels polarize their surfaces. The Body rushes to repair, sending pale, fast-growing tendrils to knit over the wound. The foreign light is shut out. The roar settles.

The rule of the world here is simple and absolute: plant is primary reality. Space, time, sound, and water are variables the plant adjusts as needed, parameters in an ongoing experiment that is itself the landscape. Animals are footnotes. Physics remains mostly intact but is willingly bent at the margins to allow a comprehension that is not yours.

You stand in a hollow of warm, glowing tissue, listening to the loud internal tides of sap and breath, surrounded by vast amounts of living matter and vast amounts of empty volume—an underground pocket, both intimate and infinite, nested inside a vegetal body that is still very young and still, audibly, figuring out what to be.
