The plateau is a single, living surface.

From a distance—if there were any distance to see it from—it would look like a broad, pale-green tableland shouldered up into cold air, its edges lost in cloud and its top riddled with slow, shallow undulations. Up close, there is no “ground” at all, only an unbroken mass of plant.

It is cold. The air bites the inside of your nose. Breath plumes white, then is stolen to nothing by a wind that never quite arrives—felt as pressure, sensed as movement, but rarely heard. No snow lies here, though the temperature promises it. Instead, frost forms slender lattices along every exposed surface at night, then melts in a fine, creeping condensation whenever the sky thins enough for light to reach down.

Water exists mostly as implication. Thin films glaze the plant-flesh in hollows and along vertical ribs, a shining skin that beads and vanishes. In deeper pockets where the plateau sags, narrow tarns sit—their basins not stone, but interlocked layers of dark, woody tissue, like knots in colossal roots. These ponds are black from above, reflective and still, touched only by the smallest of ripples when something under the surface breathes, or when the overhanging growth contracts and releases a slow, viscous drip.

There is no soil as such. The substrate is laminated vegetation: spongy, fibrous, sometimes rubbery, sometimes glass-hard. Press down, and it yields slightly, like old cork. In some places it springs back. In others, the indentation holds and becomes a new hollow, a new micro-basin to catch water, spores, wind-blown fragments. The plateau has known erosion for eons, but erosion here is the continuous rearrangement of living tissue. The original rock—if there was ever rock—is buried meters below, armoured and fully enclosed.

The terrain is intricately weathered. Not by rivers gnawing stone, but by centuries of freeze-thaw flexing, by cycles of growth and senescence, by entire layers of plant-skin loosening and peeling away as others thicken beneath. This has carved shallow troughs and elongated basins, long, low ridges like the backs of submerged animals, and intricate terraces that step down into larger depressions. Each form is smoothed, rounded, in equilibrium with the forces that made it. This is not a young, raw biome. It has settled into its own kind of permanence.

It is also never still.

The vegetation is the world and the world is crowded. Stems, fronds, pads, sheets, and nodular bulges layer over each other until there is hardly a clear line between individual organisms. Imagine lichen blown up to the scale of trees and hills; imagine coral that breathes cold air. The surface is a continual weave of tendrils and plates. Some parts are low and matlike, a dense felt of tiny leaves no larger than an eyelash, so thick you can’t see individual shapes until your face is inches away. Others thrust upward in columns the width of houses, only to bend and fuse into archways, vaults, and heavy, drooping shelves.

Nothing here is entirely rigid. Even the hardest structures—gray-green buttresses with the grain of old bone—carry within them a subtle, almost imperceptible flex. When the hidden forces that drive the plateau’s motion pulse through, these arches and columns respond, twisting by fractions of a degree, shuddering in slow, deep waves.

Though there are no human constructions, the growth forms behave as architecture. Over time, patterns of reinforcement and vacancy have emerged as if the plateau has been testing designs and keeping what works. There are buttressed corridors: parallel riblike trunks defining lanes of clearer walking, roofed over by thick, pale canopies that trap the chill breath of the world. There are dome-chambers where the ceiling is a single organism, its underside puckered with countless small pores, exhaling a faint steam that clings as a low fog.

There are towers, too, but they are not built—they are accreted. Narrow stacks of layered discs rise a hundred meters or more, each disc a flattened ring of succulent tissue with a central hollow. From outside they look like a pile of enormous, irregular coins. From within, vertiginous chimneys open, lined with glossy, translucent membranes that slowly pulse, moving moisture and gases vertically through the structure. High above, the tower-tops spread into flat, plate-like crowns that tilt and swivel with the slow precision of instruments tracking some invisible source. Their undersides are dark maroon, almost black, absorbing what little energy the pale sun offers.

The plateau’s plant-mass does not merely respond to the weather; it manufactures its own. Whenever the inner activity intensifies, heat rises through hidden vascular channels, and the warm exhalations meet the icy air at the canopy line. Small, localized cloudlets form—ghostly white domes that sit just above the tallest growths, shedding slow drizzle back down onto the tissues that birthed them. Sometimes they tear loose and drift, orphaned mists sliding along the surface like low, lost thoughts.

Rain from the sky is rare but not unknown. It comes as long, slow events—gray, unhurried weather that settles over the plateau for days. Drops strike softly, muted by the cushion of growth. There is no patter because there is no hollow ground for sound to resonate in, no stone or leaf-litter to clatter on. Water simply appears, thin and continuous, and is pulled down into the living substrate almost as quickly as it falls. There are never torrents, never streams in the customary sense—only thin, creeping films that slide along channels worn into plant-flesh, shining like mercury and disappearing into absorptive wells.

The silence here is not empty; it is the silence of very small things happening everywhere. Sound, when it exists, is so quiet it might be imagined. A faint, papery creak when a thick frond adjusts its angle. The nearly inaudible hiss of air as it is drawn into and expelled from millions of microscopic openings. A muffled, subterranean knocking as two great roots slowly rub past each other somewhere deep within the mass. Occasionally, when the tension that has been building in a structure for decades finally resolves, there is a single, soft, booming crack—felt more in the soles of the feet than heard—as some internal strut yields and a section of the surface subtly subsides, by centimeters, into a more stable configuration.

Life beyond the plant is sparse but not absent. Small, pale arthropods wander the surfaces, their bodies flattened, limbless in the conventional sense, equipped instead with fringed cilia that set up barely visible ripples as they glide atop moisture films. They feed on exudates and flaking, senescent tissue, scraping it away with rasping mouthparts. Their movement is almost frictionless, silent, and unhurried.

Far rarer are the larger creatures. Every few days the plateau will heave along a hidden fault, a broad, slow surge that throws light into new angles and shifts temperature gradients. After such events, you might see one of the long-necked grazers: tubular-bodied, a soft gray that almost matches the frost, scaling the buttressed corridors as if they’ve followed them all their lives. They are the only animals here that make anything like a voice, and even that is minimal—a low, breathy cough as they exhale, a sound that is immediately swallowed by the vegetable labyrinth.

Above, in the cold sky, the birds—if they are birds—are infrequent, dark specks gliding long distances between sips of the plateau’s warmth. When they land on the high crowns, the plant-skin often stiffens beneath their feet, as if recognizing their weight. Sometimes it opens, just a little, and tongues of spongy tissue rise to meet them with slow curiosity. The birds do not stay long.

Technology exists here, but not as human artifact. It is embedded in the plateau itself as a kind of living industry.

Beneath the visible layer, great organs churn. There are vats: hollow, warm chambers where fluids cycle and are transformed. Columns act as pistons, pumping bioliquids through conduits as wide as rail tunnels. Entire ridges serve as radiator-fins, giving off excess heat as the internal reactions proceed. The plant does more than simple metabolism. It refines.

Along certain long troughs, the surface is lined with glossy, metallic-looking growths—veins and plates that gleam faintly with their own luster even under the flat daylight. These are not minerals; they are plant-generated alloys, excreted in thin laminations and then reabsorbed elsewhere. In some exposed cross-sections—where wind and the plateau’s own shifting have torn away the top layers—you can see stratified bands of different hue, like the layers of an industrial slag pile: dull copper-greens, bruised purples, smoked silvers. Each layer is the memory of a long-ago “processing run,” when the plateau concentrated and reorganized trace elements from rain, wind-blown dust, and its own decaying tissues.

There are regions where this internal industry has sculpted enormous, organlike structures. Imagine a power station made entirely of cambium and cellulose: branching tubes, thick-walled reservoirs, and slowly turning rotors of living tissue, all kept at a slightly higher temperature than the air. The rotors are not instruments in the mechanical sense, but they operate on the same principle—forces applied at one point generate motion, which transmits effects to distant parts. The plateau is full of such resonant machines.

From the surface, their presence is perceived only as a subtle, constant turbulence. Underfoot, the ground carries a faint, always-changing vibration: a complicated, low-frequency tremor that swells and ebbs in cycles of hours and days. Standing still, you feel it through your bones more than through your ears—an almost inaudible thrumming, as if you were standing on the casing of a huge, buried factory whose engines seldom stop.

This turbulence is not destructive. It is maintenance. The plateau rearranges itself continually, testing stress, softening some fibers, hardening others. In narrow corridors, you might feel the walls drawing in by a fraction of a hand’s width as tissue is redistributed. On open expanses, long waves roll through, lifting the surface by a few centimeters and then letting it sink, like the passing of an invisible beast made of pressure and sap.

On rare occasions, the internal adjustments approach a kind of storm. These are the plateau’s convulsions. A deep, sustained vibration builds over hours until puddles dimple and the tiny ponds tremble in concentric rings. The highest crowns tighten, their plates closing like shutters. The air thickens with exhaled moisture and faint chemical tangs: bitter-resin, cold-metal, something like spoiled citrus. Then, without a single audible crack, an entire sector of the plateau will shift—surfaces bulging, hollows opening, laminae delaminating and sliding across one another in slow, massive, centimeter-per-second flows.

Crevices open—long, narrow slots revealing inner layers lit by their own dim bioluminescence, blue-green and veined like an MRI of a forest. The slits breathe cool vapor. Some close again within minutes as fresh tissue grows over them, knitting into new configurations. Others remain, stabilizing into permanent canyons a few meters deep, lined with delicate, frondlike filters that glean the heavier molecules from the moving air.

Light moves in and out of these changes. The general illumination is normal daylight: sometimes overcast, sometimes a strained, thin sun that throws hard, clear shadows across the pale-green masses. But the plant itself modulates it. In dense areas, the canopy is patterned with translucent windows—thin, stretched membranes that defuse the light into a steady, almost clinical brightness. Elsewhere, it is thick and opaque, plunging corridors and under-arches into a shadowed twilight where the only glow comes from the faint bioluminescence deep in cracks and vents.

At night, the sky is black and sharp, but the plateau does not go dark. Thin lines of light trace its hidden circulatory network just below the skin: ghostly filaments that pulse with slow waves of fluorescence, running for kilometers before fading into darkness again. These waves are not random; they map the flows of some intricate internal economy, like the power grid of a city seen from orbit, switching on and off according to demands deep within the living mass.

Fauna respond to this slow lightning. The tiny arthropods align their bodies to it, turning in place without moving forward, as if orienting to a magnetic field. The few larger creatures retreat into folds and alcoves when the waves peak, pressing themselves against the warmer conduits and remaining utterly still until the glow passes.

Time here feels odd. The plant is not hurried in a human or animal way, but it is constant, restless. Every patch of surface is in some state of becoming—expanding, thickening, hollowing, or preparing to shed. The erosion of old forms and the construction of new is so perfectly balanced that, over decades, the general shape of the plateau remains the same. A ridge is still a ridge, a basin still a basin. Yet nothing you can touch or stand on is more than a few centuries old. The world has been worn into itself again and again, always settling to roughly the same shapes, like a memory the system keeps returning to.

There are rules, though no one wrote them.

One: hard edges are temporary. Wherever sharp corners form—where a column splits, or a ledge fractures and leaves a knife-like lip—the plateau begins to soften them. Growth concentrates there, exuding thin ribs that overlap and blunt the angle. Within years, it is gone, replaced by the familiar rounded forms that shed stress smoothly into their surroundings. The landscape abhors stress risers.

Two: voids are precious. Any truly empty space—an air pocket, a wide cavern, a chamber hollowed accidently by combined erosion and growth—draws attention from the larger organism. Sensing vacancy as both opportunity and threat, it seeds the void with filamentous probes. Some collapse the space, scaffolding it with new tissue. Others reinforce it with flexible ribs, preserving it as a resonant chamber, perhaps because certain shapes and volumes amplify the vibrations the plateau uses to coordinate itself.

Three: foreign matter does not last. Stones dropped from the surrounding, unseen mountains—even if they once existed—would be slowly swallowed, encased, and decomposed. Metallic meteors, fallen branches from some hypothetical off-plateau forest, bones from the rare animals that die here: all are taken in, stripped of useful elements, rearranged, and either incorporated as structural reinforcement or vented in altered form in those metallic-looking slag bands. There are no ruins, no artifacts, no tools—only the plant’s own processed residues and the endless reconfiguration of its own body.

Four: the plateau resists sound. Vibrations above a certain amplitude are damped. Loud voices (if there were anyone to make them), falling rocks, impact shocks from some external event—all would be met with reflexive softening. Surrounding tissue loosens, becomes more gel-like, absorbing the wave, then re-hardens. Even the wind is muted. It pours over the plateau like water over a sponge, losing energy in the innumerable fine-scale structures, until all that’s left is that near-silence: the air’s cold presence, without its voice.

And five: the system pursues gradients. Temperature differences, chemical imbalances, pressure disparities, electromagnetic anomalies—any place where “more” and “less” exist side by side is a place where the living mass will act. It threads conductive tissues between zones, equalizing, or sometimes exaggerating the gradient briefly to harvest energy as it relaxes. This is its technology: industrial-scale exploitation of tiny differentials, integrated across an entire landscape.

From the sky, if one could see enough of it at once, the plateau would look serene: a mottled, frost-bitten green expanse, slowly trading clouds with the empty air. From within, standing knee-deep in low sponge-plants at the base of an arch grown from fused trunks, feeling the faint, ceaseless tremor traveling through the soles of your boots into your bones, watching a far-off crown-plate tilt almost imperceptibly to track some shift in light, you sense the turbulence.

Not violence. Not peace. A constant churn of intent, cold and vegetal, patient and industrious. An engine-world of plant, always rearranging itself, always quiet, always on.
