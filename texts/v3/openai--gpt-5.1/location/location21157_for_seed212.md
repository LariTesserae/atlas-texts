It begins as a pressure.

Not a sound at first, but a weight in the air: the sense that everything around you is already vibrating, already in motion, before your ears decide to call it noise. Then the sound arrives all at once—roaring, grinding, a continuous layered thunder with no clear source, as if the world itself is rushing past you even while the ground seems still.

There is no soil here, not in the usual sense. What passes for “ground” is a solid, low-lying plain of fused plant matter—an endless, living pavement, the warped skeleton of a forest melted flat and then swollen up from beneath. Broad, overlapping plates of vascular tissue, flexing, thick as stone, rise and sink like tectonic leaves. They meet at seams that yawn open to reveal moist depths beneath, then seal again in slow compressions that squeeze out humid gusts of organic breath.

The air is warm, dense, and damp in bursts, like standing near a waterfall that never quite appears. Beads of water form everywhere—on surfaces, in the air itself—gathering on the undersides of semi-translucent fronds overhead before dripping steadily downward in uneven, pattering curtains. It doesn’t quite rain; the sky is visible in broken fragments, bright and hazy, but the canopy of plant-architecture filters and refracts the light into a pale, shifting green.

The vegetation is not “in” this place. It is the place.

Imagine a city where every building, every road, every piece of infrastructure, has been replaced by an organism grown to fill that function without ever having learned the distinction between body and environment. Here, “streets” are wide vascular conduits: glossy, dark-green arteries, each as broad as a river, their surfaces ridged with pulsating ridgelines. Fluids—sap-like, shimmering with suspended particles—rush visibly beneath translucent skin, creating a muffled, internal thunder that merges with the larger roar of the landscape.

They stretch out across the lowlands in looping grids and spirals, converging on swollen nodal bulges: raised platforms of knotty plant-flesh, perhaps city plazas or relay-joints. Each node is a tangle of branching towers and looping arcs, as if a banyan tree and an overpass had grown out of each other. Smooth, ribbed trunks rise, explode into complex lattices, then fuse back together overhead, forming vaulted tunnels through which warm drafts continually surge.

The scale is regional rather than planetary; you can imagine a boundary somewhere beyond the horizon, but you cannot see it. A valley, perhaps, or a shallow basin, freshly opened—young land. There is a sharpness to the relief: sudden ridges of plant-rock where the living ground has buckled; abrupt fissures that knife downward into hot, humid darkness; cliff-like faces of layered, fibrous tissue that have only recently been exposed, their inner channels still bright and wet.

Moisture collects where the living ground dips. Shallow basins hold sheets of water that are almost never still. Their surfaces dimple continuously as fine roots rise up from beneath, break water, then withdraw again, tasting the air. The water isn’t entirely water: it carries filaments, clouds of spores, microscopic leaves that unfurl and curl again in the span of seconds. Sometimes the pools are hauled sideways, as if an invisible hand has taken the ground and flexed it; the liquid surges toward a newly lowered edge, sloshing up over the borders of its own basin and down into cracks, vanishing in a fizz of accelerated growth.

There are no animals: nothing that runs or flies or watches. Movement belongs solely to plants, and they have claimed it completely.

Where you might expect a bystander, a passerby, there is instead an extrusion: a column of living tissue unfolding from a wall, splitting into petal-like layers, then collapsing back into the mass. Where you might expect insects—foraging, swarming—their roles are played by finer structures: hair-thin tendrils that seethe in sheets along surfaces, sweeping, tasting, exchanging microscopic packets of fluid. They advance like a tide, roll over a region of tissue, withdraw, leaving the color subtly changed—darker, or paler, or faintly luminescent.

The deafening sound comes from everywhere.

Deep beneath the fused plain, vast trunks and rhizome-plates grind against one another, shifting position by millimeters, driven by growth pressures. At the mid-levels, flexible buttresses creak and groan as they warp, redirecting tension, opening new cavities. Overhead, long, hanging catenaries of vine-architecture tremble and strike against each other in the warm currents, clapping and shrieking like a thousand taut ropes pulled, plucked, and released. Certain structures resonate in sympathy, creating stable tones that hang in the air like sirens, only to be obliterated, seconds later, by the next rearrangement.

The sound never settles to quiet. Instead, it forms layered regimes: near you, a steady chattering flicker where stemlets unroll and coil back with audible clicks; farther off, a percussive booming as a whole deck of living walkway flexes, snaps back, and flushes its sap in a single convulsive heave. The cumulative effect of millions of such motions—pumped sap, splitting tissue, tearing fibers, sealing wounds—is a continuous, chaotic roar with no silence between events, only lesser and greater peaks.

The world is young, but not in chronological years. It is young in the way a healing wound is young: already closed over, but still reorganizing itself feverishly, not yet sure of the final shape.

Structures rise and are unmade on timescales you can watch.

A “tower”—a spiraled column of tightly braided stems—swells and stretches as you look. It pushes upward through a gap in the upper canopy, splits, frays, then peels apart like unrolling scrolls. Segments fold downward, forming new bridges to adjacent nodes. Others slough off entirely, dropping in long, bending arcs, impacting the ground with a fleshy thud. As they land, they don’t simply lie there; within seconds, their undersides erupt in a spray of fine rootlets that latch onto the surface and begin drawing water, the entire fallen arc curling, tightening, merging, until it has become a new ridge, a new wall.

Nothing is inert. Every apparent surface—floor, wall, ceiling—is busy.

You can see it in the textures: the way hexagonal leaf-tiles—each the width of a hand, overlapping like scales—shift minutely in phase with the pulsing beneath. They breathe. Their stomata gape, close, release faint, shimmering plumes of vapor that rise for a meter before being seized and dragged sideways by some unseen convection channel. In shaded crevices, nodes like knuckles swell and deflate in slow rhythm, balancing pressures across the enormous, city-wide organism.

Plant-as-architecture has pushed beyond any familiar template. There are vaulted halls where the ribs are living columns, the ceiling a sheet of taut parenchyma laced with veins as thick as your arm. There are canyons whose walls are braided trunks, following impossible knots and Möbius loops, their configurations changing visibly as you trace them with your eyes—connections forming where there were none a moment before, others sliding apart and reconfiguring into new adjacency.

The “urban” quality emerges not from tools or planners, but from pattern: a density of organized channels, repeating motifs, pseudo-streets, pseudo-buildings, everywhere.

Flow is the governing principle. Fluids, gases, and signals rush through vascular corridors the way crowds rush through avenues. Here, a suspended tube arches overhead, six meters in diameter, translucent, filled with a gilded torrent of sap, accelerating as its cross-section narrows; there, its path splits, diverging into a manifold of thinner streams that dive into the understructure, lighting up downstream branches with a golden bolus that chases itself through the network.

At certain nodal plazas, the entire organism performs sudden, system-wide redistributions.

Without warning, the thunder deepens, the ground beneath you stiffens, then flexes. The canopy’s shimmering light dims as pigments rearrange themselves. All along a visible horizon, arterial conduits contract simultaneously, forcing fluid toward a point you cannot see. Somewhere distant, a structure ruptures—by design rather than accident. The sound of it is a stuttering, ripping bellow, carried through the resonant bones of the city-body.

In its wake, new growth accelerates locally: a forest of thin pylons stabs upward from a previously flat plain, branching and fusing faster than feels possible, knitting themselves into a new load-bearing framework in minutes. Leaves burst forth at their joints, inflate like sails, then harden into panels that lock together, forming shelter-like overhangs.

It feels like an architectural decision, but there is no architect, only self-organizing, plant-level logic amplified to city scale.

Water, again, is not absent but rationed and redirected. The air is mostly humid corridors and dry eddies, rather than uniform dampness. You walk through bands: a suddenly sticky, heavy zone where every surface sweats condensation and your skin feels filmed, followed by a dryer channel where broad, waxy plates siphon moisture away with relentless efficiency, exhaling warmth like a giant lung.

Where two such climate-regions meet, the vegetation becomes experimental.

Fungi-like forms exist here, but they’re more plant than fungus: flat, fan-shaped protrusions that extend from vertical trunks, overlapping in tight stacks, their undersides covered with vibrating ridges. These ridges thresh the passing air, generating microturbulence that keeps spores and aerosols traveling along preferred paths. The fans themselves appear and disappear in bursts; a smooth vertical surface suddenly blisters with dozens of them, they reach full extension in heartbeats, pulse for a minute, then withdraw, leaving only faint scars.

Despite the noise, there is a sense of intentional silence: the absence of voices, of call-and-response, of anything that listens as a separate creature. Instead, the city-plant listens to itself through stretch and flow.

Rules of this world emerge from its turbulence:

- There is no stable “finished” environment. Every configuration is provisional. Load-bearing beams thicken and thin. Corridors twist or seal off without warning. What seems like open space may, moments later, be spanned by a suddenly-formed bridge as two growth-fronts recognize an opportunity and fuse.

- Erosion, here, is internal as much as external. Structures are not merely built; they are eaten from within and repurposed. A massive buttress that took hours to form may, in minutes, be riddled by invading sheets of new tissue that digest it, converting its mass into a scaffold for a different shape. The result is a landscape of sharp, youthful features constantly under revision.

- Space is layered vertically but accessible biologically. There are no stairs, no ladders; there are ramps of overlapping leaves that fold out of walls, helices of thick, pliant stems that momentarily align their nodules to form steps, then withdraw once passage is complete—if not for you, then for sap, or for signals.

- Time is compressed for growth and change. What on Earth might take seasons happens here across hours or less. The cost is constant stress, constant maintenance: everywhere, repair layers ooze, seal, harden; everywhere, redundant paths spin off from trunk lines, in case something fails.

Weirdness saturates it, not as decoration but as the basic texture of causality.

In some sectors, geometry itself misbehaves. A walkway along a curving trunk returns you, impossibly, to your starting node after a short loop—even though you watched new branches and junctions interpose themselves while you walked. Spatial continuity bends under the pressure of too much connectivity; the plant-city seems to find shortcuts by reassigning adjacency at the tissue level, making distant points neighbors by knitting them together—even if that means ignoring conventional three-dimensional layout.

You can watch it happening. Two corridors that were separated by a visible gap of open space suddenly sprout tendrils toward each other. They meet, fuse, multiply their bonds until the gap is wholly filled, while other tissues at the periphery pull back, thinning, as if the world has slid along a rail of higher-dimensional organization. The effect is that space folds locally around desire lines of flow.

Light behaves strangely in response. Some walls and ceilings are suffused with chlorophyll pigments that sip photons greedily, leaving those regions perpetually dim, even in direct line-of-sight to bright openings. Adjacent surfaces, lacking such pigments, reflect and scatter light wildly, glowing as though lit from within. In certain concave vaults, bouncing light and refracted moisture create stable illusions: apparent windows and shafts that show views of other parts of the city-organism, or of regions that are not straightforwardly accessible. Whether these are simply reflections guided purposefully through an inner network of transparent tissues, or actual topological links opening glimpses elsewhere, is unclear.

Sound, too, is guided and focused.

High overhead, broad, bowl-shaped leaves cup and redirect the chaos into directed beams: roaring corridors of noise that travel specific routes, perhaps as a signaling mechanism. Enter such a corridor and the ambient thunder narrows to a single, horrifyingly loud band of frequencies that seem to drill directly into your inner ear. Step a few meters to either side, and the soundscape shifts; new frequencies dominate, old ones cancel out, revealing narrow, surprising intervals of relative quiet under overhangs where bark-like material has learned to absorb and damp vibrations.

But true silence never descends. There is always the buzzing substrate of growth: the faint crackle of cell walls yielding, the susurrus of leaves flexing, the wet hiss of fluids under pressure.

Without animals, there is no predation, but there is competition—an internal, vegetative war.

You see it where fabrics clash. A district of fine, feathery fronds—pale, translucent, easily bent—meets a district of thick, muscular vines that twist in braided ropes. At their border, the fronds surge upward in great curtains, capturing light, while the vines respond by pushing out hard, glossy leaves that angle to reflect and concentrate glare onto the fronds, scorching them. But as burns appear, the fronds change their chemistry; scorched tissue darkens, then calcifies into a rigid, opaque plate that blocks the reflected light, forcing the vines to find another strategy.

Elsewhere, a carpet of low, mat-like growth creeps across the living pavement, its fine root-hairs dissolving older tissues beneath and drinking the released fluid. The pavement responds by shearing itself upward along fracture-lines, lifting into irregular slabs that tilt and lean against each other. The mats cascade over the newly vertical surfaces, briefly triumphant, until the exposed edges thicken and exude a bitter, resinous sap. Within minutes, the mat discolors and recedes, curling inward around its own center, which soon sprouts upright spikes—perhaps learning the trick of verticality from its failed advance.

The world is not just turbulent; it is driven by an almost frantic iterative intelligence, as if the combined biomass is continuously running experiments on itself at every scale.

Urbanity expresses itself in recurrent motifs: massive central trunks (some the width of small hills) that serve as hubs; radial distributions of vascular streets; concentric rings of specialized tissues. You can trace a circulatory “metro line” by following bundles of veins that maintain consistent diameter and pulse frequency across long distances. Junctions are marked by flare-like clusters of nodules that flash with brief, pale bioluminescence whenever a major fluid surge passes through—a visual echo of traffic lights, if traffic lights were made of living cambium.

Despite the absence of artifacts, the place feels inhabited. Not by separate entities, but by patterns of flow that replay and refine themselves, as if the plant-city remembers where it has previously needed to route more resources, and reinforces those paths.

Sometimes, a huge section of canopy will suddenly retract, exposing a swath of landscape to direct sky. The sudden burst of unfiltered light ignites a frenzy: latent buds that had lain flat and invisible explode into structures—spike-towers bristling with arrays of prismatic, glassy plates. These plates snap and swivel, tracking some invisible parameter, never quite settling. After a period—minutes, hours—the canopy flows back into place, engulfing the experiment, and the new-built spikes either merge, dissolve, or are subsumed into supporting frameworks.

Condensation plays a major, capricious role. On some days (if “day” is even meaningful here), the air is thick with drifting sheets of ultrafine droplets, each droplet nucleated around floating plant debris so small it might as well be dust. As turbulent flows toss this mist around, it collects along lines of convergence—like underwater thermoclines, invisible until they suddenly bloom into textures: ribbons of heavy fog that snake along certain corridors, hugging some forms and entirely avoiding others.

Where fog density crosses a threshold, special structures respond. Filigree nets of micro-branchlets extrude into the cloud, their surfaces studded with microscopic cups that fill almost instantly. The nets shiver as weight accumulates, then flip, delivering their harvest to waiting gutters that carry it downward with gurgling urgency. The process makes a sound like distant applause, lost in the larger roar.

There are seasons here, but they are not simple cycles. Instead of spring-summer-autumn-winter, the entire system passes through modes of reconfiguration triggered by thresholds in stored water, light flux, internal tension.

In one mode, called perhaps the “Unfurling” if anyone were here to name it, nearly every surface sprouts exploratory structures—tendrils, sensory fringes, thin lantern-like bulbs that swell with translucent gel. These bulbs glow faintly, lighting the world from within as flow patterns shift to survey new configurations of space. During Unfurling, the city-plant is restless, its turbulence most obvious: walls breathe deeply, bridges sway as if in an invisible storm, often tearing themselves apart to rearrange.

In another mode, a kind of consolidation, the forms thicken. Thin structures rip down their own length, folding back into trunks. Surfaces harden, cracking visibly as they dry out. Noise changes character: fewer sharp snaps, more deep, slow booms as the largest elements settle into longer-lived configurations. The deafening roar becomes marginally more structured—like a long chord that has found a few dominant notes.

Yet even then, there is no stillness, no perfect calm. The system cannot stop; it can only redistribute the intensity of its changes.

Rules at the limit of reality begin to fray inside this turbulence. On some ridgelines, you see buds that do not extend into space so much as into other conditions of space. A bud begins as a normal swelling on a branch, then, as it grows, its boundary becomes hazy. Parts of it appear nearer than they should be, others farther, as if some of its volume is being grown along a direction you cannot turn your head to follow. When these buds “open,” they do not necessarily reveal petals. Sometimes they reveal tunnels that emerge in two different parts of the world at once. Sometimes they reveal only light—impossibly collimated, monochromatic streaks that slice across the air and are immediately swallowed by receptive tissues elsewhere.

In rare pockets, the noise drops—not to silence, but to an audible murmur. These pockets are surrounded by elaborate baffles of leaf and bark, anechoic gardens where every sound wave is quickly damped. Within them, you find extraordinarily delicate structures: webs of transparent filaments arranged in intricate three-dimensional patterns, vibrating at frequencies too high to hear directly. They quiver in interference patterns with one another; where patterns align, nearby tissues bulge, grow, or wither, as if these silent thickets are computation nodes, decision organs, reading the whole city’s state through coupled vibrations.

No tool, no machine, no alien artifact disturbs this continuity. Technology, if it can be called that, is endogenous and vegetal. The plant-city is its own infrastructure, its own processor, its own weather-maker.

The low elevation of the region manifests in broader patterns. In the distance, beyond the main tangle of vertical forms, you can glimpse a horizon line shimmering with haze. Between here and there, the ground dips into a shallow, peat-dark basin where moisture collects in a series of linked tarns. These are quieter—relatively. At their surfaces, instead of roaring, there is a deep, continuous hum as immense root-pumps draw water upward in ceaseless columns. Giant, hollow reeds stand at the edges, quivering with each intake, sometimes venting excess vapor in explosive snorts that send shockwaves through the surrounding growth.

The basin serves as a heart of sorts: water-hoarding, equalizing. All the city’s vascular streets trend subtly toward it or away from it. Here, reality’s breakdown is expressed in hydrology: water doesn’t always obey gravity. Up-slope, you sometimes find hanging pools—concave cups of living tissue filled to the brim, clinging impossibly to vertical walls or even to the undersides of overhangs. The liquid within them neither drips nor spills; internal forces and micro-root lattices grip each molecule. When the system decides to redistribute, entire hanging reservoirs detach their contents as coherent boluses, sending them racing along interior arteries in defiance of direction, as if gravity were a local rule that can be turned off for moments.

Above all, what defines this landscape is saturation: of matter, of motion, of intention without mind.

Dense: wherever you look, something is already occupying the space, or about to. Even the gaps are provisional; they exist so that things can pass, not so that emptiness can endure. If you stand still—if you imagine a human presence in this world—the space around you closes in, gently at first: leaves adjusting to your outline, tendrils riffing your shape, perhaps as just another irregularity in the flows to be sampled and accounted for. You are not ignored, but neither are you recognized as special. The city-plant incorporates anomalies the way an immune system incorporates antigens: by enveloping, cataloging, and, if necessary, dissolving them.

Yet there is nothing hostile in the ordinary sense. There is only this grand, deafening experiment of living architecture, building and unbuilding itself across a warm, damp, low-lying region newly emerged into being, still discovering—which, here, means growing—what it can be.
