The first sensation is the sound.

It is not a single roar but a woven wall of it: a deep, ceaseless thunder like an ocean inside stone, layered with shrieks of stressed air, the grinding percussion of colossal machinery, and, underneath, a low note that seems less heard than felt in the bones. The air vibrates. Conversation would be impossible. Thought itself feels like it has to push through noise.

Dim light filters down through moving structures far overhead. It is never quite dark, but there is no direct sun: instead, reflections, glows, and scattered beams stray in from somewhere above a restless ceiling. The world here is warm and damp, perpetually beaded with condensation. Moisture slicks every surface, runs in threads along edges, and pools in hollows, but the place is not flooded; water is always present, always moving, never entirely at rest.

The ground is low and relatively level, an expansive basin or shallow trough that reaches outward well beyond what the eye can follow in this gloom. Yet “ground” is deceptive. There is soil in places—dark, new, loosely packed, shot through with bits of strange glassy minerals and metallic dust—but most of what carries weight are broad engineered surfaces: plates, ribs, beams, lattices, and meshes that span gulfs and cradle terraces of life. The environment feels geologically young, as if it has just recently come into being. Exposed edges are sharp; there is no rounded, weather-softened age here. Fresh fractures gleam. Sudden escarpments plunge into unseen depths, their faces damp and almost vertical, as though the world has been cut rather than worn.

Overhead, on the scale of a city turned inside out, hangs the purpose of this place: a lattice of colossal, rotating, oscillating, folding machinery. Vast rings and spirals hinge around invisible axes. Planes of semitransparent material slide and reconfigure, redirecting invisible energies. Long, blade-like fins rotate in stately, relentless sweeps, shearing the air, driving continuous cyclones that howl along prescribed paths. Cables thicker than tree trunks arc through the dimness, trembling with power and dripping with condensed mist that hisses when it falls onto charged surfaces below.

The architecture is urban in density of construction, but oddly empty of people. Platforms, corridors, and towers extend for kilometers, but many are unoccupied—long spans of structural complexity with no sign of inhabitant, as though the city is built several orders of magnitude too large for whoever or whatever lives here. You can look across an open void and see another terrace, another set of towers and ramps, but between you and them is only distance: warm, vibrating, sound-thick air.

Everywhere, the built world and the living world are entangled.

Vegetation has seized the damp spaces and microclimates with exuberance. From fractures in metallic decks, from the seams of panels, from the porous faces of synthetic stone, plants spill out: glossy fronds, jointed reeds, mats of tiny translucent leaves that glow faintly where they drink stray radiation. Vines the color of oxidized copper loop between beams like braided cables, their surfaces etched with shifting iridescent patterns that seem to respond to passing currents of field and air. Huge, paddle-shaped leaves rise from low, black soil in saucer-like basins, broad enough to collect entire rainstorms of condensation; their surfaces are beaded with droplets that never fall, held in place by faint humming fields.

The warmth keeps growth continuous. There are few flowers by terrestrial standards; instead, reproductive structures are subtle and strange: combs and fans that release motes of luminescent dust when the local air-pressure waves hit certain frequencies, or seed-pods that detach and drift in the constant artificial updrafts, tiny aerodynamic shells designed to travel along the same flows that feed the machinery.

Though lush, the vegetation is compartmentalized. Whole terraces exist as dense hanging gardens, dripping and layered, but between them stretch broad spans of bare structural desert, where nothing lives because the fields and forces are too intense or too unstable. These gaps enforce a kind of patchwork: islands of life within a continent of engineered emptiness. Even at the scale of a step, there is abrupt change—one moment a riot of green and gleaming wet surfaces, the next a stretch of naked composite material, geometrically perfect and utterly lifeless, humming with invisible purpose.

Water is everywhere in small ways. It condenses along the undersides of beams, lines up in shining rows on tension cables, appears as ghostly steam where hot conduits meet cooler air, and collects in narrow channels cut strictly where the builders wanted it to go. Opportunistic rivulets run off these channels to feed pockets of soil or drip into long, narrow basins. Some of these basins are artificial wetlands: shallow, murky, lined with mesh that anchors plant roots. In them grow lacy fronds that filter particulates out of the water, and jointed stalks that open and close in time with the subsonic pulse of the infrastructure, as if breathing with the machine.

From below—far below—comes a deeper thunder, the sound of titanic flows. You cannot see them, but you feel their consequences: the floor occasionally trembles underfoot as if something massive shifts in slow rhythm beneath the visible world. This is a place built on top of processes the scale of continents or planets: reactors, gravity engines, field inverters, or something beyond those analogies. Physics here is not merely exploited; it is sculpted. Air does not always behave as you expect; droplets curve along paths no wind seems to dictate; sometimes, in corners, dust motes hang in bright static arcs as if pinned along invisible forces.

The tech is everywhere and deeply embedded, but rarely obvious. Surfaces that look like stone respond to proximity with faint changes in temperature or texture. Rails that seem purely structural shiver when certain creatures scuttle along them, translating weight and presence into data that flows away as patterned vibrations. In places, there are transparent columns filled not with liquid but with slow-moving clouds of microscale machines—dense at the base, thinning upward—that continually rebuild, maintain, and adapt the structures around them, extruding new fibers, dissolving old ones. The distinction between building and ecosystem blurs: some of the vines carry conductive sap whose patterns modulate field strengths in surrounding latticework, making the plant part of a feedback loop that stabilizes something enormous and unseen.

Life responds to this in ways that feel alien and oddly precise. Fauna is abundant, but the space is large and the noise is great, so animals appear as bursts of motion and then vanish again, filling the emptiness without ever becoming a crowd.

Something whip-thin, many-limbed, and almost translucent glides along the underside of a beam, its limbs rearranging mid-movement, fingertips finding purchase on microscopic irregularities left by construction nanites. When one of the overhead fins swings past, forcing a wave of pressure and roaring air through the space, the creature flattens instantly, contouring itself to the surface, its skin darkening to match the beam’s composite patchwork. When the turbulence lessens, it drops, extends a membrane, and is snatched up on a local vortex, spinning noiselessly out into the dim.

Across a gap, perched on a stack of hexagonal blocks, a cluster of beings like oversized insects but with too-flexible bodies and fine, radially arranged limbs raise disk-like sensory organs to “listen” to the machinery’s patterns. Their carapaces shimmer with electricity; tiny blue arcs flick between them as they redistribute charge, making them miniature living components in the circuit of the city. Periodically, one launches itself into the air, caught and carried along by an ascending column of warm wind, rotating as it goes, its limbs spreading like an antenna array.

There are fliers adapted to the perpetual drafts: broad-winged, bat-like yet aerodynamically impossible, stitching through the gale in tight controlled loops. Their wings are thin fields, not tissue—seen only when droplets condense against them and then are flung away in fans of mist. They dive into the wakes of moving machinery, exploiting the chaos to snatch smaller organisms—darting things like glass needles—from the churn.

Sound is not merely background; it is ecology. The deafening constant roar is sculpted in layers, and life here has learned to read and join it.

In some niches, small creatures—rounded, many-gilled, with soft, resonant shells—anchor themselves to ducts or wall protrusions. They open and close their bodies in response to certain frequencies, amplifying some notes, canceling others locally. They create quiet pockets, little acoustic shadows where more delicate forms can persist. In these rare muffled alcoves, you might hear other sounds: the drip of water, the rustle of leaves, a faint clicking conversation between translucent arthropods with long, segmented antennae that taste the ever-changing spectrum of fields and adjust their behavior accordingly.

Other animals exploit the sound directly. High along a tendon-like cable, a row of perched beings emit piercing tones that you can barely distinguish through the general roar, but whose interference patterns trace complex shapes in the air—shapes that only they, with their specialized sensory frills, can perceive. For them, social space is built of pressure waves superimposed on the mechanical thunder, an invisible architecture in which dominance, warning, and invitation are all changing standing waves.

The rules of this world are stitched from interaction between the built and the living.

Gravity is not entirely uniform. Cross certain boundaries—unmarked to the human eye but clear to the creatures that live here—and weight seems to tilt. A droplet drifting slowly downwards encounters an invisible plane and, suddenly, slides sideways instead, pulled along by the geometry of a local field. Certain cliff-faces support forests growing at impossible angles, their “vertical” trunks perpendicular not to the global down but to some local, engineered direction. You can see it in how thin cascades of condensed water do not fall straight; they curve, forming long, luminous arcs through the dim.

In some open chasms, swirling columns of almost-stillness hang: voids in the roar, cylinders where the air is calm while chaos rages inches away. These quiet shafts are migratory routes; narrow-bodied, ribbon-like creatures slip up and down them, riding gentle convection currents, never touching the walls where fields would tear them apart. At the edges of these calm zones, plants grow in precise ring-shaped arrangements—the fields allow roots only in specific trajectories, and life obeys those invisible instructions.

There are hints of intelligence in the making of it all, but not necessarily of immediate presence. Habitation exists: recessed bays with arranged objects, surfaces smoothed by use rather than by design, modular enclosures that can fold open like petals. Yet they stand mostly vacant, at least at this moment. A balcony that could overlook a kilometer-wide processing chamber holds only a residue of pale dust patterned by vibrations, a bowl grown from some self-hardening organic polymer, and the iridescent husk of something that once grew there, now empty. It feels as though the makers or occupants are elsewhere, trusting the automated systems and the adapted ecosystem to keep the place alive.

Occasional pulses of unexpected light punctuate the dimness. Far away down a long axis, a filigree tower suddenly flares along its length, each segment igniting in sequence with cold white radiance that throws hard-edged shadows across the terraces. In that brief instant, the whole space becomes visible: the breadth of it, the layered decks losing themselves in haze, the vertical drops, the grid of structural members receding into vastness. Then the light gutters, and the world returns to glows and reflections. In the afterimage, you retain the sense of scale: this is not a small habitat, not even a single city. It is a region, a belt, a slice of something enormous.

Weather here is continuous but artificial. There are no clouds in the usual sense, but plumes of condensate rise where cooler flows meet hot exhaust from the unseen heart of the system. These mists spread along predefined paths, hugging ceilings, curling around beams. Sometimes they thicken into luminous fog sheets, backlit by the infrastructure’s own faint bioluminescent and electroluminescent components—microscopic maintenance swarms, tiny organisms, embedded circuitry. As they move, they deposit films of moisture that feed epiphytic mats: layers of fungus-like life whose filaments extend into the material of the city, exchanging chemical signals and perhaps data.

Predator and prey relationships thread through all of this.

Near a water channel, a sudden shift in the roar—a component frequency cutting out, then surging back—triggers a reaction on a whole micro-terrace. Small herbivores, like fleshy coils with ciliated undersides, retract into protective folds among root tangles. Moments later, something unseen rushes by overhead, displacing air in a way you feel as pressure on your skin more than as wind. A heartbeat later, a rain of shredded leaf fragments descends, caught in the machine-driven gale.

On a vertical face where gravity has been twisted, a sheet of plant matter looks like moss from a distance. Closer, it resolves into dense rosettes of tongue-like fronds that snap shut on drifting spores and tiny fliers. Within this living carpet, slightly larger creatures—flat, hexagonal, stacked like coins—shift slowly from one clump to another, consuming both plant and animal, leaving behind latticed excreta that harden into pale, angular patterns. These in turn reroute condensation and change the local electric field, sending ripples upward into the machinery’s feedback loops. Predation here is not just biological; it feeds back into the city’s tuning.

Despite the alienness, certain patterns feel familiar: edges where life is concentrated—along water, at transition zones between field regimes, in turbulence-sheltered pockets. Open expanses stripped of biology where conditions are too harsh. Corridors where species move in regular flows, like streets. Perches that command views over both built and living structures. Refuge spaces carved out against violence, whether mechanical or ecological.

If there are humans or their descendants here, they are rare now, or they inhabit scales and layers not immediately visible. Perhaps they move unstably between gravity wells, or fold themselves into higher-dimensional niches in the infrastructure. You sense their hand in the system’s logic: in how the city maintains breathable temperatures despite the waste heat of whatever titanic processes power it; in the presence of stair-like inclines and handrail-width ledges entirely unnecessary for other observed life; in markings—smooth grooves, repeating geometric motifs—cut into walkable surfaces where no animal seems to care.

And yet the place no longer feels like it belongs to them, not entirely. Too many symbioses have arisen between wild life and machine: plants that will die if a certain frequency of hum ceases; maintenance swarms that incorporate viral genetic fragments from local microbes; predators whose hunting tactics rely on predictable pulses from distant reactors; scavengers that emerge only when particular diagnostic bursts silence parts of the roar. The rules of the world now assume both the infrastructure and the ecosystem. Remove one, and the other would shudder, collapse, and then reform into something else.

In the distance, beyond multiple overlapping layers of structure and foliage, the sound shifts again. Something vast is changing state. The low note that lived at the edge of perception deepens, spreads, drowns out some of the higher machinery shrieks. With it, the air’s vibration pattern alters; water droplets on leaves rearrange into new standing patterns; certain animals retreat into crevices while others pour out of them, recognizing a recurring cycle: the turning-on or off of a subsystem, a regular storm in the engineered weather.

Slowly, high above, one of the colossal rings begins to tilt, its motion almost imperceptible yet accompanied by an earthlike rumble. The light dims further as its bulk eclipses one of the distant, unseen external illumination sources. Shadow spreads across terraces and chasms, and as it passes, some parts of the vegetation flare briefly with internal bioluminescence—cool greens and blues like swallowed starlight—then fade, as if recalibrating their photosynthetic strategies to the new orientation.

Everything here is in motion: machinery cycling through complex programs; air churning; water condensing, flowing, evaporating; fields cycling; organisms hunting, hiding, feeding, reproducing. Yet because of the scale, it also feels strangely empty. You can stand—or imagine standing—on a balcony above a chasm that plunges for hundreds of meters and stretches laterally for kilometers, and see no single large, obvious inhabitant, no crowd. Only the suggestion of life in flickers of movement, in leaves stirring against the direction of the wind, in flutters along cables, in faint glows that drift and vanish. There is so much space between presences; so much built volume, mostly air, humming with invisible structure.

This landscape is a young, immense, dimly lit engine-garden: an urban-scale machine whose physics exceeds contemporary understanding, seeded with life that has taken root in its damp seams and carved out niches amid violence and thunder. Its sharp, new surfaces have not yet worn down; its fields have not stabilized into perfect equilibrium. Things here are still becoming, adapting, testing the boundaries of what forces will allow.

And the noise never stops.
