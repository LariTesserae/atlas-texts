The first thing is the height.

Not a single peak, not a clean ridge, but a continent of worn stone raised so long ago that every sharpness has slumped, every cliff has become a leaning wall, every canyon a softened crease. You are on a high, almost level roof of the world: a broken plateau, its horizons blurred by distance and by hanging, shredded clouds that snag on the taller remnants of mountains. The air is thin and cool, but not hostile; you can fill your lungs, though it has that faint, metallic clarity of altitude that makes every sound feel too close.

Water is scarce and yet everywhere in suggestion. It clings rather than flows. Most days the light is clear and unsentimental, pale sun through a high sky, but the stone sweats. Overnight temperatures sink just enough that dew condenses on every surface: on rock, on metal, on bark, on glassy plant-membranes. It beads and runs in slow threads into hollows, feeding shallow pans and long, narrow trenches of water that evaporate by midafternoon. Rain comes rarely, but when it does it is abrupt and violent—hard vertical shafts that hammer the plateau for an hour, then stop as if a switch were thrown. The land remembers each of those storms in the polished channels etched into its crust.

At ground level, though, there is almost no “ground” to see. The solid base of this high world is nearly buried in plant.

Not a lush, tangled jungle—it is too cold, too open for that—but a total colonization of the stone by living tissue. The plants here are not separate things in soil; they *are* the soil, the walls, the streets, the roofs. Imagine a city that was once carved in rock—terraces, plazas, shafts, viaducts, massive retaining walls holding back the edges of the plateau—and then imagine every exposed surface, over ages, annexed by a single, multi-form botanical organism and its allied species.

The prevailing color is not green. At a distance, the landscape reads as a faded, lichened bronze: tawny, grey-violet, soot-black, and muted moss-colors, caught in a constant, low shimmer as wet surfaces catch the sun. Green appears in pockets, more vivid for its rarity—sheets of saturated emerald photosynthetic tissue on sun-facing planes, sprigs of neon moss where runoff collects.

Walk close and the built environment emerges.

The “city” has the scale and layout of a large, spread-out industrial town, but its logic is sideways. Once, perhaps, there were steel gantries and concrete causeways, warehouses and cooling towers clamped to the fins and steps of the plateau. Now those geometries survive only as fossils in plantmass: straight ribs of woody tissue that keep perfect right angles where normal growth would curve; broad decks paved in fibrous, living mat laid over the ghost of some old factory floor; arcs of fused, glassy plant-lignin that remember the skeletons of cranes.

There are streets, but they are not paved; they are absence. The city’s “roads” are long canyons in the biomass—a meter or two lower than their surroundings—hairless, shorn corridors of pale under-tissue where something (machines, or the plants themselves in another mode) regularly passes and scrapes or burns growth back. They wind and loop through the plateau’s terraces, varying from broad avenues, where five or six tracked engines might pass in a row, to narrow service paths only shoulder-wide. Their surfaces have a faint give to them, like rubber over stone, and a faint warmth, as if residual metabolic heat seeps through from deep below.

Buildings rise on either side of these paths, but “building” is descriptive only in outline. Massive, columnar trunks grow straight up from the old foundations, then bend and flatten, flowing outward into thick plates that knit together in midair, forming roofs and overpasses, sometimes entire artificial cliffs: vertical, ridged surfaces twenty or thirty meters high. Under-sides dangle root-like cables of plant-fiber, some stiff, some supple and slowly twitching. From many of those hang dead, inorganic objects, caught and absorbed over centuries—chunks of rusted metal, lengths of track, cracked ceramic cylinders, shelves of old, unrecognizably blistered machinery—embedded like bones in fat.

Inside these structures is where the weirdness sharpens.

Pass through one of the many oval apertures—doorways extruded like lips from the wall tissue—and you enter air that smells of cold sap, machine oil, and ozone. The inner walls are smoother and darker, laced with pale conduits that pulse faintly with fluid. Floors slope in ways that do not align with the outer architecture: stairs that climb sideways into walls, balconies that do not overlook anything, just hang in air within huge, hollow domes whose upper curvatures vanish into pulsing gloom.

Light inside is never simply darkness versus fixture. Some chambers are bright from captured daylight, fed in through vertical shafts lined with transparent plant-membrane, diffusing the sun into a pearly glow. Others are lit entirely from within the plant: bands and nodes of bioluminescent tissue, mostly blue-white but with slow-cycling washes of amber or violet, flickering not on any human timescale but to some deep organismic rhythm. Elsewhere, old industrial lighting systems still function intermittently: long metal tubes that jitter and snarl with man-made fluorescence, popping on and off at random. The two forms of light interfere in strange ways—when the fluorescents flare, some of the plant’s glows dim as if suppressed; when they sputter out, the organic light surges, bright as if angrily reclaiming its territory.

Sound fills these spaces—even outside, in the open.

The plateau hums. Not figuratively: a loud, physical hum, a layered mechanical and biological sound that never fully stops, only changes in timbre. Some of it is obvious: machines are still running here. In the middle distance you catch the steady throbbing of a large engine—deep, four-beat rotations, like a cargo ship buried somewhere beneath your feet. Closer, a higher, raspier whine spins up and down as some automated assembly line cycles through a process and idles, then cycles again. Metal-on-metal rattles, too: chain loops shifting, conveyors starting and stopping, the abrupt clack of pistons meeting end-caps.

But threaded through all that are the plant’s own voices. When wind trails over the plateau’s edge, the great membranes that line certain chasms begin to vibrate, producing a drawn-out, dissonant chord that rises and falls as the gusts shift. On gentler days, you hear a constant patter: the drip of condensed moisture from a thousand leaf-surfaces into collection basins, down threads into storage bulbs in the deep. At intervals—more felt through soles and bone than heard—comes a subsonic boom, like a distant collapse, as somewhere a whole section of plant-architecture flexes, or the plateau’s crust settles a millimeter against an internal skeleton of roots and old steel.

The air is never truly quiet; your own movements are only one tiny part of a shared, loud metabolism.

This place is not crowded, though. For all the scale of construction and all that noise, there is emptiness. Long stretches of corridor curve away with no figures in them. Plaza-equivalents—wide, shallow bowls in the plateau where, once, perhaps, vehicles marshaled—are now covered in a living carpet only thinly patterned with structures: low humped nodes, vertical ribs, a scattering of slender photosynthetic spires. Between them there can be fifty, a hundred meters of unoccupied surface. Above, the roofs are high and mostly bare, occasionally punctuated by immense chimneys—hollow, fluted towers that vent warm breath into the sky, giving off a muffled roar, releasing steam and faint, glittering particles that fall back like extremely slow snow.

Animal life in this entire region is oddly sparse. You do not see a bustle of birds or insects. No small things dart in the corners of your vision. Instead, once in a while, something appears, moves, and disappears again, with that dreamlike certainty of being real but inexplicably scarce.

High along the edges of the plateau proper, where plant sheets end and old rock or metal protrudes, you might glimpse a thin, almost skeletal quadruped, hairless and pale, with branching antler-like sensory fans rather than horns. It picks its way along a ledge, places its limbs delicately on bare mineral, and then, in a twitch of wrong perspective, seems to step backward into the plant-wall, folding and vanishing between two layers of tissue as if it had always been only a surface pattern.

Once every few hours a great shadow passes across an open basin. Look up and you will see a flying organism the size of a small aircraft—but it glides without flapping, its wings stiff, translucent, refracting light into oily spectrums. It emits a continuous, modulated tone that harmonizes with the plateau’s hum. Sometimes, for a moment, its shadow does not match its body: it lags, or surges ahead, or splinters into several overlapping silhouettes, then re-joins.

Other creatures are harder to classify as fauna or machinery. Blocky, low-slung things traverse the roads: segmented, tank-like forms with treads grown from woven root and frames plated in barked metal. They are covered in exploratory fronds and small, rotating sensor-eyes of dark glass. They move with the purposeful, stop-start rhythm of industrial vehicles, yet when they encounter a patch of new growth they slow, extend fine filaments, brush and test it before either crushing it back or allowing it to remain. Their exhaust is not smoke but a hot, herbal vapor, condensing almost instantly into glittering droplets on nearby surfaces.

You soon suspect: these are not distinct categories. The “plants” are machines; the “machines” are plants. The city is an organism that once was hardware and is now biology and has never fully decided between them. Its industry is its metabolism. The loudness is digestion, maintenance, self-exploration.

Water, again, is the rare treasure around which much of this metabolism organizes.

Under the plateau’s skin, there are great cisterns—voids scoured from old mines or storage bunkers, now lined with layered, living tissue. They collect the brief rains, the nightly dew, the occasional thin runoff from surviving mountain snowfields higher up. Within those cisterns float lattices of root and pipe, churning and cooling, exuding a constant soft hiss. Moisture is pumped upward and outward along capillary bundles that run through every column and wall, vented as mist along sun-facing slopes, forming thin, translucent webs of condensation that sustain the outermost photosynthetic sheets.

Viewed regionally, the whole plateau-city is a water trap: every upward surface subtly tilted inward, every groove leading eventually to a storage basin, every piece of architecture curved to catch or condense. Yet at your feet the world still feels dry between drips, the air only marginally humid. In the long gaps between storms the system starves slowly, shedding parts of itself—allowing outer husks to go dormant and crack so inner layers can drink.

Time here is wrong in another way.

The erosion of the rock is complete; the plateau’s macro-shape is an end-state. But within that skeletal stability, microstructures are in flux. Walls slowly migrate; doorways seal over or open overnight. A corridor you passed earlier is subtly different when you retrace your steps: the slope is steeper; a rib has become a full buttress; a thin membrane now seals what was open air. The plant-city adjusts to unseen constraints—shifting loads deep in the stone, the fluctuating function of very old machinery, or more abstract variables: information-flows, electrical potentials, long-degraded programming still trying to “optimize” a biological infrastructure it no longer fully understands.

In some places, this confusion of rules becomes obvious.

You reach a terrace where gravity is inconsistent. Not dramatically so—you do not fall upward—but as you walk, your weight changes. Three steps feel normal; on the fourth your body is abruptly light, your footfall too long, you almost overstride; two more and you are heavy, legs leaden, as if walking through chest-deep water. Objects dropped here fall in curved paths that do not accord with the slope or wind. Tiny water droplets hang in the air slightly longer than they should, then slide sideways along invisible planes, eventually coalescing into strings that the plant eagerly tongues with flexible filaments.

In a broad basin, thin sheets of transparent plant-tissue rise straight up from the ground and stand motionless in the air, cutting the space into tall, shifting prisms. You realize they are not just surfaces but boundaries: sound changes when you cross them, becomes muffled, or occluded, or strangely amplified. Stand on one side of a sheet and the distant engines are loud; walk through it, and the outside noise drops to a murmur while a new interior hum emerges, as if you have stepped into a slightly out-of-phase copy of the same place. Light bends slightly, too; edges misalign. You can see your own shape in other cells at odd angles, moving with you but not synchronized.

Here, reality is no longer a single, continuous medium but a laminated stack, and the plant is the only thing that appears to know how to root through all the layers at once.

Signs of old human or human-like presence remain, warped but legible. Rails embedded in floors where tram systems once rode now serve as conductive veins for slow, shuttled charges. A gantry crane’s track runs the length of an open trench, but the crane itself has been half-consumed—its trolley replaced by a bulbous, living pod that crawls along the rail, extruding and retracting manipulator-vines. Decorative reliefs on a great, curved wall—the branding of some long-dead industrial concern—have been gently traced over and thickened by plant growth, each raised letter now a spine that feeds a dense fan of cilia collecting condensing fog.

Technology level is industrial, but it is a distorted industriality.

There are no handheld devices, no fine digital interfaces. Instead: big engines in pits, piston banks the size of houses, boilers and cooling towers either decayed or half-alive. Large-bore conduits shudder as dense fluids, seeds, or unknown slurries are pumped through them. Turbines spin, driven not by fossil fuel but by temperature gradients the plant enforces: it guides warm fluids here, cool fluids there, forcing circulation that turns blades and, in turn, drives generators. Some of those generators still feed old, ossified networks—thick cables disappearing into walls where they now simply heat the plant’s flesh or trigger odd, ritualized motion in forgotten machines that do nothing but cycle.

Elsewhere you find rooms filled with control panels, banks of rusted dials and lever arrays half-swallowed by bark and vine. Needles twitch, lights blink, but the labels are obscured, language eaten and replaced by new patternings: raised ridges and dimples tracking meanings you can’t read but which root-fingers trace with almost human care. The plant remembers that once there was “control” and now simulates it, perhaps plugging its own slow, chemical thinking into these dead consoles, causing a small toggling of metal that, somewhere far away in the depths, still matters.

Weather passes over all this in its own detached cycles.

Clouds often come in from one direction only, always the same—blunt, grey masses that flatten as they reach the plateau, their undersides torn into tendrils by the rising breath of the cistern chimneys. They release brief, fierce rains mostly at the edges; the center often remains under a hard, dry, silver-white sky. Wind is channelled by the plateau’s relic topography and its grown superstructure into large, stable flows. Some avenues howl constantly with funnelled drafts; others are windless pockets, the air there heavy, full of engine fumes and plant exhalations, warm enough that condensed water streams continuously down the walls like sweating skin.

The dynamism of the system shows in subtle, slow choreography.

Great, horizontal petals of tissue fold and unfold over certain basins during the day, tracking the sun like titanic heliotropes. Entire multi-story walls slowly tilt over the course of hours, catching light on different surfaces in turn, then re-erect at night. When a rare predator appears—a long, ribbon-like creature curling from shadow, knife-jawed, gliding a meter above the ground as if swimming through magnetism—the city reacts: nearby membranes stiffen, corridors constrict, lights shift from blue-white to a more yellowish tone, and that section’s hum deepens. The predator darts through, finds nothing moving, veers away. As it leaves, the structures relax, and normal function resumes.

Despite the noise and motion, there is an underlying sense of extreme age, of finality. The plateau has settled into a long, deep exhale. Wars, collapses, extinctions—whatever upheavals shaped this place—are long past. The plant-city is not expanding so much as holding, adjusting, reconfiguring within hard boundaries imposed by dwindling water and thinning air and the decayed irrationalities of its own half-forgotten design. It is not in its prime; it’s in its long afterlife, an industrial organism that has outlived its makers and now simply *is*.

Yet within that “simply” lies a strange, persistent tension.

The rules of physics still mostly apply; gravity pulls, fluids flow, metal rusts. But they bend around localized strangenesses like water around stones. Time slows slightly in some galleries—you watch a droplet fall, and it takes a whole, measured breath to cross a meter. In others it is subtly faster: a scrap of paper exposed on a ledge yellows and crumbles over the span of a few hours. Electronica that *should* be dead—simple coils, iron cores, wire—continue to resonate, as if the background fabric of reality is being pinged and occasionally answered by something deeper, older, running under or alongside this plateau.

It might be that whatever raised this region in the first place did more than heave rock. It may have twisted the underpinnings of space-time a fraction of a degree off true, and the plant-city, with its great, slow, distributed nervous system, has learned to use those twists the way a forest uses wind: not understanding it, but growing in its shape, exploiting it where possible.

Standing at the edge of a vast terrace, with the city-organism spreading away across the plateau in all directions—low, sparse, punctuated by taller, stranger growths and the occasional jut of naked, utterly dead stone—you are aware of multiple scales at once.

At the smallest: droplets catching light on a single cilium; a root-tip probing into a hairline crack, sensing faint dampness far below.

At the middle: a corridor reshaping over the course of a day; a single industrial engine cycling through its erratic duty.

At the largest: the entire plateau breathing over seasons, thickening and thinning, its plant-skin tightening during dry, bright years, swelling and extruding new structures after rare cycles of plentiful rain.

And just beyond your peripheral comprehension: the deeper, slower transformations, where reality itself erodes, not into flatness, but into something else—layered, folded, laminated like the tissues of the city itself.

This is a high, cool, damp-scarce world where plants have eaten architecture and industry has merged with sap. It is loud with the noises of its own persistence. Animals drift through rarely, like questions the place asked itself once and mostly stopped needing to answer. The terrain is ancient, almost finished, yet within it processes churn, and the rules you trust buckle slightly underfoot.
