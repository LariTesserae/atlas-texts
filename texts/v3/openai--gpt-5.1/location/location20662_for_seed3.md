The city does not sit on the mountain so much as hang in its decision to exist.

From the distance—if there were such a thing as distance here—you first notice the vertical. Not a single peak, but a columnar region where the world has simply chosen to go up. Cliffs phase into each other like overlapping panes of smoky glass. Sheer faces run for hundreds of meters, then stutter, misalign, slip sideways and resume, as though a mountain range had been slid into existence in misregistered layers.

At the base and between the rising planes, the ground is surprisingly quiet and clean: bare, grey bedrock still smelling of new stone when the air turns the right way. The rock is matte, faintly porous, not yet humbled into soil or dust. Cracks are thin and young, just beginning to network. Where water gathers, it is not from old rivers but from condensation and run-off, as if the sky sometimes gives up on holding itself together and beads on the stone. 

Pockets of green collect where the water lingers. They’re not wide fields, but concentrated gardens: thick mats of ferns, low shrubs with waxy leaves, crawls of moss so saturated they look almost black-green. Between these oases: long scoured stretches of stone, pale lichen freckles, and the occasional solitary tree clinging to a vertical seam—itself lush, but alone against vast surfaces.

The air is cool and carries a dense, metallic tang. Breathing it feels like walking into a room where machines have just stopped: a residue of warmth in a cooled space, a sense of recent activity.

Above, the light is technically present, but reluctant. A permanent twilight, fed by a sky that doesn’t commit to color. Instead it is layered strata of dimness, like overlapping sheets of smoked glass, glowing faintly from within. It never quite becomes night, never quite day; brightness circulates around the horizon like something lost, refusing to rise or set. On some cliff faces, this glow pools and bends, as if the sky’s dim light can slide down into a valley and rest there.

You realize the “distance” you thought you saw is not depth but repetition. Terrain echoes. That ledge on the far side of the ravine is also this ledge, folded around an angle that doesn’t exist in Euclidean geometry. Valleys widen and narrow not with the lay of the land but with the attention you give them. Walk toward a particular spire and it may recede, slipping into a background that should be miles away yet still intersects with your current path. Movement here is less about going somewhere and more about choosing which version of “here” to collapse into. 

In the midst of all this stands the city.

It pours down several of the larger cliff complexes and spiders across the ledges between them, a vertical-industrial organism. Think of scaffolded shipyards, stacked worker housing, narrow factories, bridges, cranes, elevators—then turn the whole ensemble ninety degrees so that streets are not horizontal bands but planes that sometimes slope up a wall as if gravity forgot itself.

There are tiers of steel and poured composite, ribs of blackened concrete driven like staples into the still-forming rock. Long trellis-span bridges arc from cliff to cliff, some solid, some half-built, some apparently finishing their connections into bare air—only, from a slightly different angle, you notice they do connect, just to a version of the opposite cliff that isn’t currently aligned with your own. Trams flicker along these routes, sometimes gliding into what looks like the side of a cliff and vanishing into a route that can’t logically fit inside the visible stone.

The city is dense in structure yet sparse in its occupation of the land. It hugs the verticals, leaving large voids of open rock and cool air between clusters of construction. Blocky factories, turbine housings, and power stacks cling to overhangs and protrude out into ravines, but they don’t stitch the space into a continuous urban mass. There are many buildings, much infrastructure, but they are arranged with so much height and depth that emptiness still dominates the spatial experience. You can always look up or down and find long falls of nothing.

Pipes run everywhere. Some are conventional—metal, riveted, wrapped in insulation—carrying water drawn from condensation reservoirs and pumped along the cliffs. Others are stranger: glassy conduits inside which a dim, viscous luminescence drifts slowly, taking minutes to move a meter, like glowing oil resisting time. These pipes cross the air on suspension brackets, burrow into rock, reemerge from walls at subtly different angles than they entered. At certain junctions, their light bleeds into the surrounding stone, etching faint veins of dull glow that sink slowly deeper over the years, as if the city is unintentionally teaching the mountain to remember electricity.

Water is present but never abundant. It collects in gutter-tanks under eaves, in broad, shallow pans that ring the edges of factories, in cisterns chiseled into sheltered pockets of the rock. Thin waterfalls appear and vanish down the cliffs after a condensation event, trickles that thread through metal grating and vanish into catchbasins. The sound of water is intermittent: a drip chorus in one walkway, the distant hush of a temporary fall elsewhere, then long stretches of dry stone and humming machinery.

Vegetation responds to this rhythm with intensity where it can. On the underside of certain bridges, mosses and epiphytes hang like dense beards, drinking the condensed breath of the engines above. In shadowed gutters where mist always forms at the shift of the dim light, thick ropes of vine climb the supports, broad dark leaves unfolding into the narrowest bands of permanent twilight. There are pocket-gardens on outcrop balconies, stacked terraces of damp soil hauled in from somewhere else, irrigated by elaborate skeins of pipe: luxuriant patches of broadleaf herbs, climbing beans, spongy groundcovers, and small fruiting shrubs that look half-wild, half-engineered to cope with erratic moisture.

In natural crevices away from the built clusters, vegetation forms hidden chambers of cool breath: lusher, more tangled, but hemmed in by the scarcity of depth in the rock itself. Roots hit raw stone quickly and must spread sideways in shallow sheets. This makes the plants themselves wide, splayed, clinging. Trees grown here are not tall, but expansive—leaning out over empty space, their crowns a dense, wind-sculpted mass of leaves catching every stray drop of sweat from the cliffs.

Fauna abounds in the margins, in the overlaps.

You first notice the fliers. Not birds, exactly. Some are almost birdlike—winged shapes launched from balcony rails, gliding in the updrafts rising along the cliff-face. But their wings kink and hinge at impossible extra joints, unfolding surface as they bank, changing shape mid-flight. Seen close, you realize their feathers are more like layered, translucent plates, each one faintly opalescent. In the dim sky, they catch stray strands of light and scatter them, leaving ghostly streaks hanging in the air for seconds after they pass—light that forgets to stop being disturbed.

Between buildings, insect analogues clatter and hum. Metallic-shelled beetle-things graze on lichen and metal oxidation, rasping at railings with mouthparts that generate a faint, tuned whine. Some of them seem to eat rust itself, leaving cleaned, raw surfaces behind. Others drink from the viscous glowing pipes through hair-thin taps, the light inside their bodies slowing down as it passes, making their abdomens glow with a delayed, throbbing echo of illumination.

On the rock ledges below the city, pale quadrupeds climb with unsettling ease. They’re goat-sized but more elongated, with sets of splayed, many-toed feet that stick to new stone like suction. Their horns are twisted, reflective spirals that catch and store dim light for hours, so that at certain periods the cliff-sides are dotted with drifting, horn-shaped glows. They graze not just on the sparse grasses and shrubs but on something else—the “latent” matter of the rock, perhaps. After they pass, the stone in their paths seems microscopically more stable, slightly more eroded, as if their feeding accelerates time for the geology, encouraging it to age.

Inside the city proper, the boundary between wild and engineered life blurs. Vent-shafts exhale warm air, and from them soft-bodied creatures emerge—translucent floaters that ride the thermal currents like jellyfish in invisible water. They drift through alleys, pulsing faintly with the frequencies of machinery they’ve just passed over. Their presence dampens sound for a moment, absorbing noise before releasing it again somewhere else with a washed-out echo. One screams of a distant siren may reappear as a faint keening hours later down in the loading docks.

The city is industrial, but its industry is slow and oddly contemplative. Massive presses and forges operate on cycles that take hours per stroke. Cranes swivel with the unhurried certainty of glaciers. Conveyor belts carry raw stone or dense, dark ore from one platform to another, but you can watch a single block complete its journey over what feels like half a day. Power flows are tuned to this scale: humming transformers the size of buildings, flywheel stacks turning with thick, patient force, storing energy not for moments but for seasons.

From any mid-level balcony you can look across and see entire sections of the city apparently frozen: gantries half-extended, buckets suspended along cableways, valves captured just as they begin to open. Watch long enough, and you realize nothing is truly stopped—everything is simply moving at a rate that resists casual perception. The cranes will complete their arc, the buckets will tip, the valves will open. It just may take weeks.

Noise here does not come from frenetic movement but from scale. The low roar of wind threading the canyons of infrastructure. The continuous bass thrum of generators that turn so slowly their rotation is more suggestion than fact. Distant metal-on-metal booms that ring out like the heartbeat of a leviathan turning in its sleep deep inside the rock. Pipes moan softly as temperature differences push and pull at their contents. Layered above this, a patchwork of more immediate sounds: the rattle of a tram car passing across a misaligned bridge, the hydraulic sigh of an elevator door, the clank of tools somewhere overhead or far below.

Sometimes, sounds fail to stay where they were made. A shout that should echo once and fade instead curves along a handrail, slipping through the metal and emerging tens of meters away with only a small delay. A dropped object might ring down a corridor, turn a corner on its own, and continue its reverberation in a stairwell you haven’t reached yet. The world is not quite consistent about what travels in straight lines. The city’s builders work with this casually, speaking into grilles that carry their voices to remote platforms, listening to the rock to read machine status in grumbling choruses that drift in from no obvious direction.

Light, too, has its own local rules. The dimness is not uniform; it collects and thins in ways that don’t track with the visible geometry. A shadow thrown by a catwalk might continue across empty air long after the structure ends, a floating band of slightly cooler darkness. Stains of old illumination cling to walls, patterns of brighter and duller patches not linked to any lamp. In certain courtyards, reflections pool more convincingly than their sources warrant, as if light is slowly filtering downwards under its own weight, concentrating in the low places.

This gives the city—and the cliffs it clings to—a sense of layered time. You are never quite sure if what you’re seeing belongs to now or to a previous alignment of light and form. A staircase that in one period leads to a functioning workshop might, at another, ferry you up to a balcony overlooking an empty volume of sky where the workshop will someday be.

Erosion, in the conventional sense, is barely underway. Rock faces are sharp, their fractures clean; ledges are risky, unmarred by generations of crumbling. Yet processes have begun, coaxed along by life and machinery. Wherever water repeatedly touches stone—under a condensation gutter, around a leaky joint in a pipe—the rock has started to curve, hollowing into smooth basins that look older than they can be. Underfoot, the most walked-on surfaces are just beginning to polish, grinding a patina of dust that hints at future soil.

The whole region feels embryonic, halfway between raw geology and fully realized landscape. The city exists not as an afterthought imposed on a stable mountain, but as a scaffold around which the mountain is learning how to be.

Sometimes, at the edges of the built zones, this process becomes visible. You can watch a hairline crack in the rock thicken—not with crumbling but with decision—in real time, as if the stone is choosing where it wants to break in a thousand years and starting the paperwork now. Ledges that were a hand’s breadth wide last season are a step’s breadth this season, grown not by falling rock but by the gradual re-negotiation of where vertical begins. Entire narrow spires that once stood solitary now have ghostly twins, shimmering near them, gradually condensing into full solidity over the course of months. 

The citizens do not seem alarmed by this. Their architecture anticipates partial unrealities. Many beams end in adjustable joints that can be slackened and re-tightened when their anchor-point slides sideways in ontology. Walkways include gaps and telescoping segments to absorb topological shifts. It’s not uncommon to find a stair that passes through a short stretch of “thin” space, each step faintly double-exposed; they nail grating over it and paint warning markings on the approach. Children run through it on dares.

The fauna have adapted more fluidly. Those cliff-climbing quadrupeds occasionally step onto surfaces that don’t quite exist yet, hanging in mid-air for a moment, then continuing when reality catches up, their bodies briefly elongated or compressed like someone mis-scrubbed them in time. The gliding fliers can change course in the middle of nothing, using air that you realize, belatedly, may not belong entirely to this layer of the world.

In the ravines below, where stone descends out of sight, a deeper din sounds: slow, resonant churnings. Something massive, mechanical or otherwise, is at work down there. Ore processing, perhaps, or the world’s own gears catching. Sometimes the sound rises thin and high, as if strained through a great distance. Other times, it comes up full and close, as though the bottom of the canyon has moved to stand just beneath the lowest bridge, then sunk away again.

Despite the density of structures, there are long stretches of emptiness at every scale. A single narrow tower might rise from a ledge to vanish into the upward dimness without any neighboring buildings for tens of meters around. Bridges span gulfs that open onto nothing but air and mist. Whole terraces of aligned foundations await construction that does not come quickly, each plinth sporting only the beginnings of columns—stubs of reinforcement steel, temporary cranes sleeping in place. The sense is not of abandonment but of a project unfolding on a timescale that makes waiting a standard architectural phase.

In that waiting, plants sneak in. Green creeps along cable housings, spreads in tank shadows, fills any drip-line with layered leaves. The vegetation is lush where it can be, but these patches are islands in a greater sea of bare stone and metal. It makes life highly legible: you can stand on a high catwalk and see, scattered along the cliffs, every bright knot of growth, every damp corner where something is flourishing. They shine against the general austerity of the rock, their darker greens nearly luminous in the dim ambient light.

The climate remains consistently cool, moderated by the enormous thermal inertia of the young stone and the near-constant wind. There are no violent storms, only slow mood shifts: one period the air is dry enough that every breath feels grainy; another, low clouds move through the ravines, leaving a sheen of moisture over everything, beading on cables and window panes, filling the pockets and cisterns. The city reacts with simple mechanisms: shutters creak closed to keep mist out of machinery; collectors fold open to catch droplets; drainage channels sing softly as they guide new water toward reservoirs.

Change here is layered and patient. The city adds another span, another deck, another soft-lantern district along a rock wall. Ledges widen and cliff-lines bend on geological time with a slight assist from grazing creatures and industrial vibrations. Seeds fall into a new crack, and by the next shift of the dim sky, there’s a sapling. By the next, a small, dense tree leaning out over another new void.

Nothing is still, but nothing is in a hurry. Even the weirdness abides by this slowness: reality doesn’t shatter; it sighs, stretches, and resettles. A bridge that once connected two platforms perfectly now meets one half a step too high, so someone installs an extra stair and paints a line. A corridor that used to deliver you to the base of a tower nudges you instead to the side of a different structure; a sign is hung, and the old route remembered only in how the echoes travel.

Standing on a balcony at what passes for evening, you feel the whole landscape breathing very slowly: the mountain learning itself, the city teaching it, life and machinery weaving into the rock’s first ideas of erosion and structure. Above, the dim sky presses down in layered twilight, its uncommitted light pooling and thinning along the cliff faces. Below, the abundant creatures clamber, glide, float, and hum, busy at rhythms that are, for them, simply normal.

Somewhere far along the cliff-line, a half-real spire takes another step toward solidity. A pipe groans as its contents settle a fraction slower. Moss slicks another centimeter across bare stone. The city hums on, loud but unhurried, suspended between what is and what has not yet decided to become.
