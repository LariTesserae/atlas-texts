You don’t see sky here.

The first awareness of the place is vertical: a sense of weight above you, an immeasurable mass of rock and packed sediments pressing down, muffled and absolute. Yet the space itself is open—galleries, canyons, chambers, and avenues hollowed out beneath the crust, large enough to hold districts, rail lines, whole groves of structures.

What replaces rock as “ground” is not stone at all, but plant.

Not lush, not choking—more like a gigantic scaffolding of living matter. The floor is a sparse, fibrous mat of pale, woody tissue that gives very slightly underfoot, a dry and quiet springiness. It’s cool down here, the air holding steady at something like a high, overcast autumn day: not cold enough for your breath to fog, but cool enough that every surface feels faintly chilled to the touch.

There is almost no free water. No puddles, no trickles, no dripping stalactites. The dryness is immediate in your sinuses, a crisp, papery feeling. Yet the place is not dead; it has found another way to move nutrients and memory around.

The walls and ceilings are a living architecture of vascular plant-matter, pale gray-green to bone-white, shot through with faint color like veins in marble. Imagine roots that decided to become rooms and streets instead of threads in soil. They have grown into seamless ribs and arches, hollow trunks pressed together into pillars, great buttresses of lignified tissue that once might have been roots or stems on some ancestral surface.

From a distance, the city looks like it has been bored out of a single continuous organism.

### The plant-city

There are buildings here, but “building” is a compromise term. Much of what counts as infrastructure is simply plant growth disciplined into shapes.

Blocks of “habitation” rise in terraces from the fibrous ground, facades patterned with regular openings that suggest windows and doors. Yet, up close, you see that the edges are rounded, the corners grown rather than cut. The surfaces are striated like the inside of a cut root—concentric growth layers, each season-eternal ring slightly different in tone.

Some structures are nakedly industrial: vertical cylinders like silos or chimneys grown from densely packed vascular bundles, ringed by metallic bands and platforms that allow access. Tubular corridors loop between them, some purely biological—translucent, strung like intestines—others sleeved in metal and composite cladding, where human or something-like-human intention has overridden the plant’s own priorities.

Rails run through the city: narrow-gauge tracks that curve across plazas and disappear into tunnels whose walls pulse faintly. Small, squat locomotives—metal, riveted, utilitarian—pull chains of flatbed cars loaded with spools of cable, crates of mineral pellets, sections of mechanical equipment. Their engines are not loud. They chuff and hum rather than roar, exhaust diffused through filters that exhale only a faintly sweet, resinous smell.

Above these obvious artifacts of industry, an invisible infrastructure spreads through the living mass itself. Within the walls, the plant’s own vascular system functions as both plumbing and wiring. Thin conduits of glass, metal, or chitin tap into these arteries, piercing the living tissue at carefully ringed junctions, harvesting pressure, osmotic gradients, chemical signals. Somewhere behind each conduit is a set of instruments, valves, and regulators that maintain a truce between city and substrate.

The city is not crowded. Structures are spaced with deliberate emptiness, separated by broad, low plazas of bare fibrous mat or gently knotted root. There are avenues where, for long stretches, nothing stands at all—just darkness receding between columns of growth, lit intermittently by pale lamps and luminescent fungal fans. The settlement is clearly “urban” in its planning, its networks, its persistent machinery, but it is not dense. Vast, unused spaces yawn between clusters of construction, as if the inhabitants are still in the early chapters of a much larger plan.

### Light and atmosphere

There is light, and its presence feels slightly absurd at first, given how far from any sun you stand.

Soft panels of bioluminescent tissue are embedded overhead, like stretched skins in the hollows between arched ribs. They glow with a steady, diffuse brightness: not daylight-strong, but enough to read by, enough to flatten shadows. Their color tends toward a very pale greenish-white, tempered by occasional patches of warmer amber luminescence, where industrial fixtures have been bolted in—glass globes powered by whirring dynamos somewhere deep in the walls.

In places, the plant substrate has developed its own light solutions: curtains of tiny, lens-like structures that concentrate the bioluminescence into thin shafts, washing streets and platforms in dappled patterns. Some galleries are lined with elongated leaves or leaf-analogues, each fringed with rows of glowing nodules, like chandeliers stretched into flat planes.

The air smells dry and resinous, with undertones of cold dust and something like crushed cedar. You don’t hear wind; there’s no weather as you know it. Instead there is a constant, almost subliminal breath—slow tides of air drawn through ventilation hollows and vascular chimneys, cycling heat and gases across the region. Occasionally, when one of these breaths catches a resonance in the plant’s structure, you hear a deep, drawn-out note: a low flute tone or organ pipe hum that lingers in the distance.

Sound here is generally muted. Footsteps on the plant floor are absorbed rather than echoed. Machinery is damped, wrapped in growth or gaskets. Voices, if present, don’t carry far. The loudest regular sounds are the intermittent groan and clank of rail junctions, the quiet hiss of pressure equalizing when a plant-valve is opened, and the occasional stuttering burst of a piston pump forcing something—nutrient, gas, data-fluid—through an unwilling conduit.

Every so often, a section of wall creaks—a slow, woody complaint as some internal growth pushes outward, restrained by bands of metal or woven fiber. The city is under constant negotiation with its substrate, restraining, channelling, and occasionally conceding ground.

### A world still forming

Erosion here is a strange word; nothing is wearing down by rain or river. Instead, things are being shaped by slow chemical flows and opportunistic growth.

In some districts, the living walls are smooth and finished, bearing the scars of frequent pruning and regrowth until they’ve achieved something like permanence. In others, the architecture is barely stabilized: walls bulge, corridors narrow as the plant thickens, unused doorways half-sealed by new growth. Soft, pale excrescences appear overnight and are trimmed back, with saws and heat-knives, into regular lines.

Near the periphery of the city proper, the landscape becomes more embryonic. Here, the plant-substrate has only partially hollowed itself. Huge knotted masses of root-trunk press together, leaving only incidental voids between them—tight crevices lit by single points of light, irregular chambers still in the process of being tamed.

In one such frontier district, a team of machines eats at a wall: not mining rock, but gnawing and cauterizing living tissue. A band of metallic, insect-like cutters scuttle across the surface, carving grooves that ooze a dark, syrupy fluid. Absorption pads press in immediately behind them, drinking it up before it can drip. Behind the cutters, manipulators and scaffolds reshape the raw, wounded plant into new columns and beams, sealing off unwanted vascular pathways, training others into clean, ringed conduits.

There’s a sense that all of this is very new. The tunnels, the galleries, even the great trunks themselves feel like a first draft—an underworld in the early stages of being decided.

### The ecology of a dry, living labyrinth

Though no liquid water pools here, life is thickly invested in the substrate and the spaces within it.

The plant itself is the dominant fact: a continent of tissue, kilometers thick, interfacing with the mineral world only at certain root-like anchors. It manages moisture internally, hoarding it deep in its own cells as bound solvent or locked in gelatinous reservoirs. To external eyes, it is “dry”—bark and fiber and lignin all the way down—but to those who can tap into its innards, it is a reservoir of everything a plant-world needs.

Smaller plant forms parasitize or collaborate with this mass. Here and there, colonial growths of leaf-discs stud walls like a rash of shields, each disc thin and dry-surfaced but hosting on its underside a tiny forest of capillary hairs. These harvest trace vapors from the air—a process audible as a faint crackling when many are active at once.

Matte-black vine-structures creep with astonishing slowness along ceilings and under balconies, their surfaces covered in dense arrays of micro-spikes that interface with the host’s vascular conduits. They appear to be thieves, but their “victims” show no alarm; taps and valves are sometimes deliberately grafted onto their nodes, suggesting a managed symbiosis.

Fauna exist at multiple scales and strangeness levels:

- Small, fast things: thumbnail-sized arthropods with translucent shells and flickering internal lights, which traverse the plant walls along predefined “veins”—colored tracer lines where chemical attractants ooze. They busily clean surfaces of micro-debris, tending to fungal gardens invisible to the casual eye.

- Hanging grazers: objects like inverted, slowly pulsing umbrellas cling to the undersides of higher arches. Their many soft tendrils brush across the wall surface, filtering out exudates and spores. Occasionally, one will contract and detach, dropping silently to a lower terrace, where it laboriously hauls itself to a new perch.

- Mid-sized runners: quadrupedal, fur-less creatures—somewhere between mammal and insect in silhouette—move quietly along the main concourses. Their skin is patterned to echo the striations of the walls, and they wear harnesses or integrated harness-analogs of grown material. Some carry tools or containers; others are clearly wild, their harness-like growths asymmetrical and overgrown, colonized by small fungi. Though there are predators, visible violence is rare in the heart of the city; predation likely happens in the deep, unlit hollows where the plant-city’s influence is weaker.

- Ghosts in the ceilings: occasionally, high above, something long and ribbonlike slips between beams and disappears—a creature that prefers the upper vascular channels, perhaps gliding from node to node in slow, controlled falls, barely disturbing the air.

Nor is all fauna entirely organic. There are hybrids: machines founded on living cores or animals with integrated prosthetics. A common sight on the industrial edges is a cluster of spiderlike maintenance bots whose central “bodies” are, unmistakably, lumps of living plant tissue. Veins from these cores extend into their metal legs, powering them through some obscure electrochemical engine. They move with a slow deliberateness, pruning surfaces, applying sealants, or reattaching monitoring devices dislodged by growth.

Predator-prey dynamics, here, are rarely explosive; everything is slowed by low free water and the plant’s control. Yet if you were to stand still for an hour at a vantage point and watch, you’d see patterns—smaller forms avoiding shadowed vents at certain intervals, mid-sized grazers giving wide berth to particular, seemingly quiet junctions, where perhaps the walls themselves still occasionally trap unwary flesh.

### Technology, constrained and adapted

Industrial here does not mean smoky smokestacks and roaring foundries. The tech level is high enough for complex machinery and networked infrastructure, but it’s hemmed in by the constraints of a living environment that does not forgive heat and shock.

The factories are more like dense, humming orchards of instruments. In one district, a series of tall plant-chimneys have been hollowed and stuffed with columnar machines: rotors, piston stacks, chemical reactors, all fit like vertebrae into the hollow trunks. There’s a constant, low tremor as these inner devices work, harvesting sugars and complex organics from sap streams and converting them into plastics, alloys, and strange composites that integrate seamlessly with the host substrate.

Instead of conveyor belts, many processes use pulse-flows: sealed segments of vascular channel in which capsules of material are pushed along by controlled pressure changes, like blood cells forced through arteries. Glowing gauges and mechanical shutters line these conduits, regulating flow. When a batch needs to be diverted, a segment of wall literally rotates within its own tissues, re-routing the channel, as though the city were having a controlled, localized heart arrhythmia.

Power is drawn from multiple sources: thermal gradients between deeper, warmer strata and cooler upper levels; piezoelectric harvesters that exploit the subtle, constant swell and contraction of plant tissues; perhaps even chemical energy tapped from ongoing growth and decay cycles. In machinery rooms, you see arrays of crystal-studded plates pressing gently against flexing plant surfaces, feeding thin copper veins that run off into the darkness.

The technology doesn’t dominate the plant—nor does it seem fully submissive. It’s a negotiation. Everywhere you can see places where the plant has overgrown a conduit, forcing engineers to reroute. Elsewhere, harsh, metallic brackets choke a growth node, preventing an inconvenient bulge from destroying a corridor.

Urban planning acknowledges that nothing here will stay fixed for long.

### The rules of this underworld

Some unspoken principles seem to shape what can and cannot happen here:

1. **Nothing is truly static.** Any surface not actively maintained begins to change—very slowly, but inexorably. Walls develop new buds, floors thicken or crack, ventilation openings narrow as new tissues grow in. The city’s inhabitants (and their machines) must constantly patrol, prune, and direct.

2. **Water is precious and internal.** The dry surfaces conceal an intensely managed hydrology. Access to the plant’s own internal moisture is tightly controlled. Cutting too deep, or tapping a high-pressure reservoir inexpertly, can flood a chamber with sticky, nutrient-rich fluid that, without quick cleanup, would attract a frenzy of scavenger microfauna and rampant fungal bloom.

3. **Light is locally manufactured.** There is no day-night cycle imposed from above. Instead, districts manage their own “times” by dimming and brightening bioluminescent panels. In industrial zones, the light stays on continuously; in habitation areas, the panels pulse through gentle cycles, never fully dark, always enough to see by, but with a rhythm that living things have attuned to.

4. **Sound must be damped.** Vibration risks propagating through the plant’s tissues, triggering defensive growth or stress responses. The quiet is not a cultural choice alone; it’s an ecological necessity. Loud impacts are rare, and where they must occur, they’re cushioned and isolated by non-living inserts.

5. **Boundaries are negotiated, not built.** Traditional walls—stone, steel—are rare. Most divisions are managed by gradients: changes in chemical signals in the plant’s tissues that instruct it not to carry certain compounds beyond a given point, changes in growth density, shifts in light color or pattern. Where hard walls exist, they’re as much an admission of failure as of strength: places where negotiation with the substrate broke down, and the only solution was to implant a rigid barrier.

6. **The outside is theoretical.** Though tunnels do, somewhere, lead toward the deeper crust and possibly the surface, the city’s daily reality is inward and downward. Above the plant-city lies rock and pressure; below it, deeper root masses and mineral zones. The true “horizon” is not a line against sky but the vanishing point where tunnels converge into darkness.

### Quiet motion

Despite the overall hush, life here is actively rearranging itself.

A procession passes along a main concourse: a series of pallet-platforms suspended beneath arching, jointed plant limbs that grow out of the walls themselves. The limbs flex in sequence, handing the load from one to the next like a bucket brigade. The pallets carry bound bundles of fibrous rods—pruned plant growth, perhaps destined for conversion into composite materials.

Off to the side, in an open plaza beneath a huge, vaulted ceiling, a stand of specialized “trees” rises from the fibrous ground. Their trunks are smooth and pale, branches short and angular, arranged in deliberate geometries. Suspended from each branch are coils of metallic mesh. Very slowly, a dusting of fine mineral powder is being drawn from the air and the plant’s own exhalations, captured in these meshes. Once thick enough, the coils will be harvested, the captured minerals smelted in low-temperature furnaces that glow cherry-red behind multiple layers of insulation.

Far down a side tunnel, you glimpse a darker, less-managed zone: walls rough, growth less disciplined. Here, a pack of soft-bodied hunters flow along the floor, shivering with peristaltic motion, guided by faint bioluminescent flickers. They pause at a damaged ventilation pore, harvest the swollen fungal mass clogging it, and retreat, leaving the passage just that little bit cleaner—and themselves fed.

Above, unseen, the plant’s own massive root-stems flex minutely as they adjust to new stress patterns, to the shifting weight of grown structures, to the constant, subtle rearrangement orchestrated by industrial intent.

---

### The strangeness at the core

What makes this place truly strange is not any single feature, but their combination: a desert made of living wood, cool and almost airless; a city that must garden its own foundations; technology that is less installed than grafted; an ecosystem whose dominant presence is not individual animals or even people but the substrate itself, patient and pervasive.

Everywhere you walk, you are technically inside a plant.

Its skin is your floor, its bones your pillars, its vascular system your infrastructure. It has accepted the city not as a parasite but as a partner—or perhaps merely as a localized infection it has not yet decided to fight.

And the feeling you get, moving through these cool, quiet halls, is that this partnership is still being worked out, moment by moment: each trimmed bud, each new rail line, each redirected sap-channel an argument in an ongoing negotiation between growth and design.

The landscape is under construction not just in the sense of human (or thoughful) effort, but in the sense that the world itself—this underworld of plant and stone and controlled dryness—is only now discovering what it wants to be.
