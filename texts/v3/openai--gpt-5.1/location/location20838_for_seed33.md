At first it feels like you are standing inside a memory that the universe is trying to forget.

The world opens in all directions, but nothing here is close. Space itself seems thinned, stretched out. Horizons are steep rather than distant: everywhere you look, the ground does not roll—it falls. It drops away in black, plunging chasms and slow, curving voids, as if some colossal hand had scooped out the planet with calm, geometrical care.

There is almost no water. Nothing glistens. No ice films, no hidden seepage. The surfaces are bone-dry in a way that is more than simple absence of moisture; it's as if “wet” has never been a possibility here, as if the local laws never allowed it. The rocks have a matte, swallowed look, as though any shine would violate a rule.

You stand near the lip of an immense shaft whose full bottom is invisible. Dim light, the only light, breathes up from below—a sourceless, grey-violet luminescence that does not quite cast shadows; it only makes darkness stratified. The sky above is not exactly dark, either. It is a high, static vault of muted charcoal, patterned with vague, drifting polygons of slightly lighter tone. They move, but so slowly your eye only detects their progression by returning to them minutes later.

The air is cool and bone-light. Not cold enough to bite, just enough to keep everything from relaxing. When you inhale, the taste is metallic and flat, like air that has been filtered through centuries of stone. It is very thin. Your footsteps make no echo, not because there are no surfaces to catch the sound but because sound itself seems reluctant to propagate.

Below you, stretching along the cliff face and across suspended arcs of rock, is the city.

“City” is almost too human a word. The constructions spiral and cluster along vertical planes and negative spaces more than along the ground. They are made from the same mineral as the cliffs—dull, porous stone veined with glassy, dark ribbons—but carved into an impossible architecture: tapering pylons that lean into voids and melt into terraces; arcs that begin as buttresses and end as hollow tubes; stacks of hexagonal hollows like cross-sections of a honeycomb dragged out and then folded back into themselves.

There are no seams, no joints, no tools’ marks. The built forms look precipitated rather than assembled, as if they grew out of the stone by crystallization of intention rather than effort. Corners hesitate before coming to angles, edges curve a little too long before becoming straight. Distances in the city are treacherous: a span that looks a few meters could take a slow hour to cross, and a long bridge, glimpsed from the side, might be walked in just a dozen steps. You do not feel this as a trick; it is simply how space behaves here.

Urban density is high, but volume is nearly empty. The city clings in layered bands around the chasm’s walls, its structures built for thousands, tens of thousands, but the number of actual presences is vanishingly small. Tower-blocks of chambers open onto plazas that hang over nothing. Galleries coil around the void like petrified waves. And in so many apertures—doorways, windows, vents—there is only darkness and a faint, cool pressure, as if rooms are filled not with air but with the memory of air.

There is no vegetation, not in any recognizable sense. Yet something has taken the role that plants might have had.

On some ledges and ceilings you see growths: low, matte swellings, spreading like tide-lines. They are the color of old ash, or the underside of a bruise. They have no stems, no leaves, not even fungal caps—just thin, irregular sheets of living texture. Where they thin at the edges, faint patterns glimmer inside them, like embedded circuits or the ghosted venation of transparent leaves. Occasionally they stir, not in response to wind (there is almost none), but to nothing you can detect: a slow ripple, a shiver that passes across a whole patch like the surface of a sleeping creature taking a breath. When they contract, the stone beneath them is subtly etched, as if their mode of feeding involves quietly rewriting the mineral itself.

Some of these growths extend down the sides of buildings in vertical tongues, forming frayed curtains that hang over openings. Passing close to them, you feel a strange cool on your skin that doesn’t align with any airflow. The sensation is less like temperature and more like a brief, local weakening of the rule that your body has boundaries. If you linger, your fingertips begin to forget their exact position in space.

The city is old. Everything here is in a settled state—not decaying into ruin, but resting in a form long ago reached and now held. Edges are softened by time, but they haven’t crumbled. The widest causeways have shallow depressions in impossible patterns: countless narrow grooves and overlapping curves, not like human footpaths but like the worn-in tracks of something with too many limbs, or of many different things overlaying each other until their history becomes an abstract script. Even the stone rails that line the wider drops are polished in places, but not at a human hand height; the smoothed zones are scattered, too high or too low, occasionally drifting sideways for no reason your body can map.

There is weather, but it isn't like cloud and rain.

At irregular intervals, the light within the chasm shifts, not brightening so much as rebalancing. Entire faces of the rock go flatter or deeper in tone, as though someone were slowly turning a single giant polarizing filter. During these phases, very thin strata of the air condense into visible layers, subtle and shimmering, stacked like invisible glass shelves. Dust motes—there is always a soft suspension of motes here—collect briefly on those layers, forming faint contour lines that make the three-dimensional geometry of the space temporarily legible. Then they slough away, the layers vanish, and the air reassumes its usual, almost featureless character.

Sometimes the void breathes. You feel it through your feet first: a wide, extremely long-period oscillation in pressure that travels through the rock. After a while, the air answers with a rush that is felt more than heard: a deep, subsonic inhalation down in the shaft, followed long minutes later by an exhalation. When the chasm exhales, light dims slightly and the ash-growths flatten themselves to the stone, their ripples freezing until the cycle passes.

Sound barely exists. Far below, at long intervals, something like the crack of shifting stone emerges, stretched thin and slowed to a mournful, almost melodic creak. It travels up the shaft as a ghost of a sound, dissipating well before it becomes loud. Within the city, the only regular noise is a dry, feather-light ticking—sometimes from the ash-growths, sometimes from the stone itself, as hairline fractures extend or contract microscopically in response to whatever unseen stress shapes this place.

Life—animal life—is rare, and at first you might think it absent. Then a motion draws your eye: a cluster of faintly luminous shapes drifting along the vertical plane of a distant building. They are suspended at odd depths against the stone, not attached to it, like translucent cuts in the air. As you watch, the cluster subdivides; each segment separates from the whole with the delicacy of a bubble budding from another bubble. They drift apart, their edges soft, then phase together again in a new arrangement, forming an outline that suggests a limb, then a spinal curve, then something unnamable. They make no sound. The only indication they have noticed anything beyond themselves is the way the local geometry shivers when they draw near a ledge—edges sharpen slightly, distances tighten, as if their presence reasserts a more Euclidean order in a small radius.

Occasionally, across a yawning gap, you see a more concrete creature: something with jointed lengths like folded glass piping, hinged at oblique angles, moving very slowly along a wall. It climbs with no visible method of grip, simply reorienting itself segment by segment, rearranging its own structure relative to the surface. Where it passes, the stone’s texture clarifies briefly into crisp, crystalline facets; minutes afterward, the facets relax back into their usual, half-melted grain.

There are rules to this world, but they are perilously close to slipping your mind whenever you try to state them. Gravity functions, but not always in a single direction. In some parts of the city, long interior shafts pass through buildings, lined with doorways on all sides. Looking down them, you see floors and thresholds arranged as if any wall could be the “ground” for whoever uses it. Only faint traces—smooth patches, worn bands, the orientation of the ash-growths—hint at the chosen directions of whichever beings once claimed those surfaces.

Distances are inconsistent in a specific, repeatable way. Between paired structures—a tower and a facing wall, say—you might find that crossing directly on a narrow bridge takes far less time than the apparent span, while walking the perimeter of the gap along the ledge takes longer than the visible arc should allow. If you try to triangulate, your sense of measurement simply slips. It does not confuse you with vertigo; it simply refuses to resolve. You can traverse, but you cannot map, not in the way you are used to.

Material feels half-real. Stone is solid under your hands, but in some zones, when you look too closely at a surface, the granular details do not magnify; they multiply. Under scrutiny, a single pockmark becomes a cluster of pockmarks, each with their own micro-shadows, each ready to resolve further. If you force focus, the surface answers with a quiet kind of resistance: your eyes slide off, your attention blurs. This passive defense is strongest on certain threshold-stones that ring major voids or frame key arches. It feels like a world protecting its own continuity from being too precisely interrogated.

Technologically, there is nothing recognizable: no machines, no tools, no stray scrap of manufactured metal or plastic. Yet functionality is everywhere. Channels cut through rock that carry nothing obviously flowing, but hum faintly when the chasm breathes. These channels intersect in nodes that ring like dry bells if tapped—though the sound never gets far. Some plazas feature low, circular depressions ringed with standing monoliths. The basins are flat, undecorated, but any dust that settles within them tends to spiral toward the center over hours or days, forming precisely wound patterns like coiled galaxies. Step into such a basin and, briefly, your weight feels less committed. A single stride might land you farther than expected—or not far enough at all.

The absence of water does not mean absence of cycles. The ash-growths extend and contract, the drifting luminous lives bud and regroup, the immense shaft breathes, and above, in the dim sky, those vast polygonal plates continue their slow, silent rearrangement. Sometimes, very rarely, two plates overlap in such a way that the light changes significantly: not brighter, exactly, but more directional. Shadows, diffuse until then, sharpen along certain lines. In those moments, you see tiny details you never noticed: minuscule motes suspended at fixed points in the air like an invisible scaffold; hairline seams tracing across the surfaces of distant buildings, suggesting they are parts of a larger, articulated whole; expressions of stress propagating through ash-growths as ripples of subtle color.

There are hints that something once used this city—or still does, but in a mode you can’t quite share. In some high chambers open to the chasm, the walls are inscribed, not with symbols, but with very fine reliefs that depict no scenes, no figures, nothing narrative. Instead they record states: configurations of the space itself, etched like contour maps of impossible topographies. Some show the chasm as wider, narrower, braided into multiple lesser shafts. Some represent inset cavities that do not exist now, or terraces that have never been carved. Perhaps they are not records of the past or plans for the future, but annotations of alternative states the world can slip into.

Every so often, without obvious cause, a small local change ripples through the fabric of the place. A stair that curled gently down around a column now continues upward instead; the join is clean, ancient, as if it had always been that way. An arch that framed empty darkness now opens onto a shallow, stone-lined court, with steps you are sure were not there before—but those steps are as eroded as everything else, worn with passage by something that may never have existed in your timeline.

These edits are quiet, unannounced, and in them you can feel the high weirdness of this world: this is not merely a setting where reality is fragile, but one where reality is *dynamic infrastructure*. The city is an organism whose medium is geometry. It breathes by altering its own consistency.

And pervading it all, the near-silence. No wind hiss, no distant roar of water. Only the soft ticking of flexing stone, the rare, stretched groan from the depths, the almost inaudible rustle of ash-growth smoothing itself across the mineral skin of the city. The quiet is not emptiness, though. It is the focused attention of a system in motion, running on laws that intersect your own just enough to allow you to stand here and witness.

This landscape is a cool, eroded, settled chasm-city on the edge of what you understand physics to be. It extends beyond your sight along the vertical, its terraces and arcs layering out of view, its other shafts cross-connecting somewhere in the depths. Rare lives move within it: translucent spatial edits in motion, articulated mineral creatures that rearrange the solidity of the stone. There are no tools because the fundamental tools here are not objects but permissions: the allowed ways in which space, matter, and pattern may shift.

Nothing is growing in the way you know growth, and yet everything is in process. The world resettles itself continuously but calmly, as if in slow negotiation with its own rules. The city is not abandoned, not alive in any mundane sense; it is *ongoing*. You are the anomaly here, a dense, water-filled body walking through an environment that has never depended on water, sound, or conventional life to sustain its strange, ordered, quietly animated existence.
