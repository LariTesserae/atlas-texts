The air is thin and clean, and everything is wrong.

You stand high—high enough for the horizon to curve faintly, high enough that light feels a little harsher, stripped of warmth. But there’s no raw rock underfoot, no soil, no ice. The ground is made: plates and lattices and tessellated surfaces, a continent of construction that pretends, badly, to be geology.

It’s cool up here. You see your breath sometimes, but only when the wind drops and the condensation systems overcompensate. A faint mist pearls along edges and handrails and the undersides of projecting beams. The moisture doesn’t come from clouds; the sky above is an almost painful, bright blue-white, washed clean by altitude and by some invisible maintenance of the air. The rain, when it comes, is scheduled and directional—fine lines of water dropping from unseen nozzles high above, sweeping across the built landscape in slow, combed curtains.

This is a plateau the size of a region, but the plateau itself is an artifact: a stacked, engineered mesa, rising from unknown depths. You might imagine some natural bedrock hidden beneath, but nothing you can see is unshaped. The “cliffs” along the distance are angular walls of metal-ceramic composite, slotted with maintenance galleries and half-sealed expansion seams that mimic strata. Sharp buttresses project out in precise increments, catching the angle of the unfiltered light like fresh talus—but each “shard” is machined, neatly bolted, numbered.

The terrain is young in its forms. You feel it in the edges: no soft rounding, no long history of wearing down. Surfaces are crisp, corners intact. Where there is “erosion,” it is engineered or has only just begun—sealants peeling, surface treatments crazing into hairline cracks, rust blooming where it was never meant to. Scratches and pits in the plating suggest impacts from something—falling debris, migrating machinery, or fauna you’d rather not meet up close.

Water appears in pockets and belts rather than as a continuous presence. Where two broad planes meet, someone has carved—no, fabricated—a “valley”: a kilometer-wide trough lined with modular panels, sloped to gather runoff. Along its center runs a quiet artificial river, dark and slow, its banks edged by tethered stones that are hollow and too regular. The water moves with a steadiness that feels more like pumping than gravity, drawing a subtle fur along the surface where micro-nozzles aerate it. It doesn’t babble; it circulates. Occasionally a vent in the riverbed exhales a cluster of bubbles, and the smell of treated metal and faint ozone rises with them.

Despite all this, the landscape is open. You can see for tens, sometimes hundreds, of kilometers—rows and belts and tiers of infrastructure stepping down, receding into high, bright air. Giant latticework pylons climb from one artificial terrace to the next, carrying thick bundles of conduits. On some of them, a skin of pale growth clings—lichen-like, but made of translucent polymer filaments that shimmer in the light and flex minutely with passing currents of air. They’re technically vegetation: deliberately sparse biofilms and hardy semi-living constructs seeded into the environment to scavenge trace nutrients and filter whatever stray particulates drift through. There are not many; each cluster stands out, a patch of soft irregularity against the regulated geometries.

Real vegetation, or something closer to it, hides in the seams. In the protected angles where two walls meet under an overhang, condensation collects, forming narrow moist streaks that darken the panels. Here you’ll find wiry mats of plant analogues rooted in micro-perforations in the surface, drawing both chemical feedstock and water from within the structure itself. They’re low, tough, pigmented in deep violets and grays instead of green—adapted to intense light, thin air, and random schedules of rainfall. Sometimes they are interrupted by stubby stalks ending in hard, sensor-like capsules that turn slowly, tracking not the sun, but something else: fluctuations in the invisible fields that seem to hum through this place.

Everything is built, but not all of it is entirely understandable.

If you listen, the first thing that strikes you is how quiet it is. Not silent—never silent—but the scale of this construction suggests thunderous industry, and what you get are distant, softened sounds: a low thrum like blood in your ears that might be machinery resonating through the superstructure; the occasional far-off clank, long delayed; a slow whoosh that might be a controlled venting of gas or a deep, regulated breath.

Closer at hand, life makes most of the noise. Abundant fauna have adapted to this made environment, or were made alongside it. A cluster of filament-stalks quivers and you realize they’re not plants at all but a roost: tiny hexapodal things clamber through the mesh, their bodies like coiled springs wrapped in soft fur that glints metallic at certain angles. When they move too quickly they blur, not because of speed but because their outlines smear, as if the rules that hold them together are less certain than they should be. One pauses, looks at you—if “looks” is the right word for the way its bifurcated head seems to face in three directions at once—and emits a small, glassy chirp. The sound arrives cleanly, but there is an echo in a place it shouldn’t be, as if the world remembered the note before it was made and is replaying the memory.

Things like birds glide overhead, but their wings don’t beat. Instead they articulate and re-articulate, telescoping, folding into geometries that shouldn’t provide lift. Their shadows trace across the ground in subtly wrong ways, sometimes lagging behind their bodies, sometimes foreshortened at impossible angles. Every so often, a shadow crosses your path with nothing overhead to cast it.

The industrial character of the place is everywhere and nowhere. You see factories—if that’s still the right word for them—embedded like tumors into the walls of the great terraces. Chimneys rise, but what they emit are faint glows, not smoke: a pearly luminescence that drifts up a few meters, then dissolves into static sparks that fizz and crawl along invisible boundaries in the air. From certain angles, the air is full of filigree: faint lines like the ghosts of wires or scaffolding, hanging without support, flickering in and out as if caught between possibilities.

On one plateau below, an immense assembly yard stretches out—grids of platforms, rails, and gantries. The platforms carry half-finished machines: segmented trains with no apparent tracks to run on, exoskeletal shells big enough to house buildings, enormous ring-structures that gently rotate despite any obvious power source. Workers are scarce; this is an industrial landscape largely without visible operators. Occasionally something like an engine coughs to life, a dull, arrhythmic growl. It runs for a few seconds, then fades, leaving behind the taste of iron and cold in your mouth.

Light here is bright in a way that feels deliberate. The sun—or the thing playing its role—is too consistent. Shadows are sharp and high-contrast, but they don’t always obey the time of day. You can watch the shadow of a railing and see it shorten while nearby, the shadow of a vent-pipe lengthens, both from the same source. There are no clouds in the proper sense, but you sometimes see translucent planes move across the sky, like sheets of polarized glass, and as they pass, the whole tone of the landscape shifts—cool white becoming hard blue, then a slightly green-tinted silver. Surfaces that had been matte suddenly exhibit a fine internal glow, revealing patterns and inscriptions that weren’t visible before.

Those inscriptions are another kind of ecology. The built substrate crawls with tiny, slow-changing markings—etchings, stamps, barcodes, sigils—records of manufacture, maintenance, and events. In certain zones, they thicken into a tight calligraphy that climbs up walls, flows around joints, and vanishes into cracks. Some parts of this writing are obviously mechanical—inventory codes, safety notices—but other parts flare into something else: repeating diagrams of shapes that don’t quite sit comfortably in three dimensions, or sequences of symbols that rearrange themselves when you’re not looking directly at them. It gives the unsettling impression that the infrastructure is not only maintained but narrated, and that the narrative is still being revised.

You notice the damp more the longer you stay. Condensation beads along certain seams in ways that defy thermodynamics: droplets forming on surfaces that are, by touch, colder than the surrounding air. In shadowed recesses, thin veils of water hang in place as though pinned to the air by invisible hooks. Watch long enough and you see them drift, slowly, like aimless jellyfish. When they brush against some of the sparse plant-analogues, the droplets are absorbed, and the plants briefly emit a faint radiance, then settle back into drabness.

In some of the deeper troughs and cuts through the artificial plateau there are “fog falls”: columns of mist descending from no visible source above, interacting with the superstructure in stubbornly unphysical ways—flowing upward along underside surfaces, pooling in midair as if encountering a floor that only exists to vapor. Fauna gather at the bases of these columns: long-bodied, jointless creatures that flow more than walk, trailing filaments behind them like unraveling thread. They dip their filaments into the fog, which beads along the threads in defiance of gravity, crawling up toward their mouths. Where the fog touches them, their forms flicker slightly, as if they’re being re-rendered.

The place is densely populated in an odd sense. There are many presences per volume, but not all of them are firmly “there.” You feel watched not just by animals but by gradients in the environment—by localized intensities of static or warmth that shift when you move, by faint hums that rise in pitch as you approach and fall away again as you retreat. At the intersections of major structural beams, the air is thicker, like nodal points where the world has been nailed together more forcefully. Standing in one, you can hear multiplicities: the same background hum layered faintly atop itself, a split-second out of sync, as if the place is existing through several slightly divergent frames and has not fully decided which one to keep.

Yet for all of this, change is slow. The factories flicker but rarely roar to full life. The wildlife moves in patient circuits. The artificial river’s level fluctuates across hours in a narrow band. You see evidence of cycles—a tarnish line on a wall where condensate has repeatedly risen and fallen, a band of dying biofilm marking the old extent of some now-withdrawn chemical plume—but these changes are gradual. Things accrete here more than they erupt.

You pass a long, gently curving corridor open on one side to the stark panorama beyond. The inner wall is layered with pipes and conduits, three and four deep, each humming faintly, some warm, some cold. Between them, colonies of small, translucent things cling, their bodies like knots of glassy tubes. They extend feathery appendages into the air, filtering something—maybe micro-particles, maybe stray probabilities. When the bright pseudo-sun shifts tone, you can see they’re full of tiny, moving lights, swarming like fireflies trapped in crystal. Every now and then, one of the colonies releases a part of itself: a little globule that detaches, hovers, then drifts out into the open corridor, where it bursts into a cloud of nearly invisible motes that tickle your skin as they settle.

Down on a lower level, a “town” sprawls across several terraces, except its scale and emptiness make the term feel off. Blocks of low-built hab modules sit shoulder to shoulder, connected by ducts and overhead walkways. Everything is wired, piped, channeled. You’d expect the noise of habitation—voices, vehicles, machinery—but the sound here is a subdued murmur at best. Doors cycle open and closed on automatic schedules even when no one passes through. Lights flicker on in series in the interiors, illuminating rooms that hold only organized stacks of modular shapes, humming quietly to themselves. In a courtyard, a single robot—if that’s all it is—moves on a loop, polishing the same length of handrail, again and again, only its reflection in the metal beginning to look noticeably older than the device itself.

Along the edges of this “town” are railways with no trains, just slender lines of metal embedded into the surface. Follow one with your eyes and you see it meet others, merge, split, climb the distant structural walls in improbable arcs, sometimes ending abruptly in midair. At these breaks, the rails fray into nothingness, as though remembering a continuation they never received.

There are hints of rules, but they are not the right rules. Drop a small bolt over the side of a parapet and watch its fall: for most of the distance it accelerates as expected, then in the last ten meters, its descent warps, slowing in a twitching, hesitant way, as if encountering layers of slightly displaced gravity. It lands with a muted sound not quite consistent with metal on metal, as though it came to rest partway through the floor. Later, you might find that same bolt perched somewhere else entirely, with tiny, precise bite marks on it, as if something had eaten the bits of it that didn’t belong here.

The sky remains clear, the brightness almost clinical, but near the extreme distances, the world seems to curve and kink. Lines that should be straight have a faint serpentine bend to them when you glance from the corner of your eye. Two towers that appear parallel from one vantage become skewed from another. At very specific spots on the plateau—no markers, you just stumble into them—the entire environment sharpens into a frightening clarity, every bolt head and hairline crack and drifting mote in exquisite focus. Step half a foot to the side, and it all relaxes again into the earlier, hazy normal. It’s as though the reality here has calibration points.

Over very long spans of time, this constructed highland is changing, but the change is glacial, tectonic, almost ceremonial. Some segments of the superstructure are slowly twisting relative to others, not through mechanical hinges but through a gradual redefinition of what direction “down” means in those zones. You see the evidence in stress patterns: fine spiderwebs of new bracing grown like metallic fungus where different parts of the place are being pulled in different conceptual directions.

And everywhere, in the quiet, the fauna go about their layered lives. In a narrow skybridge, you see transparent insectoids crawling across the glass, except the glass is opaque, and their little bodies cast shadows on surfaces they never touch. In a drainage channel, semi-fluid organisms roll slowly upstream against the thin trickle of water, leaving behind them trails that temporarily solidify, then evaporate into faint, chiming sounds.

The atmosphere is one of patient, saturated strangeness: a bright, cool, high-built world, densely occupied not by crowds or traffic, but by systems—biological, mechanical, conceptual—all interlaced. Nothing natural in the old sense remains. Yet the place has grown its own versions of river and moss, city and mountain, animal and myth, all laid over a substrate of metal and logic that is beginning, quietly, to misremember what it was designed to be.
