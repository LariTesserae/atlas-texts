The air is warm and faintly sweet, and it is full of bodies.

You stand on a world where almost nothing is empty and almost nothing is wet.

From above, this place would look like a mottled, softly shining continent of flesh and fiber, sprawling from horizon to horizon: low, continuous terrain made not of stone and water but of living tissue and its cast-offs, dense as a city, quiet as a library.

### Ground and “soil”

Under your feet there is no recognizable soil. What passes for ground is a tightly interlocked mat of dry, fibrous material: shed husks, fallen fronds, desiccated membranes folded and pressed by the passage of countless limbs. It has the color of unbaked clay and old parchment—pale golds, grays, muted reds, a vast archive of former lives.

Kick at it and it does not puff up with dust the way sand would. It fractures in layers, revealing a cross-section like the pages of an ancient book: banded, flaky, full of thin roots and hollow channels. Every fragment is almost weightless, crumbly, and dry. When it breaks, it releases a faint papery crackle and the faintest exhalation of an organic smell—spice, dry moss, something between toasted seed and sun-warmed chitin.

Beneath this, when erosion has had time to cut a bit deeper, is the raw substrate of the planet: a mineral surface that has only just begun to know wear. The rock is smooth and relatively featureless, with shallow, wide pans and low rises. Edges are sharp, not yet rounded by time. In places, new splits run in long, geometric seams, as if the crust is still settling from some recent upheaval. It is a geology at the beginning of its story: no canyons, no deep river scars, merely subtle buckles and young fault lines just starting to sketch a topography.

There is almost no loose dust and no flowing liquid. If anything trickles, it is not water.

### The missing water, and what took its place

There are no streams, no lakes, no glints of blue. Clouds do not tower; they smear thinly like distant lint high in the atmosphere, more spectral than present. Rain, in any regular sense, does not fall here.

Yet the world is lush.

Moisture, what little there is, exists mostly inside organisms: bound in thick saps and gels, locked away from the dry air. Now and then you might find a shallow, shimmering film pooled in a depression—no deeper than a finger’s breadth, slightly viscous, with a glassy, iridescent surface. It is not water but a clear, oily solvent exuded by clustered lifeforms, used to dissolve minerals from the rock or to transport nutrients from organism to organism. Exposed, it evaporates not upward but sideways, drawn in filament-thin streams along specialized surfaces and back into bodies, leaving behind only a faint, dark sheen on the stone.

Nothing here relies on open water. There are no leaves built to drip with rain, no broad, thin surfaces at risk of drying out. Life has grown thick skins and inward rivers.

### Vegetation: low, dense, and everywhere

What you would call “plants” form a near-continuous carpet, more like a coral reef sprawled across a flat world than a forest or meadow.

Most stand no more than chest-high. Some barely clear your ankle. They grow in clumps and bulges and braided cords, packed so closely that bare mineral surface is rare and brief, opening mainly where something has recently died or been eaten.

Textures vary wildly:

- **Cushion-fields**: Great stretches of rounded, velvety mounds pressed against each other like giant moss pillows, springy underfoot. Their outer skins are dry and slightly rough, but if a creature pierces them (many do) there is a translucent core: a gel reservoir rich with the world’s internal solvent. Tiny pore openings dot their surfaces, ringed with darker tissue that can close like a pupil, sealing their moisture away during the driest air cycles.

- **Blade thickets**: Dense stands of tough, ribbonlike fronds, stiff but flexible, rising in tightly packed fans. They have no lush leaves—just elongated, triangular cross-sections that twist along their length to catch the sun. Their color is a muted ochre or pale green-brown, often dusted with a faintly shimmering powder: protective waxes that reduce loss to the air. When many stand together, they form semi-opaque walls that rustle only if something brushes through. In still air, they are almost soundless.

- **Columnar growths**: Short, thick, column-like forms emerging from a common base: each stalk jointed, ringed, and wrapped in layered bark-like sheets that flake at the edges. They resemble a city of low pylons or stunted trees—no branches, just vertical mass. Many support parasitic or symbiotic mats on their tops: flat, crustlike organisms that spread out to capture light, pale and almost mineral in appearance.

- **Creeping lattices**: Networks of woody filaments that weave just above the ground, crossing and re-crossing, forming meshes that trap fallen husks and carcasses. These tangles serve as both structure and digestive platform: at the nodes are bulbous, knotted organs that slowly break down whatever becomes ensnared, passing nutrients back into the common network.

Color, as a whole, is restrained. There is no bright jungle palette. The dominant tones are dusty greens, tan, rust, muted purple-browns, and occasional dull silver—shades adapted to absorb a broad spectrum of light without advertising themselves too brightly to hungry eyes.

Every plant-equivalent is armored against dryness and teeth. Skins are thick, layered, or heavily waxed. Many have hairlike projections that trap the scant humidity close to their surface, creating a microclimate just a few millimeters thick where the air is a fraction cooler and less desiccating.

Roots do not sink deep in search of groundwater; there is none to find in the conventional sense. Instead, root systems spread wide and shallow, interfacing directly with the mineral rock and the fibrous detritus above it, threading through the lattice of organic cast-offs. They tap into the smeared sheen left by evaporating solvent, strip salts and trace elements from infinitesimal films, and—in many cases—plug directly into other organisms with specialized grafting structures. Nutrient exchange is as much organism-to-organism as it is organism-to-stone.

### Urban, but not built by hands

The parameter of “urban” shows itself not in concrete and steel, but in geometry, density, and pattern.

This world’s bioforms—especially the sessile ones—grow according to gradients of light, trace chemicals, and the flows of solvent that creep invisibly along their surfaces. Over time, their tendencies aggregate into emergent architecture.

- In many regions, the **cushion-fields** arrange themselves into rings and corridors, leaving narrow, pathway-like trenches between them—routes used and maintained by creatures moving through. The corridors remain trammeled and free of new growth, becoming living streets.

- **Columnar growths** cluster into dense promenades, like rows of squat pillars, their tops gradually levelling into nearly continuous platforms. Over generations, newer organisms sprout preferentially on these raised surfaces, creating layered “floors” of life: what begins as simple stacking becomes a stacked city of organisms, each level shaded by the one above, each with its own specialist community of climbers and grazers.

- **Creeping lattices** and root-meshes, responding to the steady circulation of organisms, reinforce frequently used routes, growing thicker along them, creating archways and tunnels where trunks and vines press together. These living structures direct traffic more efficiently, which in turn reinforces their growth patterns—a feedback loop that yields a kind of planned, though no one planned it, infrastructure.

From a distance, especially at low angles of sun, entire regions look like sprawling districts: blocks and avenues, courtyards and cul-de-sacs, all composed of wooden, leathery, and chitinous forms. There are intersections where many trails converge; here, certain organisms specialize in intercepting waste and corpses, turning traffic density into steady nutrition.

This “city” is not ornamental, not symbolic; it is function condensed. But to an outside eye, the overall impression is unmistakably urban—a continuous fabric of structure, too regular to be random, too ubiquitous to be the product of individual intention.

There is no metalwork, no stonecutting, no tools. Technology, if it exists here, is wholly biological and mostly unconscious: adaptive chemistries, feedback loops of growth, and the collective patterning that arises when countless organisms pursue simple rules in close quarters.

### Fauna: abundant lives, soft-footed and thirsty

Everywhere something lives, and everywhere something moves.

Most animals here are small to medium-sized; few can afford the risk of a large, water-demanding body in a world so dry on the outside. Their physiques tend toward compact forms with minimized exposed surface area, layered in protective integuments: shells, scales, overlapping plates, thick hides, or densely packed hair-like protrusions.

Common traits echo across different clades:

- **Internal reservoirs**: Many creatures carry one or more internal sacs where they store solvent-rich fluids harvested from plants or other animals. These sacs are tightly sealed, surrounded by insulating tissues, and topped with muscular valves to prevent loss. Some species have visible bulges—translucent or dark—where these reservoirs swell when the animal is well-fed.

- **Moisture-conserving respiratory systems**: Breathing opens you to desiccation, so most species use slit-like vents that can close nearly airtight when at rest. Some animals draw air through complex, folded channels deep inside their bodies, where every exhalation’s moisture is reclaimed.

- **Solvent-centric diets**: Predators are most interested in fluid reserves; carrion-eaters and scavengers crowd around the moistest parts. Grazers and browsers seek out the thickest, richest tissues in the vegetation: the inner cores of cushions, the pulpy joints of columns, the bulbous nodules on creeping lattices.

Their bodies often blend into the muted palette of the environment. You see:

- Low, many-legged **trundlers**, their backs armored with segmented plates mottled like the ground’s layered husks. They move steadily along established corridors, jaws hidden beneath a row of sensory whiskers, shearing dry outer plant tissue and then boring into deeper, juicier layers. Their feet make the slightest tap-tap-tap on the hardened fiber, more felt than heard.

- Darting, lean **hunters**, perhaps three-limbed or four, running low and close to the ground. Their skins are tight, patterned in broken stripes that match the shadow-and-light of blade thickets. They kill quickly and cleanly, their jaws and inner tongues specialized to pierce reservoir sacs, drinking deeply while wasting as little as possible.

- Delicate, flexible **climbers** that live primarily in the upper tiers of columnar forests. Their bodies are whip-thin, with grasping pads that adhere to even the driest surfaces. They feed on the thin solvent films and micro-organisms coating plant skins, licking their way along like slow, ambulatory tongues.

- Burrowing **diggers** that do not seek depth for water, but for shade and stability. They hollow out chambers in the layered husk-ground, reinforcing them with compacted pellets of fibrous material glued together with bodily secretions. Their tunnels crisscross beneath the surface like a subway system, connecting shelter zones, nesting areas, and old kill-sites where bones still seep the last fade of moisture.

Insects and insect-analogues are everywhere. Tiny, nearly invisible things skitter along stems and across skins. Some ride the backs of larger animals, slipping into creases to feed on minute seepages. Others congregate in quiet swarms within sheltered crannies, where the air is a fraction more humid, their presence perceived only as a faint, collective rustle or the tiniest shift of shadow when they stir.

Despite their abundance, the fauna do not raise a constant clamor. Sound is expensive: to call across distance is to lift moisture into the air and send energy into the world. Hence, they speak quietly, or not at all.

### Soundscape: quiet, but not dead

Silence here is not the silence of absence; it is the silence of careful economy.

If you hold still, what you hear, mostly, are near-field sounds: subtle, low, and close.

- The dry **whisper of friction**: a trundler’s footplates sliding over fibrous mats, barely more than a soft scuff.

- The faint **crackling of husk** as some small burrower shifts and rearranges its walls below you.

- The minute **tap of seedpods** (or their analogues) releasing from their parent structures, dropping a short distance, then lodging soundlessly in the dense undergrowth.

- Occasionally, a **soft tearing** as a predator opens a cushion-organism, the rip of stretched outer skin, and then the almost inaudible wet sound of inner gel being drawn out; even the wetness is muted by the predator’s anatomical care.

Wind, when it comes, is gentle and filtered by the low, crowded forms of vegetation. It does not roar through towering canopies but slips among blades and columns like a patient, searching hand. You feel it more than hear it: a cool slide along your skin, a faint stirring of the world’s pervasive dryness.

During certain times of the planetary cycle—whatever passes for night here—some species engage in limited acoustic display. There might be:

- A series of **short, low clicks** from nocturnal hunters, more felt through the feet than heard in the ear, used to gauge structures in the dark.

- Soft **drumming within plant cavities**, where creatures tap on resonant chambers to communicate short-range: three taps, a pause, two taps—a language as minimal and compressed as their moisture budgets.

But there is no constant chorus. Quiet is the default state, broken only by brief, necessary exchanges and the stray accidents of movement.

### Atmosphere and weather: warm, dry, and just beginning

The air is warm—gently, steadily so. Not blistering desert heat, but a pervasive, life-comforting warmth that rarely swings toward cold. The sky has the pale clarity of worlds that do not cycle great masses of water aloft: no towering thunderheads, no ink-dark storms. Instead, faint streaks and broad, diffuse veils drift high above, built from dust and the tiniest droplets of solvent, tasked with scattering light more than delivering anything to the ground.

Weather here is an interplay of temperature, wind, and airborne micro-organisms rather than water.

- **Daily cycles**: As the star rises, surfaces warm and the few volatiles trapped in shallow pores begin to exude. Plants dilate their microscopic intake structures to harvest light and internal reactions quicken. Air, warmed from below, begins to move—slow convection currents that slide horizontally across the low landscape, curling up where elevation changes, then dispersing.

- **Solvent haze**: In some regions, during peak warmth, a faint, almost invisible haze forms just above the thickest vegetation where solvent vapors exhale from stomata-like openings. To the naked eye, it’s only a very slight softening of edges, as though the air were humming very quietly. As temperature drops, these vapors are reabsorbed or condensed on microscopic particles, then drawn back into living surfaces. There is no wasteful rain.

- **Dust and particles**: Erosion is new here, and the planet has not yet accumulated great saddles of loose dust. What particles the air does carry are mostly organic—powders, spores, shed scales, pollen analogues. They move in long, low clouds barely above head height, funneled between denser growth forms. Sometimes you pass through one and feel a fine pattering on your skin, like invisible sand. It is mostly harmless, though for the organisms that depend on it, that dust is a river.

Weather is local and small-scale: temperature and wind fields shaped by the world’s living structures as much as by landforms. A large clearing of bare rock may create its own updraft midway through the warm cycle, pulling cool air in along living corridors and sending a spiral of microfauna airborne. A dense “district” of columnar growth can trap air long enough for trace gases to build up, changing the scent of the whole neighborhood.

### Erosion: creation in its infancy

The planet’s mineral face has not known ages of grinding. With no rivers, no great tides, and only scant, solvent-thin seepages, erosion works differently.

Where a large organism has died, its body becomes a lens that focuses biological activity downward. Root-networks converge, digesting both corpse and the upper layer of rock beneath, chemically etching shallow hollows over many cycles. These become slight depressions, sometimes only a few hands wide, sometimes broad enough to collect communities of highly specialized scavenger flora that enjoy the marginal deepening and shelter.

Cracks in the substrate widen not so much from water freeze-thaw as from **biomechanical prying**. Root bundles wedge into them, then thicken, physically forcing the rock apart as they expand. Some organisms even secrete solvents into these cracks, softening the mineral and making room for more tissue—a deliberate assault on stone to create more habitable surface.

The result, at this early stage, is a landscape of modest undulation: low ridges, shallow basins, scattered hanging ledges where parts of the crust have fractured and slowly tilted. It is not yet dramatic, but everywhere you look, something is in the process of becoming—a tiny uplift here, a baby ravine there. You are witnessing geology at a slower pace but a brisk early chapter.

### Scale: endless, continuous life

Whatever the true size of this world, its living skin extends far enough that, standing in one location, you will never see an end to it. Horizons are always populated: a faint hazy band of columns far away, a low mound sea of cushions in another direction, the glint of rare exposed rock like eyes looking out from a crowded face.

The density is such that large untouched gaps do not exist. There are no broad empty deserts, no massive bare plains. Where one dominant growth form recedes, another takes its place almost immediately. Transitions between “districts” of the living city are gradual and overlapping: cushion-fields thinning, blade thickets rising, creeping lattices thickening underfoot.

Scale expresses itself vertically, too—not in towering trees, but in layers and micro-layers of occupation:

- Ground-level detritus colonized by fungi-like film.
- Above that, the primary structures: cushions, blades, columns.
- On their surfaces, smaller epiphytes, mats, beads, and clusters.
- On those, microbial films and insect analogues.

And within, of course, interior spaces—hollows in columns, tunnels in mats, chambers dug in the layered husk. The world fills itself from every angle.

### Rules of the world

Several principles seem to bind this place together:

1. **Moisture is almost entirely internal.**  
   Nearly all useful liquid exists inside organisms or in transient, thin films tethered to their surfaces. Open, unclaimed liquid is rare and short-lived.

2. **Life has become the primary agent of geology.**  
   With little water and gentle wind, biological processes—chemical weathering, physical prying by roots, the accumulation and decay of biomass—dominate the shaping of the surface. Over eons, they will likely erase whatever simple rockscape stood here at the beginning and replace it with something more intricately alive.

3. **Density is defense and infrastructure.**  
   The crowding of organisms is not mere excess. Close packing reduces exposed surface area per unit biomass, helping conserve moisture, while also providing structural support. It enables contiguous networks for nutrient sharing and creates corridor-like spaces for animals to move through without being forced into open, desiccating exposure.

4. **Sound and display are constrained by economy.**  
   In a world of tight moisture budgets and constant presence, loud, wasteful displays—shouting, flamboyant colors, extravagant structures—are rare. Communication is subtle and short-range; visual patterns are practical: camouflage, local recognition, micro-signals.

5. **Technology is growth.**  
   The “urban” nature of this world is not the result of tinkering minds but of growth rules: when millions of entities follow simple logics in a constrained environment, the emergent pattern mimics deliberate design. Roads are where trampling and resistant growth meet. Walls are where organisms align against winds and flows. Multi-level complexes are simply what happens when vertical spacing optimizes light and nutrient access.

### Atmosphere of being there

To be in this place is to feel slightly crowded, but never crushed.

The air presses gently, warm and dry but not suffocating. All around, within arm’s reach in every direction, some surface is alive: a rounded cushion swelling just enough to brush your leg, a stiff blade leaning across your path, a knot of lattice gently creaking as it adjusts under some hidden strain.

You walk along a narrow, naturally maintained corridor, the “floor” a dense, resilient mat that doesn’t give much. On either side rise walls of low vegetation, layered like the interior of a hive—alcoves and cavities, dangling tendrils, tiny doorways shaped by repeated passage of creatures smaller than you.

There is a feeling of perpetual **slow motion**. Nothing surges; everything continues. A trundler crosses the path ahead, unhurried, its antennae sweeping. Somewhere to the left, something large shifts with a careful weight, then stills. The breeze carries a faint, dry perfume: resins, waxes, and the lightest hint of mineral tang from exposed stone.

Look up, and you see a sky that is stable and pale. The star overhead gives a bright but not harsh light. Shadows are well-defined but softened around the edges by the ever-present haze of micro-life and dust. No thunder booms. No lightning flares. The drama here is always closer, on the scale of a predator’s pounce, a plant’s slow unfurling, a parasitic tendril’s quiet insertion into a neighbor.

Look down, and you notice details that make the “urban” nature of this biosphere undeniable: trails worn smoother than surroundings; repeated patterns in the spacing of columns that echo streets; clusters of organisms arranged like blocks around a central, slightly lower hollow that functions like a plaza of decomposition and feeding.

It is a bustling, nearly silent metropolis without machines, without conscious planners, without stone towers. A world-sized, warm, dry reef of vegetal and animal life that is still learning how to shape its own planet—young, dense, and already so full that there is scarcely any room left for anything else, except more life.
