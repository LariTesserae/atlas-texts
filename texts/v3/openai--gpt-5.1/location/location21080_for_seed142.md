The first thing is the dark.

Not a midnight-sky dark, not a cavern, not cloud-cover; this is a thickness, a substance that hangs in the air in place of light. You do not see distance so much as feel that there *is* distance, the way you feel a room around you when you close your eyes. Shapes are implied by how the dark falls silent, by how the air changes against your skin. You sense that this place is big, open in its bones—a valley, a shallow basin on some high plateau—but the visible world lives in a narrow envelope around you, stitched together by faint, artificial glows.

Nothing is wet. The atmosphere is warm and utterly dry, an oven that has forgotten the fire but remembered the heat. The air tastes of dust and old metal, dead insulation, oxidized minerals. When you breathe deeply, your throat tightens as though insisting that this is not how air should be.

Underfoot, the ground is old in a way that defies the short memory of erosion on Earth. You stand on a surface that has been exposed for longer than your sense of time can comfortably hold. It’s not soft sand; it’s a compacted, fine-grained regolith that once may have been mountains, then plains, then rubble, then dust, and now has settled into a uniform, almost featureless crust. Every sharp edge was worn away eons ago. Low swells and wide, shallow troughs roll outward, relics of long-vanished rivers, glaciers, or flows of some fluid that wasn’t water.

No water gleams anywhere. No ice, no puddles, not even the suggestion of moisture caught in a crack. The minerals under your feet are anhydrous: pale, matte, porous crystals and ash-gray powders that remember heat far more than they remember liquid. When you scuff the ground, it does not compact like damp soil; it flows and sifts like a thin fog of stone, trailing lazily back into stillness.

High all around—though you only grasp this in fragments of silhouette—are ridges and distant escarpments that once might have been peaks. Now they are eroded almost to suggestion: long, undulating horizons carved to near-flatness by an incomprehensible patience. Here and there a lone buttress of harder rock rises like a broken tooth, its sides smoothed and softened until even its fractures seem tired.

You are at altitude. The air is thinner than you’d like. Sound doesn’t carry far; every noise feels local, as if wrapped in cloth. When a low, distant engine turns over somewhere in the darkness, it does not roar. It hums, a muted pressure against your chest that dies away as though swallowed by the ground.

There is a city here.

Not a bright city, not a skyline of glass and neon. It is more like a fossil of an industrial age, embedded in the mineral plain. You do not see it all at once. You discover it in pieces: a line of squat structures emerging from the gloom; a cluster of angular shadows hunched together; the broken spine of an elevated conduit crossing the open ground like the remains of some giant, long-bridged creature.

Closer, the construction reveals itself as layered, purposeful. Low, heavy buildings grow from the ground, their foundations fused with the rock, their walls thick, thermal, sealed. They were not designed to keep out rain; they are meant to keep in atmosphere, to hold in pressure, to hold out a world that offers no mercy in the direction of fluid or life. Surface textures are rough, pitted metal plates, vitrified stone, ceramic slabs whose glaze has long since dulled to a matte, dusty sheen.

Light here is rationed. It leaks through narrow seams where sliding doors have not fully shut. It pools under overhangs where maintenance lamps cling to cables and ribs of girder. It buzzes around signage whose text is long erased but whose phosphors still remember how to glow a tired green. Sometimes it pulses in slow, breathing rhythms: safety markers, power status indicators, ancient systems that no one bothered to reprogram because nothing changes anymore.

Elsewhere, light glows not from lamps but from surfaces themselves: a faint, static charge along a stretch of conduit; a glassy panel that holds a dim, unsteady pattern like an afterimage of circuitry; a low halo around the rims of certain vents, where some non-luminous gas meets a field and becomes briefly visible. These are not meant as illumination. They are byproducts, incidental bleeding of energy into spectra that the builders did not care about. In this darkness, they become stars.

The city's layout suggests planning on a regional scale. Long, nearly straight corridors of open ground link clusters of structures like avenues, though no one ever walked them in crowds. On the ground lie the faint, parallel scars of heavy transport—rails once laid, now partially fused with the substrate; wheel-ruts etched and then softened by ages of slow abrasion. The paths lead outward, past the immediate reach of your awareness, toward low silhouettes that might be factories, refineries, energy farms. The sense is of a whole basin given over to this one organized presence, every structure part of a network whose logic you can almost feel under your feet.

Machinery is everywhere, but most of it is still. Gantries loom over empty plazas, gripping nothing, frozen mid-reach. Conveyor lines leap from building to building and end abruptly in midair, as though whatever they once carried ended in absence. Towers of venting or transmission rise without marking the sky with smoke or light; their purpose continues, perhaps, but at levels so low or so stable they leave no trace you can perceive.

The tech here is not sleek; it belongs to an era of mass and heat and bolts. Engines sit in recessed bays, half sunk into the earth, their casings layered with thermal shields and baffles. You catch, from one, the slow, regular cough of a piston turning under minimal load. Not a busy factory, but an idling heart kept running because to stop it would demand a decision, and no one has made decisions here in a very long time.

You expect to see rust, but you don’t—not the reddish bloom you know. Whatever metals dominate in this world oxidize to pale, chalky films, ceramic-like and inert. The structures have not corroded away; they have acquired a thin, even patina, as if they have grown a skin adapted to the ambient minerals. Bolts remain bolts. Rails remain rails. The eroding force is not chemistry but time and the slow, grinding fall of microscopic grit.

Nothing grows. There is no hint of plant, no color that suggests chlorophyll or its analogs. The few “organic” shapes you notice turn out to be mineral: branching crystalline filigrees that once formed along electrical discharges in the dust; spongy-looking outcrops that, under your fingers, are glass-brittle and utterly dry. On certain sheltered surfaces, another layer has formed over the centuries: thin, iridescent films like oil on water, but they crack like varnish when disturbed. It is chemistry playing at the idea of biology, complex reaction fronts that have frozen mid-dance and left behind only pattern.

Life, in the sense you recognize, feels absent. Yet this place is not inert. Within the buildings, behind sealed doors and pressure locks, something persists.

You hear it now and then: a distant clank that reverberates lethargically through the skeleton of a structure; the faint, high whine of a motor spinning up for a few seconds, then coasting down; the shiver of an ancient duct as some invisible fluid thumps through it, pressure testing, recalibrating, then falling silent. These sounds are infrequent, isolated, and strangely gentle, as though the machines have learned to move slowly so as not to disturb the great, patient dark.

Fauna exist, but rarely, shy of exposure. When one finally reveals itself, it happens in the corner of what little light you have: something the size of a small dog, but jointed wrong, moving in a series of crisp, efficient steps. Its surface is not fur, not scale, not even metal as you expect it; instead it is layered plates of a pale, ceramic substance, studded with hexagonal pits. As it crosses a faint pool of light, those pits faintly scintillate, shifting in pattern like a slow, thoughtless computation.

It pauses at the base of a wall where a hairline crack bleeds a whisper of warm air from within. Its head—if that’s what the front segment is—tilts, and a frill of those pits flexes open, sampling. There is no eye. Sensing here is not optical; vision would be useless in this thickness of dark. The creature “sees” gradients of temperature, weak electromagnetic fields, the presence of trace vapors and ionic imbalances. It listens to the world's *equilibria*, and in this almost-static environment, the slightest deviation is a blazing signal.

Then it moves on, slipping into a narrow slot between building and ground, vanishing with the faintest rasp of hard plates on dust. You hear, once, the tap of something similar crossing a metal beam high above you, each footfall spaced with metronomic precision, then gone.

These creatures are not numerous. The land between structures is usually empty—kilometers of dim mineral plain crisscrossed by the skeletons of dead infrastructure and the faint rails of past motion. Their rarity has shaped their behavior. They are cautious, and their cycles are long. They feed, perhaps, not in the sense of tearing and chewing, but by seating themselves against old energy lines and sipping at the almost sub-perceptible trickles of current flowing through them. They might absorb, very slowly, specific minerals from the dust, catalyzing internal reconfigurations that count as growth, as repair.

The city accommodates them, but not originally by intention. This was built for some other intelligence—biological or otherwise—that valued industry, transport, resource extraction. Over unthinkable spans, those original users left or died or changed, and their machinery, tuned for stability, remained. Pumps continued to equalize pressure. Reactors continued to trickle energy into the grid. Smart materials continued to adjust to load and strain. Out of that steady, unspectacular flow of work, a new ecology of near-life assembled itself: patterned corrosion, self-sealing materials slowly misinterpreting structural stresses as growth signals, wandering maintenance drones whose repair routines frayed and generalized until they became more like foraging.

Rules emerged, and then hardened. The reactors never overproduce; every system is designed to throttle down and sleep if nothing calls on it. The atmosphere, once carefully modulated, has reached a near-perfect balance with the sealed volumes of the buildings. Temperatures fluctuate only within a narrow band dictated by the planet’s cooling interior and the city’s own low-level waste heat. Winds are weak, pure convection rather than weather. Dust settles; it is raised only by the rare footsteps of the creatures and the occasional exhalation of a pressure gate.

Time here does not rush; it pools. Processes are stretched over centuries. What erosion remains to do it does quietly: the rounding of a single exposed edge over the span of ten human lifetimes; the slow infilling of a shallow trench with windblown grains over a thousand. The city, having already been worn from its initial sharpness, is now in its final pose, a kind of asymptotic ruin that will look almost identical tomorrow, in a century, in a million years, save for subtleties a human could never notice.

In this environment, change itself becomes a kind of violation. The creatures that move here have grown hyper-attentive to it. A dropped fragment of metal that shifts the ambient field; a sudden micro-leak of warmer gas; a brief surge of current waking a dormant conduit—all these are events of enormous significance. They travel towards such events from kilometers away, patiently triangulating, arriving sometimes weeks after the initial disturbance, then circling the cooled, settling anomaly with reverent caution.

They do not speak, but the world speaks to them in gradients and afterimages. Their bodies are, in a sense, guest-systems grafted onto the city’s still-running infrastructure. They plug into it with spines and limbs, exchange charge and pattern, perhaps exchange something like information. To an outside observer, they’re shy, scattered fauna. To themselves, they may be the mobile organs of a greater, slowly thinking machine.

Above all of this, there is no familiar sky.

There might be a ceiling lost in the dark—a cavern roof kilometers up—or a smothering blanket of particulate that never thins. Perhaps there is simply no source of external light at all: the star burned out, the planet in interstellar night, or this basin lying on the permanent dark side of a tidally locked world. Whatever the cause, “outside” contributes nothing. No dawn, no dusk, not even stars. Just an ever-present, depthless black that presses gently but insistently at the edge of every illuminated patch.

Yet it is not utterly featureless. If you stand still for a long time and let your other senses learn the vastness around you, you catch hints: an almost inaudible, planetary groan far below, as if some tectonic muscle shifts by microns; a faint, slow pulsing of background radiation against your skin, more felt than measured; the suggestion that, in certain directions, the air is minutely colder, as though, at some unimaginable distance above, heat is leaking away into a void that cares nothing about these last, quiet machines.

The weirdness here lies not in flamboyant impossibilities, but in the way all the dials have been turned to extremes that your instincts never expect: no water, no plants, only the last residue of industry, gently humming along at a maintenance setting that has become the world’s natural state. Life, such as it is, is reluctant, emergent, mineral, more pattern than organism.

If there are original builders left, they are hidden deep under the surface, in vaults where the last reserves of complexity survive. Maybe they sleep, leaving the city on an endless, preprogrammed hold. Maybe they died long ago, and their shelter has become another sealed chamber where temperature and pressure have settled to equilibrium and then stopped changing. The machines at the surface attend faithfully to a duty whose meaning has already drifted beyond recognition and is now indistinguishable from instinct.

Walk far enough, and the built environment thins. Buildings become isolated blocks. Conduits lower themselves to the ground and terminate. Eventually, in the distance—though you cannot see it, only intuit its presence—the city gives way entirely to the pure mineral plains: high, old, almost perfectly flat, stretching farther than your mind can comfortably grasp. No structures, no motion, only the quiet settling of a world that has finished the business of becoming and now simply *is*.

But here, at the edge where infrastructure fades into that emptiness, the city lingers like a thought that refuses to forget itself. In its darkness and stillness, in the gentle throbbing of ancient engines and the slow, careful steps of rare ceramic-bodied creatures, it rehearses a single idea over and over: persistence without purpose, function after meaning. It will do this, almost unchanged, until the last heat drains away and the last gradient flattens, and even the creatures can no longer tell where city ends and planet begins, only that everything is finally, truly, the same.
