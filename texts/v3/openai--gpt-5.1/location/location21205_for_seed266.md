First comes the height.

It is not hills, not even mountains as a human world would name them, but an almost continuous continent of stone and alloy rising into its own layer of sky: a banded, folded shell of the planet thrust upward and frozen in slow motion. Imagine a range so tall that weather forgets how to cross it, and instead grows native to its slopes — cloudless, dry, ruled by slow gradients of pressure and long pale winds.

From a distance — if distance is even meaningful when everything is this big — the land appears as a tangled massif of ridges and plateaus, broken chimneys, buttresses and buttressed buttresses, as if the crust had been heaved and then patiently planed down over a hundred million years. The colors are warm, but not from sunlight alone: iron-rich reds and bruised violets, soot-black glass, vast expanses of pale, sun-bleached mineral crust that reflect light like old bone.

Water is missing in the literal sense. There are no rivers cutting through the stone, no snowcaps, no glint of ice. Erosion has long since found another agent. The air here is thick with a dry, invisible solvent — a gas that does what water once did elsewhere: it seeps, it dissolves, it carries. Over aeons it has etched the rock into strange softened forms, leaving walls that look melted, pinnacles that seem to have drooped and then re-frozen mid-sag. Where the gas pools in low ledges and hollows, the rock is bitten into honeycombs and delicate lacework: caverns open to the sky, roofs collapsed a million years ago, all now part of a single, continuous monument to patient corrosion.

Between the great stone masses, the land drops away not into valleys as such but into voids: gorges and trenches so deep that their floors are invisible in the haze, only suggested by faint, upward drifting dust. Narrow causeways stride these depths — natural buttresses carved by the solvent-gas and later chiseled by tools — linking one plateau to another. They are sparsely used, but used: the world is not empty.

There is life here, and it is wrong in the familiar ways of evolution under alien rules. Vegetation, in the ordinary sense, never took hold. Instead, the heights are speckled and streaked with growths that are part mineral, part tissue, part eddy in the chemistries of the air.

On the rock faces, in the shadowed undercuts, you see the mats first: dull sheens of dark, iridescent film that cling like dried varnish. Up close they resolve into clusters of microscopic, crystal-thread organisms, knitting themselves into a felted layer no thicker than a fingernail. They are neither plant nor fungus as Earth would define them, but something cooler, slower: they feed on the solvent-gas itself, on trace metals in the dust, building lattices that grow not upward but inward, into the cracks of the stone. When the air stills they look like nothing at all, just a stain. When a faint breeze passes, the mats flex as if exhaling, venting faint, volatile scents that never quite become smells — more like a change in texture to the air.

Here and there, where layers of this living felt have accrued over millennia, they develop vertical ambition. Columnar growths rise from the cliff shelves, ribbed and hollow like pipes in an organ, their surfaces quilted with interlocking plates of semi-flexible crystal. They are not trees, but they fill a similar niche: tall anchors for the sky, slow distillers of chemistry, long-lived. When the solvent-gas thickens at certain pressures — the closest thing this world has to weather — it flows through their hollow cores, reacting, leaving behind new filigree, new patterns. They grow denser, heavier, change color from slate to copper to a deep, almost luminous indigo with age. When they finally fracture under their own mass, their broken segments tumble into the chasms below, feeding strata of life we cannot see from here.

The fauna — abundant by the measure of movement and eyes and mouths — thread themselves through this vertical world.

They are built for height and for dryness. Limbs are long, bodies narrow; mass is a liability when everything is ledges and drop-offs. The most visible are the gliders: creatures with membranous spans not of flesh but of thin, quasi-crystalline film that stiffens when charged. They roost, folded, along the vertical faces like a second shadow. Then, when they launch, they release tiny pulses of static that ripple across their wings, allowing them to ride not just air currents but subtle gradients in the planet’s electromagnetic field. From the higher plateaus they drift in flocks that look, at a distance, like drifting shards of glass, catching the sun.

Their calls are faint, infrequent: soft clicks that carry along the rock, small electric pops like static discharge when two wings brush in flight. Mostly, though, they are silent. The environment discourages noise. Deep echoes here last a long time; anything loud would ring against the cliffs and fill the narrow canyons for minutes, revealing its source to every hunter up and down the vertical mile.

On the rock shelves, more cautious forms move. They are low to the ground, multiple-legged, their limbs jointed like a folding ruler: able to compress into a compact shape for leaping, then unfold to span broad gaps. Their surfaces are dull, dust-colored, ridged not with fur or scales but with laminae of mineralized chitin that double as micro-sails. When they stop, they bristle these laminae into upright vanes that harvest faint electrical differentials from the air, powering slow digestive processes. They rasp at the living films, grazing without killing; the mats simply close the wounds with new threads.

Predators are rarer but present. Sometimes a ledge seems to lift, a seam in the rock opens, and what you thought was bare stone unfolds into a lattice of sensory fronds — a sessile hunter, deeply rooted into the cliff, waiting decades between movements. At other times a flicker crosses the sky: a glider larger than the rest, its wings darker, its membranous surfaces veined with piping that glows faintly as charge flows. That one does have a voice, but it uses it sparsely: a low, infrasonic warble that propagates through the stone more than the air, mapping the geometry of the canyons in a slow, three-dimensional sonar.

The atmosphere itself feels old. It is warm — not hot, not harsh enough to burn exposed tissue, but with a kind of low, omnipresent heat that comes from the planet’s interior more than its star. The high plateaus sit above most of the convection cells, in a zone where winds are gentle but continuous, always combing the cliffs. Clouds form only as thin, high smears far overhead, too tenuous and too dry to ever condense into rain. Lightning is rare, and when it comes it tends to be horizontal, crawling along the margins of density layers like luminous veins, silently rearranging the ion balance for a hundred kilometers at a stroke.

Within this landscape of height and dry chemistry, there is a city. It is not a single point, but a web — a linked chain of built environments draped along a thousand kilometers of cliff.

From afar, the city reads as a new kind of striation in the rock. Natural layers run mostly horizontal; the built ones split those lines at oblique angles, geometric, repetitive. Platforms jutt out from the vertical face like broad, overlapping petals, each the size of a small town. They are connected by a fine mesh of bridges, cableways, and rigid trusses that cross the gaps with an almost casual defiance of the depth beneath.

Construction material is what the land provides: dense, composite stone extracted from deeper strata, sintered and alloyed with harvested metals. Many surfaces are clad in dull, oxidized plates that match the colors of the cliffs, as if camouflage were a lingering instinct. Others are bare framework: beams latticed in intricate openwork, more air than solid, designed to flex under slow shifts in the rock rather than crack.

The infrastructure hums, but quietly. Engines here are not roaring combustion but slow, continuous, deliberate devices. Along the lower edges of many platforms, enormous rotating vanes hang into the void — gravity turbines, using the faint but persistent down-draft of the warm, rising atmosphere along the cliff walls. Their movement is languid: a single rotation might take an hour, but the torque is immense. Cables carry that mechanical power inward to run cranes, presses, grinders, and deep-burrowing drills that nibble at the cliff itself, hollowing out chambers and conduits within.

There is electricity, too, harvested more than generated. Arrays of slender rods bristle from the city’s uppermost edges, combing charge from the sky, and the living mats on the rock are not the only ones that know how to drink the air. Thin, shimmering fabrics stretch between anchor points like webs, capturing microscopic shifts in potential and feeding them into slow banks of capacitors. The architecture glows here and there with a muted, internal light: not bright beacons but steady lines tracing edges, doorways, walkways, designed more for orientation than display.

Factories cling to their platforms like barnacles. They are not vast monoliths but a collection of modular shops: stamp-hammers for shaping metal ribs, slow furnaces that sinter regolith into ceramic shells, fabricators that extrude cables from blended organic-mineral composites. Exhaust is a whisper, vented into the solvent-laden air where it becomes one more set of reactions for the living films to metabolize. The impression is not of a ravenous, expansionist industry, but of a carefully integrated one, scaled to match and coexist with the glacial pace of the environment around it.

The density of construction is high only in layers. A given platform might be busy — tangled with walkways, rail-lines, crane tracks, stacked habitation — and yet when you step back in your mental scale, you see how immense the vertical surface is, how far it stretches above and below and to the sides without any human-made mark at all. Whole stretches of cliff between city-plates are bare save for the slow, patient ecologies of mats and columns. It feels as if each built cluster is an island, separated from its neighbors not just horizontally but by gulfs of unoccupied stone.

Sound, here, never rises to cacophony. You hear the susurrus of wind slipping past the beams. The distant creak and low grind of a turbine, deep and steady as a heartbeat. The occasional clatter of machinery inside a workshop, half-muffled by walls and absorbed quickly by open air. Voices — if you stand near enough to where life-forms gather — are low, clipped, pitched not to carry far. Even the fauna keep their emissions minimal; their wings whisper, feet click, gasses vent in faint sighs. Silence is not absolute, but it is the baseline, the medium in which other sounds appear like stones dropped into a lake.

The rules of this world feel deliberate, as if every parameter were tuned for slowness and height. Change happens, but languidly. Rock flows, on geological time, and the city anticipates that flow: expansion joints big enough to walk through, structures tied into the bedrock in ways that let them creep along with it rather than resisting. The living mats spread a few centimeters in a decade, and this is considered fast. The crystal-columns take centuries to grow noticeable bulges. Even the animals — though frantic by comparison — move with a certain economy: no pointless displays, no rapid oscillations. Exertion is costly where metabolism is built around rare redox reactions in a dry gas instead of quick aqueous chemistry.

Weather, if one can call it that, expresses itself more as a slow breathing of the entire vertical system. There are cycles in the solvent-gas concentration: over years, over lifetimes, the air composition modulates as reservoirs buried deep in the crust vent or close. During the “high” phase, the etching intensifies; pits and hollows deepen, the lace-like caverns extend. The mats swell with greater energy intake. Slightly more frequent rockfalls pepper the chasm floors. In the “low” phase, everything rests. The gas thins; winds weaken. Dust settles in a finer, more even coat over ledges and beams. The city adjusts workflows: some factories idle, others — those dedicated to maintenance and internal recycling — take precedence.

Weirdness here is not spectacle but coherence on unfamiliar principles. Biology without liquid water, architecture without flat ground, industry without smoke or roar. Creatures and machines alike treat electricity not as a violent spark but as a slow, structural fluid, something you shape and store like clay. Gravity is not a background constant but a resource to lean into, a flow you can tap via hanging apparatus and falling loads. The cliff is not an obstacle; it is the world. Horizontal surfaces are anomalies, treated with suspicion, sometimes even discomfort. To beings grown here, a broad open plain would feel exposed, disorienting — nowhere for air to move in interesting ways, nowhere for sound to travel along stone.

The city’s relationship with the non-human ecologies around it is complex and, for now, stable. The living mats coat not only rock but also many of the older structural elements; some of this is intentional. Certain panels and conduits are left unsealed, inviting colonization. The mats in those places are metabolically tuned — through generations of selective placement and careful harvesting — to exude thin, conductive films that the city taps for low-level current. Elsewhere, however, life is held at bay with regular, minimal intervention. Scouring teams — vehicles that move like slow beetles along the undersides of platforms — scrape back creeping film before it can compromise supports.

The fauna treat the built environment as just another stratum of cliff. Small grazers skitter beneath gantries, exploiting condensation of rarer trace gases in sheltered corners. Larger gliders weave through the cable-forest, sometimes perching on the outer edges of habitation blocks, watching the soft glow of internal lights with flat, reflective eyes. There are stories, no doubt, among the platform-dwellers of things that live deeper in the rock: disturbingly intelligent, geologically patient entities that sense the minute shifts of pressure and electric fields, and dislike the growing lattice of galleries and tunnels. Whether these are myth or an emergent recognition of real patterns, they fit the pace and tone of the place.

At night — if one can call it that, given the planet’s slow rotation and the city’s altitude above most of the atmospheric scattering — the cliff becomes a different sky. The star overhead moves almost imperceptibly, and its departure is a drawn-out dimming, colors sliding from white-gold to amber to deep rust. As direct light fades, the latticework of platforms and beams begins to reveal itself in outline. Not through bright neon or floodlit facades but via a multitude of small, functional luminescences: corridor guides, instrument panes, the faint pulse of charge regulators, the pale shimmer of electrostatic webs still harvesting night differentials.

The rock itself sometimes glows. Stressed seams deep within the cliff produce piezoelectric fields that elicit a ghostly light from certain mineral inclusions — a faint foxfire along fracture lines. In the dark, from a distance, the cliff face resembles a star field turned vertical: clusters and strings of light tracing the invisible tectonic skeleton beneath the stone.

From the platforms, the abyss below is black, then slowly reveals its own dim constellations: the bioluminescent flicker of organisms that live in the deep gas-saturated shadow, far below the city’s stratum. Their signals come in slow waves, pulses that can last for hours, spreading along the walls in curves that echo the global oscillations of pressure and chemistry. Those patterns, too, are part of the land’s identity: a planetary heartbeat written in light against the stone, too slow and too vast for any one life to fully comprehend, but constant enough to lend a sense of underlying rhythm.

This is a world of vertical vastness, dry chemistry, and patient processes; a continent of cliffs eroded not by water but by a soft, invisible gnawing in the air. Within it, a city has found a way to hang, to lean into height and slowness instead of defying them. Life — abundant yet quiet — clings to surfaces and drifts across voids, threading mineral with tissue, machinery with biology, until the distinction begins to blur. Everything is separated by space — between platforms, between ledges, between one cluster of beings and the next — and yet all of it shares the same slow, overarching pulse.

The place does not rush to reveal itself. It is content to be learned gradually, in layers, like the cliff itself.
