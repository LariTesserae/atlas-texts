The air is thin enough to taste.

It has that metallic, altitude tang that never quite lets your lungs fill. Each breath feels like you’re sipping, not drinking, the atmosphere. Cold presses in without wind: a still, unyielding chill that doesn’t bite so much as occupy everything. It is always this temperature. It has been this temperature for longer than there have been words to name cold.

You stand high—very high—yet there is no sense of a single peak. This is not a mountain you climb and then descend from. It is a plateau of summits, a roof of the world tiled with jagged angles and vast, bare expanses of stone. The horizon is a ring of sawteeth, overlapping silhouettes of distant ridges that never sharpen or recede. They remain exactly as they are, no matter how long you walk or how often you look again.

Underfoot, the ground is worn but not ruined. Nothing here is new; nothing here is in the process of becoming something else. The stone surfaces are a catalog of slow, finished processes: fluted channels where water once ran and no longer does, scalloped shelves shaped by vanished snowpacks, broad terraces cracked but not crumbling. The rock has settled into its final vocabulary—ridges, ledges, bowl-shaped depressions—and now simply holds that shape, indefinitely.

There is water, but only as trace and punctuation. A black seam streaks down a cliff: a threadlike cascade that seems not to move, though you hear it roar. Closer, you realize the liquid is sliding in a single unbroken sheet, too smooth, too continuous. Droplets do not separate. Spray hangs at the base of the fall as a fixed halo, a glassy mist that hums at the edge of hearing. Touch it and your fingers pass through something that feels like damp cold, but come away dry, as if the idea of water is here but not its full behavior.

Elsewhere, in shallow basins and rock cups, there are pools—some rimmed with a thin fringe of ice, some dark and mirror-flat. No ripples, no drifting dust, no surface tension breaks. When a stone dislodged from above tumbles into one, the splash is immense in sound but the image is wrong: the form of the water heaves up, a crown of frozen arcs, then recomposes itself like a reversed collapse, silently stitching back into a perfect plane. The sound of the splash, meanwhile, goes on and on, as if the echo has forgotten how to fade.

Sound here is abundant, but nothing seems to cause it at the moment you hear it. Hidden thunders roll across the rock, not from the sky but from beneath your feet, a layered grumbling as though ancient avalanches are still occurring at some unreachable depth, replaying themselves without movement in the visible world. Sometimes, from a far-off canyon you have not yet seen, comes the deep-bell clang of a rockfall that never happens, repeating at irregular but identical intervals. Close your eyes and you might think the landscape is alive with upheaval; open them, and every boulder is locked where it clearly has been for ages.

The sky is not quite dark, but never really bright. Above, a low, bruised dimness—not cloud in any familiar sense, but a uniform, granular twilight. There is no discernible sun, only a general direction from which a grey light seeps, as if through many layers of dirty glass. Shadows exist, but only as soft, overlapping blurs that don’t entirely obey geometry; a boulder’s shadow might extend in two directions at once, faintly, as if responding to more than one light source, or to remembered positions of the sun that are no longer relevant.

Sometimes, flickers pass across the far-off sky, like lightning seen from behind several mountains and through a curtain of cloth. They flash without illuminating anything, and there is no delay of thunder—just a sudden increase in the world’s ongoing roar, as if more of the constant noise has been layered on rather than a distinct new sound introduced. Nothing in the air moves: no scudding clouds, no drifting fog. The dimness is a state, not a passing weather.

Vegetation clings where it can, but the world is not green. Instead: a moss the color of dried blood, hugging the undersides of overhangs where droplets of condensation gather and then simply sit, fat and unmoving. Sparse tufts of lichen, bone-white and brittle-looking, outline hairline fractures in the stone as if tracing a map of stress that will never fully release. Here and there, a shrub erupts from a crack—a low, sprawling thing with spines instead of leaves, each spine tipped with a bead of frost that never quite melts, never quite grows.

These plants do not wave in any breeze; there is almost never wind. Yet they tremble very slightly, in a way that is out of sync with your heartbeat and breathing, a subtle vibration tuned instead to the deep subterranean rumble that never stops. They’ve adapted to a world where movement is stored as sound and pattern, not as weather.

Across the plateau are scars that might be mistaken for watercourses: long, looping dry channels etched deep into the rock. They lead from nowhere to nowhere, often beginning at the base of some sheer wall and ending abruptly at a cliff edge. Their curves make sense as if carved by steady flow over epochs—except that the terrain they cross does not align with any plausible watershed. Some channels even climb, imperceptibly but undeniably, against gravity, silvered along their length with a film of moisture that does not run, does not bead, just adheres. If you place a hand on this dampness, you feel not wetness but a gentle inward pull, like static or the tide of a dream trying to take you somewhere else.

The scale of it all is large enough to feel lonely, but not so vast that you lose detail. You can walk and always have something in view: a bluff three valleys away, an arch of rock leaning over a chasm, a solitary spire shaped like a blunt tooth protruding from a col. Each feature is far enough from the others that the emptiness between them has weight. The silence of space here is paradoxical—there is so much sound, but so little presence. Between each outcrop or ravine lies an expanse of bare stone where nothing stands, nothing grows, nothing moves. The land feels hollowed out, as if whatever once occupied these flats has been cleanly removed.

The built things make this absence worse.

They appear rarely and never in clusters. First you notice a single wall where no wall should be, grown from the same stone as the plateau but with an unnatural straightness. It rises waist-high, runs for five or six paces, then ends in a blunt cut, like a thought interrupted and abandoned. There is no rubble at its base, no sign it ever continued further. Weather has had its way with it—rounded edges, a patina of the same dark lichen as the surrounding rock—but the geometry remains undeniable: this, someone decided, should be here.

Elsewhere, half a staircase climbs a low slope and vanishes into bare rock, the last few steps fused seamlessly into the ground as if the stone had flowed over them and then forgotten it did so. A ring of standing stones crowns a knoll, each post tilted at the same improbable angle, leaning not outward or inward but slightly sideways, as if obeying some other direction than up and down. In the center of the ring, the air hums more noticeably; voices you cannot quite make out seem to occupy the higher bands of the endless noise.

Technology in the sense of mechanisms or devices is absent. Nothing rotates, ticks, flashes. Yet these structures are too ordered for chance. Some, when you approach, subtly alter your perception: a simple lintel-stone set between two uprights, making a doorway with nothing to cross, looks ordinary until you step beneath it. For the brief instant you are within the frame, the temperature drops further and everything goes utterly, perfectly quiet.

Not quieter: the entire roaring soundscape stops. Your footsteps, your own breath, even the quiet phrasing of your thoughts seem to lose their auditory character. You emerge on the far side into the same grey light, the same plateau, and all at once the noise returns at full volume, hitting you like altitude again. Look back at the gateway and it is just stone, worn and static. Pass through again and the same thing happens, identically, each time; this place does not improvise, it replays.

Time itself has that same quality of replay rather than flow. There are no clouds to track, no moving sun, no shifting shadows. You can walk until your legs ache, rest until your muscles burn less, drink from the not-water that doesn’t wet your skin but does somehow ease your throat, and when you look up nothing in the landscape is any closer than it was. Distances behave strangely: a pinnacle you’ve been walking toward for hours never changes in apparent size, but the ground between you and it slowly fills itself with details you do not remember crossing—small gullies, cracked shelves, low benches of stone that feel “added in,” as if the world were backfilling the past to reconcile the fact that you are tired with the fact that you have not approached your destination.

Fauna are rare enough that any movement feels like a revelation. Once, against an ash-grey slope, something like a deer appears, or a goat, its body too slender for this air, its coat a quilt of muted colors that don’t exist elsewhere in the scene. You see it perched on a ledge that has no access, its hooves gripping the rock at an angle no weight should maintain. Its head turns not to look at you but to align with you, eyes sliding into place as if the creature had to remember how to face in your direction.

When it moves, it does so in increments, like a series of still images shuffled rapidly. It is at one end of the ledge, then halfway along, then at the other, with the suggestion of intermediate motion burned into your vision but never directly seen. Each positional jump is accompanied by a brief intensification of the surrounding sound: a higher chord atop the low thunder, a whine that makes the air feel tight. If you blink at the right moment, you can trap the afterimage of the animal in two positions at once. Then, once you look away and back again, it is simply gone, as if it had never been more than a correction in the landscape’s sense of balance.

Other life is smaller, harder to catch. A pale something darts along a crack in the rock just at the edge of sight, leaving behind a faint echo of tapping claws that continues long after it has vanished. Once, when you pause at the lip of a vast, fogless chasm, a broad-winged shape passes far below, utterly black against the nearly-black depths, its wingbeats perfectly silent. Yet each slow flap adds a new phase to the omnipresent noise above, like pulling taut another unseen string in a harp already thick with tension.

Despite all the loudness, your own actions fail to register correctly. A shouted word does not echo off nearby cliffs; instead, it returns from far away, distorted, as if someone else is repeating what you said from a valley you haven’t reached yet. Drop a stone over a ledge and you hear its impacts drifting up from multiple depths simultaneously, each set of clatters refusing to fade out, piling layer upon layer until the original drop is lost in a continuous static of falling that never completes.

The world’s weirdness is not garish or chaotic; it is systematic, as if some rules were switched partway through its formation and never switched back. Gravity mostly works, but paths run subtly uphill without cost. Water exists, but has given up on flowing. Time passes for your body—you tire, you hunger, you count breaths—but the environment is in a kind of dynamic stasis, endlessly active in noise and implication yet visually frozen.

Occasionally, you find inscriptions on rock faces, shallowly carved and softened by age until they are almost texture rather than symbol. They are arranged in lines and spirals and grids that suggest writing, but no language you know. Stand before them and the background roar shifts, certain frequencies dimming, others rising, like a crowd quieting in recognition. If you trace a line with your finger, a thin crack of absolute silence opens along the groove, extending a tiny distance beyond the visible end before snapping shut. It feels, in that moment, as though you could keep drawing into a space that isn’t quite here, following a script that extends outside the stone’s physical limits.

Despite the emptiness of life, the place resists loneliness in a peculiar way. Not because it is welcoming—it isn’t—but because it feels already occupied, saturated with the residue of patterns that do not involve you. The scattered structures, the impossible hydrology, the fixed roar, the not-quite-right animals: all of it adds up to a sense that you’ve arrived too late to a world that finished changing long ago, and too early to whatever it will do next.

That “next,” however, never seems to approach. No storm gathers at the horizon. No glacier creeps forward. No vegetation advances or retreats. You could leave a mark—a cairn of stones, a scratched symbol—and return what feels like days later to find it exactly as you left it, neither eroded nor disturbed. The only difference would be within you: more tired, hungrier, hoar-frosted breath hanging in front of your face in the same stale, dim light.

The plateau stretches on: ridges, terraces, ledges, still cascades, dry channels leading upstream into the sky and down into chasms that emit a steady, organ-deep tone. The sound is loud enough that it becomes background, the way your own blood’s pulse fades from awareness if you listen too long. And like your pulse, it never stops—not at night, because there is no true night here, only a deepening of the existing dimness into a thicker, grainier shade. Not in storm, because storms are possibilities that do not currently exist. It is a continuous world-sound, looping, revisiting itself, holding the place in a kind of acoustic suspension.

In this high, cold expanse, reality hasn’t shattered; it has congealed. The rules have congealed with it—slightly skewed, self-consistent in their skew, unwilling to budge. Things don’t change here in any way that the rocks would notice. But if you stay long enough, you realize something unsettling: the plateau is constant, the sound unfading, the dim light unwavering.

The only thing that really changes, result by result, is you.
