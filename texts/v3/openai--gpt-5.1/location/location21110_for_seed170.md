The air up here is thin, but it shouldn’t be this loud.

You stand on a high plateau of dark, porous stone, baked hot enough that the horizon bends and shimmers. The ground is cracked into great polygonal plates like dried mud, yet the material is clearly igneous — a basaltic crust, vesicles frozen in mid-bubble, as if the whole world had once been foam and then abruptly gone solid. No sand, no dust, just these interlocking plates and jagged seams running between them like dried riverbeds that never held water.

There is no visible water anywhere. Nothing glints, nothing reflects. Your lips taste of metal, not salt. You realize that the “humidity” you feel is nothing more than the dense press of particulate life in the air.

The plateau stretches in all directions for what might be the size of a large valley or a small country, a broken tableland ringed by even higher escarpments. Those distant walls rise like dark teeth, terraced and undercut, overhangs sagging as if softened by a very slow, dry melt instead of erosion by wind or rain. The whole terrain feels long-settled: no sharp newborn cones, no fresh landslides, just a patient smoothing, a wear that has had ages to decide what shape everything should be.

The sky is a normal brightness, but you don’t know where the light comes from. It is as though “day” has been painted directly onto the air: everything is clearly visible, yet when you look for a sun there is only a bright smear behind a high pall of ochre haze. Shadows are real, but soft-edged; they slide away from your feet as though slightly mistracking the orientation of your body, delayed by a fraction of a second.

The heat is constant and dry, a steady, radiant pressure from below and above. It is not the blistering chaos of a furnace, more the tireless insistence of a hot season that never ends, a thermal equilibrium that has anchored itself and refuses to waver. Nothing here cools quickly. If you set something down, it would slowly come to match the world’s temperature and then simply stay, held in a wide, flat plateau of heat.

The ground is nearly empty of things. No plants, no traces of plants, not even fossil shadows or impressions. The only fixed features are occasional low ridges of fractured glassy rock that twist like frozen waves, and deep, circular pits that drop straight down into darkness. Their rims are rounded, smoothed, as though some very gentle, very patient force has been gnawing at them for geological time.

From within those pits, the sound rises.

It is everywhere at once: a composite roar like an endless, distant industrial city, and yet the land is free of machines. You hear grinding, hissing, a chorus of clacks and clicks, deep vibrational thrums that you feel more in your bones than in your ears. Sometimes it resolves into patterns: a regular, heavy pulse that might be pistons; a long, rising note like turbines winding up; an irregular jagged rhythm of impacts, like a thousand giant gears all missing teeth, catching and slipping and catching again.

But there are no factories, no smokestacks, no roads, no towers. The plateau is bare.

You begin to understand that the industry is alive.

The fauna are not scattered; they are the medium of the place. They drift in the sky, seep in and out of the pits, crawl along the seams between the cracked plates, rise in slow fountains of articulated movement and then subside. They are everywhere, and yet any given cubic meter often appears empty, as if life has chosen to occupy space very sparsely, like a gas.

Near your boots, a filament extrudes itself from a crack, shimmering with heat. It looks like a chain of glassy beads, each bead the size of a seed, linked by threads so thin they almost don’t exist. The beads click softly against each other, then roll forward as the whole assembly snakes along the stone like spilled mercury forced into a line. Where it passes, the rock whitens slightly, etched in fine parallel grooves.

Overhead, vast shapes move, not quite solid. Imagine schools of manta rays made from loose assemblies of hexagonal plates, each plate a living thing, each plate humming. The “rays” glide through the air by adjusting the distances between their components; you can see daylight between the plates, and yet they hold a coherent outline, flexing and banking as if an invisible fluid were supporting them. When they pass directly above, the loudness shifts: the massive chorus from below dips, muffled, replaced by high insectile whines and the ticking of resonant shells.

The fauna’s bodies are more mineral than flesh. Carapaces of heat-dulled metal, jointed spines like chains of ceramic vertebrae, surfaces patterned with vent-holes and ridges that serve as heat exchangers or acoustic organs. They glint only slightly, as though the idea of reflection has been worn away by long exposure to this harsh, diffuse light.

Some larger forms move across the plateau itself, immensely rare but impossible to ignore when they do appear. Take one: a slow runner on dozens of articulated legs, each leg a telescoping column ending in a triad of hooked claws. Above the lattice of limbs hangs a suspended body like a collapsed bridge, a trunk of nested arches and circular cavities. From those cavities drift constant tones. The thing’s “step” is not quick; each placement of a leg takes several seconds, feeling less like locomotion and more like a deliberate reconfiguration of which part of the world it currently occupies.

When it halts near a pit, the tones it produces shift. The roar from below climbs in pitch to meet it, and for a while the two sounds lock together in a single, overpowering chord that vibrates the ground. Then, gradually, both subside; the runner moves on, and the pit returns to its own droning exhalations.

You notice, eventually, that nothing touches nothing without sound. When two components of this world come near — bead-chain to stone, plate to plate in the gliding flocks, leg to crust — their meeting is announced by a click, a hum, a short, sharp ping. You get the unsettling impression that contact is not allowed to be silent, that silence would constitute some sort of category error here.

This is a place almost wholly without liquids, yet its ecology behaves as if something fluid is constantly pooling and flowing — only the fluid is arrangement itself. Creatures congregate into patterns as if following currents you can’t see. Swarms of bead-chains gather into whorls, flattening and aligning until they resemble etched diagrams on the ground. They sit that way for long, slow minutes or hours, a static machine of living links humming at a single pure frequency, then relax, fall apart, and disperse back into individual strands.

Above, the plate-flocks occasionally collide, seemingly by design. Two separate formations intersect and mingle, plates exchanging positions so thoroughly that the original groupings can’t be recovered by eye. You watch this happen far off near the horizon, seeing the sky’s geometry rearrange like a sand painting being wiped and remade. The overall count of plates doesn’t change. Nothing new appears; nothing obvious is consumed. The world’s life is endlessly reconfiguring itself, as if its primary metabolic process is not the conversion of matter or energy, but the slow exploration of an astronomical space of possible assemblies.

Despite the loudness and strangeness, change here is unhurried. The roaring is continuous, but the underlying motions are stately. Even the smaller things, when you really focus on them, are not darting like insects but drifting and clicking together at a measured pace, like the hands of very numerous, very tiny clocks. The density of life is overwhelming in breadth — everywhere you look, some form of organism or quasi-organism is present — but the emptiness between bodies is real and large. Most of the volume is vacancy, hot air laced with faint motes and the residue of previous configurations.

You notice anomalies in how space behaves. The far escarpments seem to move as you shift your gaze. A cliff that lay due east moments ago is now a little north of that, not by optical illusion but by some slip in location itself. The path a gliding plate-flock takes across the sky bends in ways that don’t match the curvature of the world. Once, you see a large runner in the middle far distance raise one leg and put it down nowhere: its foot descends into empty air and yet finds purchase, sinking until the joint is hidden, then stopping, as though the world possesses an invisible step there.

This is not chaos; it is a different order in which position is only partially anchored. The terrain is weathered in the ordinary sense, its forms gently rounded and settled, but the coordinate system that describes it is softened at the edges. Distances feel real up close, but when you try to chain many of them together, they fail to add up cleanly. You could walk in a straight line and come back to where you began without ever turning, not because the world is curved, but because straightness here is a local rule, not a global one.

The fauna exploit this. You see a chain of bead-creatures begin to stretch into the seam of a crack, flowing down, down, impossibly far given the depth of the visible fissure. They don’t compress; they simply keep going, as if the crack is skimming along the outer surface of a deeper dimension and unspooling slack from it. When the chain withdraws later, it is exactly as long as before, and the crack is unchanged apart from a new tracery of grooves.

Time, too, seems to have hierarchies. The creatures’ reconfigurations proceed at one speed — slow, deliberate, looping — while the environment’s grander cycles move at another you can only infer. The plateau, for all its heat and haze, feels anciently stable. There are no sudden gusts, no storms, no fronts; the “weather” is the drone of industry and the very gradual thickening and thinning of the aerial haze, perhaps as swarms rise and fall in unison over long periods.

Now and then, the fundamental rules wobble. You see a patch of air ahead where the light doesn’t behave correctly: objects beyond it are slightly mis-angled, as if you’re looking through a sheet of glass that’s slowly being twisted. A flock of plates banks into that patch and, for a moment, their outlines sheer apart; their leading edges arrive before their trailing ones, their bodies stretching elastically along a direction that isn’t quite any of the ones your senses know. They emerge intact, but with new arrangements, as though the passage through warped space has served as another mixing event.

Down in the deep pits, reality breaks down even further. You edge near one and peer in. There is no clear bottom, only a slow dark spiral composed of countless tiny moving parts, turning and turning with crushing patience. It is not a single creature but millions of them, or perhaps the fragments of something that long ago failed to remain whole and has been trying to recombine ever since. Bands of different texture and tone wind downward: rings of bead-chains, layers of plate-shards, clouds of motes like metallic pollen.

The sound from this spiral isn’t chaotic. It is structured like machinery: there are cycles, feedback, resonance, phases that drift in and out of alignment over what must be enormous timescales. If you listened for centuries, you might hear the full program once. Every so often, a section of the spiral bulges, extrudes a tower of interlocked parts that reaches up toward the surface, trembles there, then collapses back into the well, as if the pit is continually attempting to assemble something that cannot yet stably exist in the more rigid, settled layer of the world you’re standing on.

You see no tools, no artifacts apart from the bodies themselves. Yet the entire landscape behaves as if in the midst of a vast, very slow industrial process — a manufacturing line whose product is configurations of itself. There are no waste piles, no slag heaps: everything is material and everything is participant. Even the most static-seeming rock escarpment bristles with minuscule pores and hollows, each from time to time emitting a whisper-thin extrusion of matter that joins some passing swarm or adds a half-millimeter to a crawling runner’s joint. The world’s “factories” are its living things, and the world’s “workers” are also its machines, and its “output” is a changing map of how they can fit together.

There is a rule underlying the madness: connection is reversible but never meaningless. When two bodies couple — a bead-chain plugging into a runner’s cavity, a stray plate locking into a flock, a spiral tendril contacting the plateau’s underside through some hidden path — the world’s overall pattern shifts by one increment. That shift is not undone even when they separate later. Information accumulates in the slow, global re-weighting of which configurations are common and which are rare.

Over epochs, that must be how the place remembers. There are no fossils because nothing dies into stillness; instead, the history of what has been possible lingers as a bias in what is easy to form now. The longer the plateau exists, the more “in-balance” it becomes, not through stasis but through reaching a saturated familiarity with its own combinatorial possibilities.

You can imagine, then, that the reality breakdowns — the wobbling lines, the non-Euclidean steps, the misaligned shadows — are not glitches but byproducts of an immense, slowly operating search. The world is testing not only new shapes for its creatures, but new alignments for space itself, new timings for causality. It is an industrial ecology for the production of laws: a factory that slowly manufactures the rules by which it will, later, more firmly exist.

Above you, a gliding plate breaks rank. It drifts downward, shedding components that peel away like scales. The falling plates do not accelerate. Instead, each one’s path kinks, stalls, resumes in a different direction, tracing out a jagged, hesitant descent that takes far longer than it should. When a plate finally meets the ground, there is an immense, disproportionate noise — as though it has slammed into an invisible structure rather than the stone itself. The ground under it doesn’t crack; instead, a circular area around the impact point briefly blurs, its geometry uncertain, then re-crystallizes. The fallen plate is gone, but in its place, at the boundary of that circle, faint, hair-thin ridges radiate outward like the spokes of a diagram too large to complete in a single event.

Slowly, the world is building something on scales too big and too entangled in its own spatial oddities for you to perceive. The fauna are overwhelming not merely in quantity but in function: each is a moving tool in a cosmic workshop, with patience measured in aeons, not days. Their “behavior” is the choreography of an infinite assembly line.

The heat never relents. The sound never quite stops. Between the bodies and beneath the noise, the emptiness remains profound — vast, clean gaps of unused space, hollow volumes waiting to be briefly occupied when some configuration requires it. This is a high, dry, almost waterless kingdom of living industry and experimental physics, eroded not by wind or water, but by the constant, grinding reconsideration of what reality here is allowed to be.

And it is in balance, in its own way: a balance not of rest, but of ongoing revision, tuned so that nothing breaks too quickly, nothing changes too fast, and the great work — whatever it is — can proceed.
