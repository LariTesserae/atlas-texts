The light comes first.

It is not sunlight, though it has that same blunt insistence. It pours from the ceiling of the world: a vault of pale matter that glows from within, as if lit by a buried, distant aurora. The light has no direction. It falls from all above‑ward, from a shell of translucent tissue and stone, and the result is a shadowless brightness that feels wrong to any surface‑dweller’s instincts. Everything is illuminated, yet nothing quite casts a proper shadow. There is only a faint deepening of tone around edges, a soft blur where forms meet each other.

This world is below something else—far beneath an unseen crust, yet not in tight tunnels or crushed caverns. It is an underworld measured in continents, a hollow whose scales are too large for intuition to hold: vaulting spaces tens of kilometers high; depressions so broad that a single plant‑structure could be mistaken for a mountain range until one sees that it moves.

The air is cold and thin. Breath mists in the brightness. There is a dryness to it at first inhalation, but it lies: the damp is everywhere, tucked into surfaces and linings, hidden from the open air like a secret. The walls and floor and even the bright ceiling are wet on the inside. Condensation pearls constantly on every exposed face—on pale organic panels, on fibrous arches, on spongy plains—then is licked away, drawn in, or falls in the slightest of drizzles.

Imagine a planet whose crust has swelled and bubbled, then hollowed, and the hollow never fully cooled. Everything here is young in geological terms. Edges are still sharp—cliffs uncrumbled, cracks unweathered—yet almost none of it is rock in the conventional sense.

The land is plant.

Not “covered in plants.” Plant is the medium, the bedrock, the main material. What passes for terrain is a vast interlocking body of living tissues and dead structural mass, a world‑sized rhizome that has not chosen one form but ten thousand at once.

In one region, the “ground” is a loose, pale mesh, like roots magnified to the scale of towers, braided into a web that runs in all directions. Each strand is thicker than a building. Between them: gaps, pits, shafts descending into deeper layers of the vegetal crust. Some bridges have formed—living knots where strands grew toward each other and fused, creating broad, flat causeways. Others are new and fragile, skinning over voids with translucent membranes that quiver when something heavy crosses.

Elsewhere, it shifts into plates: huge, overlapping laminae of semi‑woody tissue, stacked like geological strata but still flexible. They ripple in slow waves; when tension builds, they crack with sounds like distant thunder, then knit together again with pale scar‑growth. Condensed droplets stand along their ridges in rows, trembling, sliding down to reservoirs below.

There is a forest here, but the word is wrong. Instead of trunks rising from ground to ceiling, columns of vascular tissue squeeze downward from the luminous shell and pierce the lower mats. Some are hollow, sky‑high siphons moving sap and vapor instead of air. Some are latticed, the interior a honeycomb of vertical channels lined with photosensitive cells, drinking in the omnidirectional glow. Between these pillars, long curtains hang: fronds and veils, sheets of narrow leaves dangling for kilometers, waving infinitesimally in internal pressures and distant tremors.

Roots, stems, leaves, bark—all the categories blur. Thick, knuckled “roots” hang from the upper vault and never quite find a floor, suspended in vast emptiness, their tips blooming into nets that catch stray spores and dust. “Trunks” burrow sideways through the air, forming arches and tunnels, then dive down and spread out into plates of tissue that take the place of soil.

The brightness from above is constant. Day does not turn to night. Instead, there are variations in hue and intensity—a slow, tidal cycling of the glow that passes like weather across the ceiling and through the plants’ bodies. Sometimes the entire vault glows a harsh blue‑white, as if the world were plunged into ice; sometimes it warms to a faint green‑gold, and the tissues saturate in color, sugars coursing more quickly through veins the thickness of rivers.

There is water, but not in easy lakes. It is in pockets: hanging cisterns grown from cup‑shaped membranes that swell and drip; capillary basins where condensation gathers, ringed by spongy lips that sponge it back down as quickly as it forms. In some depressions, the plates of plant‑stone dip low enough that liquid can accumulate into true pools—flat, dark lenses that mirror the unreal ceiling. Film grows on them immediately: slicks of micro‑plant, delicate mats of tissue that seem to be both leaf and root at once. They thicken, bear weight, then break apart, drifting as rafts to new pockets to colonize.

Streams here are not cuts through soil. They are veins that have chosen to open. Along the slopes of certain ridged structures, narrow channels form where two plates meet. Condensation flows by the smallest of gradients, gathering into rills that shine, then vanish as a thirsty pore or searcher root drinks them down. Where liquid manages to escape into longer courses, it soon finds itself plunging off the edge of some hanging shelf in a tight, cold waterfall that falls, and falls, and then simply mists into invisibility before it ever reaches whatever lies below.

The scale is so great—and the air so relatively empty of particulate—that sound barely carries. Distances swallow noise. A crack that marks the splitting of a hundred‑kilometer plate rolls outward as a muffled groan that takes minutes to pass and is then gone, leaving a hush so complete that one can hear the tiny ticking of droplets forming, the slow squeeze of sap in nearby tissues, the soft tearing as something roots deeper.

Fauna exist, but sparsely, like punctuation marks on a page mostly written in green and pale. They are small against the enormity of their backdrop, and spaced so far apart that each species has evolved for solitude.

From a ledge—a rib of plant‑stone jutting out over a void—you might see one. A pale shape gliding in the light: not truly flying, for there is no great atmosphere to ride, but swimming along invisible gradients of humidity, following slow exhalations from vents in the larger plant‑masses. Their bodies are leaf‑thin, stretched membranes stiffened by trace metals scavenged from nodules in the living substrate. They tilt and spiral, sipping from droplets suspended on webs of filaments, and when they rest, they plaster themselves flat against the glow‑lit undersides of plates, vanishing into pattern.

Closer, in the tangle of lower strata, you might disturb something like a lichen‑colony given mobility: bundles of filaments wrapped around flexible cores of cellulose, inching forward by unrolling and reattaching. They graze the living surfaces not with jaws but with exudates, dissolving tiny patches and absorbing the slurry. Each step leaves a faintly healed scar, a different texture on the old growth.

Larger animals exist but are rare and solitary, territories measured in hundreds of kilometers. Perhaps once in a decade a watchful human here would see, passing in the remote distance, a shape like a drifting island: a massive, slowly pulsing form the size of a town, festooned with dangling root‑tentacles. It moves by slow, peristaltic squeezing of gas bladders, following deep nutrient gradients. Epiphytes coat its back; tiny parasitic grazers pepper its shadow. Yet even this is overshadowed by the plant‑body of the world itself. The line between animal and plant blurs here, and perhaps that is the wrong axis entirely.

For all its vastness, the place feels empty. Between the huge, living constructions is sheer space—a hollowness that drinks up presence. In some directions, you can walk (or climb, or creep) for days and see no movement but the slow flex of tissues and the whisper of forming droplets. The emptiness is not silent, but its sounds are small and sparse: a drip into a distant unseen pool; a soft patter as condensation finally overcomes tension and slides from a leaf‑sheet edge; the far‑off crack of a structural readjustment as some root‑column thickens and tears free from an old anchorage.

And yet, it is not static. This is a young world, restless within its coldness.

Plates rise and subside over spans of hours; what was a safe, flat expanse of fibrous ground can buckle upward into a ridge, leaving yawning cavities alongside. Knotted buttresses that seemed as permanent as cliffs can soften, swell with fluid, and slump, re‑knitting themselves in new orientations. Membrane walls thicken, split, and extrude long tendrils that cross empty air to touch other structures, test their surfaces chemically, then either withdraw or fuse, forming new bridges of vascular exchange.

Sometimes, entire regions convulse.

Without warning (for the inhabitants, at least), a swath of ceiling tens of kilometers across dims as its glow shifts from hard white to bruised violet. The columns hanging from it begin to tighten, thickening like muscle contracting. Tension radiates outward through rhizome and plate. Fissures open; vents unplug. Cold vapor billows, briefly making the giant spaces visible as volumes, turning invisible currents into pale rivers of fog. Then a “storm” in the local sense arrives: a violent episode of growth and shedding. New tissues erupt, outracing sound, unfurling kilometers of new surface in a matter of hours. Old layers are sloughed off in ragged slabs that tumble and smash against lower growths, cracking, breaking, exposing pale, wet interiors.

Where these chunks fall, they make their own weather. The impact squeezes air aside and triggers localized condensation. For a short while, it “rains” in a narrow cylinder beneath where they struck: a curtain of cold droplets, glinting in the half‑light, hammered from the very air by the violence of adjustment. Then the tissues below swell to absorb the nutrient windfall; the curtain thins; the temporary “storm” passes, leaving a new topography behind.

Humans—or something close—have left marks here, but they are few, poor in metal, rich in patience.

The primary material is the world’s own body. Where a mass of plant‑stone flows like a frozen wave, someone has chipped shallow steps into its hollow side, just enough for bare feet. The edges weep sap where the living layers were cut; over generations, scar tissue has thickened the steps, turning them from sharp flat planes into curved, polished troughs. Along narrow traversable ridges, rope‑bridges hang, braided from desiccated fiber cords that were once veins or phloem strands. They stretch huge distances relative to the builders’ scale, sometimes vanishing into thin air as they cross chasms without visible endpoints.

Shelters nestle where terraces meet closing plates—little wedge‑shaped spaces where the macro‑plant’s slow growth and shift has created repeating pockets. Here, the people have reinforced the natural membranes, stitching them with cords, patching thin spots with pressed mats of dried tissue. Doorways are more often slits and valves than solid shutters, aligned with the natural flex lines of the structure, so They can open and close as the host‑plant moves.

There are marks on the glowing ceiling, though reaching it is a feat. Thin, fungal ladder‑growths appear in certain regions, almost like a gift or infection: ridged shelves of tissue marching up the side of a column. The people—small, rope‑strong—climb these, carrying tools chipped from denser, woody nodules or from rare inclusions of brittle mineral scraped from where plant‑roots have gnawed actual rock. At the top, carving into the semi‑transparent luminous material, they etch deep symbols: star‑like points, arcs, simple figures of their own bodies holding curved lines. To them these are records, prayers, navigation aids—reminders of where the ceiling once shone brighter, or dimmer, at some crucial time.

Their technology works with, not against, the living substrate. They know which membranes can be persuaded to thicken by scoring them in certain patterns, which tendrils will bend toward a point of constant warmth (a small, sheltered chemical reaction fire, fed with oily plant resins), and which kinds of tuberous growths can be coaxed to swell into load‑bearing beams if tied and compressed. Their “metal” is rare and ceremonial: thin flakes of strange, dark material retrieved from deep crevices where the plant‑world’s roots have torn up something older. They hammer these flakes cold into hooks and cutting edges, then lash them to handles of flexible, dried stem.

In this mostly silent world, their few deliberate sounds carry meaning. A stone flake struck against another in a certain pattern is a signal: danger, or safe path, or food. A low hum, produced by blowing across a hollow vine, travels remarkably far along the dense tissues, vibrating in the body of the world itself. The people do not shout; they lean against plant‑stone and sing into it, sending slow pulses of sound along structures that can be heard, faintly, tens of kilometers away by anyone touching the same growth.

The rules of this world are not those of an inert cavern. Gravity pulls downward, but “down” is locally distorted by the massive living structures that have grown by ignoring strict radial symmetry. In some places, tissues climb so high and thick that they create their own microgravity anomalies: the air behaves oddly, lighter vapors clinging to surfaces instead of rising, droplets sliding sideways along invisible gradients. The plant‑body senses these anomalies in ways unknown and grows in spirals and whorls, reducing stress. The people know by trial that thrown objects do not always fall where expected.

Time, too, has its own texture. There is no clear day or night, yet there are cycles: the ceiling’s glow pulses on an arc that may be longer or shorter than any human circadian preference. The plant responds in great metabolic waves—phases of expansion, exudation, and contraction that define “seasons” here. During expansion, growths creep faster; bridges thicken; new membranes appear across gaps, making travel possible where before there was only emptiness. During contraction, tissues become brittle, edges sharpen, and some pathways shear or curl away, abandoning communities who have not moved in time.

Turbulence is thus not just about storms but about predictability itself. Paths are impermanent. Voids open where there were none. Entire micro‑ecologies are occasionally pinched off and set adrift like rafts of root‑laminate, carried by slow convective flows of air and vapor until they snag on some distant mass and fuse.

Weirdness, by human standards, sits mostly in the margins, in small, unsettling details.

There are places where the plant‑world seems to remember shapes it has never seen: archways that mimic ribcages, arrays of nodules laid out in spirals reminiscent of eyes, patches of surface that form something like writing in no language the current inhabitants understand. Few come here; such places feel like someone else’s failed attempt at organization.

In some deep hollows, where condensation is thick and the light is separated into spectral bands by the geometry of membranes, one can see subtle illusions: shimmering forms that move against the grain of expected growth, flickering patterns that look almost like other places superimposed. These are not spirits but artifacts of light in a complex, moist lattice—a physics of caustics and scattering taken to a scale where the brain insists on seeing recognizable things.

Life and landscape are so entangled that to wound one is to reshape the other. A cut in a major vascular column, even a shallow one, sends pressure waves through the entire region; days later, in some remote shelf, pores open or close in response. The people understand this in practice as taboo: never cut too deep here, never burn that kind of tissue, never dam this slow seep. Those who ignore such rules are remembered in stories: not as villains, but as the ones whose careless actions caused an entire valley to sag and split, or a trusted bridge‑root to shrivel, stranding a clan in a starving cul‑de‑sac.

And through it all, the quiet persists. No roaring rivers, no howling winds, no animal choruses. Only the small, ceaseless work of condensation and growth, of cracking and resealing, carried out across a scale that erases drama and leaves only a steady, vast reconfiguration.

Standing on a narrow, fibrous ledge, looking out over a gulf where veils of plant tissue hang like clouds and columns descend from the luminous ceiling like the roots of some inverted forest, you might feel that you are inside a single organism’s unfinished thought. Space itself seems like spare volume in a body that has yet to decide what to do with it.

Everywhere you look, there is potential, not yet resolved. Fresh edges, unsoftened by age. Structures only just settling into their roles. A bright, cold, cavernous greenhouse the size of a world, endlessly rearranging itself, with a few scattered, careful hands trying to learn its moods fast enough to survive within them.
