The first thing is the sound.

It is not a storm, and not machinery, and not quite a voice. It is pressure made audible: a continuous, deafening roar that comes from everywhere and nowhere, as if the air itself is being forced through an invisible throat stretched across the sky. It has no rhythm, no waves; it is a single, unbroken volume that somehow never quite reaches pain, as though something in your inner ear has already surrendered and numbed itself.

Yet the air is thin and cool. You can breathe, but it feels like drawing in the aftermath of lightning: dry, metallic, with a faint, crystalline bite. There is no water here at all. No rivers, no snow, no ice. The coolness has nothing to do with moisture; it is more like the temperature of deep shade inside a cave, or the fading chill of long-lost night.

You stand on a shelf of raw, unfinished mountain.

“Mountain” is only an approximation. The land is violently elevated, yes, but it does not look like anything worn into shape by wind or rain. Everything has an abrupt, newly-peeled quality, as though the crust of the world has been pulled upward in one motion and then left as-is, still in the first moment of its existence.

Faces of stone plunge down all around: sheer planes hundreds of meters tall, their surfaces too smooth for weathering, interrupted only by knife-thin ledges and protrusions like the frozen ripples of a single great shove from below. The rock doesn’t seem to be any one type; it flickers in character as you look, black glassy bands sliding into porous pale stuff, into dense metallic seams that mirror-gleam in the dim light, into translucent layers that seem to glow from within like smoke trapped in crystal. The transitions are not gradual. You trace a line with your eyes along a cliff and it just changes: basalt to sandstone with no sedimentary logic, as if parts of entirely different planets have been spliced together edge-to-edge.

Far below, and far above, and far out to every side, similar structures rise and fall: a labyrinth of colossal upthrust slabs and ridges, some forming arc-shaped walls around hollow basins, others curving and twisting upward in impossible spirals that do not quite obey gravity. The scale is valley-wide, range-wide. You glimpse, in the distance, mountainous arches that start in one direction and end somewhere they could not have reached, given the geometry between.

This is an elevated realm, but not a completed one. You can feel the incompleteness under your boots. The ground is too clean. No loose pebbles, no drifted dust. Just bare, recently-revealed surfaces. Here and there are things that look like fractures or incipient canyons etched into the stone—long straight gouges that simply stop mid-slope, as though the process of being carved out lost interest halfway and never resumed.

Across one such half-born canyon, you see a bridge that cannot decide to exist.

It is a band of matter stretched from one lip to the other, thin as a few beams laid side by side. But its edges shimmer, not in any luminous way—more like the edges of a mirage. Parts of the bridge seem to be slightly out of phase, refusing to settle into a single arrangement: one instant you think you see a set of poles driven into the rock on each side, lashed with cords and planks; the next instant, in the same place, the thing has the smooth look of a single grown extrusion of stone. The shape is broadly the same either way, but its *origin* is undecided.

That indecision suffuses everything.

The vegetation here is lush in its own strange vocabulary, and yet sparse in placement. You can walk long minutes across barren ledge or flank of cliff with no sign of life, your footsteps swallowed by the vast roar, before suddenly stumbling into a pocket of teeming growth clinging to some improbable niche.

The plants—if “plants” is the right word for living architecture that never touches water—are dense wherever they appear. When they find a crack, a seam, a tiny fault, they erupt from it in layered cascades: fronds, tendrils, stalks, veneers, all pressed tight together like a miniature jungle. Their saturation against the bare rock makes them look almost obscene, as though the world is supposed to be mineral-only and something organic has intruded.

They do not look terrestrial. Their bodies shine as if oiled, yet they are not wet. Surfaces catch the scant light in a smooth, waxlike way, but when you touch one, the texture is wrong: not wax, not leaf, not bark. It has the feel of cool, flexible ceramic. Some structures resemble branching coral trees made of jointed porcelain; others form spirals of overlapping plates, like stacked shield-scales, iridescent only when seen from particular angles.

Instead of leaves, many have broad, thin membranes stretched between rigid veins, like sails. These membranes flutter soundlessly (not that you could hear them above the omnipresent roar) in the relentless pressure of the air. Where they meet the stone, they thicken and twist into rootlike anchors. But those roots do not dig down—they splay along the surface, following unseen lines, clustering around tiny veins of metallic mineral or the faintest of planar cracks.

They are not drinking. There is nothing here to drink. Perhaps they are feeding from stresses, from gradients of force in the stone, from the invisible quantities that permeate this place instead of water and soil nutrients.

Occasionally, one of these growths has put forth special structures: bony cups oriented toward the sky, or layered discs that face directly into the nearest canyon, where the air’s pressure-gradient is strongest. Those cups and discs vibrate visibly, on the edge of blur, as if the constant, deafening roar were being caught and strained through them, sieved, metabolized. There are no flowers, no berries, no recognizably reproductive organs. But you can see pale dust—or is it light?—rising slowly from some of them, a faint spray that drifts and curls in ways that do not match the flows of air.

The dimness here is not merely lack of sun. Something about light itself is unreliable. It seems to filter in from directions that do not map to the sky.

When you look up, you see a sky that is not quite a sky but a vault of shifting layers: a low, dark cloud deck hangs very close, following the broken topography like a ceiling sagging over ruin. It has no shapes, just a grain, like dense smoke seen from inside itself. Sometimes a brighter patch suggests some distant, hidden light source above the shroud; sometimes the entire expanse dims together, as if someone were turning down the world’s rheostat.

In those darker moments, faint geometries appear overhead: not constellations, but filaments and frames, glimmering for a heartbeat in the cloud-stuff. Lines that run straight for too long to be natural, grids that almost form, then haphazardly fold into non-Euclidean tangles. They are gone the instant you focus on them.

Yet the land is not entirely unlit. Certain minerals in the cliff faces hold a dull glow, as if they are catching a spectrum you cannot see and surrendering only a meager fraction to your eyes. Some plant membranes also maintain a low, steady radiance, enough to limn their veins and edges. In hollows and fissures, a persistent dusk prevails, no matter what the sky is doing.

From time to time, there are brief escalations of sound: the perpetual roar swelling at the edges of perception into something like a long, low syllable that never repeats quite the same. Each such shift coincides with a strange visual event—distant ridges that appear to move by a fraction of their own width, edges that slip slightly, as though the map beneath the terrain had just been edited. The world rearranges itself, slowly, imperceptibly, except in these tiny betraying moments.

There is no animal life. No flight, no scurry, no cold watchful eyes. The lush growths do not host insects, nor birds, nor any movement except their own slow flexing in the pressure-field. You expect some rustle, some skitter, some small collision of living against living, but nothing comes. The intense roar seems to have emptied everything else from the soundscape.

Once, across a wide ravine, where the far cliff rises like the vertical inside of a broken tooth, you notice a line of regular forms against the rock.

They are too straight to be natural.

As you pick your way along the rim to a better vantage point, the world jolts. Not physically—your feet stay where they are—but perceptually. For a second, the ravine is not a ravine but a flat plane of grayish material marked only by shallow grooves. Then it’s back, with its drop and opposing wall. The interval is too short to interpret, like a single dropped frame in a film.

When your vision steadies, the regular forms resolve: cut stones.

They step up the cliff in a broken staircase, reaching no more than halfway to the top before terminating in sheer face. Each block is roughly rectangular, but none is quite the same size as the next; they look individually shaped by hand, fitted together into a path. The material is not the same as the native stone. It’s lighter, more granular, and flecked with fragments of the luminous mineral you’ve seen elsewhere.

Some of the steps have been partially reabsorbed by the cliff behind them, as if the wall has flowed forward over them like cold wax. One block is nearly swallowed, its outer edge still crisp but its rear half vanishing cleanly into rock that gives no sign of disruption. There is no interface, no boundary; the built and the born have blended.

Higher up on the same wall is something like a niche. It is shallow, a mere pocket in the stone, but smoothed in a way no other surface is smoothed, as if repeatedly touched, polished by palms that no longer exist. Within it, traces of pigment cling: earthy reds, deep charcoal, and a harsh, dry white that reflects the scant light with disproportionate clarity.

The shapes of the pigment are impossible to resolve. They rearrange as you attempt to follow their lines: circles that turn into spirals that turn into meshes of straight segments that insist on being read as maps, or diagrams, or alphabets, but never settle into any of those. Looked at from the corner of your eye, they almost resolve, then slip away again. Your brain keeps saying: “That’s familiar,” and failing to say from where.

You find other traces scattered through the region, always sparse, always isolated.

A ring of stones on a narrow ledge, too evenly spaced to be accident. Their interior is empty. Maybe once they enclosed a fire, but there is no soot, and even if there were, the roaring air would have scoured it ages ago—if “ages” means anything here.

A line of poles standing in a row along the crest of a ridge. They’re not carved so much as chosen and lightly shaped: lengths of some fibrous, pale material—perhaps dead trunks of the ceramic vegetation—sunk into sockets bored into the rock. Each bears a small object on top: a shard of mirror-like mineral, or a thin disk of plant substance hardened into stone, or a latticework of knotted fibers that petrifies on your gaze. Their pattern suggests observation, measurement, or plea. They are all pointed toward one gap in the surrounding mountains, a notch where the sky-cloud’s grain curves strangely inward.

There are no villages, no clusters of dwellings, no streets. Only these single structures or loose collections of artifacts, each with the ambiguity of something half-surrendered back to geology. It feels as though a people came through once, making do with hand tools and simple fabrication—splitting stone, binding plant-material, weaving fibers into stronger cords—but the world itself resisted the idea of permanence. Whatever they raised, the ground did not so much erode as *reinterpret* back into its own language.

You recognize none of their tools, but you can infer them from the marks: straight incisions in the rock walls where something hard and narrow was driven repeatedly to carve a channel; holes bored with a twist that must have taken enormous patience; scratch-areas, as if someone scrubbed at the stone until its surface changed.

Near one such worked surface, the roar of the world feels slightly less than elsewhere. Not quieter—still overwhelming—but fractionally modulated, as though passed through a baffling layer. On the wall, the pigment marks are more nuanced, more layered, and for a moment they stabilize, giving the impression of a diagram of forces. Arrows—no, vectors—showing direction of something that is not quite wind, not quite gravity. Curved lines suggesting the shapes of possible futures of this landscape, diverging from a single moment.

Then your eyes flick away and, when you look back, it’s all confusion again.

The weirdness here is not spectacle. It is structural. Reality itself has seams showing, and they are misaligned.

Distances are mostly consistent but occasionally elastic. You choose a point across a valley wall, estimate its remoteness, begin to walk—and five minutes later you’re somehow closer than you should be, or just as far away. Not always. Just often enough to erode your sense of scale. Your own body never feels stretched, never distorted. It’s the map between points that flickers.

Edges fail to meet correctly. You walk along the crest of a ridge and notice that, viewed from one angle, the ridge slopes gently downward toward a saddle; from another angle, that same stretch is clearly higher than the land at both ends. Move along it, and it is simultaneously both. Your feet accept it; your sense of geometry does not.

Perspective behaves as if updated by a slow, lagging hand. A distant spire of rock maintains its appearance as you circle around it, only correcting to whatever its “actual” shape is after an invisible decision is made by the world. Sometimes that correction happens not at all, leaving you holding two conflicting models in your mind: one where the spire is a leaning pillar, one where it is a curving arch. Both fit your memories of moving around it. You could draw either one and call it true.

Despite these violations, change is slow. The raw geology is not actively rising or shattering under you; the plants are not leaping forward in growth. Whatever process is “under construction” here is operating on long, patient cycles. The noise of the place—the enormous, omnipresent roar—is like the sound of a machine running in low gear, its motion too gradual to see.

Perhaps that is the sound of the world cohering.

You become aware, little by little, that the roar changes slightly depending on where you stand. Not because of echoes—there are none, sound is too full for that—but because different regions of space have different underlying “tones.” You can walk along a cliff and feel the texture of the roar shift from a full, rough growl to an almost smooth hum, though the overall volume never wavers. The vegetation seems thickest where the underlying tone is roughest, as if they grow at the edges between these domains.

At such edges, thin seams cross the stone, not like cracks but like the faint outlines of overlapping plates. Where two “tones” meet, the rock sometimes visibly misaligns by a fraction of a millimeter, producing micrometer steps that your fingertips can feel. Running your hand along one, you sense that on one side gravity is tilted by an immeasurable angle, on the other side the tilt is different. The difference is too small to walk differently on, but enough to make stacked stones here slump in paradoxical ways, as though they are being gently pulled in contradictory directions.

Perhaps that is why built structures never last. They are asking to be coherent in a field that is not yet finished deciding the rules of coherence.

There is no noticeable weather. No rain, no snow, no storms in the usual sense. The cloud-vault overhead churns at one slow speed, its grain endlessly recombining. Small particulate never falls from it. Yet sometimes, across entire ridges, the light dims not because of cloud-thickening but because those distant, glimmering geometries overhead have become more solid, as if another layer of framework has slipped between the world and whatever lies beyond. During those intervals, the plants’ dim glow brightens slightly, as if receiving more nourishment.

Then, gradually, the frameworks thin and fade. Light returns to its default twilight. Nothing jumps; everything eases from one state to the next without sharp boundaries—except in your memory, which insists on slices and moments.

Down in the deepest hollows—the places where the raw canyon floors curve away and vanish into murk—the world seems even less committed to a single arrangement. You peer over an edge and cannot tell whether the depths are ten meters down or a thousand. The canyon floor glimmers faintly, like the surface of a perfectly still liquid, but you know there is no water. Sometimes that glimmer resolves into a tessellation of polygons, like a crystalline crust. Sometimes it looks like bare rock shot through with glowing veins. Sometimes, as you stare, the bottom *approaches* without any sense of motion from you or the walls: features grow clearer, their scale increasing, until you realize that what you thought was far away is now just below your feet.

Blink, and it’s far again.

Near some of these unfixed depths stand small constructions—stacks of flat stones, each separated by thin rods made from plant-fiber hardened to near stone. They resemble altars, or possibly instruments. On top of one stack sits an object: a bowl or lens of translucent material, filled with nothing visible, yet slightly distorting the ragged edge of the canyon when seen through it. The distortion is not magnification or bending; it’s more like a version of the world where the noise has been turned down.

Stare through that bowl and the roar dims to a low murmur, not physically—but in how your mind receives it. The canyon’s instability becomes less; the bottom selects one version of itself and holds it for as long as you maintain your gaze through that thin medium. The vegetation beyond looks simpler, more solid, less iridescent.

When you lower your head and see with naked eyes again, the full confusion returns, rushing back like un-dammed water, except there is no water here, only the flood of unresolved possibilities.

You begin to suspect those simple technologies—stacked stones, poles, hardened plant-fibers, pigment diagrams, translucent bowls—are all oriented around one struggle: not survival in the usual sense, but coexistence with a reality that has not fully crystallized.

These people, whoever they were, did not build with engines or metals. Their tools were simple: lever and wedge, cord and brace, grindstone and pigment. Yet their structures are placed at key junctures: at tone-boundaries of the roaring field, at edges of spatial elasticity, overlooking depths where distance refuses to hold steady. Primitive craft applied with surgical sophistication, as if they understood the skeleton of the world better than you, and used only what their hands could directly shape to brace it, watch it, soothe it, or, perhaps, petition it.

All that remains of them are these isolated marks and objects, half-absorbed.

Everything else is stone, un-weathered and unsettled; and the strange, ceramic vegetation; and the vast, continuous noise that never once drops to silence. No birds to scream against it. No animals to test its boundaries. No insects to fill the microspaces. Only the slow, patient negotiation between matter and law.

Far off, beyond a series of interlocking ridges, you see something like a tower.

It rises from a high, flat shelf near the lip of a drop-off so deep that even in this place of noncommittal distances you know it is extreme. The tower itself is slender and faceted, not quite vertical. It has the look of grown crystal, and yet there are ledges, protrusions, and shallow external grooves that give it an almost carved aspect.

Between each facet runs a faint line of brighter glow, as if the tower’s edges are lit with the same dull energy that fills the mineral seams elsewhere. Around its base, clustered densely, are the largest concentrations of vegetation you have seen—thick copses of ceramic fronds and translucent plates that twist upwards as though in support or supplication.

Even from here, you can feel that the roar is different around that tower. It is not louder, but layered; multiple notes stacked on one another, a chord instead of a single tone. The air between you and it seems less prone to jarring shifts. The ridges that lead toward it line up more sensibly, as if the rules of perspective gather their strength along that path.

Perhaps the tower is natural, a geologic experiment. Perhaps it is the only truly built structure here, made by those vanished hands over generations. Or perhaps it is something in between, a focus around which reality has tentatively chosen to cohere. Whatever it is, it feels like an anchor.

Yet even that anchor, you suspect, is precarious. In a place where erosion has barely begun, where dust has not had time to exist, where ridges can decide to lean a different way between one moment and the next, no anchor is permanent.

Here, the landscape is in the long, patient act of becoming. Not forming mountains from erosion and uplift, river and glacier, but forming *the very idea* of mountains, of up and down, of near and far. Everything—the raw cliffs, the living ceramics, the half-swallowed steps, the humming seams in the stone, the dark cloud-vault fretted with ghostly frameworks—is part of that embryonic negotiation.

And through it all, the deafening, inhuman roar continues: the sound of a world arguing itself into existence, so slowly that lifetimes of its former inhabitants (if they still exist, somewhere off-stage) would have passed without seeing a single stone fall, or a single ridge truly settle where it is now.

You stand on bare, unfinished height, in thin, cool air, in the faint, shifting light, surrounded by sparse islands of lush, alien growth and the faint ghosts of a primitive craft that tried to talk to the bones of this place. There is no water, no animals, no true silence. Only this: a landscape poised somewhere between dream and decision, its reality still in the process of choosing what it will eventually have been.
