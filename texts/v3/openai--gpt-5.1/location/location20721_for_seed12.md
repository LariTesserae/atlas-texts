The first thing is the weight of the water.

It is everywhere, not as a flat sea but as a world‑deep flood held in the bones of the sky. You are high, impossibly high, yet there is no clear “up” that leads to air. The elevation is not a mountain rising from an ocean; it is more like a mountain swallowed by ocean, its upper shoulders still faintly structured, everything above and below blurred into a continuous, slow medium.

Light falls here in a dim, sedimented way. It is neither night nor day, but a permanent late‑glow—like dusk translated into liquid. Pale bands drift down from somewhere far above, breaking around the contours of half‑formed stone and the vague silhouettes of structures. The color is not exactly blue, not exactly green: more a thick, metallic dusk‑color, as if the last light of a sunset had been dissolved and left to steep.

The stone itself feels new.

Surfaces are smooth in great planes, as if freshly cracked from some deeper mantle, without the usual signatures of age—no long scars of rivers, no softened slopes of wear. Edges are clean where cliffs drop into darker chasms, or where shelves jut and then suddenly end in nothing. What passes for erosion here is embryonic: a faint pitting, a beginning dust of silt, a few channels so shallow they only slightly redirect the slow drift of suspended particles.

Despite the flood, you can tell: you are high above some deeper basin. The pressure is substantial but not crushing—enough that your own body, if you had one, would feel gently compressed, the way deep breathing feels when lungs are held just a bit too full.

Across this young stone, life has grown as if in defiance of timing.

Vegetation is lush, but in aquatic forms. Vast fronds cling to the raw rock, unfurling in sheets like banners half‑caught on rough edges. Some are translucent, layers of pale green and amber veining, overlapping so densely that the rock beneath is only a suggestion. Others are darker, almost black, absorbing what little light filters down. Every exposed face of stone that isn’t actively crumbling is gripped by growth.

Long strands trail into the open water, creating thick curtains between ledges. They sway slowly, not with any strong current, but with a gradual planetary shift—an almost tidal pulse so weak it might take hours to notice a change. These curtains mark spaces the way walls would: separating one hollow from another, one broad ledge from a neighboring, slightly deeper shelf.

Between fronds, other structures emerge. Bulbous nodules the size of a fist or a head cluster together, connected by thin, fibrous cords that vanish into the rock; they pulse faintly, not in a sharp rhythm, but in a long inhalation‑exhalation over many minutes. Filmy mats spread in layered sheets over any horizontal surface, trapping fine grains of fresh sediment, building up soft, cushion‑like terraces. Pale, root‑like threads penetrate cracks, prying the newborn stone into crumb.

It smells—if smell could exist here—of mineral and faint rot, of things just beginning to decompose in a place that has only recently learned what “decompose” means.

The scale of the place is not planet‑wide, but much larger than a single cavern. You can drift for long minutes over the same continuous escarpment, the same broad plateau dotted with low pinnacles. It feels like a drowned high valley or a chain of elevated plateaus now all under water: a regional landscape, unified by this single, overwhelming element.

And within this newly flooded height, there is a city.

You do not find it as a sudden skyline; it emerges gradually from the rock, because at first, the built things look almost natural. Then repetition reveals intention. Here, blocks of stone have been split on lines too straight for accident; there, a series of flat, parallel ledges descend a cliff like steps meant for beings heavier and surer than the few creatures that drift nearby.

The materials are the same as the surrounding geology—no imported metals, no shining alloys. Dark, young stone, broken, stacked, wedged. Slabs have been leaned into triangular shelters against overhangs, their edges sealed with some kind of resinous plant matter that has since swollen and blurred. Pillars are carved not as smooth cylinders but as faceted posts, each edge slightly fluted from the creator’s tool.

Tools, here, are stone and bone, perhaps shell. You see marks: repeating grooves where something scraped or chiseled, a bevel too regular to be wind‑work. The “technology” is primitive in materials but elaborate in pattern. Ropes made from braided plant fibers still cling to some structures, swollen and fuzzed but not yet rotted through—evidence that this flooding is not geologic‑ancient but recent in the life of the city.

The urban spaces cluster on the broader ledges. Imagine a town built along the terraces of a high canyon wall, now submerged. Low walls define enclosures—courtyards, work‑yards, narrow lanes. Simple corbelled roofs create spaces just barely tall enough to shelter something from what once must have been rain, sun, or wind. Now, water moves through every doorway.

The density is startling: structures almost touch, leaving tight vertical slots like chimneys that now funnel the faint, upward‑creeping plumes of warmer water from geothermal vents below. Ladders—once lashed from bone and beam—hang off ledges, draped with algae and soft, wafting growth. Some have collapsed and drifted down, half‑buried in the softening mud at slope bases.

It is quiet.

Sound travels far in water, but there is very little to make it. No constant roaring current, no smashing waves. Just a low, omnipresent murmur: the minute crackling of forming rock, bubbles slipping from hidden fissures, the papery rasp of fronds brushing one another as they sway. Sometimes, a distant thud—some block finally giving way, tumbling down a sheer face and striking others in slow, muffled impacts that reverberate for long seconds before fading.

Fauna are rare, and thus each encounter is vivid.

Now and then, a long, jointed creature like an eel made from translucent cartilage passes along the bottom of a vertical crevice, its thin limbs folded close against its body. Its eyes are not eyes but small, milky hollows ringed with sensitive frills. It does not so much swim as undulate, feeling with its whole length.

Near one ruined entrance, something more massive hovers: a turtle‑like being with an irregular, stone‑encrusted shell, its head small and pointed, eyes banded with a milky film that suggests it only half cares about vision. It moves rarely, more like a floating boulder gently re‑positioning with a change in microcurrent.

Smaller presences dart and vanish: finger‑length fish with semi‑transparent bodies and golden internal organs; clusters of tiny crustaceans clinging in fur‑like mats to shaded corners. They seem to feed on the soft film that coats every unloved surface, grazing and leaving behind cleaner patches that slowly fur again in the muted light.

Most of these creatures do not enter the densest parts of the city. Those interior corridors are strangely empty, occupied instead by still water and the densest mats of plant‑life, some of which seem adapted specifically to these sheltered spaces. Filaments reach up and then dangle, each tipped with a faintly luminous node no larger than a seed. They collectively give some corridors an internal glow, a subtle star‑field effect that only stands out because the outside light is even dimmer.

The rules of existence here feel slow and tipping toward permanence. Change happens, but not quickly. The flooding has already happened; now comes the patient re‑negotiation between stone, water, and life. Blocks that were jammed together in hasty walls are settling under the steady weight of waterlogged mortar and invading roots. Gaps widen a hair’s breadth each season; ceiling stones sag a millimeter over a decade. You sense that if you came back much later, entire terraces would have slumped into rubble and become just another step in the canyon wall.

The weirdness slips in at the edges, where you try to understand what this place used to be and cannot make it match any familiar world.

For one, the “city” is entirely without openings to a surface. No windows that clearly faced open air, no soot staining interior ceilings, no obvious chimneys. Doorways face sideways into what are now vertical channels or horizontal ledges, but nothing looks like a street wide enough for sky‑traffic. It is as though this place was built in expectation of enclosure, even before the water came—meant for a world of caverns and slot‑valleys, not for vast open plains.

And some of the built forms are not quite practical in any straightforward sense. On several broad shelves, tall, narrow towers rise, delicately stacked from flat stones decreasing in size as they ascend, like inverted staircases. They are not habitable—too slender, no inner space. Many have toppled, but a few remain, needle‑like, festooned with clinging vegetation. Their purpose seems almost ritual or aesthetic: markers, signals, or simply an ingrained urge to move stone upward despite no sky to reach for.

More puzzling still are the hollows carved into the new rock: shallow basins, perfectly smooth inside, each ringed with concentric etched lines. They are located at intersections of paths, on ledge corners, at meeting of walls. Now they hold only still water and drifting specks, but their original function is left hanging. They suggest an attention to cycles—of liquid, of light, of something—long before this all‑engulfing flood.

There is an underlying hint that the flood was not purely natural. The stone carries occasional unnatural geometry: patterns of right angles cut deep into cliff faces and then left unfinished, as if an attempt had been made to score the world into shapes and then abandoned. Some of these grooves run inward and down, toward places where creamy bands of new mineral are forming, faintly warm to the touch. It is as though earlier inhabitants began to awaken the stone itself—to channel heat, to draw out material—then were overtaken by the rising water, leaving their half‑bored arteries to weep mineral into the flood.

The sense of time here is thick.

Nothing hurries. Sediment falls like dust through liquid dusk, collecting in corners, softened by plant growth. New vents open with geologic slowness, releasing warmer plumes that encourage local blooms of more exotic fronds: ones with fine, feathered tips that catch chemicals instead of light. Whole communities of organisms live on gradients of temperature and mineral, their lives playing out in slow choreography.

If there are still thinking beings here, they are scarce and cautious.

Maybe, once in a long interval, a shape appears along a high parapet of stone: a silhouette against the dim stream of light above. Something bipedal, heavy‑boned, moving with the exaggerated care of an air‑dweller still awkward in water. Hands brace on rough-hewn railings as it looks out across its drowned quarter. It leaves behind ghost‑swirls of displaced water that take an age to blend back into the background stillness.

Its tools, if any, are strapped against its body: a long haft of bone or hardened wood, tipped with chipped stone, wrapped in fibers. It may use them now not to cut wood or skin animals, but to pry loose stubborn encrustations, to harvest slow, mineral‑fed growths that serve as food, medicine, or pigment.

Farther within, the oldest thought‑spaces might have been repurposed entirely. What were once communal halls—low, wide, supported by squat pillars—are now sealed from open water by layered mats of woven plant. Inside, the water could be different: warmer, clearer, seeded with deliberately chosen life. A garden in a reservoir, tended over slow generations. A refuge from the vast indifferent flood outside.

Above everything, the sense of height persists, even though you are submerged. Every glance “up” reveals only more water until the dim settles into near‑black, but the shape of the terrain, the arrangement of the city, the direction of the faintest currents all whisper that somewhere below, the world falls farther—into trenches or basins yet more drowned and more foreign.

Up and down, high and deep lose their usual meanings here. What you feel more strongly is newness and incompleteness: the world in mid‑gesture, as if you had walked into a room just after someone overturned a great vessel of water and left, and now all the furnishings, all the inhabitants, all the tiny processes are slowly, patiently, learning what it means to live in this permanent, quiet, half‑lit flood.

Nothing is finished. The stone is still becoming landscape, the city is still becoming ruin, and the life that has layered itself over both is still inventing what sort of world this will ultimately be.
