The first thing is the sound.

It is not the sound of water, because there is none. It is not wind, because there is almost no free space for air to move through. It is a dense, continuous mechanical thunder: deep grinding, steady humming, the toothy clatter of meshing cogs, the distant roar of something huge turning very slowly and never stopping. The noise is so constant, so layered, that it feels like the air itself is thick with vibration, as if silence were never invented here.

You stand in a canyon that is also a lung, a machine, a throat.

The walls rise steeply and close, almost vertical, so high that the upper reaches are only suggested: dark planes, ribs of stone, and beyond that a persistent low glow, as if a sun is being strained through kilometers of rock. The canyon is narrow, more a fissure than a valley, only a few meters across at its tightest points. It twists, but even where it widens, it never quite opens; you always feel inside something.

Everything is cold. Not the bitter, biting cold of ice, but a still, mineral chill: the sort that lives short of freezing, where metal feels unfriendly to the touch and your breath never turns to fog only because the air is too dry. The air tastes of dust and unfamiliar solvents—oily, metallic, perhaps faintly sweet like antifreeze, but there is no water vapor at all.

At your feet is not soil. The ground is a compacted mosaic of things that never knew water: crushed mineral plates, splintered ceramic, torn fragments of resin and glassy slag. It has the look of a dry riverbed that never carried a river, only slow avalanches of debris. Between the fragments is a finer dust that does not cake or clump; it behaves like a gas slowed down, shivering minutely with the constant vibrations of the canyon.

From this ground rises vegetation—dense, impossibly dense, packed so closely that nothing like a path exists unless the machinery has made one. But these are not plants in any familiar sense. They are dry, crystalline, and metallic, yet they have the unmistakable logic of growth.

Imagine forests composed of latticework and blades:

• Columns of matte-black filaments, bundled like bamboo but faceted, each stem a prism that splits the dim ambient light into muted, oily colors.  
• Fan-shaped arrays of thin, rigid leaves that clink together like chimes when a distant machine throbs just right.  
• Cushiony mats of interlocking scales—leaf-analogs—that are not soft at all, but layered sheets of a resinous mineral, each plate articulated to flex by micrometers.  
• Thorn-thickets of hollow, glassy rods the color of bruises, tipped with porous nodules that exude faint vapor—non-aqueous solvents distilling slowly into the dry air.

Every surface that can be colonized is colonized. The canyon walls are hidden beneath bands of lithophyte growth: encrusting “mosses” of microspikes, veins of crystal “vines” that plunge into cracks, climbing frames of metallic tendon that knot around outcrops. Even the exposed machinery—where it breaks through the living crust—is filmed with thin biofilms of iridescent dust: microorganism-equivalents, dry as ash, living in the electric fields bleeding off the structures.

There is no green. Color comes in deep oxides, dull metallic blues, pale lilac sheens on surfaces under tension, smoky amber blooms where something has recently grown quickly and then stopped forever in a stable form. The lushness is not of softness and leaves; it is the maximal occupation of space by forms optimized to grow where no water flows—prismatic branches, porous sponges of graphite-like tissue, dendritic microstructures making the most of every cubic centimeter.

Nothing moves by its own will. No animal stirs. No wings beat, no insect skitters. There is no chirp, no rustle of bodies. The only motion is imposed: vibration, flex, slow settlement.

The rules here seem clear: chemistry is non-aqueous and terribly conservative. Everything that came to a workable balance long ago has stopped evolving in form, entered a frozen equilibrium. The whole scene has the calm of great age: not lifeless, but finished. Whatever grows now does so by repeating perfected patterns.

The canyon’s extremity is vertical: sheer mineral on one side, a wall of vegetation-mineral composite on the other, and, cutting through them both, the immense presence of built structures.

The built world here is old and mostly buried in the growth it helped sustain. You see it in partial arcs of corroded alloy, in the clean straightness of beams that disagree with geology’s curves, in the geometric regularity of voids where something once slid or rotated. Pistons the width of tree trunks extend into the walls and disappear into the undergrowth of metallic ferns and spines. Segments of toothed wheels—each tooth taller than you—are half-embedded in a petrified tangle of growth, their once-sharp edges worn smooth by slow abrasion.

These machines are not scattered like independent artifacts; they are components of a single great mechanism that fills the canyon like a spinal column and ribs. But only fragments are visible: here a segment of track emerging, there a massive cylinder whose surface crawls with hexagonal plates of lithic vine. No moving part you can see is actually moving—visibly. The motion is deep within, transmitted as continuous tremor and sound.

The air, dim as it is, glows faintly from multiple sources:  
• Strips of ancient, perpetually dim phosphorescent material along buried conduits;  
• The slow, steady red of internal components glimpsed through narrow vents;  
• Biological phosphors, not bright but persistent, in some of the crystalline flora, like veins of firefly light frozen into rigid structures.

Because the canyon is so narrow, light seldom arrives directly. It scatters off surfaces, is split and softened by prismatic growths, is swallowed by matte black lichens that feed on radiative energy. The visual field is all gradients and suggestions, edges outlined by dull halo instead of sharp shadow.

In this dimness, perspective compresses. The closeness of everything—the packed density of growth, the clutter of built remains, the high enclosing walls—removes any sense of horizon. There is nowhere to look “out” to; there is only more “in.” The landscape is an interior, a pocket world nested within something vaster that you can’t quite imagine.

There is no sky, not in any conventional sense. Overhead, the fissure narrows to a jagged line where not even the milky glow from above can directly enter. In some places, the canyon’s ceiling is fully closed by overgrowth and structural arches. There, the sensation is almost subterranean, but the air quality and faint currents suggest that above the ceiling there are still spaces, perhaps many stacked layers of similar canyons and machinery.

The vegetation is in balance—not expanding, not receding. Every structure bears the marks of long-established erosion: rounded edges, surfaces colonized up to the limit of tolerance, broken parts stabilized by new growth that uses them as scaffolds. Fallen beams are netted by crystalline creepers that have long since immobilized them. Old fracture lines in the canyon walls are filled with mineral “root” systems that long ago reached equilibrium with the stresses in the rock. Nothing looks recent; nothing looks about to change.

And yet everything is loud.

The loudness is paradoxical in such a still place. It comes primarily from below and within. Somewhere beneath the canyon floor, enormous machinery turns slowly. The cycle might be measured in minutes or hours, but to the structures around it, it is constant. The resulting bass is more felt than heard in your chest: a low, continuous droning thrum. Superimposed on it are the more mechanical sounds of rotation and contact: a deep grinding like stone rolled over stone; a faint but insistent clank at regular intervals from some long lever reaching its end of travel; the slow squeal of metal under steady load.

The “plants” respond passively: their hard foliage chimes and scrapes; vibrations propagate as a kind of mechanical breeze. Filamentous growths tremble, not because they choose to, but because the world shakes them forever. The result is an accidental orchestra: a canyon of ringing, rattling, buzzing vegetation that amplifies the internal machine’s music into a complex, chaotic roar.

Still, nothing is evolving. This is not a vibrant, rapidly adapting ecosystem; it is one that found an invariant niche—tapping stray energy, feeding on trace aerosols of exotic solvents, metabolizing slow exhalations of the engine—and then settled. Erosion is not destruction but maintenance: old surfaces powdering into new substrate, then freezing into the same forms again.

Particles drift in the dimness, suspended not by convection but by electrostatic repulsion and the constant micro-shaking. Tiny shard-flakes of broken crystalline tissue, metallic dust, polymer pollen of a sort that never expects to be carried by an animal, only redistributed by vibration and slow, ballistic leaps as machines punch the ground from below. Some patterns of dust accumulation look deliberate: fine linear drifts along protrusions that may function as collectors, feeding chemical gradients back into buried rootlike networks.

The textures of the world are all hard. The few “soft” spaces are illusions: mats of fine filaments that look like cushions but resist like steel wool; hollows filled with dust so stable it holds its shape like sculpted stone. You could lie down here and be cradled by no give at all.

Everywhere, you see the stratigraphy of time expressed as layering of growth over machine over rock, repeated. In some cross-sections, an exposed wall reveals: native bedrock; a horizon of ancient fractured alloy—an old conduit crushed by tectonic pressure; then a layer of dense vegetative crystal that once colonized that metal; then another band of structural material, perhaps a later reinforcement; then newest of all, a thin crust of present-day growth—identical, in pattern, to the one far below.

The world’s rule seems to be: cycles, but no development. Repetition without iteration.

Even the industrial aspect is strange. There are no visible control systems, no clear interfaces, no signs of recent maintenance. The remaining structures were built for scale, to move great masses over long times, but now they serve as permanent underpinnings. If there was once a purpose—pumping unknown fluids, stabilizing crust, processing minerals—it has collapsed into perpetuated motion, an endless task whose original intent has been lost but whose continuation is self-sustaining.

Some structures betray design choices optimized for a different environment: drain-like channels that have never seen water but may once have guided some other liquid; heat-exchanger fins now colonized by radiator-plants that sip their waste energy; anchor pylons that pierce deeply through the canyon floor, suggesting that this fissure is just a glance into a far larger engineered depth.

No ladders, no walkways; any human-scale accommodations have been buried or eroded into the general mass. The few recognizably anthropic artifacts—perhaps: a railing half swallowed by lithic growth, a row of corroded bolts in a perfectly straight line across an otherwise random face—only underscore how long ago the builders were here, and how complete the takeover has been.

Weirdness, here, is subtle. It lies in the fact that this pocket-world is closed and yet feels like a lung of something vaster, that it is so mechanically alive and biologically complete while remaining utterly dry and utterly static in its grand patterns. It lies in the absence of animals in a place with so much energy, as if the very concept of motile, self-deciding life never took root. Life here is structural, architectural, bound to load-bearing and crystallization, not to behavior.

Occasionally, a sound cuts through the general roar—a sharper report: the brittle crack of a crystal branch finally reaching its yield point and breaking after centuries of flexing; the dull thud as the fragment drops a hand’s span, chimes along neighboring structures, then wedges into a new resting place. This, too, is erosion, and also regeneration. The exposed break will slowly accumulate new growth until it is as rounded and colonized as everything else.

And then: it continues. The canyon remains itself.

If you could somehow visit again millennia later, you would find it almost exactly the same. Some beams would be more buried; some cracks more filled; the overall composition unchanged, the machine still humming, the vegetation still pressed into every feasible gap, the dim light still diffused through dust and crystal.

A place at equilibrium: a dry, cool, impossibly lush mechanical ravine, sealed into the bones of a larger world, saturated with presence, deafening with motion, and yet—at its deepest levels—perfectly, almost unnervingly, still.
