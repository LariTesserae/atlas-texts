Above the tree line, there is no “ground” as such.

What you would expect to be rock — sharp summits, broken scree, plateaus swept bare by wind — is instead a continuous, living fabric: a mat of overlapping stems, bark-thick lichens, woody leaves, ropey roots. The mountains are high, unmistakably so: the air is thin and cool, and the horizon folds away in long distances, ridge after ridge under a sky scratched clear by strong, high light.

But beneath your feet the mineral bones of the range feel unfinished. Where the plant crust parts, you see raw stone: pale, almost soft-looking, etched with only the first hesitant lines of erosion. No deep gullies yet, no weather-worn faces — just faint channels where water has started to choose a path. The whole massif feels newly lifted, shrugged upward from the planet’s interior not so long ago in geological terms, its flanks still learning how to shed rain.

Water is everywhere and nowhere at once. There are no broad lakes, no roaring rivers carving their authority into the landscape. Instead, the moisture rides just beneath the surface: beads along root-clusters, glossing the undersides of leaves, sweating from stone like condensation on cold glass. After every brief, bright shower, the entire slope weeps. Droplets gather along fibrous ledges of living tissue and fall with soft, uneven ticks into narrow, vegetal runnels. These runnels are not quite streams; they are corridors where the plant-mass has thinned into open-walled channels, their beds a pale glimpse of mineral.

In the cool air, this constant evaporation turns the middle elevations into a soft haze. But the light is fierce. It slams down from a high, unsheltered sun and ricochets off wet surfaces and pale, bare rock. Reflected glare makes each droplet into a tiny, blinding lamp. Shade is uncommon; where it exists, it is created not by discrete trees but by layered tangles of vegetation drawn together into vaults and ridges, like buttresses in a cathedral of photosynthetic tissue.

Plants are everything here: groundcover, rock, wall, bridge, sometimes even sky.

There is no clear distinction between moss and tree, shrub and vine. Instead there is a single, continuous organismic city: thick mats that twist into pillars, pillars that flatten into walkways, walkways that fray into curtains. Some masses are tough and barked, bearing the scars of wind and frost; others are almost translucent, swollen with water, drooping like heavy curtains of algae. A ridge you step onto has some give, as if you were walking on an old ship’s hull, its planks softened by damp.

This living crust is not uniformly dense. In many places it thins to a mere black-green felt clinging to virgin stone, or opens into cavities that drop into cool, dim interiors. These hollows are not caves in the classical sense. They are cavities grown by roots pulling away from rock, leaving arcs of open space where condensation gathers and slow trickles of water tap down into shallow basins. In such places, the temperature falls several degrees, and the air smells of mineral dust and sap.

For all the vegetal abundance, the landscape feels strangely empty. The plant-mass reads more as architecture than community: a single, intricate structure with few moving parts. You can stand on a ridge and see far — to distant branches of the range, to high cirques glazed with wet, to shallow saddles where the living skin has not yet fully closed over the rock. Between your vantage point and those distant forms, there are long reaches of almost featureless texture: subtle drifts of color, gentle shifts in height, but few discrete, standing figures.

Life other than plant is rare, and when it appears it seems uncertain about its own right to exist.

A small, six-limbed grazer the size of a hare, patterned like lichen, morphs perfectly into a patch of mottled bark when it freezes. It moves only in very short bursts, as if afraid that too much motion will cause it to be noticed by the place itself. Spindly, translucent insects skate across the slick faces of condensing stone, feeding on microscopic films in the water. Far overhead, once in a long while, a pale-winged shape works the thin air, its call lost in the dominant roar.

Because there is a roar.

The landscape is not loud in the usual way of mountains — not with wind or rivers. The air scratches past with a cold, dry rush at the highest ridges, but it is not the principal sound. The main sound is built, imposed, engineered.

In certain saddles and hanging basins, the vegetation thins or has even been cut away, leaving shelves of bare, newly exposed stone. In these clearings stand machines: tall, skeletal frames of metal and ceramic, angled like tuning forks or the arms of cranes, anchored deep into the young rock. Some have radial fans that turn with slow, implacable steadiness; others are fluted towers pierced with narrow slots. Heavy cables snake outward from them, disappearing back under the plant-crust, their paths traced by the way vegetation avoids or grips them.

These installations do not move quickly, but their operation fills the air.

Massive fans drag air in from the high passes and drive it through resonant cavities, amplifying and shaping its flow. Somewhere within the stone, slow pumps draw up water from the near-surface aquifers, forcing it through vibrating conduits and pressure valves. The structures are arranged into vast, invisible instruments: the whole mountain basin is a kind of organ or turbine-harp.

The sound they make is almost constant, a layered, deafening presence that blankets the range:

– a deep-standing hum, too low to localize, that vibrates in the ribs and teeth  
– long, rising and falling tones like the breath of a colossal, unseen bellows  
– sharp, metallic shivers when some part of the structure adjusts itself a fraction of a degree  

The mechanical voices shift only slowly. A tone might rise half a note over the course of a day. A certain resonance might vanish only to reappear weeks later as water levels and air densities change. Storms add their own harmony, but even they are translated through the great machines into controlled, lingering chords.

Sound, in this world, is engineered as climate.

The industry here is not extraction but modulation. These machines mediate between atmosphere, water, light, and the living mass that carpets the mountains. They regulate humidity by controlling where and when air is cooled or compressed. By altering pressure gradients, they coax clouds to gather or disperse along chosen ridges. Under the crust, slow-acting heating elements adjust ground temperature by small degrees, encouraging condensation in some galleries and evaporation in others.

Their work proceeds on timescales too slow for a casual observer to see. Over years, a valley will green into a thicker, shaggier version of itself as the plant-mass is fed by slightly increased rainfall patterns. Over decades, a particular slope will dry and crack, and the vegetation there will shrink back to a thin felt, making room for a new installation that requires broader rock exposure. In centuries, perhaps the entire character of the range will shift — but at any given moment, everything feels unfinished, in transition.

Around each machine, traces of human or near-human presence cling like lichens.

Small, scattered structures — more shelters and workshops than cities — are tucked into niches in the living crust or bolted directly to the young stone. They are compact, made to withstand noise and brightness and exposure: narrow-windowed bunkers, corrugated shells hugging the leeward side of a ridge, modular crates linked by flexible couplings. Many sit empty, their interior lights long since dark. Some are half-reclaimed by the plant-mass, which grows over edges and into vents, sealing doors with fibrous curtains.

Catwalks of perforated metal or stiffened root have been laid across more treacherous distances, narrow as veins, leading from control sheds to the bases of the towers. In places, the plant-crust has been persuaded to adopt these paths, thickening around their struts, turning them into living bridges: steel ribs inside a vascular sheath.

Power flows are hinted at in small ways. At dusk, thin lines of dull red run along some of the cable paths, heat dissipated from unseen workings. On very clear days, faint halos of ionized air shimmer above the tallest resonators, distorting the high-bright sky. Sometimes, when a machine shifts its operating mode, there is a momentary lowering of the roar, a brief, startling quiet before new harmonics slam into being.

Within this enormous control system, the plants themselves are not passive.

Their growth patterns pare down along the gradients laid out by sound and breeze. Thick mats gather in the acoustic shadows behind major towers, where the air is slightly stiller, moisture pooling like invisible fog. In the direct line of fan-blasts, vegetation hugs the stone tightly, forming glossy, low cushions armored against desiccation. Certain species appear only near particular resonant frequencies: delicate, glassy filaments that wave like underwater weeds wherever a precise, high tone continuously vibrates the stone; flat, ribbed growths that stack themselves along vibrating cables, harvesting the micro-motions as a source of mechanical stimulation.

Over very long periods, the machines and the plant-mass are learning each other.

The roaring installations shift water and airflow; the vegetation thickens or thins in response, subtly altering how that air and water move. The feedback is complicated and slow. Engineers — if any remain — cannot fully disentangle these interactions. Perhaps that is why the tech feels scattered and modest in footprint despite its scale of effect: each new machine is placed cautiously, its influence watched over many seasons before the next one is risked.

The rules of this world run along these lines:

– Gravity and light behave as expected: the sun strikes hard, the thin air grows sharper and colder with height, and the shadows it casts are clean and deep.  
– Geology is young. Fractures are fresh, blocks are angular, and soil is a fledgling film, produced almost entirely by the decay of roots, stalks, and microbial life. Where the plant crust has not yet closed over the stone, you see clean planes and unrounded edges.  
– Water is very near but seldom free. It prefers to live in capillaries: in the pores of stone, in root-tunnels, in small, endlessly replenished hanging pools. When it moves, it does so in thin threads and sheets, rarely in broad volumes.  
– Plant life has occupied every structural role. There is no bare cliff that, left to itself, will not eventually be clothed in tissue. If a ledge stands empty, someone has recently cut it clear.  
– Non-plant life exists, but sparsely, in niches where the roar is weakest and the condensate richest. Its abundance seems constrained more by space in the soundscape than by food.  

Sound is the least forgiving law. Silence is unnatural here, and precious. In the few pockets where the machines’ influence cancels itself — odd corners in acoustic shadow, where phase and geometry conspire to create stillness — the atmosphere feels almost sacred. In such a niche, you can suddenly hear small things: water ticking off a root, the faint crackle of expansion in a warming stem, the almost inaudible breaths of some shy grazer. The plants themselves seem different in these places, their colors slightly deeper, their surfaces less scoured.

Across the region, the scale is mid-range: you can imagine mapping it valley by valley. Each basin has its own arrangement of resonators and collectors, its own pattern of living architecture. One valley might be mostly open, its plant crust low and carpetlike, machines standing stark against the sky like an unfinished refinery. Another might be thick with overhanging galleries of vegetation forming high, airy tunnels around the towers, the great fans only partly visible through curtains of leaf and stem.

Between these engineered hollows, long ridges stretch quietly under the sun. The plant skin is thinner there, less disrupted: just enough to hold moisture and fend off naked cold. These in-between spaces carry the sense of a world still in negotiation, undecided whether it will become all plant or more machine, whether the guiding hand that set up the resonators will continue or has already left.

From some high vantage — a bare spire where even the plant crust has not yet found a hold — you can see the pattern more clearly. Patches of engineered sound marked by towers and fan-blades. Smoother expanses of life-thickened slope. Pale, raw shoulders of mountain where rock is still emerging, barely scored by new-born trickles.

Everything is bright. The stone, the water, the plants’ upper surfaces, even the machines: all catch and throw back the sunlight. Shadows are thin, like ink lines. The cool air does little to soften the glare. If you look toward the sun, the resonator towers stand as black teeth in a field of white, vibrating slightly in the shimmering, humid glare, their deafening song a constant presence in your bones.

It is a quiet world made loud; a sparse world made full by a very particular kind of fullness. High, wet, bright, raw, and patient, it is still becoming itself, inch by inch, hum by hum, under the steady, thunderous breathing of its own machinery.
