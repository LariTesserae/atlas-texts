The place is mostly noise.

Not heard at first as “sound,” but as a solid pressure, like the inside of a great engine, like the hum of a city magnified until it becomes its own weather. It fills the water the way grain fills a silo: every gap is packed. If there were lungs here, they would not so much breathe as vibrate.

You are underwater, but the word “under” is misleading. There is no clear surface, no sky you can point to. The medium is a warm, heavy fluid with the slow momentum of syrup and the optical habits of a fogged lens. It has the refractive shimmer of water, but its behavior is less obedient. It moves in sheets and slabs, in strangely planar tides that pivot and slide past one another along invisible hinges. It shudders with interference patterns from distant, unseen impacts.

The light is dim but restless. There is no sun, only gradients: a deep green-brown in one direction, a faint copper haze in another, and, in between, zones of pulsing, arrhythmic illumination. The brightness comes not from above, but from within—industrial ghosts scattered through the water, machines and structures that shed weak, sickly halos, or short flares that strobe and vanish. You never get far enough away from anything bright enough to be a “source” that you can see it cleanly. There are only hints: a lattice silhouetted, a segment of a pipe, a row of portholes briefly lit, then gone into murk.

The world is low in elevation, yet there are still reliefs. Think of a plain that only just sank, a wide and shallow basin where a continent gave way and went under before it had time to rot down into mud. Fresh angles remain. Ridges still hold their edges. But all of it—what used to be air and stone and soil—is smothered in a new, rushing medium that has not finished deciding what rules it intends to follow.

The ground, where you find it, is young and sharp. Where the fluid clears enough to see, the substrate appears as fresh fracture: black glassy rock rising in keels and fins, like cooled lava snapped apart before it finished flowing. Edges catch the weak light and send back razor-thin reflections. Chasms split abruptly, narrow and sheer-sided, cutting through the strata as if created all at once, not eroded across time but sliced, edges still almost too clean to be real. Some cracks flare faintly at their seams, as if new matter—or new space—is still extruding there.

Vegetation clings to everything that stands still long enough. Lush is the nearest word, but the growth is too dense, too confident, for any garden we know. There are no leaves in the conventional sense, no stems arching toward a sun. Instead, layered mats, curtains, and filigrees of living matter knit the landscape together into a continuous second skin.

Some of it is reminiscent of seaweed, yes: broad, dark fronds that undulate slowly, overlapping like draped cloth, thick as stacked blankets wrapped around ridges and pylons. But when they flex, they do not follow only the currents; there is a deliberateness, a tension to their motion, as if they are actively negotiating with the pressure fields, pulling more tightly around structures, loosening in ripples when some invisible stress passes.

Elsewhere, the vegetation becomes three-dimensional lace—lattices of translucent tubes, branching and rebranching, filling entire cavities with bulbous networks. These swell and contract in silent convulsions, pumping the heavy fluid through themselves, filtering, separating, recombining. They inhale the noise of the world as much as its material; you can see it in the way their membranes jitter, the way their internal channels glow while a nearby generator roars.

On the undersides of overhangs and along the flanks of the sharper ridges, low, thick biofilms coat rock and rusted steel alike, glistening as if perpetually exuding fresh surface. From them rise forests of hair-thin filaments, so dense they become opaque, like black fur. Every strand quivers in place with the subsonic roar of the environment, forming interference fringes of motion that travel through them like ghost-waves, visible but not quite trackable.

There is no animal life. Nothing swims past; no things with eyes or mouths or directional purpose cross the field of view. And yet the place is not still. It is more active than many lively reefs. Life is present, but collapsed into a purely sessile ecology—an entire biosphere committed to staying attached and letting the world move through it. Filters, absorbers, membranes, baffles; every organism is a bracket bolted to existence.

Amid this near-continuous biological saturation, the built world protrudes like an unfinished idea. Structures dot the landscape, scattered and half-hidden. They are not ruins, not yet; they are more like machines caught in the act of being overtaken by something faster than rust.

Picture a series of squat, cylindrical housings, each the size of a small building, arranged with the tentative geometry of a lost industrial park. They stand on stunted legs sunk into the newly broken crust, partly embedded in the crystalline fractures. Cables radiate from them, thick as tree trunks, vanishing into the murk in all directions. The housings spin, or try to: some rotate smoothly, humming with a resonant pulse that the water carries as more pressure than sound; others judder in half-turns, start-stop, their motion arrested by clotted mats of growth that have woven themselves deep into the moving joints.

Elsewhere, articulated frameworks rise from the floor, skeletal superstructures reaching up and then bending, midway, as if pushed by invisible tides that do not affect the surrounding vegetation. These frames carry an armor of plates, sluice-gates, vanes—the hardware of a fluid-management system—but they no longer sort water from water; they sort states of reality. When they open or close, the dim light nearby twitches in color and depth. Distances contract; a distant ridge jerks closer by a fraction, then slides away again.

It becomes clear, slowly, that the “factory” here is not merely industrial in the old sense. It is not manufacturing objects, but circumstances. The engines embedded across this drowned basin seem to be tasked with holding something in place that resists being held. The constant deafening sound is partly mechanical—a roar of turbines, compressors, shuddering conveyor belts—but woven into it is something more desperate: the groan of geometry being realigned, over and over, remade each second against its own momentum.

The noise has layers. The lowest is a continuous, infrasonic thrum, a planetary heartbeat that makes every surface vibrate. Above that, deep rolling crashes echo irregularly through the fluid, as though enormous masses are colliding far off—slabs of matter, or whole segments of this realm’s logic, striking and grinding. On top of that there are higher shrieks, metallic and sharp, as if steel beams are being torqued until they almost fail. All of it is carried perfectly by the warm medium, saturating every point. Nothing is truly quiet. Even in apparent stillness, any tendril, any fragment of debris, shakes microscopically.

The turbulence is not only physical. The medium itself behaves inconsistently. A rushing current will surge past, tossing silt and organic filaments, then abruptly fold as if hitting an invisible corner, splitting into two perpendicular flows that pass through each other without mixing. Sometimes, at the edges of your perception, the water thickens to near-solidity; a plume of suspended particles will pause as though poured into an unseen container, its boundary perfectly sharp, defying diffusion.

In patches, gravity drifts. A scatter of fragments—shards of metal, flakes of rock, chunks of broken conduit—may hang mid-water, oriented wrong. The matting vegetation finds them irresistible, sending out exploratory cords that bridge the empty gaps, building solid forms out of suspended scaffold. In other spots, the same debris accelerates with unnatural haste, zipping downwards or sideways when a distant machine changes some setting you cannot see.

There are rules here, but they are in the process of arguing with themselves.

Look longer at the vegetation, and its relationship to the machines deepens. Around the bases of the major engines, the living matter forms dense collars and saddles, plugging into ports and vents, wrapping over manholes, growing along etched pathways in the casing as if reading and fulfilling some diagram no longer comprehensible to the devices’ original makers. Bands of tissue pulse in synchrony with rotating machinery within, acting like cardiac muscle on steel hearts. Where an intake has ruptured, the tangle thickens inwards, becoming a living seal, selectively allowing the strange fluid through or holding it back according to unseen rhythms.

Bright, discrete industrial lights are rare, but the interaction of life and mechanism produces a dim, distributed glow. Certain membranes, when stretched across vent-grids and exhaust slits, flicker with soft bioluminescent ripples every time a pressure wave surges through. These pulses propagate across whole fields of growth, wavefronts of light marking the passage of invisible flows. At first they seem random. With time, they suggest patterns—responses to distant machine states, to structural shifts, to yet another dimension of traffic.

The heavy sound does not scare anything here because there is nothing to be scared in the animal sense. Yet the biosphere responds to it. Areas near particularly loud emitters grow thicker, more ornate. There, the mats and fronds and lattices grow in nested shells, like ear-structures, funnels and spirals that seem tuned to particular frequencies. They do not flee the thunder; they drink it.

As reality frays, it does so visibly. In some corridors between cliffs and machinery, perspective is wrong. A walkway-like pipeline extends off into gloom and then, midway, doubles back on itself without turning, so that its far end is somehow also its midpoint. Vegetation takes advantage, looping through these paradoxes, forming knots of trunk that emerge from themselves at different scales. The fluid inside those loops swirls in directionless eddies, flowing continually but never quite arriving.

Elsewhere, a simple planar wall of steel, coated in thick growth, ripples—not the surface, but the wall itself, its plane bending in topologies forbidden by sober Euclidean geometry. The vegetation is untroubled; it follows, adheres, spreads, covering impossible folds as if they were ordinary corners. Where the curvature is sharpest, a thin, pale light seeps out, not illuminating anything around it so much as adjusting the conditions of vision; things briefly appear flatter, then suddenly more three-dimensional, then distances stretch.

Above, if “above” means anything, the density of structures increases. Gantry-like frameworks crisscross the space, burdened with drums, tanks, and coiled hoses that have long since fused with rampant organic filaments. Some drums spin wildly, churning the surrounding medium into spiraling vortices. Others have snapped their moorings, hovering in quasi-stable vortical nests generated by their own motion and by the geometry-warping field of nearby devices. Across them all, the vegetation has formed continuous bridges, turning isolated machines into a single knotted network, a metropolitan canopy of flesh and metal.

The scale is large enough that this is not a single installation but a region—a sunken industrial valley spanning kilometers. The terrain rolls slightly: a series of shallow basins and ridges, each packed with machines of subtly different design. Some basins hum at slower, deeper notes; their currents swirl languidly in immense gyres. Others are shriller, more frantic, full of smaller devices clattering and rattling, their moving parts flickering in streaks as they spin at the edge of their tolerances.

There is motion imposed from above as well. Occasionally the entire medium convulses downward, as if an unseen hand pressed on the top of this world. The pressure spike races through, flattening growths against their substrates, silencing some engines for instants as their rotors slip in their housings. In those moments, reality seems to pause, then lurch, like a skipped heartbeat. When it resumes, some objects are not where they were. A whole ridge may be rotated a few degrees, its fractures aligned differently with the machines embedded in it. The vegetation barely seems to mind; it simply stretches, rips in places, re-fuses, as though accustomed to such rewrites.

The few specifically built enclosures—a cluster of boxlike modules clamped to a cliff, a lone turnout station welded onto a pipeline—are no longer accessible in any sane sense. Their doorways open into spaces that don’t correspond to their external dimensions. Through one hatch, you can see an interior corridor that recedes far beyond the size of the module, zigzagging endlessly, lit by a stuttering, handheld flashlight that nobody holds. Through another, you see only an ocean of static: fine-grained, monochrome turbulence that should be impossible underwater.

No animals, no people, no drifting corpses. Just absence. Tools lie here and there, encased in growth, but never hands. Helmets rest on ledges, half-swallowed by mats. Inside those helmets, nothing. No skulls. It is as though whatever process drowned this valley either removed its workers first, or assimilated them so fully they left no anatomy.

The technology, despite its scale and complexity, feels abandoned by its own story. Yet something is still running. Power exists. Conduits hum. Sparse, blinking indicators persist like stubborn stars. On one broad panel, overgrown but partially visible, a row of analog gauges swings not around a circular dial, but up and down along an impossible axis, registering values that cannot be deciphered; some needles quiver at corners of their slots, vibrating fast enough to blur.

Every so often, a massive, distant event shakes the very logic of the place. A foundational machine—one you cannot see, buried deep—fires or fails. The medium goes briefly transparent; you see far. Ranges of wrecked frameworks, whole districts of machinery, bands of hyper-dense vegetation, recede into a hazy horizon like underwater mountain chains. Then distance reasserts itself, collapsing back to fog, as though your ability to look that far was a glitch, a diagnostic mode accidentally triggered.

During such pulses, the noise peaks past deafening, beyond what underwater pressure should carry. It has an impossible clarity: you can pick out the scream of every bolt, every bearing, individually, in a single psychic instant, as if your hearing has been swapped for a more direct contact with the world’s stress. In those flashes of understanding, it becomes evident that what these machines are fighting is not the flood, nor entropy in the usual sense, but the dissolution of causality. You feel the strain of holding sequences in order, of insisting that this follows that, that yesterday leads to today leads to tomorrow, against a rising tide of simultaneity.

The lush vegetation collaborates. It has grown into the gaps in logic, clogging potential fractures with its own multiplicity. Where cause and effect fray, biology pours in, making bridges of metabolism and feedback loops. It is as if the valley is being rewoven not just in steel and computation, but in tissue and chemistry. The line between system and ecosystem has blurred completely. Valves are now sphincters; filters are now gills; cables are vascular bundles; data paths are pheromone gradients.

Yet no creatures roam through this complexity. No eyes watch from crevices. The world’s only motion is that of environment adapting to itself, a place in ceaseless auto-correction, in love with its own instability.

From a distance—if such a perspective were possible—you might see the whole as a churning, dimly glowing knot sunk into a shallow depression of some broader surface. The surrounding world, if it exists, might be quieter, simpler. But here, in this low basin, the conditions are amplified. Water—or whatever stands in for it—is almost everything. Sound is almost everything else. Matter, life, and machine are condensed into a dense, baroque tangle where each new adjustment spawns three more anomalies to be contained.

No birds will ever cross above this valley. No fish will ever thread its corridors. It is a storm of infrastructure and biology, a drowned industrial garden that manufactures local reality at industrial scale, then struggles, always, to live with the result.
