The ground is flat only from a great distance.

Up close, it is a low world of folds and channels, like a vast, ancient tidal flat that has forgotten the sea. The horizon lies far away, smudged in dim grey-blue light, but between you and it there is almost no clear, open surface—everything is occupied by some presence: bodies, vapors, currents, things that act like machinery without ever having been built.

It is cold. Not killing, not polar, but a chill that never leaves the air, a temperature that feels like the aftermath of heat long since drained away. Surfaces bead with condensed moisture: fine droplets clinging to rock, to exposed mineral planes, to slick, translucent membranes that are not plants but not quite bare matter either. Now and then thin rimes of ice form along sheltered edges, outlining hairline fractures in white.

There is water everywhere, and nowhere still.

It hangs as breath-fog, low and skeined, combed by unseen flows; it crawls up vertical faces in thin, patient films, turning rock surfaces into weeping curtains. It gathers as shallow sheets in depressions carved long ago, no more than ankle-deep, but stretching for kilometers as mirrored skins that ripple not just with wind but with slower, stranger motions beneath.

You can see how old this place is in the way it has surrendered to softness of form. No sharp peaks, no fresh breaks: everything has been rounded, planed, settled. Long, barely-raised ridges trail like the ghost of an eroded mountain chain. Their composition is mixed—dull grey stone, seams of black glassy material, veins of faintly luminous crystal that pulse in irregular rhythms, as though remembering a different age of pressure and heat.

Despite the age, the terrain is restless.

The ground is not dead rock but layered strata of mineral and something-like-fungus that never quite becomes life as you know it. Pale, porous mats spread over wide areas, only a few millimeters thick, like frost that never melts. They are not plants. They do not reach upward. Instead, they are flat, reticulate tissues that drink the perpetual condensation from the air and wick it along microscopic channels, distributing moisture like an artificial irrigation system across bare stone.

Where several layers overlap, they form translucent, jellylike drapes that sag into puddles, delicate membranes trembling with every distant vibration. Their surfaces are worked with branching, circuit-like patterns in darker material, not quite veins, not quite circuitry. Slow pulses of faint phosphorescence track along them, mapping pathways you cannot read.

From a distance, these mats make the ground look cushioned, almost padded. Up close, they are packed tight against each other, each colony pressed flush to its neighbors, leaving no naked rock except along sharp, recent fractures. Between mats, fluid seeps continuously, not quite water, not entirely something else: thicker, with a subtle internal shimmer, as though filled with dust-sized organisms or nanoscopic machines. It drips in a constant soft patter from overhangs, accumulates as pendulous drops that never quite fall, stretches into impossibly thin strands before breaking.

There is no built structure here, no walls or towers, no pipes with right angles. But the place feels heavily industrial, because work is happening everywhere, at every scale.

In the middle distance, something roars.

The sound rises like distant surf, but sharps into a rhythmic pounding—deep, arrhythmic, mechanical. It does not come from a single factory or engine; instead, the whole plain seems to exhale it. Pulling closer, you see wide zones where the ground is not stable but continually flexing: broad plates of mineral tissue that bow, then relax, like diaphragms of a buried machine.

Each slow inhalation pulls fog and droplets downward into cracks; each exhalation drives steam-laden air outward, whipping low filaments and throwing fine spray that catches the faint light and briefly shines. These breathing-planes extend for hundreds of meters, separated by narrow gullies of dark, standing water. The sound is their collective respiration, scaled up to landscape-size.

No human factories stand here—but the entire terrain is an assembly line, an engine disassembled and melted into geology.

Fauna—if that is the word—fills every available gap.

They crowd the air: translucent, disc-like forms the size of a hand or a dinner plate drift in loose flocks a meter above the surface, their edges frilled and constantly flexing. They are thin as mist, made of layered film and dew. Capillary threads trail down from them, dipping into damp mats, drawing up fluid, exchanging something unseen. When disturbed, they shudder and emit a brief, high keening that sets nearby membranes shivering in sympathetic resonance.

Smaller entities boil in clouds a few centimeters above the ground: flecks of iridescent dust, each a rough polygon, tumbling in coordinated murmurs. Individually they look inert—tiny mineral chips, perhaps—but as swarms they flow with predatory intent, wrapping around slower, gelatinous lumps on the ground and, over minutes, milling them down to nothing. The very grit of the place is alive with this appetite.

Along watery channels, larger things move.

Imagine a cross between a centipede and a conveyor belt, made of interlocking plates of soft stone and cartilage-like crystal. They are low, broad, and long, some stretching tens of meters, their backs textured with spines that are also antennae that are also chimneys. As they glide along valley floors, their undersides scour the mats, peeling and ingesting layers, leaving bare, damp rock that is rapidly recolonized from the edges.

They move with mechanical regularity. Each step is a tick, a clack, a piston-thud, amplified and multiplied until the air close to them is a storm of rhythm. There is no intelligence in their eyes—they have none—but there is a clockwork purpose in their trajectories. They follow invisible routes etched in the landscape’s memory, never colliding, always routing around one another with millimeters to spare in tight ravines, as if constrained by programming rather than instinct.

Their exhalations are cold plumes of vapor rich with tiny, glittering filaments that unravel as they cool, falling back into the mats. What they eat, they remake, feeding the substrate that in turn feeds them. It is a closed, roaring economy.

Everywhere, sound.

Not an industrial park's hum, but an entire region’s machinery in open air. A continuous composite noise: the low, infrasonic groan of flexing plates; the staccato percussive marching of mineral-centipedes; the distant, periodic crack-like thunder of something large adjusting itself beneath the surface; the higher, glassy tinkling as frost grows and snaps along crystalline ridges; the soft, omnipresent hiss of condensation forming, dripping, evaporating, reforming.

Above all this is a strange, syncopated chorus produced by the floating discs. Their keens overlap into shifting chords that never resolve, sliding microtonally, occasionally spiking into piercing, localized alarms when larger predators glide near.

Predators, yes—though their forms strain the word.

Some are vertical spires of loosely aggregated shards, like shattered glass held together by static. They rear from the water, three or four meters high, only to collapse and reform a few meters away. Within their semi-random tumbling, patterns recur, a kind of motif: certain planes always reappear in related orientations, like a face half-guessed in a crowd of mirrors. When a swarm of mineral-flakes passes too near, the spire’s internal fields lock, pulling them abruptly into its whirling body. The noise they make—microscopic impacts multiplied by billions—becomes a shrill, grinding scream that warps the air like heat haze.

Others exist mostly in the water. You see their outlines as gaps in the clouded reflections: long, smooth shadows that ripple under the surface then leap vertically, rupturing the film with columns of droplets. The spray they produce refuses to fall correctly. Microdroplets hang in midair longer than gravity should allow, trembling as though listening. Some fall upward, back into the breach the body left, which has not yet refilled with water but with a slow, oily dark that swirls against the direction of flow.

The rules of physics are not entirely stable here, though they are not discarded wholesale. Gravity mostly works, but takes occasional liberties with the smallest scales. Time seems linear, yet certain motions loop. You watch a droplet fall, strike, splash—and in the edge of your vision the same droplet sequence replays, a ghost version, again and again, trailing an afterimage of its own past.

The light, too, is wrong.

The sky is a low, unbroken lid of cloud, dim and uniform, tinted with the faintest violet. There is no visible sun. Illumination comes from everywhere and nowhere, diffuse and weak, enough to see, not enough to warm. But the landscape betrays secondary sources: faint glows along fracture lines; slow, occluded pulses inside semi-transparent formations beneath the water; the minute, strobing flicker in the air over certain patches of ground where something is mid-transition between states, neither matter nor energy as you know them.

Shadows behave unevenly. Around strong emitters of sound, they fuzz, as if the vibration shakes them loose from whatever anchors them. In quiet pockets—if you can find any—they sharpen unusually, outlines becoming too crisp, as though the light has been momentarily coerced into obeying classical rules there, overcompensating.

Cold fog flows like a fluid, obeying contours more rigidly than wind should allow. It forms rivers in the air that track the landscape’s buried machinery, slipping down invisible conduits, recoiling from certain zones. Where it touches some of the fungal-mineral mats, beadlike growths appear almost instantly: nodules of semi-crystalline tissue that swell, thin, and then burst with a pop, scattering fine shards. A few of those shards do not fall. Instead they hang in the air, slowly arranging themselves into geometric lattices, floating frameworks that hum at barely-audible frequencies.

These lattices are nervous systems in open air.

They extend themselves along lines of invisible force, testing gradients, then retract, repeatedly, as though probing. Where they intersect the drifting discs, their frequencies align; the discs dim, sink closer to the ground, and briefly lock in place, quivering. A moment later the lattice disintegrates, and the discs resume their wandering, but they are changed: their keening shifted a quarter-tone higher, their trailing filaments now branching in more complex patterns. Information has been written into them like code.

There is no sign of civilization in the human sense, but the whole region is shot through with a logic like industry: extraction, processing, distribution, transformation, feedback. No one built these processes, yet they run with the inevitability of designed systems. It feels as if some ancient factory ran so long that it fossilized into ecology, its workflows taken over by self-stabilizing mineral-life networks.

The age of erosion shows here not as wear, but as convergence. Everything unnecessary has been weathered away. Only cycles remain.

Look far across the plain and the apparent flatness breaks into pattern. You can trace enormous rings, kilometers wide, defined not by walls or roads but by changes in texture: bands where the mats are thicker, where the floating discs fly lower, where the centipede-conveyors converge. Within each ring, subtle color shifts in the stone—slightly greener tints, slightly finer grain—suggest long-term gradients of resource flow.

At ring intersections, the weirdness intensifies.

Space seems oversubscribed there. Features that should not be able to coexist share the same location: a pool that is simultaneously full and empty, its surface reflecting a sky brighter than the one above; a fissure whose depth can’t be focused on, because the eye never reaches bottom, its interior cross-cut by horizontal sheets that appear nearer than the lip; a cluster of shard-spires that rotate about a common center without actually moving in any interpretable direction.

Sounds at these nodes overlay badly. You hear distant machinery-roars from directions that don’t match visible sources. Your own footsteps lag by a fraction of a second, the echo preceding the strike.

The fauna here is denser, stranger. Tiny, transparent ribbon-creatures, as thin as scratches on glass, loop through midair in elaborate knots. They appear to trace the contours of invisible forms—outlining shapes of pressure or curvature in the fabric of space. Where they knot too tightly, they wink out of existence with little static pops, leaving behind pinhead-sized holes in the air through which you glimpse what could be other rings, other plains, other arrangements of the same cold, wet, unending machinery.

Despite all this, nothing in the landscape acknowledges you.

You can stand in the path of a centipede-engine and it will not deviate; you are forced to move or be slowly, inexorably pushed aside by a mass that does not recognize obstacles. The drifting discs pass through your space, their threads brushing skin with the dry-cold feeling of static that has no charge. The swarm-flakes swarm only things that fit their gradient triggers. You are chemically uninteresting, topologically irrelevant.

This is a saturated world—full to bursting with presences and processes—and yet it has no slot reserved for an observer.

Weather does occur, if that’s what you call episodes when the condensing atmosphere tips into a different mode. Sometimes the dim sky lowers, thickens, and begins to shed not rain, but a fine, continuous fall of liquid filaments. They do not fall straight; they arc in slow spirals, as if tracing magnetic fields. Where they land, they vanish instantly into whichever substrate they meet, leaving the surface visually unchanged but sending ripples through the entire fabric of the ecosystem.

In those moments, sounds crescendo. The breathing-plates accelerate their cycles. The shard-spires multiply and grow more coherent, holding shapes longer between collapses. Underwater predators breach more often, and their upward-falling spray becomes dense enough to almost fog the air with reverse-rain. The very low hum of the floating lattices rises in pitch until it edges into hearing range, a mosquito whine that seems to come from inside the skull.

When the filament-fall ceases, everything gradually subsides back to its baseline frenzy. The world returns to its ordinary, unacceptable strangeness.

Night exists here only as a thickening of the dim, a deepening of blues and greys. True darkness never fully comes, because too many things make their own faint light. At “night”, the ancient erosion-scars take on new meaning: long planar faces of stone display slow-moving interference patterns, like oil on water seen in monochrome. These drifting bands of brightness and dark are not reflections, but stress patterns in the stone itself, flexing so slowly you would not detect motion—if not for the fact that, every few minutes, the pattern jumps discontinuously to a new configuration, as if the rock were solving equations in fits.

Fauna reacts to these invisible shifts. Swarms split or merge in perfect correlation with abrupt pattern changes. The ribbon-creatures adjust their knots, aligning to new invisible seams. The giant centipede-engines, usually indifferent to anything, sometimes freeze mid-stride for long seconds when the stone beneath them changes pattern, as if waiting for recalibration, then lurch forward again with renewed certainty in their path.

The more you attend, the less separable categories become.

Is that a “rock” or a dormant organism? Is that “fog” or the active phase of some distributed mind? Are the breathing-plates functioning as lungs, pistons, pumps, or some hybrid of all three recast at geologic scale? Industry and ecology have blurred completely; the old distinction between natural and artificial no longer applies.

What remains is a low, cold, water-slicked expanse: ancient to the point of abstraction, eroded past familiar forms into a final, densely-packed regime of self-perpetuating processes. No designers, no cities, no artifacts remain—but their ghosts may be encoded in the cycles that keep running.

In the distance, past multiple rings and breathing fields, the horizon seems to kink—not curve, not rise, but kink, as though the plane of this region meets another at an impossible angle that still looks flat from here. Between you and that discontinuity lies more of the same: thin water sheets, translucent mats, grinding shard-spires, swarming dust, cold breath, and the ceaseless, overlapping roar of a world that is both factory and organism, long since settled into its final, incomprehensible form.
