The first thing is the noise.

It is not a single roar, not a waterfall or a machine, but an enormous woven fabric of sound: clicks stacked on trills stacked on whistles stacked on wet, pulsed bellows. Millions of small throats, carapaces, membranes, shells, sacs, tongues, lungs, and whatever else this place has learned to vibrate are all working at once.

The sound doesn’t crash so much as press. It has weight. It fills the hollows, crawls between ribs, sits in the sinuses. It is deafening not because it peaks, but because it never stops.

You are inside something built.

You know this because there is no bare rock, no soil, no untouched surface. Every plane around you carries pattern. Vaulted overhead, underfoot, stretching away in terrace after terrace, is a continuous city of grown matter: towers, plateaus, bridges, ducts, buttresses, shells, membranes. It is architecture in the sense that it has been made, arranged, maintained; and yet there is no straight line, no right angle, no tool-mark. Everything swells, buckles, folds. Every surface carries pores, ridges, hair, mossy thickness, slick film. It is a completely constructed world, but the builders have only ever been alive.

High elevation: you can feel that in the thinness of the air when you manage a full breath between pulses of noise. Outside the vast envelope of this organismal city, there would be cliffs, skies, distances. You catch hints of that through occasional openings: far-off dim gradients where the structure frays into darkness. But the inhabited region — the part that hums — is an extended bowl, a high cirque filled in over eons by things that grow and never quite stop.

Warmth wraps it all, soft and stable. The air is heavy with exhalations: damp, animal, green. Nothing here is hot or cold in any sharp way. It is the warmth of a body at rest, of compost working, of breath en masse. There are no obvious seasons, no sharp fronts; change happens by slow drift.

Water is here the way sleep is in a city: always somewhere, never everywhere.

You see it as slickness in corners, beads on dangling roots, a film shining on vaulted undersides. Runnels of condensation gather in grooves and flow along the undersides of arches, then widen into pendant basins — shallow cupped cavities that glisten and overflow in slow ticks and drips. Sometimes a trickle thickens into a small hanging river that runs along the spine of a corridor, upside-down from your ground, held in a furred channel that wicks and guides it until it spills into a deeper pocket.

There are deeper cisterns, too: oval sumps lined with spongy, layered tissue, their surfaces barely disturbed by the constant drop of condensation from far above. These are not lakes in the sky-bowl; they are organs, distributed and repeated, storing and releasing water so that the whole constructed body of this place never dries out. You might walk for a while across surfaces that are dry to the touch, only to cross some invisible gradient and feel, under your soles, that the ground has become thicker, cooler, faintly yielding. Here the moisture is just under the skin, and each step leaves a sweat-darkened print that slowly blurs and is swallowed.

Vegetation exists, but you have to expand your sense of the word. There are things that photosynthesize, yes — pale plates spread under stray shafts of faint light that leak in from rents higher up, clusters of threadlike leaves that drink the dimness with patient sufficiency — but much of what would count as “plant” elsewhere has here fused with animal and fungus and coral and something else.

On what functions as the ground: dense mats of filamentous growth weave into tough carpets, pale to the eye but saturated with life. Some of it looks like moss, except that it shudders in slow waves as minute feeders move through its body. Some spreads in scalloped sheets that peel upward at the edges like tongues, tasting the air. Colour is muted: ochres, umbers, bruised greens, the pale browns of old bone, all seen through very low light.

From the walls rise columnar forms with bark-like surfaces, fluted as if eroded by wind — though the air here barely moves. High up, their trunks dissolve into nets of interlaced branches that merge with the general overhead mass. Between them hang curtains of fibrous growth that look like vines until you notice they’re not simply hanging: they’re engaged. Small bodies slip in and out of them, feeding, nesting, gnawing. The entire tangle is alive and being re-made in place.

Everything is layered. At a regional scale, this bowl-and-ridge structure is packed with growth so dense that “distance” becomes a matter of how many membranes and hollows you pass through rather than any straight-line measure. There are broad avenues of relative openness, where the floor flattens into terraces and the overhead vault lifts. There are constricted throats where two bulging walls nearly meet and you have to turn sideways. There are vertical shafts where you glimpse other levels stacked above and below, the same architectural tissues repeating, altered gradually by height and wetness.

The built environment is old. You can sense it in the way forms are not sharp or fresh but worn into a kind of smoothness. Edges have rounded; earlier grown structures have slumped and been buttressed by later growth. Some layers are flaked, peeled back, hollowed by centuries of feeding and re-filling. There is no sign of collapse as catastrophe; there is just a deep, slow settling. Here and there, you find what might have once been large, load-bearing arches now honeycombed into lace, yet they still hold, supported by new ribs that have grown out from below.

This is erosion by digestion rather than wind: chewing, boring, rasping, excreting. Countless creatures have taken the original built substrate and reworked it into something even more complex. The result is a landscape in balance: not static, but stable, each slow cycle matched by another.

Technology, in any mechanical or tool-based sense, is absent. No metal gleams, no crafted joint or knotted fiber shows an intention separable from living behavior. Yet everything is constructed. The walls you touch are not random outgrowths; they are placements, extensions, maintained by uncounted organisms whose instincts have, over many generations, built up an environment as surely as tides build a delta.

Somewhere in the distant past, an initial scaffold may have been laid down — a foundational growth clustering in this high basin: perhaps a ring of early coral-forest that trapped mist and nurtured its own expansion. Over long ages, it thickened upward and inward until it became its own terrain, substantial enough to reshape wind and cloud and so feed itself further. Life here is biological engineering at the scale of a mountain range, but nobody designed it from the outside. It is pure iterated habit.

Light is scarce. The bowl’s rim and the high vaulting over it block most of the sky. What leaks in is filtered through many layers of translucent tissues, ducts, and hanging structures. The general illumination is a dim, murky twilight: enough to see forms, not colours; edges, not detail. Far overhead, in cracks where the built canopy thins, you can make out a deeper grey that must be the true sky, but it never resolves into distinct clouds or stars. Instead, there is a slow pulsing brightness — not bioluminescent glow, exactly, but a slight rise and fall as clouds outside pass over, their presence translated through metres of grown fabric into a faint breathing of the light.

You move mostly by feel, by the texture underfoot and the small motions of air. Sight is an accessory sense. Yet the organisms that live here have traded sight for other acuities: vibration, scent, pressure, and the particular qualities of sound.

And there are organisms, everywhere.

Fauna is not an embellishment of the landscape; it is its primary substance. If you stand still, after a while you start to pick them out, though at first they were only part of the noise.

Along the ground-scape, small, many-legged forms pour like liquid over every surface: translucent little grazers the length of a fingernail, dark, hard-backed scuttlers, pale worms that arch and loop, bodies glossy with mucous. They feed on the mats and shedding surfaces, rasping, nibbling, excreting pellets that others immediately rework. From a distance this is just a subtle crawling. Up close, it’s a constant tide.

Clinging to the walls are broader-bodied things, flattened like thick leaves, their undersides clustered with gripping pads. They move in slow, deliberate surges, their upper surfaces bristling with sensory hairs that tremble in the resonant air. Where two meet, they press their rims together briefly and exchange some invisible cargo — spores, pheromones, microorganisms, genetic packets — then withdraw.

Through the midair — if it can be called air, thick as it is with drifting fluff and microscopic exhalations — winged beings constantly pass. Some are large enough to stir a small breeze as they go: membranous wings, veined and ragged, stretched between segments of a soft, muscular central body. Others are tiny flecks, no more than flitting shadows in the half-dark. They roost in folds and hollows, packed in such density that their combined bodies form new surfaces on which smaller animals then graze.

Larger than all these are the slow giants that move along the main terraces and galleries: multi-metered masses, their bodies obscured by layers of symbiotic growth until it’s hard to say where animal ends and environment begins. Some resemble low, moving hills of shaggy filaments, with a dozen supporting limbs hidden beneath. Others crawl like immense slugs, leaving behind shiny, nutrient-thick trails that are immediately colonized by rapid-growing films. Their passage is not rapid; to watch one cross a wide terrace might take an hour. Yet they are always in motion, always reshaping the microtopography.

You might notice, too, bulges in the walls that sometimes detach, unfold, and walk away — what you thought was simple architecture revealing itself as an organism in dormancy that has now resumed its wandering.

Predators exist, but their presence is not marked by sudden violence so much as slight, focused rearrangements in the living traffic. You notice a lane of ground suddenly emptying, small creatures pivoting at once and flowing away; a moment later, a low, nearly invisible mantle of tissue glides through, studded underneath with gripping mouths. It pauses over a patch of residual movement and clamps down, sound swallowed in the omnipresent noise. When it lifts again minutes later, the patch is bare: the mat scuffed, the wall cleaner. Then it oozes on.

Every cubic metre here is saturated with life. There is almost no bare volume. Even the air is thick with spores, drifting larval stages, filament fragments, microscopic flyers. If you hold your hand up, after a while a faint itch accumulates: landings, probings, minute bites and tests. If you were to sleep, you would wake with a film of life already sampling you.

And always: the sound. The deafening sound.

Some of it is communication: mating calls, territorial warnings, simple recognition. Much is just byproduct. Digestive trills echo in cavities that happen to resonate in useful frequencies. The grinding of structures against each other as animals move through tight passages creates constant subsonic groans. The slow expansion and contraction of large, sessile lung-like bodies that help ventilate deeper spaces produce rhythmic booms. Even the drip of water into cisterns here does not tinkle; it lands on stretched membranes that convert each fall into a drumbeat, thudding softly, incrementally, yet repeated across thousands of cavities to form a continuous whispering rumble.

Sound functions as infrastructure. Some species orient themselves by the interference patterns of their own clicks bouncing off the omnipresent tissue. Others time their life cycles by slow oscillations in the general soundfield — deep background waves that rise and fall over days, weeks, years, as the entire basin inhales moisture from passing clouds and exhales it between rarer rains.

This place is high-altitude, yet you will not find alpine clear air; the built environment has sealed itself, mostly, from the harsher extremes outside. When rare storms pass over, some openings in the canopy widen and accept the cold, fast air, funneling it deep in narrow pipes that warm it while wringing out moisture. Rainwater finds entry in channels specifically grown to accept and store it; the rest runs off the outer shell of the basin, never touching the interior.

Thus the climate within remains steady. Warm, humid, dim. The slow dynamics are in moisture and population, not in temperature. There might be a centuries-long oscillation where the canopy thickens, making the interior darker, cooler, changing which organisms dominate. Then some shift in outside rainfall patterns leads to a thinning: parts of the upper structure dry, crack, open once more to the sky, letting in slightly more light and less trapped water. The whole immense organism adjusts, but never quickly. Generations of smaller creatures live and die in the time it takes for one major rib to thicken or shrink.

Scale is regional. If you were to map it, you’d find districts.

Here, in the wetter depths near one of the great cistern clusters, the environment is swamp-like in microtexture: soft, spongy ground, curtains of drooping, absorptive tissue, fauna that swim through saturated mats as much as walk. A little further upslope, dryness increases; fillings between structural bones are thinner, the sound sharper. High ridges within the bowl — the “heights within the height” — may even have surfaces that crust over and crack, allowing tiny trickles of external starlight to leak in through hairline gaps. In those rare places, you might see a real patch of sky: a scattering of cold points far above, partly occluded by the slow pumping of some translucent sac.

But even there, nature is still constructed. Wherever a crack widens, something grows to narrow it again: fringe growths that drink the stray rays, mats that prefer their thin dryness, colonies of lichen-like composites that only flourish in the faint, more honest light from outside.

Rules emerge from all this:

- Nothing remains bare. Any available surface, any new cavity, will be colonized. Fresh substrate is food and home. The transition from “recently exposed” to “fully inhabited layer” is fast at small scale, even though the overall landform shifts slowly.

- Light is a luxury, not a baseline. Most life has adapted to darkness, using chemistry and vibration rather than sight. Where light is present, it is immediately captured by specialised tissues and organisms. Photosynthesizers cluster greedily at any leak; most others avoid it, sensitive to subtle damage the rarer, harsher rays can cause.

- Sound is both hazard and resource. Being able to shout louder than the background hum wins mates and territory, but making too distinctive a signal also attracts predators that hunt by pattern recognition. This tension has filled the soundscape with complex, rhythmic, layered calls rather than simple blasts; each species has carved out a niche in frequency and timing, resulting in that woven noise.

- The built substrate is alive enough to heal, but slow. Large tears in the canopy or structural rib will eventually be filled by growth, but over years, not days. In the interim, many animals exploit the change. Cracks become flyways, hunting paths, mixing zones. When they close, those species move on.

- Vertical position is destiny. Near the base of the bowl, where condensation and runoff collect, life is wetter, softer, more motile: things swim, seep, slither. Near the mid-level terraces, life is about clinging, climbing, grazing broad surfaces. Higher up, nearer the canopy and any contact with the outside, organisms need to endure occasional dryness, wind, and maybe even brief glare; shells are thicker, bodies more compact.

- There are no tools because there is no distinction, here, between home and body that would require them. Organisms reshape their environment by growing, excreting, adhering, dissolving. The “architecture” is a conglomerate of extended anatomies. To move a wall, something simply grows differently there for a while.

Despite the overwhelming density, movement at large scale is slow. The giants hardly ever hurry. The overall arrangement of terraces and chambers shifts on timescales longer than memory. The population pulses — local booms and crashes in certain species as microconditions change — but always within a surrounding stability: food webs so entangled that empty niches are quickly reoccupied, yet no single player can dominate for long.

Standing in a broad gallery, you might see:

Under your feet: a thick, springy mat of living and dead woven fiber, busy with millimetre-scale activity. Tiny black beetle-like grazers; soft, pale worms turning detritus; minute translucent things hopping in jerks that only emphasize the slowness of everything larger.

To either side: walls rising in uneven buttresses, surfaces textured like eroded sandstone but yielding if pressed too hard. From the walls protrude shelves where mid-sized creatures cling — eight-limbed, their bodies a flattened disk, slowly scraping the biofilm.

Below, a little distance away: the gallery steepens into a slope that vanishes into dimness. At intervals along the descent are openings: holes leading into deeper pockets, each rim ringed with tendrils that taste whatever comes and goes.

Above: the vault curves, mottled with nodules and dangling bundles of fibrous stuff, all rocking very slightly with the throbbing air. Tiny flyers flit in and out of them like sparks in reverse — going from the open space into the dark hollows rather than emerging.

Far away, from somewhere beyond your sight, comes a repeated, ultra-low, stomach-deep pulse, like a giant heart beating through stone. Once every few minutes, a soft shudder passes through the floor with it.

And wrapping all this: that continuous soundfield. A chorus so intense that after a while, paradoxically, you begin to hear shapes within it: regular grids of chirps, long slow rises and falls of collective calling from some distant swarm, the infrasonic sigh of the entire basin breathing. It becomes the water in which your other senses swim.

Nothing here is supernatural; no impossible physics intrudes. Gravity pulls, organisms metabolize, water condenses and evaporates, structures bear loads or fail in predictable ways. The weirdness is only in the scale of life’s success — in how, on this high, warm, moist plateau, biology has been given enough time and enough gentle, steady input to roof its own sky and turn bare stone into a single, continuous, constructed habitat.

Outside, wind still carves cliffs and cloud still tears against unseen peaks. Inside this resonant bowl, change is slower, denser, burdened with bodies and histories. The place continues, deafening and patient, adding another fractional layer of tissue and shell and husk to itself as it waits for the next drop of water, the next push of growth, the next age.
