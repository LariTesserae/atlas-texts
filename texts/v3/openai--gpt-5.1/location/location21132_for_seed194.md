There is no sky here.

Above you, below you, on every side, the world is the same continuous substance: plant.

It is not a forest, though there are things that might once have been trees. It is not a cave, though it encloses you like stone. It is closer to being inside the body of something that never finished deciding whether it was alive.

Darkness is absolute. No starlight, no phosphorescence, not even the dim comfort of heat glows. This is not a place adapted to seeing. It is a place built on pressure, sound, and a slow, vegetative awareness spreading through lattices of living tissue.

You stand—if “stand” is the right word—within a hollow only a few body-lengths across. It is roughly spherical, the size of a small hut, and it is the entire world, or close enough. Beyond its boundaries, the plant-mass continues on for an unimaginable distance: kilometers? Hundreds of kilometers? It doesn’t matter. This pocket of emptiness is the only interruption in an otherwise unbroken solidity.

The air is cool and utterly dry, almost abrasive in the lungs, tangy with complex plant resins and the dust-fine fragments of long-decayed growth scraped from wall and floor by touch and movement. There is no trace of water. No dampness in the air, no beading on any surface, no smell of rot in the usual sense—only a steady, desiccated breakdown, a reduction, like old paper crumbling under your fingers.

The surfaces enclosing you are vegetal, but they don’t feel like bark or leaf. The “floor” is a deeply grooved mat of fibrous tissue laid down in interlocking ribs, hard as very old wood but still faintly yielding if you press. Here and there it flakes away in thin sheets, revealing lighter layers beneath—pale, almost chalky conduits—then darker knots that ring dully when tapped. The wall curves up and out of the floor seamlessly, as if the space were carved from a single continuous growth. There are no clean planes, nothing planar or rectilinear unless something once lived here and forced it into shape.

And something did.

You can feel it in the arrangement: this hollow disagrees with the natural tendency of the growth around it. The plant-substrate beyond prefers coarse, slowly shifting folds—vast, sagging buttresses of living wood and ropey masses of what would be roots in another world. The room you are in is more disciplined. Old tool-marks ripple under your fingers, shallow divots where crude stone or bone implements once chipped away at the wall. The ceiling bows low at two points, flattened by repeated pressure, like a head that has leaned there thousands of times.

This is a built place, but built the way termites build, or corals: by persuading biology to grow into architecture and then editing it with whatever tools exist. Not blocks and beams, but channels and domes coaxed from a cooperative organism.

The city this room belongs to was never large in height or grandeur—low elevation, always close to the original substrate—but in its time it was intricate. You can sense the ghost-lines of corridors radiating from this pocket. They extend into the plant, branching outward in three dimensions, a honeycomb of hand-dug tunnels and naturally hollow growth-chambers stitched together. Most of them are sealed now, pinched closed by slow, patient thickening of the plant around them, sutured over like scars. The hollow you inhabit is one of the last open spaces, an air bubble trapped in a nearly solid, ancient mass of plant.

The age of the place is overwhelming. Everything here has settled into its final decision long ago. Erosion, in the usual sense, never came through with wind or rain or tide; instead there was the digestion of time. Layers of living tissue grew, died, mineralized, and were compacted, the way peat becomes coal. Creases fattened. Open shafts narrowed. Sharp edges blurred into smooth humps. The city has not been abandoned merely for years or centuries, but through eras of slow consolidation. It feels like standing in the fossilized alveolus of a lung belonging to a vanished species.

Despite the void-like dryness, it is all still plant, or the memory of plant. There is no stone here, no sand. The “mineral” feel comes from lignin turned glassy and complex carbons compressed until they ring.

The density of presence is extraordinarily low. For all that the walls press close, there is very little happening per unit of volume. No small scuttling life. No darting insects. No whisper of wings. This pocket and its choking network of nearby passages are hollow threads wandering through an almost perfectly solid living-mineral mass. Imagine an enormous loaf of bread in which someone has hollowed one or two tunnels with a drinking straw; you are inside the straw.

Yet the place is not silent.

The sound here is relentless, though its sources are distant and its origins distributed.

At the lowest edge of perception there is a continuous, cavernous roar: not loud in decibels at your eardrum, but total in the way it saturates everything. It is like holding your ear to the trunk of a colossal tree on a day of great wind. The vibrations come from the plant-body itself, from stresses shifting through an organism the size of a continent, from the grinding of old tissues against old tissues as they creep by fractions of millimeters over centuries.

Interwoven with this are slow, percussive reports: a hollow boom, then long minutes or hours of nothing, then another answer from a different direction, the timing too irregular to be mechanical. This is the sound of cavities elsewhere collapsing as the plant thickens and settles, or ancient conduits finally sealing under their own accumulated weight. Through the medium of tightly-bonded fibers these impacts travel easily, reaching you here as rounded thuds that you feel in your bones.

Closer still, just beyond the walls, are sharper noises, made more urgent by their muffled proximity. Dry fibers creak and pop as microscopic fractures propagate. Occasionally a slender tendril somewhere in the surrounding mass fails with a brittle snap that echoes like a twig breaking in a quiet forest—but here, in the absence of all other detail, each tiny fracture is magnified by the stillness into something significant, an event.

The sum of it is loud not because it would damage hearing, but because it never stops. There is no true rest. The space is a drum and the city a set of distributed hammers, striking according to the inscrutable schedule of biological geology.

No animals ever made this city their own. The people—if they were people—were plant-tied themselves, closer to clever offshoots than to something that could step away. They worked with hand-tools—stone, hardened plant, perhaps simple metal dug from rare mineral nodules caught in the root-like tangles—but nothing more advanced. Their technology was an elaboration of what the substrate already offered: fibers braided for tension, hollow stems as conduits, resin hardened to form cutting edges. Architecture was a matter of scoring growth-ridges so that the great plant thickened in one direction and thinned in another, breeding it into supporting walls and open vaults the way a gardener trains vines over a frame.

Now they are gone. The city persists as structure, not as society.

Even without water, the plant-architecture never fully died. It adapted to a solventless life. Instead of fluids, it pushes vapors and gasses through rigid microtubules. Nutrients are shuttled as powders and molecular films over crystalline cell-walls. Where sap might once have risen, slow waves of phase change migrate: trapped volatiles evaporate here, condense there, drive infinitesimal flows over geological timescales. It is chemistry written in dryness.

The coolness of the air is a byproduct of these ancient processes. Heat diffuses sluggishly through the dense mass, and the surface—wherever that is—is far removed from your pocket. The temperature changes so slowly it may as well not change at all from moment to moment. If this world has a day and night, they are etched into the plant-body as layers of slightly different stiffness and color, not as anything you could sense directly. Perhaps once every few centuries the tone of the ambient roar shifts as external stresses redistribute; that is their weather.

In the complete absence of light, other rules of form and behavior dominate. Surfaces are textured for touch, for pressure sensing. The room’s floor organizes itself into low ridges radiating from one side to the other, as if laying down a quiet, tactile map. Near where an entrance once was, the walls are lined with knotty protrusions—hand-holds, once worn smooth by passage. Now, after ages of dust settling and micro-flaking, they are faintly rough again, but the shape is still there: a succession of grips leading up and around a curve where a tunnel used to be.

Above, the ceiling bears evidence of more deliberate manipulation. A wide swath in its center is planed flatter than the rest, and across it shallow grooves intersect, no deeper than a fingernail. They are a script of some sort, or once were—a record pressed not into stone tablets but into the living skin of the plant. Over time, the plant responded, growing over some lines, deepening others, so that message and metabolism are fused. Whatever was written is now a blurred pattern of ridges that resemble the veins of a titanic leaf.

The weirdness of the place shows itself not in spectacle but in quiet wrongness of expectations. The absence of animals is too complete. No burrows, no gnawing marks. No parasites in the plant-flesh. Not even microbial fuzz, at least not in any familiar way. The decay products here are chemically intricate but structurally clean, as though decomposition itself is handled internally by the plant-body and leaves no loose ecosystem of scavengers. What dies is reabsorbed, broken down along pathways that do not invite opportunists.

Touch the wall and you feel a faint, slow pulsing—not a heartbeat (too regular, too fast) but a long exhale that takes minutes, an inhale that takes longer. Stand perfectly still and you can convince yourself it is your imagination, but it is not. The plant uses pressure waves through its own tissues the way nerves use electrical impulses, though at a vastly different pace. It is thinking, somewhere, about something. Or at least maintaining an unfathomably distributed self.

This thought-slow world changes, but only as sedimentary layers change: almost all at once when sufficient tension accumulates, then not at all for ages. A corridor collapses; a chamber pinches shut; a new void appears far away as old material, compacted beyond usefulness, fractures and is left as a bubble of emptiness. Cities like the one your chamber belongs to are fossils not because new ones are not made, but because the agency that made them has receded into deeper, more protected volumes, or diffused into the plant-body so thoroughly that “builder” and “built” are no longer distinguishable.

Somewhere beyond your room, other hollows exist: a larger chamber where walls are lined with hexagonal sockets, each once cradling a seed-tool or a spore-implement; a low, broad hall whose floor is mosaicked with overlapping scale-like plates of polished woody tissue, each slotted into the next. In pitch darkness these places have become museums of touch, but there is no one left to walk them. The plant continues to support their shapes because unmaking them would cost energy, and energy budgets here are brutally simple.

Every so often—on a scale of decades, perhaps centuries—one of these old civic centers fails. A support ridge reaches the limits of its compression strength. A long-ago carved span, grown thicker but also more brittle, shatters. The collapse sends a shudder through the surrounding substance, a booming report that joins the deep background roar, then the broken elements begin to knit. Not like a wound healing quickly in flesh, but like cracks in glass slowly filling with seeped resin. Millimeter by millimeter, century by century, the broken edge rounds, the gap narrows. Millions of such slow healings have flattened the once-vivid architecture of the city into this state of almost featureless antiquity.

The rules of this world are few and uncompromising:

- There is only plant and void.
- Water plays no part; all transport and transformation are dry.
- Light is irrelevant; chemistry and pressure are the mediums of information.
- Animal life never took hold; competition and predation are internalized within the plant-body’s own regulatory webs.
- Time is allowed to stretch and sag until every process, even the loud ones, unfolds like very thick honey.

Within these constraints, the city persists as a quiet contradiction: a human- or person-scaled intention still legible inside an inhuman, self-sufficient mass. The ridges you might call streets, the domes that were once homes, the scored walls of communal chambers—they are all held together, not by the care of inhabitants, but by the sheer inertia of growth laid down long ago, now coasting toward thermal equilibrium.

If you listen long enough, patterns emerge in the ever-present noise. The deep roar gains a gentle modulation, slow as the waxing and waning of a far-off tide. Over unpredictable stretches, certain thuds seem to answer each other with equal delays: an enormous, plant-bodied call and response. Perhaps the plant’s distributed mind is still rearranging itself. Perhaps these are only the random failures of strained supports. In a space without light, narrative is built from sound, and the city—hollow but not empty, fossil but not stone—continues to tell its very slow, very loud story to itself, in darkness, for as long as its tissues hold together.
