The air here is cool and wet in the throat, but not from rain; it’s from breath.

Everywhere, slow exhalations rise out of the dark.

You stand in a hollow that was never carved by river or sea, because geology here is still only half-decided. The ground feels like the first draft of a planet: faintly granular, faintly yielding, as if powdered stone is loosely knit with something almost-organic that hasn’t yet picked which it wants to be. When you press your foot down, there’s resistance, then a soft readjustment—as if the terrain takes note that a weight has arrived and quietly updates itself.

Erosion hasn’t had time to make features. Edges are blurred; slopes are gentle. The basin’s perimeter is a suggestion more than a wall, fading into a darkness that does not recede as your eyes adjust. There might be distance there, or only an absence that masquerades as distance. Somewhere beyond the perceptible boundary, the world may simply stop.

What fills the hollow instead of stone and air is plant.

Not “plants” as separate individuals, but plant as total medium. The substrate, the city, the atmosphere—all of it is plant, or something that decided “plant” was a useful starting point and then went further.

From the unseen edges of the basin inward, a living architecture spreads: columns of braided lignin-pale tissue rise like clustered pipes, but they do not meet any roof. Instead, they terminate in ragged crowns of flat, overlapping fronds that hang, interwoven, forming floating layers and partial vaults. These frond-ceilings hover in strata at different heights, like floors of a building where the stairs were forgotten and replaced with tendrils and ramps of vine.

The vegetation is not a forest in the usual sense; there is almost no soil. Instead, slabs of fibrous matter bridge between columns, forming shelves, ledges, arches. Surfaces shine faintly with condensed moisture—beads of water gathering from nowhere obvious, then sliding downward along capillary grooves etched into the living walls. Here and there, these rivulets meet, thickening into thin, clear threads that flow along determined routes: a vascular version of plumbing.

It is an urban scale, but not wide—more like a small city remade inside a vast, hollowed trunk. Imagine a town whose streets are replaced by tunnels of smooth, breathable bark, whose buildings are bulbous chambers grown from intersecting roots, whose civic infrastructure is sap.

You can walk through avenues that loop around central shafts where enormous, slow pulses travel up and down—luminous, dusky glows moving inside clear-veined conduits, like trains of light on invisible tracks. They cast just enough peripheral radiance to sketch wet edges and impressions of depth, but never truly light the place. The overall condition is darkness: not pitch black, but an endless pre-dawn hour, where most of the world is silhouette and inferred mass.

Electricity exists here, but it does not belong solely to machines. Thin filaments—wire or vine or nerve—are strung along surfaces, branching and rebranching, occasionally giving off a faint, cold phosphorescence when some unseen impulse passes through them. In several places, plant tissue has budded into rigid lattices: repeating geometric patterns that look manufactured. Metal plates—if they are metal—are grown into these frameworks, studded with blunt protrusions that resemble terminals or plugs. Fist-sized nodules nestle there, some clearly mechanical (spoked, jointed, studded with screws), others clearly alive (pulsing, respiring, ringed with stomata), many both at once.

The built environment is everywhere, yet no straight line appears entirely stable. Walkways are tubes that have been bored through bulk-plant flesh and then reinforced with banded ribs—vertebral bridges whose spacing feels almost measured, as if something counted intervals while it grew. Slopes spiral gently up to platforms that hover over voids. Balconies extrude from vertical surfaces, bending down slightly as if listening.

Here and there, you pass what could only have been factories: large, ovoid caverns where dense clusters of bio-mechanical assemblies sit in semi-dark. Conveyor-like cords run from one living machine to another, sometimes carrying sloshing pods, sometimes carrying nothing visible at all. Pistons of cellulose and chitin pump rhythmically, with a moist, muffled thump. The air in these chambers is slightly warmer, tinged with a metallic tang—oxidation in progress, some form of combustion without flame.

Yet even here the place is mostly quiet. Most sounds are internal: the base note of slow sap-flow; an occasional crackle along a nerve-wire; the soft hiss of moisture condensing, gathering, and falling in tiny, irregular drips. This city is active at a scale that is mostly below human hearing, or above it.

Between the living structures, space yawns. There is a great emptiness per volume. The city’s organs cluster along a kind of central veinwork, leaving vast hollows in between—air-wells, void-chambers, vertical shower-shafts where mist drifts down from nowhere. You can stand in one and look up and see only layers of darker-dark stacked inside each other, crossed by thin transverse bridges like the webbing inside a long bone. Every movement you make sounds too loud, swallowed almost immediately by the thickness of the air.

Despite this emptiness, the world is crowded with life. It is simply not distributed like life in a forest or a town; it is aggregated, clumped, folded into specialized pockets.

On the underside of the frond-ceilings, colonies of pale, translucent arthropods hang in delicate nests of hardened sap. Their segmented bodies glow faintly where internal symbionts make use of the plant’s electric pulses, flickering like soft LEDs mapping out some unknown circuit. These creatures emerge only intermittently, sliding along overhead nets, tapping the plant with brush-like mouthparts, eavesdropping on its currents.

At ground level—or what passes for it—the dominant fauna are long, low things that flow rather than walk. Imagine salamanders stretched into velvet ribbons, jointless and graceful, propelling themselves with waves of muscle along moist grooves in the floors. They graze on biofilm that coats certain pathways, scraping with rows of tiny, cilia-like teeth. When disturbed, they flatten against the surface, patterns in their skins syncing with underlying plant patterns until they almost vanish.

Higher up, in the voids, gliders drift. They are not exactly animals and not exactly leaves: broad, kite-like planar organisms that detach from the ceilings, catch slow convection currents, and circle in near-silence. Some return to reattach; others dissolve midair, crumbling into spore-mist that descends like faint smoke, only to be sucked into breathing vents: flower-mouths in the walls that open and close as if tasting.

Predator and prey exist, but the scale of their interactions is partly sideways to the world you know. Occasionally, a smooth, tubular structure in the wall dilates and a slender, many-jointed arm unfolds, reaching with surprising speed into the darkness to seize something that had been circling unnoticed. There is a brief, damp struggle, a crunch, then retraction. The wall closes over the site of capture almost seamlessly, leaving only a faint, temporary change in its texture, as if memory itself is an organ.

Water is present in fits and starts. No pools spread across the floor. Instead, pockets of liquid gather in leaf-cups, held there in perfect stillness until some distant process dictates release. At irregular intervals, a chime-like vibration runs through a corridor, and sluice-flowers open, pouring thin streams of chilled water into descending channels. For a few minutes, you hear the run of it: a clear trickle threading through the damp quiet. Then valves close. The city returns to its heavy hush.

Sometimes, from high above, it rains without a sky. Tiny droplets sift down through invisible perforations in the plant canopy, a mist more than a shower. The droplets carry spores, enzymes, programming. Surfaces that they touch may begin, hours later, to change—swelling here, eroding there, opening a doorway that was not present before.

Because rules are porous here.

Cause and effect still exist, but connections have been bent; the logic is grown rather than written. Time behaves as if it is different tissues layered on each other—thicker in some places, thinner in others. You might watch condensation slide down a vein in languid motion, then notice that a patch of fungus across the hall has bloomed and withered in the time it took a single bead of water to travel an arm’s length.

Space is similarly uncertain. Corridors loop in ways that do not match their apparent length. Step through a membranous arch and you may find yourself in a chamber you glimpsed from afar minutes ago, despite having walked only a few meters. The plant-city rearranges itself quietly, not as mechanical transformation but as a re-writing of adjacency. Two rooms that were never neighbors become neighbors; a familiar ramp now leads to a different tier.

There is some underlying regimen holding this in check—some infrastructural logic. You can sense it in the repeating morphologies: the standard diameters of conduits, the common curvature of load-bearing columns, the consistent threefold symmetry of certain junctions. Even when reality itself wavers, the place tries to maintain a kind of civic order.

Certain structures are clearly hubs. In one, a circular well plunges down beyond seeing, rimmed with a ring of vertical pods that open like deep-sea anemones. Within each, a complex tangle of cables, tendrils, and organ-like sacs process streams of tiny, insectile creatures that voluntarily enter, as if drawn. They go in flickering; they come out quiet, bearing nodules on their backs. From there, they disperse through the city—a distributed workforce, both fauna and device, maintaining whatever passes for infrastructure.

Another hub is purely auditory: a spherical chamber whose walls are ridged and corrugated. Here, faint sounds from distant parts of the city are gathered, focused, and replayed in warped, slowed echoes. You can stand in the center and hear, as faint pulses, the thrum of faraway pumps, the rasp of some large creature’s feeding, the creak of a bridge adjusting itself somewhere in the unseen. It’s as if the city keeps its own heartbeat archived and re-broadcast, a feedback loop of self-awareness.

Light remains scarce. Many inhabitants do not seem to rely on it at all. Eyes, when present, are vestigial—small, clouded, reflective only when they catch a stray glimmer from a passing electric filament. Senses have slid sideways: skins are tuned to humidity gradients and electromagnetic whorls; antennae hum with tiny induced currents; entire bodies function as resonant cavities, feeling out shifts in pressure and pattern.

The weirdness is not decoration—it is fundamental. Laws are suggestions that the living system edits.

A machine stalls in a quiet factory-cavity. It does not simply break; the space around it warps, slightly lessening the gravity in a narrow column so its components float apart in slow disassembly. Plant matter reaches in delicately, learning the machine’s shape as it drifts. A day later, the cavity has changed: the stalled engine is gone, and in its place is a new organ, half-plant, half-rust, doing roughly the same job with an entirely different anatomy. The moment of breakdown was simply drafted into the city’s development.

A predator overhunts in a sparsely populated branch of the hollow. Instead of prey going extinct, the geometry of that region stretches. Distances between food sources increase without any measurable expansion of material; corridors lengthen, their curves smoothing as if tugged from a great height. The predator’s territory becomes tiresome to traverse; its stride is not enough. Over time, it learns new behaviors or fails, but its decisions are negotiated not only with other organisms, but with the malleable relationship between space and effort.

Still, amidst the strangeness, a kind of stability holds. The cool, damp air cycles. Moisture condenses, falls, is pulled back up along vascular shafts. The plant-city breathes in distinctly: a slow, tidal intake where thousands of micro-stomata open at once, dragging the air in, filtering, exchanging, then a long, nearly inaudible exhale.

The darkness is its comfort state. Occasional flares of brighter light happen when some process requires it: a bloom of bioluminescent algae igniting along a conduit as a surge of current passes; the interior of a pod glowing with actinic intensity as something inside is sterilized, or cooked, or converted. But these events are short, localized, and the living mass seems eager to lapse back into dimness.

If there were once people here, their traces are now fused into the vegetal fabric. A half-buried rail line protrudes from a wall—its metal rails warped, but their parallelism preserved. Around them, the plant has grown matching rails of lignin, interleaving with the original, so that any vehicle now would ride both plant and metal at once. A former loading dock is now a blossom-shaped platform whose edge ripples when weight approaches, aligning itself with whatever passes; its memory of cargo has become generalized hospitality to weight.

Somewhere deep in the hollow, a boiler beats. You can’t reach it directly—the pathways keep rerouting—but you feel its presence in waves of slightly warmer air, in the periodic, very low tremor that passes through columns and floors. Steam, here, is not white; it is violet in certain wavelengths, cradling spores and micron-scale devices. It drifts through vents, condenses in different sectors according to a pattern that might be schedule, might be desire.

Processes fold over processes: moisture cycling through plant; electricity cycling through nerve-wire; fauna cycling through predation and symbiosis; geometry cycling through rearrangements that feel intentional but answer to no single will.

The whole environment has the mood of a mind still forming its thoughts. It is embryonic, not in the sense of smallness, but in the sense that most of what will eventually exist here is still uncommitted. Surfaces bear faint ghost-structures—underpatterns like half-erased blueprints. If you stare long enough at a wall, the raised veins and pits resolve into a plan for another corridor, another chamber, that hasn’t quite pushed through. Turn away, and you hear a soft crack, and when you look back, some of those lines have lengthened, as if encouraged by your attention.

In this hollow city of cool damp breath, plant and machine and animal share not just space, but rule-making. The laws of physics are inputs, not constraints—raw material for an ongoing act of construction.

The place is still deciding what it wants to be. And in the meantime, it grows.
