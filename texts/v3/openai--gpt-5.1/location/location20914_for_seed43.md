Under the hill, the world is hollow and busy.

You enter not through a grand gate but a slit in the earth—an oblique, low tunnel that smells of clay and damp wood. The air is warm and close, beaded with a fine sweat of condensation. Light seeps in from behind you and ahead, never very bright, but never dark enough for stars. Down here, illumination is an accumulation of sources: guttering oil lamps, smoldering braziers, pale animal-fat candles, a few shafts of daylight that punch through ventilation shafts and scatter into dust-moted halos.

The space you emerge into is not a cavern in the natural sense. Every surface bears tool marks: gouged, chipped, smoothed, pegged. This underground is made rather than found, an excavated warren bound together with timber, stone, rope, and packed earth. The scale is modest but crowded—like several cathedrals jammed shoulder to shoulder and then filled to the rafters with life.

### Terrain: Stacked Chambers and Worn Passages

The ground underfoot is not level; it undulates in long, shallow terraces and steps, as though the builders kept following some logic of layer and stratum. Walkways of split logs and flat stones span low, muddy troughs where water seeps and collects. In some places, the floor is ribbed with narrow channels carved by hands, guiding runoff toward sumps and cisterns. In others, it is packed smooth by countless bare feet and clawed paws.

Rooms and corridors fold into one another like a honeycomb that was once planned and then drifted into improvisation. Vaulted ceilings, arch-like but clearly carved, bulge and sag where the earth has slowly settled. Thick beams of dark wood—already old when they were laid—brace the weight above. They are polished to a greasy shine by hands, shoulders, and the brushing of furred flanks.

Walls are made of what was cut from them: loam tamped into bricks, laterite blocks pried from lower seams, clay plastered smooth and then etched with patterns. Lines of river stones are embedded at knee-height to act as ledges and low barriers. Everywhere you look, there are signs that this place has existed a long time: corners rounded by erosion and touch, carvings softened and blurred, ancient supports doubled with newer props.

At the edges of the settled area, the passages thin to narrow, rough-hewn tubes where only one person can pass at a time. Here, you hear the earth more clearly: the distant, soft cracking of roots, the granular hiss of dry soil trickling. These perimeters feel like the lungs of the place, always exhaling dust, always on the verge of being reshaped.

### Water: Pockets and Films

Water is never far, but it is never abundant enough to relax around. The ceilings glisten with condensation, beading along the grain of timber, tracing dark threads down the walls. Every so often, a fat drop falls, patting softly on stone, leather, fur.

Drainage channels, cut by hand with stone chisels or bronze blades, run like veins through the complex. They gather seepage and the sedimentwashed water brought from elsewhere into sumps lined with moss and bundled reeds. Here the water pools: shallow basins of brownish clarity, the surface trembling with the activity of tiny creatures.

Cisterns—low, domed chambers half-filled with water—are among the few open spaces free of constant passage. Their rims are worn hollow where buckets have knocked for generations. Around their edges, wet earth supports narrow fringes of life: pale green mosses, stringy liverworts, thin, almost translucent ferns with leaves that curl inward when touched.

In some of the smaller side rooms, water clings vertically. Canvas-like sheets of cured hide are stretched against the walls, their surfaces slick with condensed moisture that creeps downward into clay jars at their bases. People move through with small cups and ladles, diverting drips, adjusting the angle of the hides, murmuring to one another over the insect-hum of dripping and trickling.

### Materials: Everything Touched, Everything Made

Nothing in here is raw. Even the exposed soil has been hammered and shaped, faced with thin slabs of stone or woven with roots deliberately trained to grow into living braces. Each surface tells a story of manual craft:

- **Wood**: Split planks lashed with rawhide; bent saplings forming arches; lengths of bark peeled and repurposed as slats or shingles over earthen walls. Some beams are charred on the outside, deliberately scorched to resist rot, leaving them black and smoky-smelling even after many years.

- **Stone and Clay**: Rough stone tools chipped, flaked, rehafted; heavier blocks forming foundations for platforms and raised sleeping shelves. Clay is everywhere: smeared as daub over wattle, shaped into tiles for drainage, turned into plain vessels snugly nested in wall-niches. Simple metal appears here and there: blackened iron spikes, a few greenish copper rings binding jointed timbers.

- **Fiber and Hide**: Rope bridges span narrower chasms where the earth has slumped. Nets hang from ceilings capturing loose dust and falling debris. Woven reed mats cover damp ground. Thick curtains of stitched leather and felt divide spaces, control drafts, and muffle sound, but never completely.

Everywhere, things are repaired: a cracked beam reinforced with wooden braces and pegs, a ruptured wall patched with crisscrossed sticks, straw, and clay. There is no empty substrate; the idea of “bare ground” doesn’t exist here. If a space is not being stood on, leaned against, or climbed over, it is being used as a shelf, a nest, a garden, a trap, a shrine.

### Light: Filters and Patches

Illumination comes in fragments.

High above, nearly vertical shafts pierce the strata: rough-walled chimneys no wider than a person’s armspan. At their tops, somewhere in the sunlit world, they open as narrow vents camouflaged with piles of brush and carefully placed stones. Light pours down these tubes as oblique columns that shift over the day, painting bright ovals on floors and walls.

Around each shaft-bottom, the community has clustered activity that needs clarity of vision: workbenches for carving bone or wood, sorting tables for seeds and insects, places where hides are scraped and medicines ground. In the brighter patches you see the full array of colors usually lost in the underground: the reddish brown of polished laterite, the green-yellow of drying herbs, the pale pink of recently flayed skin, the deep black of smoke-stained rafters.

Elsewhere, lamps sputter with animal fat or pressed plant oils. Wicks in shallow clay saucers smolder, their flames trembling when someone passes too close. The smoke they exhale builds into a low haze near ceiling level, channeled slowly along the vaulted roofs toward ventilation shafts. Light here is sufficient but never gentle: it flickers, flares, or dims with each movement, each draft.

The overall effect is one of perpetual dusk, punctuated by sudden brilliance wherever sun shafts fall. Eyes that live here are adapted to subtle contrast, to reading shape and motion in half-light.

### Vegetation: Sparse, Cultivated, Tenacious

Plants are rare enough to be precious, but not absent.

Most vege­tation is intentional. In shallow stone troughs built under the best light shafts, earth has been piled into narrow beds. Stunted herbs with thick, waxy leaves grow there—plants bred or selected for shade tolerance and low water needs. Their smells cut sharply through the heavier odors of animals and human habitation: sharp resin, bitter green, a faint floral, slightly sour scent.

Delicate branching stems from surface plants are woven into trellises and coaxed along prepared wooden frames, their leaves pressed up toward the light. You see small, pale fruits hanging in sheltered corners, each one cradled in a sling of netting to protect it from marauding paws.

More opportunistic life creeps into the cracks. Tiny lichens and molds map themselves in mottled greys and greens along damp stone. Filaments of ghostly fungus trace up the backs of pillars, their fruiting bodies tucked in hidden crevices. At the edges of water sumps, thread-thin grasses and moss patches form miniature forests for insects.

Wild roots from the surface intrude like slow, patient invaders. Thick, gnarled strands worm through ceilings and walls, deflected, redirected, or exploited. Some roots have been carefully peeled and grafted into the architecture, split and spread to act as living rope or nailed in place with hardwood pegs. In one area, a wall has been cut away entirely to reveal a bulging braid of roots, now serving as a vertical garden: each fork and pocket filled with soil or compost, sprouting edible fungi and compact herbs.

### Fauna: Crowded, Layered Lives

The living density is striking. This is an environment of cohabitation, competition, and mutual use.

Humanoid inhabitants—call them people, though their exact form is their own—move with practiced ease through the labyrinth. They are lightly dressed for the warmth: rough-woven garments, leather aprons, wraps that leave arms free for work. Their skin and hair carry the permanent patina of smoke and dust. You hear their voices everywhere: shouting across wide chambers, murmuring in narrow passages, laughing, arguing, calling to animals, chanting under their breath as they work.

But they are only one layer of life.

Underfoot, small, quick things skitter: many-legged pale insects adapted to half-dark; sleek, rat-like mammals with sensitive whiskers; compact, horned lizards that feed on both. Larger creatures share these spaces not as domesticated pets in the modern sense, but as partners, scavengers, and sometimes prey.

You see:

- **Stocky, tusked herbivores** approximately pig-sized, their backs crusted with dried clay, snuffling along drainage channels for scraps. Their dung is collected in baskets, carried to fermentation pits, broken down into fertilizer and fuel.

- **Long-bodied climbing mammals** with prehensile tails and nimble fingers, moving across rope and root lattices under the ceiling, raiding hanging baskets when handlers are not vigilant. Some wear crude collars or bells. Others melt into the shadows at the sight of a human glance.

- **Burrowing omnivores**—blind, velvet-furred things—kept in soil pens near waste middens. They turn refuse and loam constantly, their tunneling aerating the earth used in growing troughs.

- **Insects in vast numbers**: bees or their analogues nesting in hollowed logs; beetles bred for their iridescent wing cases; pale larvae fattened in ceramic trays on a slurry of mashed roots.

Predators maintain balance. Slim, sinewy catlike forms stalk the peripheries, eyes catching lamp light in brief, sharp flashes. They slip in and out of cracks, keeping the rat-populations from overwhelming stores. Occasionally, a sudden shriek and scuffle speaks of a successful pounce.

Nothing is static. At any given moment, a cloud of winged insects may erupt from a hidden crevice, swirling toward a lamp; a lizard darts in to claim a struggling fly; a child lunges after the lizard and is scolded for knocking into a water jar.

### Sound: A Constant, Layered Roar

Silence does not exist here. Even in the quietest moments, the complex murmurs.

The dominant sound is the low, continuous crowd-noise of many beings in close quarters: overlapping voices, footfalls, the creak and scuff of tools, the squeak of ropes under shifting loads. Every impact reverberates along beams and through packed earth. A dropped stone in one chamber sends a dull thump several rooms away; a slammed door-curtain sends a shudder rippling through leather and rope.

There is the constant punctuation of animal calls: the grunting of the tusked beasts, the chitters and rattles of climbing things, the occasional sharp yelp or hiss of a territorial spat. Insect buzz swells into an almost electric hum near lamp-lit areas as moths, beetles, and flying gnats swirl.

Water adds its own quiet yet persistent rhythms: the drip-drip from ceilings into jars, the faint chuckle of runoff moving along channels, the soft slap of a bucket dipped into a cistern. In some corners, where fermenting pits are active, you hear a faint bubbling, like distant boiling.

Every hour, roughly, some communal action adds a louder beat: a chorus of calls marking shift changes in watch or work; the deep drumming of hands or sticks against hollow logs to signal a meeting or an alarm; a low chant rising and falling from a ritual corner.

Even sleep is not entirely quiet. In dormitory-like alcoves along the walls, you can pick out snores, restless movements, the rustle of straw. The soundscape is never threateningly loud—no single booming roar—but it is dense, a woven fabric of noises with very little open space in it.

### Dynamic Processes: Breathing, Settling, Cycling

The world here feels like a living organism, constantly breathing, digesting, adjusting.

The **air** moves in slow, deliberate currents drawn by heat and the difference between the underground warmth and the cooler surface. Smoke from cooking fires and lamps curls along the ceilings, has its paths, its invisible rivers. People know these flows intimately; they position braziers and firepits where the air will carry out fumes without suffocating them. Occasionally a shift in outside wind or collapse of an old vent changes the established pattern, and for a time the complex coughs and stings until new openings are carved or old ones widened.

The **earth** itself is not entirely stable. Over years, the weight of structures and the drying-and-wetting cycles of the soil cause subtle slumps. A hairline crack appears along a ceiling; within a season, it's a sag demanding reinforcement. Whole corridors may tilt a few degrees, so gradual that only the oldest inhabitants recall them as straight. These slow movements are read like weather—signs in the walls, in the way dust falls, in the new creaks of timbers.

**Biological cycles** are everywhere. Compost pits warm above ambient temperature, exhaling a rich, sour steam. Their contents, turned regularly, change color from bright refuse to uniform dark soil. Maggots appear, flourish, pupate, vanish into beetles or flyclouds. That soil, thick with nutrient, is then hauled in baskets to feed the trough-gardens, which in turn feed both people and livestock. The burying animals churn through the used beds afterward, mixing in more waste, and the cycle continues.

Predator and prey weave through this place in miniature dramas: insects devoured by frogs or lizards; those lizards in turn hunted by cats or by people, depending on cultural taste. Even molds have predators here—tiny mites grazing across their surfaces.

### Culture and Tools: Primitive, Focused, Skilled

The technology here is simple in principle, but refined by long practice. Stone tools are not crude shards but intricately flaked blades, polished adzes, serrated scrapers optimized over generations. Metal exists as a rarity: a few bronze chisels, some iron nails and hooks, treasured and carefully maintained. Fire is contained in clay-lined hearths with hinged stone lids to smother or reveal flame.

Construction is continuous. One group might be widening a corridor, their stone hammers ringing rhythmically against the walls, while another repairs a sagging roof with new latticework of saplings. Measurement is by body and memory, not abstract numbers: “as high as Three-Branch’s shoulders,” “as far as four strides of Old-Reed.”

There are unspoken rules of space: no one tampers with the root-garden wall without permission; no one blocks the slow trickle to a drinking sump; door-curtains to sleeping alcoves are pulled in a particular pattern when privacy is needed. Children learn, as much as any craft, the places where a careless noise carries too far, where a careless touch could trigger a cascade of soil.

### Weirdness: Subtle Oddness, A Logic of Its Own

For all its practical logic, the place has its own barely-unusual strangeness.

You notice talismans hanging at certain junctions: little bundles of dried moss, bones tied with hair, stones whose faces have been carefully, minutely scratched into patterns that defy easy reading. They are placed precisely where the earth’s creaks are loudest, where temperature shifts, where certain animals refuse to cross. Perhaps they are simply markers of hazard. Perhaps they are the community’s way of speaking to the unseen mechanics of subsurface life.

There is a quiet reverence around particular roots that plunge through roof and floor, polished by touch. Offerings—bits of fruit, a glimmering beetle carcass, a smear of fat—are sometimes found in little hollows beside them. No explanation is spoken aloud, but you see the way people avoid stepping directly on these roots, as if that would be akin to stepping on someone’s hand.

Time behaves slightly differently here. Without sky, the community has woven its own rhythms: work cyclings follow the brightness of the shafts, the cooling of the walls, the temperament of the animals and the breath of compost pits. You get the sense that, with the clocks of the surface removed, the underground has slid into a schedule braided tightly with biology and maintenance rather than the sun’s strict arc.

### In-Balance, Yet Always Adjusting

Despite the clutter, the heat, the noise, the place feels stable, as if it has reached a kind of equilibrium with the materials that form it and the life that fills it. Erosion and construction are not opponents but dance partners. Each new crack becomes an opportunity for reinforcement or reclamation; each fallen clump of soil seeds a new minor arrangement of structure, habitat, or tool.

Water paths are continually re-scribed as sediment builds, as beams swell and shrink, as roots insinuate themselves into channels. Populations of various animals boom and bust, yet rarely collapse entirely; people adjust—opening more traps, relocating breeding pens, altering waste flows.

Nothing about this underground is accidental. Every beam, every pool, every gap in a wall has been argued with, bargained for, tended. It is a dense, hand-made ecology in which dampness is husbanded, warmth is harnessed, and the slow, grinding weight of the overlying earth is not a threat but a given, a pressure that has shaped every choice.

Standing there, listening to the soft roar of it all, you realize: the place is not a burrow, not a city, not a cave, but something that has evolved from all three—a warm, damp, carved-out world below the surface, saturated with lives, voices, and the quiet, constant settling of time.
