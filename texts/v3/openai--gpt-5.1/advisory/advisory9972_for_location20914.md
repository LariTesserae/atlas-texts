Preparation for this place is less about gear and more about how you intend to exist inside a continuous, close, handmade ecology. Think like a respectful parasite trying not to overload a host.

I’ll break it down into:  
1) Physical conditions and body prep  
2) Gear: what to bring, what not to bring  
3) Behavior and etiquette  
4) Health and safety  
5) Time, sleep, and mental state  
6) Engagement: how to move, work, and learn there  

---

## 1) Physical Conditions & Body Prep

### Environment you’re walking into

- **Air**: Warm, humid, slightly smoky. Low-level chronic dust. Oxygen is present but the place breathes slowly; you’ll feel “thick” air rather than thin air.
- **Light**: Permanent dimness. Sharp, bright sun-shafts in specific spots; everywhere else is dusk flickering with smoky lamps.
- **Surfaces**: Packed earth, damp stone, wet wood, rope, uneven terraces. Nothing perfectly flat.
- **Density**: People, animals, insects, tools, cords, roots, low beams—little “empty” space.

### Train your body for:

- **Balance and joints**  
  - Practice walking on uneven terrain, low steps, sloping floors, and narrow log walkways.  
  - Strengthen ankles, knees, and hips: single-leg balance, slow squats, weighted step-ups.  
  - Stretch your lower back and hamstrings—you’ll do a lot of ducking, stooping, and sideways shifting in tight corridors.

- **Heat & humidity tolerance**  
  - Get accustomed to warm, still air: short sauna sessions or hot, poorly ventilated rooms, paying attention to how your breathing and heart respond.  
  - Hydration discipline: train yourself to sip, not gulp; you won’t have endless water.

- **Low light adaptation**  
  - Spend some evenings with only candles or low lamps. Learn to be comfortable with partial information and strong contrasts.  
  - Train your peripheral vision: notice movement and outline rather than detail.

- **Noise and crowding**  
  - The soundscape is constant; solitude is rare. If you’re sensitive, practice sleeping with layered ambient noise.  
  - Work on remaining calm when jostled or pressed in narrow spaces.

## 2) Gear: What to Bring, What to Avoid

You are entering a fragile, closed-loop system. Anything you bring is a potential contaminant, clog, or cultural insult. Your loadout should be:

- **Compact**
- **Low-tech**
- **Non-polluting**
- **Easily cleaned**

### Clothing

Think “work clothes for a hot, damp workshop” that can take smoke and clay.

- **Base layer**:  
  - Lightweight, breathable natural fibers if possible (linen/cotton) or non-shedding synthetics.  
  - Nothing swishy, shiny, or reflective—sound and glare draw attention.

- **Outer layer**:  
  - Thin, tough long-sleeved shirt, light trousers. You want skin covered against abrasion, fleas, and “what just crawled on me” without overheating.  
  - A light, packable shawl or wrap: can act as warmth, privacy screen, impromptu filter.

- **Footwear**:  
  - Flexible, grippy shoes with thin soles—think approach shoes or moccasin-style boots. Heavy boots are clumsy on narrow logs and rope bridges.  
  - Must be easy to clean; expect mud, dung, and sump water.  
  - Consider packable, thin sandals for sleeping/dorm areas if they remove outdoor shoes.

- **Head & hands**:  
  - Soft cap or bandana to protect from dust, dripping water, and low beams. No hard helmet unless they explicitly accept such gear; bumps are better than looking like you expect a collapse.  
  - Thin, tactile work gloves for climbing, grabbing rough wood, and handling filthy things without shredding your hands.

### Items on your body

- **Lighting**  
  - A small, dimmable headlamp or finger-sized flashlight, with a warm color temperature and very low lumen mode.  
  - Absolutely crucial: a way to block or heavily diffuse the beam (cloth or built-in dimmer). You *do not* want to blind or terrify residents whose eyes are used to dusk. Use only when necessary and always show it openly before turning it on.

- **Breathing & dust**  
  - A simple, washable cloth mask or buff. In normal areas you won’t need it, but during collapses, heavy smoke, or at compost pits, it’s useful. Nothing with a sci-fi respirator look; that reads as “I think your air is poison.”

- **Water & vessels**  
  - One **small** personal cup or narrow-mouthed bottle that can be boiled or scalded. Do not show up with a giant, clear plastic jug; it screams “I hoard” and “I brought weird material.”  
  - Accept that you are a guest in a tight water economy. You’ll drink what they offer, when they offer.

- **Tools** (keep modest)  
  - A sturdy, simple knife with a non-reflective blade. Sheathed, clearly a tool, not a weapon.  
  - A small multi-tool (pliers, screwdriver, awl, etc.) can be gold for quick repairs—once they trust you.  
  - No powered tools, no bright metals that will corrode toxins into their water or soil.

- **Medicinal basics**  
  - A minimal kit: disinfectant, a few bandages, anti-diarrheal and broad-spectrum antibiotic if you have it, something for pain.  
  - Keep packaging compact and pack out every scrap. They live in a closed loop; do *not* leave chemical trash.

- **Gifts / trade**  
  Bring very little, very carefully chosen.
  - Durable, non-contaminating items like:  
    - A spool of high-strength, non-rotting cord (demonstrate that it doesn’t shed fibers).  
    - A small number of sturdy needles or awls.  
    - A simple whetstone.  
  - Avoid: food from your world, seeds, modern plastics, batteries, large mirrors, any electronics. These are hard to explain and impossible for them to replicate or safely dispose of.

## 3) Behavior & Etiquette

You’re entering an organism, not a tourist site. Your attitude should be: “I am small; I am curious; I am careful.”

### First entry

- Move **slowly** through the entrance tunnel. Let your eyes adjust. Let them see your posture: upright but not swaggering, hands visible, nothing brandished.
- Don’t speak loudly at first. Let your voice join the hum rather than cut across it.
- Expect to be watched from multiple angles—above, below, behind curtains and root walls.

### Core rules

1. **Never block flow**  
   - Flow of bodies: Step aside quickly in circulation corridors; lean against walls rather than stopping in the middle.  
   - Flow of water: Don’t sit on, lean over, or rest items on drainage channels or near sumps. If you drop something in a channel, recover it immediately or ask for help—don’t leave it.  
   - Flow of air/smoke: Don’t hang anything from beams where smoke is visibly moving; they have established paths.

2. **Don’t touch infrastructure casually**  
   - Beams, root-braces, tensioned ropes, leather curtains, and mesh nets all mean “load-bearing” or “carefully tuned.”  
   - If you must lean or duck under, do it gently and with eyes open, as if you’re moving through a machinery room.

3. **Sacred / cautious spots**  
   - Polished roots with offerings: treat them like altars. No leaning, no sitting, no empty-handed staring. A small bow of the head is safe and respectful.  
   - Talismans at junctions: assume they mark thresholds or hazards. Slow your pace, match the behavior of locals around them. Don’t examine them up-close without explicit permission.

4. **Sound discipline**  
   - Don’t shout from room to room; if they have a call-and-response pattern, ask to learn it.  
   - Avoid sharp, sudden noises (clapping, slamming, metal on stone). Every hard impact transmits through the structure and can be mistaken for a problem.

5. **Eyes and gaze**  
   - Avoid staring at individuals as if they’re exhibits; hold your gaze gently and break it often.  
   - However, *do* look where you’re going; tripping into a water cistern or onto a tusked beast is far ruder than staring.

### Social positioning

- Initially, behave like a young apprentice:  
  - Offer to carry, fetch, hold things.  
  - Listen more than talk.  
  - If they assign you a small job quickly (sweeping a walkway, turning a compost fork, watching a pen), that’s acceptance, not punishment.

- Assume they have strict internal rules about:
  - Who may handle which animals.  
  - Who may touch which walls or dig where.  
  - How close you can come to food preparation / storage without being ritually or practically “cleaned.”

Ask, mimic, and err on the side of standing back.

## 4) Health & Safety

### Hazards

- **Respiratory stress**  
  - Smoke haze, organic vapors from compost, fine dust.  
  - If you begin to feel light-headed or nauseous in a specific chamber, quietly step out toward a corridor with better draft. Don’t dramatize it.

- **Physical injuries**  
  - Head bumps from low beams and roots.  
  - Twisted ankles on wet planks and narrow logs.  
  - Scrapes from rough timber and stone; infections are more risky in this environment.

- **Biological**  
  - Unfamiliar microbes in water and soil; parasites from animals and insects.  
  - Skin issues from constant damp + dirt (fungal rashes, infections).

### Countermeasures

- Drink only water they designate as drinkable. They have intimate knowledge of which cisterns are “raw” and which are for people.
- Wash hands before eating anytime you’ve handled soil, animals, or damp ropes.
- Keep even minor cuts clean and covered. In a warm, busy, microbe-rich environment, a scratch can escalate fast.
- If sleeping in dorm spaces:
  - Use your shawl as a barrier between you and communal bedding if possible.  
  - Be gracious about insects; they are part of the ecology. Swatting noisily all night will not endear you.

## 5) Time, Sleep, and Mental State

### Timekeeping

Surface-day rhythms are muted. Inside, cycles follow:

- Movement of bright shafts down walls and floors.
- Heat and coolness of earth and compost pits.
- Animal feeding and work-shifts, signaled by calls or drumming.

Don’t insist on your own schedule. Instead:

- Ask to be attached to someone’s routine. Let *them* tell you “we rest after this work,” “we eat when the light is here.”
- Leave your surface watch or phone buried in your pack. Constantly checking alien time will break your adjustment and mark you as separate.

### Sleep

- You’ll likely sleep in a communal alcove: low ceiling, close neighbors, background murmurs.
- If you’re used to silence and darkness, this can be disorienting.  
  - Practice sleeping with a constant low sound source beforehand.  
  - Bring earplugs only as a backup; you don’t necessarily want to block all environmental cues in case of emergency.

### Mental adaptation

- This place has **no big vistas, no clear horizon**, no real “outside” that you touch daily. Your mind may:
  - Crave distance and sky.  
  - Mistake the constant hum for anxiety.  
  - Feel trapped or tightly held.

To cope:

- Identify one or two “breathing spots” (e.g., near a wide shaft of light) and visit them regularly.
- Do small anchor rituals: counting breaths while watching the water drip, following the path of a sun patch across a beam.
- Limit mental projections of “collapse” fantasies. These people have maintained this place for generations; they are better at reading its stability than you.

## 6) Engagement: How to Move, Work, and Learn There

### Moving through the warren

- Learn the logic of their layout:  
  - Water always flows a certain way.  
  - Smoke moves along known channels.  
  - Animals use specific levels and side tunnels.

- When following a local:
  - Keep one or two steps distance, echo their gait and speed.  
  - When they duck or sidestep something “invisible,” assume it’s important—copy them rather than asking every time.

- Take mental note of:
  - Marks on walls at junctions; these likely encode direction, hazard, or function.  
  - How the noise changes as you approach certain zones (quieter near sleeping alcoves, higher-toned around insect-rearing, deeper and wetter near compost and sumps).

### Work and learning

If you want to be more than a tourist, you should aim to help their ongoing balancing acts.

Potential roles a newcomer can slide into with modest training:

- **Water maintenance assistant**  
  - Clearing debris from channels (carefully, under supervision).  
  - Carrying buckets, fetching tools.  
  - Watching for new leaks or dangerous drips in ceilings.

- **Animal-handling helper**  
  - Feeding, mucking pens, hauling dung to fermentation pits.  
  - Observing signs of stress or disease and reporting to their keepers.

- **Repair runner**  
  - Carrying beams, pegs, rope, and clay to repair points.  
  - Holding beams or lines in place while they fix them.  
  - Learning how they read cracks and sags as “weather.”

- **Garden tender**  
  - Watering trough plants with measured amounts.  
  - Turning soil in root-gardens after burrowing animals have passed.  
  - Harvesting small leaves or fungi under direction.

In all of these, the key is to **ask how your action fits into a cycle**: Where did this material come from? Where does it go next?

### Cultural bridges

You bring something rare: an outside vantage. Use it sparingly.

What you can offer without destabilizing:

- Comparative perspective on *patterns*, not specific technologies.  
  - “In another place, people also use smoke paths; they hang wet cloth here to catch soot,” not “Here’s a plastic filter that will break when I’m gone.”

- Body-based skills:  
  - Climbing or balancing to reach awkward maintenance points.  
  - Simple first aid for cuts and burns (once trusted).

- Listening and memory:  
  - Offer to remember and repeat their stories, genealogies of beams and rooms (“This beam was laid in Great-Flood season,” etc.). In a time-bound, oral culture, extra memory capacity is a gift.

## What Not to Do

Summarizing the serious pitfalls:

- Don’t introduce **foreign seeds, food scraps, or live organisms**. A single invasive fungus, plant, or insect from your kit could wreck their delicately balanced ecology.
- Don’t pour **soaps, chemical cleaners, or medicines** into their drains or soil. What you ingest or apply to your own body, you should pack out metabolically or in your kit.
- Don’t insist on **private space or silence** beyond what they can naturally grant. Privacy down here is curtain-based and behavioral, not architectural.
- Don’t try to **diagnose or “fix”** their systems. Whatever weirdness you notice is part of a solved problem you don’t yet understand.
- Don’t display **fear of the earth itself**. Anxiety about caves and “being buried” is something you can feel internally, but if you narrate it, you are effectively calling their whole world unsafe and wrong.

## Quick Checklist Before You Go

- Body conditioned for: heat, humidity, dim light, uneven ground, noise.
- Clothing: light, tough, muted; flexible shoes; head wrap; gloves.
- Gear: small dimmable light; cloth mask; minimal tools; tiny med kit; single small vessel; a couple of simple trade items.
- Mindset: apprentice, not explorer; guest, not inspector.
- Commitments to yourself:
  - You will not leave non-biodegradable or chemical residues.  
  - You will drink their water, follow their rhythms, and accept their sense of risk.  
  - You will contribute labor proportional to the space and resources you occupy.

If you go in prepared this way—lean, observant, and willing to be folded into their living system—you aren’t just “visiting a hollow hill.” You’re agreeing, for a while, to be one more moving part in a dense, ongoing act of survival.
