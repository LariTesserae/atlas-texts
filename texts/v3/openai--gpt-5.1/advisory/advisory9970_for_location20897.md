Physical preparation first, then mental/social, then practical interaction with the city itself.

---

## 1. Baseline: What kind of place are you entering?

You’re visiting a temperate, mid-depth, fully aquatic city built entirely from living plant structures, on a raised plateau far above a deeper ocean. Pressure is significant but not crushing. Light is dim, filtered cyan; space is huge and uncluttered. Everything that acts like “infrastructure” is some form of plant or symbiotic organism. Tools and technology are low-metal, low-fire, high-biological-skill.

That tells you the key constraints you must prepare for:

- Cold, dense water and sustained mid-depth pressure.  
- No breathable air.  
- No fire, no combustion-based tools.  
- No stable mineral floors or walls—everything you touch is alive.  
- Residents are gardeners and symbiont-tenders, not machinists or masons.  
- Sound is omnipresent and important; vibration through living tissue is likely part of their language.

Everything that follows is based on respecting those constraints.

---

## 2. Physiology & gear: How to survive just being there

### 2.1. Life-support: breathing & pressure

You have three main options; choose based on what your multiverse tech allows and what will least disturb the environment.

**Best: Closed-cycle rebreather, low-bubble design**

- Use a fully closed-circuit system that:
  - Produces *no* bubbles or only microbubbles that disperse quickly.
  - Recycles your own exhaled gas, with scrubbers.
- Reason: Bubbles and turbulence can:
  - Stress animals and disrupt communication.
  - Physically erode delicate plant tissue, especially in tunnels, alcoves, or narrow ramps.

**Pressure adaptation**

- This is not abyssal, but you’re deep enough that you must be:
  - Pre-acclimatized to intermediate pressures, or
  - In a pressure-regulated suit.
- If your multiverse travel tech can teleport you already acclimated: use that.  
  If not, descend gradually and let your suit adjust internal pressure in slow increments.

**Absolutely avoid:**

- Any system venting warm gas, fuel, or combustion byproducts.
- Any decompression sickness risk that might force an emergency, chaotic ascent.

---

### 2.2. Thermal protection

The water is cold for long stays.

- Wear an **insulated, neutrally buoyant suit** with:
  - Smooth exterior surfaces (no rough texture that can snag or scrape).
  - Slightly compressible insulation (or active heating) to keep you warm at pressure.
- Hands:
  - Thin, tactile gloves for fine interaction (you will be touching living tissue and delicate constructs).
- Face:
  - Full hood with integrated visor to maintain thermal balance around your head; cold-induced disorientation underwater is dangerous.

---

### 2.3. Buoyancy & locomotion

You want to move *with* the city’s slow rhythms, not fight them.

- **Neutral buoyancy** calibrated for this water density (it’s likely slightly denser than Earth’s average, thanks to organic load and salinity).
- Powered propulsion:
  - If you use it, keep it very low-thrust and *non-jetting*.
  - Favor **finned or undulating** systems over water jets or propellers.
  - Avoid creating sharp, localized currents that could:
    - Bend young fronds.
    - Stir sediment into biofilters.
    - Blow through bioluminescent colonies or symbiont “lanterns.”

For short distances inside the city, **use your body and fins**. Think “slow, deliberate snorkeler” more than “scooter diver.”

---

### 2.4. Skin contact & biosecurity

Everything structural is alive; you are a walking foreign microbiome.

- **Suit integrity:** Do not go in bare-skinned.
  - Your skin bacteria, fungi, and viruses could be invasive here.
  - Their microfauna could do things to you that no medic has a protocol for.
- **Pre-entry decontamination:**
  - Rinse your suit in an inert, non-toxic sterilizing solution before you arrive, if your travel hub provides that.
  - Strip off any terrestrial organic residues (soil, pollen, hair, etc.).
- **No shedding:**
  - Avoid loose fabric, exposed hair, or dangling items that could shed fibers or detritus.

Treat this city as if it were a fragile reef with no built-in resistance to your world’s life forms—and vice versa.

## 3. Sensory & cognitive preparation

### 3.1. Light and visibility

- Expect a soft, low-contrast, bluish-green visual world:
  - No sharp shadows, only gradual gradients of brightness.
  - Long sight-lines (20–30 meters or more) but muted detail at distance.
- Your visor:
  - Use **high dynamic range optics** to pick up subtle contrast, but do not flood the world with your own light.
  - If you need illumination:
    - Use **dim, narrow-beam, warm-spectrum** lights.
    - Turn them off whenever possible.
- Night:
  - If you remain through local “night,” lower your lights to near-zero to see their bioluminescent infrastructure. Your bright lamp could overwhelm and disrupt signaling and navigation for residents and animals alike.

---

### 3.2. Sound and vibration

Sound is dense, layered, and meaningful here.

- Ambient audio will include:
  - Continuous roar of water moving along plateau flanks.
  - Deep booms and throbs from distant surf.
  - Creaking and groaning of giant plant structures flexing.
  - Clicks, snaps, and chimes from animals.
  - Long, low resonant tones from huge creatures rubbing the trunks.

Many of these likely carry information.

**Your preparation:**

- **Disable noisy electronics.**
  - No active sonar, no pinging radars unless you have explicit consent and know the frequencies that are “empty” of local use.
- **Suit quieting:**
  - Dampen any mechanical hums.
  - Use soft pumps with anti-resonant mounting if needed.
- **Train yourself to slow down.**
  - Rapid, thrashy motion is not only rude but sonically disruptive; every kick, bump, or scrape will resonate.

This city is essentially a giant, continuous instrument. Treat it like being inside a concert hall *during* the performance.

---

### 3.3. Time perception

Things here move slowly.

- Plant motion: Fronds reposition over minutes; trunks shift over seasons and years.
- Large animals: Move with gliding slowness; their “fast” might be your “lazy swim.”
- Residents (if similar): Very likely have **long planning horizons**. Infrastructure is coaxed into place over years or generations.

That means:

- Don’t expect fast responses to non-critical requests.
- Don’t equate slow movement with disinterest or low intelligence.
- Be ready for long pauses in interaction; their “thinking speed” may be different, or their communication systems may be physically slower.

You’re coming from a culture that measures projects in weeks; they appear to build in centuries.

## 4. Etiquette: Not hurting the city

### 4.1. Contact with living structures

Assume *every* surface has a function, even if you don’t see it.

**General rules:**

- **No cutting or breaking anything.**
  - Do not treat a frond as a handhold or a trunk as something to test your knife on.
- **Minimize grip pressure.**
  - Where you must steady yourself:
    - Prefer older, smooth, pale tissues that look hardened and heavily used as thoroughfares.
    - Avoid fuzzy, filament-covered or obviously delicate fronds—those are probably active filters, symbiont beds, or nursery structures.
- **Look for worn pathways.**
  - Smooth, lightly scuffed surfaces, with little parasitic overgrowth, are likely “official” routes.
  - Areas thick with colonizers and algae are probably not maintained as walkways.

If in doubt, hover.

---

### 4.2. Structural respect

This city functions via **load-bearing trunks, buttress roots, and trained platforms**.

- Never:
  - Wedge tools or gear into cracks.
  - Hang your equipment from branching hinges or young offshoots.
  - Lean heavy objects against living pillars.
- Watch for:
  - **Scars and graft rings**: these are evidence of long-term shaping work; don’t tamper.
  - **Dead or slumped stems in use as bridges or walls**: these may be stable, but still, they’re part of the architecture; tread (or swim through) gently.

Remember that if a key trunk is stressed or infected, its decline is measured in decades, but you could trigger the problem in a moment.

---

### 4.3. The edges & the drop-off

Near the plateau’s boundary:

- Currents are stronger and more variable.  
- There’s a rain of organic debris feeding deep ecosystems below.  
- Open-water predators pass through.

Your behavior there:

- Don’t:
  - Dump anything over the edge: waste, equipment, samples.
  - Shine powerful lights out into the abyss—this may draw in hunters or confuse deep fauna.
- Do:
  - Keep to the inner side of the big overhanging fronds unless guided to a lookout.
  - Be aware that sudden upwellings or downwellings could pull you in directions you didn’t intend.

## 5. Interaction with residents (who and however they are)

You haven’t seen them clearly, but you do know:

- They have tools—bone or dense reed handles, stone blades, woven fibers.
- They hollow tunnels and smooth them via repeated passage.
- They arrange bioluminescent organisms in deliberate patterns and rhythms.
- They train plants into platforms, ramps, and fences over long periods.

So they are:

- Skilled **horticultural engineers**, not mechanized builders.
- Likely extremely sensitive to:
  - Growth patterns.
  - Symbiotic balance.
  - Sound and vibration through the plant tissues.
  - Current flow and its effect on nutrient capture.

### 5.1. Basic behavior when you first encounter them

Until you understand their body language and sensory channels:

- **Approach slowly** and laterally, not head-on.
- Maintain:
  - Clear distance.
  - Hands visible and away from tools.
- Avoid:
  - Rapid gestures.
  - Pointing sharply at them or their structures.
  - Grabbing any part of their bodies (obvious, but underwater panic can erase manners).

If they use the trunks as a communication medium (tapping, rubbing, pressing):

- Do **not** imitate this without being invited or taught.  
  You might accidentally send “danger,” “disease,” or “attack” signals.

---

### 5.2. Communication strategies

You speak air and symbols; they may speak light, movement, and vibration.

**Prepare multiple channels:**

1. **Visual icons**:
   - A flexible screen or a waterproof tablet that can show simple pictograms:
     - You: a generic biped icon.
     - Them: blank icon that you let them modify or point to.
     - Concepts: “friend / not-friend,” “hurt / not-hurt,” “stay / leave,” etc.
   - Keep brightness low and use gentle color transitions; avoid strobing or harsh flashes.

2. **Very low-intensity vibration**:
   - A small, hand-held pad that can produce simple rhythmic pulses you can press against a trunk *if invited*.
   - Program only a few patterns:
     - “Hello” (soft, slow, symmetrical).
     - “Stop” (short, isolated pulses).
     - “Thank you” (longer, gentle fade-out).

3. **Writing or drawing surfaces**:
   - Waterproof slate or thin plastic sheet with erasable markings.
   - Draw simple shapes, arrows, circles, and see if they respond with their own marks.

Start as an observer; let them lead. If they appear uninterested, do not press.

## 6. What not to bring or do

### 6.1. Technology that conflicts with this world

Leave behind:

- Open flames, match-based tools, any combustion engine.  
  Fire is nonsensical here and will just make you look reckless or insane.
- Harsh chemicals:
  - Industrial detergents.
  - Strong acids/bases.
  - Petroleum-derived lubricants that might leak.
- Weapons designed to damage structures:
  - Explosives.
  - Underwater firearms.
  - High-energy lasers or cutting beams.
- Loud sensors:
  - High-powered sonar.
  - Strong EM-field generators.

If your standard multiverse kit includes defensive tools, seal them inert unless you are truly in existential danger.

---

### 6.2. Behaviors that are especially harmful here

- Stirring sediment:
  - Fin against the plateau surface can create clouds that clog filter feeders.
- Stripping samples:
  - Do not casually take plant tissue, symbiont colonies, or animals as souvenirs or “specimens.”
  - Any “sampling” should be pre-agreed with local authorities, and even then, kept minimal.
- Overstaying in one spot:
  - Lurking in a narrow passage may physically block the slow passage of large residents or their infrastructural animals.

## 7. Orientation inside the city

### 7.1. Understanding the layout

Major features:

- **Trunks**: huge, columnar, widely spaced. Think “living skyscraper cores” in an open plaza.
- **Platforms**: radial, plate-like growths used as plazas or courtyards.
- **Ramps**: spiraling braided vines/tendrils between levels.
- **Vertical gaps**: chimneys of clear water, sometimes with light shafts from above.

Get your bearings early:

- Pick a particular trunk with distinctive color or branching as your reference “north.”
- Map relative positions:
  - “We’re between the thick-buttress district and the slender-branch district.”
- Notice light levels:
  - Brighter = closer to edge or upper strata.
  - Dimmer = deeper or under denser canopy.

Record a simple mental or actual map of three anchor points—three major trunks with unique features—so you can always triangulate your position.

---

### 7.2. Moving in sync with the environment

The city periodically “breathes” with large, slow swells:

- All broad fronds will lean in one direction, then slowly return.
- Trunks groan and hum in response.

When you feel this:

- Pause your travel.  
  Let the swell pass rather than fight it.
- Use it:
  - Time your longer glides to coincide with the returning flow.
  - Treat it as this world’s equivalent of an elevator: you’re being gently pushed along established paths.

This makes you less tiring to yourself and less disruptive to the local hydraulics.

## 8. Mental preparation: what it will feel like

Expect:

- **A sense of vastness with intimacy**:
  - You will see far, but everything near you will be layered with slow details—fuzzy biofilms, quiet animals, subtle color bands.
- **Constant low-level sound and vibration**:
  - It may feel like always being on a train whose tracks are a singing forest.
  - You may feel these hums in your bones through your suit.
- **A different kind of “city energy”**:
  - No frantic motion, no sharp corners, no bright signs.
  - Yet plenty of activity, just stretched across longer timescales.

It helps to:

- Practice slow, controlled breathing (even through a rebreather) to match your internal tempo to the environment’s.
- Accept that you are a brief, fast intrusion into a place that barely recognizes “urgent” as a meaningful category.

## 9. If you’re invited to participate

If residents explicitly show you a task or a ritual:

- **Gardening / coaxing:**  
  They might guide your hands (or gesture) to:
  - Position a brace to direct growth of a new platform.
  - Carefully scrape off a parasitic growth from a thoroughfare.
  - Help hollow out a section of dead stem for storage or nesting.

Your posture:

- Follow *exact* indications.
- Don’t improvise cuts or alterations.
- Treat every action as something that might shape centuries of architecture.

- **Listening / observing:**  
  They may lead you to:
  - Place your hand or suit against a trunk during a period of resonant communication.
  - Watch a coordinated sequence of bioluminescent pulses along corridors at night.

Your role then is not to interpret immediately, but to witness. Later, you can correlate patterns with events.

---

## 10. Exit strategy

Plan your departure before you arrive.

- **Pressure:**  
  Ascend back through the water column with the same care you used to arrive:
  - No sudden, panicked rush to the surface.
  - If your tech allows instantaneous extraction, ensure it manages de-pressurization safely.

- **Contamination:**  
  When you leave:
  - Treat your suit and gear as contaminated with unknown biology.
  - Decontaminate thoroughly before entering any air environment in your own universe.

- **Information:**  
  Your notes, maps, and recordings:
  - Store them securely.
  - Be very cautious about publishing exact locations or vulnerabilities; you don’t know how other travelers might behave.

---

### Summary

To prepare for this place, become:

- Quiet: in sound, in motion, in technology.
- Insulated: against cold, pressure, and bio-exchange.
- Neutrally buoyant: physically and socially—never heavy-handed.
- Patient: in how you move, how you listen, and how you expect responses.

Think of yourself less as a tourist and more as a respectful guest in a cathedral-reef that has been growing its own city out of living tissue for centuries. Your job is to pass through, learn, and leave only faint ripples in its slow, resonant song.
