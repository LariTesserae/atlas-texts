Preparation for this city begins with accepting that you are the wrong kind of organism for it.

Your body is built for water, nitrogen-oxygen air, and modest temperatures. This place runs on dry reactive gases and heat gradients. Every sense you have will misinterpret what it’s experiencing. Prepare as if you were walking into an industrial furnace crossed with a chemical reactor and a fragile, alien cathedral.

I’ll break preparation into: physiology & gear, environment & hazards, movement & navigation, interacting with the ecosystem, psychology, and exit strategy.

---

## 1. Physiology & Baseline Risks

### 1.1. Absolute dryness and alien solvents

- There is *no water* in this system. Any water you bring is a foreign, reactive contaminant.
- The “humidity” is resins, oils, and volatile organics. Many will be:
  - Highly flammable or at least heat-sensitive.
  - Chemically aggressive to mucous membranes and lung tissue.
  - Capable of dissolving or penetrating organic materials and some plastics.

**Human risk profile without protection:**

- Instant dehydration of eyes, lungs, and skin. Your body constantly leaks water; here the gradients will pull it out aggressively.
- Many organics in the air would likely dissolve lipids in cell membranes or denature proteins.
- Think “slow chemical burn” more than “simple dryness.”

You must treat the ambient atmosphere the way you would treat an unfiltered exhaust stream inside a refinery.

## 2. Life Support & Suit Design

### 2.1. You need full environmental separation

Bare minimum: a fully sealed, positive-pressure suit with its own life support. Realistically, you want something close to a lightweight space suit tuned for high internal humidity.

**Core requirements:**

- **Hermetic seal:** No skin contact with ambient air. No unprotected eye, ear, or respiratory exposure.
- **Positive pressure:** Slightly higher internal pressure than the surroundings so leaks blow outward, not inward.
- **Independent atmosphere:** A closed breathing loop; no “filtering ambient air.” You must not inhale anything from outside.
- **Moisture retention:** Your suit should:
  - Keep internal humidity high to prevent you from desiccating.
  - Reclaim sweat and exhaled water (a simple rebreather with condensation traps, or a more advanced recycler).
- **Thermal management:** 
  - The air is “hot and thick,” and certain mats radiate low red-black heat.
  - You need active cooling (liquid cooling undergarment or phase-change packs) far more than insulation.
  - The suit must cope with **chronic heat** and **occasional hotter pulses** from vents.

### 2.2. Chemical compatibility

The air contains reactive gases, volatile organics, and fine abrasive mineral dust.

Build the outer layer like this:

- **Outer shell:** Heat-tolerant, chemically inert material:
  - Options: PTFE composites, high-temperature fluoropolymers, ceramic-fiber fabrics, or metallic mesh over high-temp textiles.
  - Avoid simple rubbers and many standard plastics: they’ll soften, swell, or crack.
- **Joint protection:**
  - Hard or semi-hard joints with dust seals; avoid flexible rubber bellows if possible.
- **Visor/faceplate:**
  - Thick, scratch-resistant, high-temperature visor (aluminosilicate or borosilicate glass, or coated quartz; polycarbonate may deform or craze).
  - Anti-fog on *inside*, dust- and oil-shedding coating on *outside*.
- **Filters & vents (if any suit exhausts):**
  - Multi-stage particulate filtration on exhaled suit air so you don’t spray condensed water into the environment (both to avoid contaminating ecosystems and to avoid creating reactive droplets outside).
  - You do not take in any outside air.

### 2.3. Internal life support

- **Oxygen:** Enough compressed O₂ for your mission, plus 50–100% safety margin. No scavenging from environment.
- **CO₂ scrubbing:** Solid chemical scrubbers or a rebreather loop.
- **Humidity management:**
  - Internal air should be in the “tropical” range (60–80% relative humidity) to spare your lungs and mucosa.
  - Condensed water collected into an internal reservoir for drinking; treat and reuse.
- **Cooling:** 
  - If you don’t have an active cooling system, you must limit exposure to short excursions and accept reduced physical exertion.

## 3. Protection of Senses & Body Integrity

### 3.1. Eye & sensory protection

- Your visor needs:
  - High contrast enhancement (amber/bronze tint, polarizing layers).
  - Optional low-light amplification or IR overlay; the ambient light is dim and amber.
- Carry:
  - A headlamp or chest-lamp with broad, diffuse beam calibrated to not heat local mats significantly.
  - A narrow-beam spot for inspection; use sparingly.

### 3.2. Hands & dexterity

- **Outer gloves:** Chemically resistant, cut- and abrasion-resistant.
- **Inner gloves:** Comfort layer; moisture-wicking.
- Expect to be clumsy. Plan tasks that do not require very fine manipulation.

### 3.3. Skin & microbiome

Bringing human-associated microbes into this system is a potential contamination problem; conversely, local substances may foster unknown reactions on your skin.

- Full body coverage is mandatory.
- Pre-suit:
  - Clean yourself with non-residue soap.
  - Avoid oils, perfumes, or loose powders.
- Assume that if your suit breaches, evac is urgent: chemical irritation or injury can be slow but irreversible.

## 4. Environmental Hazards & How to Read Them

### 4.1. Heat as structure and life

- **Heat is life here.** Hotter zones = biologically/chemically busier.
- Thick mats, glowing faintly, and regions around vents are the most dynamic and potentially hazardous.
- Dangerous behaviors:
  - Standing over or too close to active vents during a “pulse.”
  - Resting against hot mats or ceramic structures that are near their softening ranges.

Carry:

- **Infrared sensor** (thermal camera or handheld thermometer). Use it constantly:
  - Avoid very high gradients under your feet and along your route.
  - Map heat “currents” like you would map water flow.

### 4.2. Reactive gases and invisible “liquids”

Pods release denser-than-air gas that slumps and flows along the floor and in carved channels. To you:

- Invisible “puddles,” “streams,” and “tides” of gas.
- Likely corrosive or strongly reactive with water or organics.

Treat every basin, channel, and floor depression as if it might be:

- Filled with an invisible chemical that will:
  - Attack your suit.
  - Generate heat on contact with even microscopic water (like a pile of sodium in a puddle).
- Practical rules:
  - Avoid stepping down into channels or basins.
  - Use *bridges, ramps, and elevated paths* as much as possible.
  - If you must cross, place a probe or sacrificial stick first and watch for reactions (thermal rise, condensation, discoloration, or local agitation of mats).

### 4.3. Falling structures and slow fractures

The towers and latticework are old, constantly readjusting.

- You’ll hear:
  - Creaks, pops, glass-like cracks.
  - Rolling booms transmitted through floor and air.
- A falling limb:
  - Doesn’t plummet unchecked—it gets caught and seeded by mats—but it’s still a heavy impact hazard.

Mitigation:

- Avoid walking directly under heavy overhangs or long, cantilevered structures, especially ones that look heavily scored, pitted, or recently fractured.
- If you hear a sharper, nearby crack, **stop and look up and around**:
  - Identify any moving fragments.
  - Seek shelter under thicker, more massive supports rather than under fine filigree.

### 4.4. Surface stability

The floor is fused mineral with shallow depressions and channels; overlaid dust and brittle shards can make it slippery.

- Wear:
  - High-traction, heat- and chemical-resistant soles.
- Use:
  - A walking staff or probe to test footing ahead, especially near basin edges and steep transitions.
- Watch for:
  - Areas where mats have softened underlying stone and it has sagged:
    - These may be structurally weaker.
    - Don’t trust every ledge to bear your weight.

## 5. Movement & Navigation in a Vertical City

### 5.1. Map the voids, not only the towers

The landscape is mainly vertical and “porous”:

- Dense clusters of towers and bridges.
- Huge wells of open air between them.

Your biggest physical-risk scenarios:

- Misjudging a ledge or balcony edge and stepping into open air.
- Slipping on dust/glass shards near those edges.

Mitigation:

- Equip yourself with:
  - **Harness and tether**: clip into fixed points when near big drops.
  - **Light climbing gear** compatible with your suit (chemical-inert ropes or webbing, metallic carabiners).
- Walk:
  - With a consistent rule: you stay at least one full step back from any edge unless you are clipped in.
- Use:
  - Existing railings made from dense fiber bundles when they appear—but treat them as partially living or fragile. Test gently before leaning.

### 5.2. Using the built circulation

Ramps spiraling around major columns, worn steps and notches:

- These are the primary “roads” of the former inhabitants.
- Deep wear = frequent, safe past use, but also long-term structural fatigue.

To navigate:

- From your entry point, identify one major column with a spiral ramp system.
- Stick close to that axis as your main vertical reference.
- Use simple markers:
  - Non-contaminating, high-contrast tags you can retrieve later:
    - E.g., small ceramic or metal tokens etched with arrows, placed in non-reactive niches.
- Avoid:
  - Forcing your way through tight mats or growths to create new paths. If there’s no human-scale opening, treat it as a wall.

### 5.3. Timing movements with the “pulse”

The world’s metabolism cycles as vents open/close and hot gas pulses through:

- During strong pulses:
  - Ambient heat spikes.
  - Mats tighten and shift.
  - Crusts churn, dust movement intensifies.
- During lulls:
  - Things relax.
  - Less gas in motion, slightly safer and more predictable.

Strategy:

- Spend a while just *observing one cycle* before pushing deeper:
  - Identify approximate duration and intensity of pulses.
- Plan your route to move **during quieter phases**:
  - Avoid standing near vents or under flexible membranes when the pulse increases—sudden expansions or collapses could destabilize structure or shower dust.

## 6. Interacting With the Ecosystem

### 6.1. Do not be a contaminant

Your mere presence is contamination risk:

- Trace leaks of water vapor, organics from your suit material, exhaled gases.
- Your temperature signature, if too localized and intense, could alter local gradients.

To minimize impact:

- Keep suit perfectly sealed.
- Use:
  - Low-intensity lighting that doesn’t add much heat to any single surface.
- Don’t:
  - Pour out water or release humid air.
  - Leave trash, tools, or devices behind.

### 6.2. Don’t touch the “plants”

The mats, spines, pods, and crusts are all components of a linked, living system.

Avoid:

- Leaning on mats or using them as handholds.
- Snapping spines to clear a path.
- Prying open pods.
- Poking reactive crystalline crusts at basin bottoms.

Consequences could include:

- Chemical exposure (for you).
- Triggering sudden growth or gas release.
- Collapsing or weakening structural components that double as “roots” or heat engines.

If you must stabilize yourself:

- Use:
  - Inert structural stone that’s bare and clearly lifeless.
  - Artificial anchors you place yourself (e.g., temporary expansion bolts into non-active stone).

### 6.3. Respect structural-living hybrids

Remember: “architecture is organism.”

- A ledge might be:
  - A walkway.
  - A vital heat-exchange surface.
- Hollow chambers with smoothed floors and leveled thresholds:
  - Old habitation spaces.
  - Possibly now repurposed metabolically by the mats.

When exploring former living quarters:

- Move slowly, observe how mats occupy these spaces.
- If a chamber is densely overgrown:
  - Assume it has been re-integrated into the system’s metabolism. Entry risks altering that balance.

## 7. The Ghosts of Inhabitants & Their Technology

### 7.1. Recognizing worked spaces

Signs you’re in an anthropogenic (or analogous) zone:

- Smooth floors with visible wear.
- Thresholds and railings.
- Circular plinths with staining bands (where objects rested).
- Chisel marks and tool scars.

These areas are ideal:

- As relatively stable, open spaces to rest, observe, or take measurements.
- To understand the scale, morphology, and habits of the former inhabitants.

Still, be careful:

- Modifications have partially “melted” into the living system:
  - Doorways might have narrowing, sagging tops.
  - Ramps may have thickened under new growth.

### 7.2. Don’t expect tools, but watch for structural “machinery”

You won’t find:

- Loose tools, metal, ceramics, typical human artifacts.

You *might* find:

- Intentionally weakened ribs or slots designed to be fractured in controlled ways.
- Elevated platforms overlooking wells—likely communal or observation points.

Treat these as:

- Clues to past safe routes.
- Locations where the environmental gradients were especially important (e.g., vantage points to watch weather-like gas flows).

## 8. Sensing, Recording, and Studying Safely

### 8.1. Instruments

Bring instruments that are:

- Chemically inert on the exterior.
- Capable of logging without you needing to expose yourself.

Recommended:

- **Thermal imager** (handheld or helmet-mounted).
- **Multi-gas sensor** (inside a small protective hood that lets a tiny sampling flow in, then exhausts back into your suit or a sealed scrubber).
- **High-resolution camera** with low-light capability and IR/UV filters.
- **Laser rangefinder** to map voids and distances—ensure power levels that don’t heat or damage delicate surfaces.
- **Acoustic recorder**:
  - Pick up cycle patterns, pulses, booms, and cracks.
  - Sealed microphone array outside your suit to capture structure-borne sound.

### 8.2. Sampling policy

Physical sampling is ethically and practically risky.

If you absolutely must:

- Prefer loose dust or fallen shards that are clearly detached from living mats or structures.
- Use:
  - Tongs or scoop tools with chemically inert tips.
  - Immediately place samples into sealed, rigid containers with sufficient volume to buffer evolving gases.
- Never:
  - Chip away at active crusts.
  - Peel away living mats.

Remember that your samples may react violently with any moisture later on. Treat them as hazardous lab materials upon return.

## 9. Psychological Preparation

### 9.1. Sensory atmosphere

Expect:

- Dim, dirty-amber light.
- No sky. No horizon. Only layered ceiling and wells of air.
- Constant noise:
  - Crackling, popping, groaning, distant thunder.
  - Occasional sharp fractures.

These cues can induce:

- Disorientation (no familiar up/down cues except gravity).
- Claustrophobia (sealed room, no outside).
- Vertigo (looking across or down through large emptiness).
- A sense of insignificance and being “out of place.”

Countermeasures:

- Training in confined-environment and cave-like settings.
- Establish personal rituals:
  - Regular status checks of life support.
  - Periodic orientation: “Where am I? How do I get back?”
- Don’t go alone:
  - At least two travelers, linked by radio and tethered when near dangerous edges.

### 9.2. Cultural and existential mindset

You’re entering a place where:

- Life is slow and mineral.
- Cities grow instead of being built.
- The difference between “urban” and “wild” is intention, not material.

Prepare to:

- Suspend your usual intuitions about what is “alive,” “dead,” “natural,” or “artificial.”
- Accept that:
  - The builders are gone.
  - Their alterations have been metabolized into the environment.
- Your role:
  - Brief observer.
  - Ideally, non-interfering witness.

## 10. Logistics & Exit Strategy

### 10.1. Time limits

Your stay is limited by:

- Life support (O₂, CO₂ scrubber capacity, cooling).
- Psychological strain.
- Risk of cumulative microdamage to your suit from dust, abrasion, and chemical exposure.

Plan:

- A strict maximum mission duration (e.g., several hours at most for early visits).
- Waypoints where you check:
  - Suit integrity.
  - Consumables (oxygen, power, coolant).
  - Mental state.

### 10.2. Route discipline

Before entry:

- Define:
  - Primary route (down to a certain depth or up to a certain platform).
  - One or two pre-identified alternate routes using the same main axis.
- During exploration:
  - Drop periodic non-reactive markers and log their placement.
  - Maintain continuous orientation toward your entry point (column, ramp, or other large-scale landmark).

If something goes wrong (suit breach, structural shock, disorientation):

- Priority is to:
  - Move to the nearest known, stable, bare-stone chamber or platform.
  - Stop movement near living mats and reactive basins to avoid stumbling into them.
  - Assess damage and decide whether to abort.

### 10.3. Decontamination on exit

When leaving:

- Assume your exterior is coated with:
  - Reactive dust.
  - Adsorbed organics from the air.
- Decon procedure:
  - In an intermediate airlock-like space:
    - Gently brush and vacuum dust with inert tools.
    - Optionally rinse with a compatible, non-water, non-reactive fluid (if you’ve prepared one that won’t react violently with any clinging salts or oils).
- Only reintroduce your suit to a water-rich environment **after** verifying that no dust or residues remain that might react exothermically.

---

### In summary

Approach this city as:

- An enclosed, active, heat-driven biome and reactor.
- A cathedral grown from stone and “plants” that are really chemical engines.
- A place where your water-based body is fundamentally incompatible with the medium of life.

You must bring:

- A fully sealed, chemically resistant, cooled life-support suit.
- Tools that let you *see* heat, void, and subtle motion.
- Climbing and safety gear for vertical, hollow spaces.
- A deep commitment to non-interference and strict route discipline.

If you prepare as if you were entering a living volcanic reactor that has, over vast timescales, learned to architect itself, you’ll be closer to ready—not comfortable, never safe in a casual sense, but as prepared as a human organism can plausibly be for a world that has never known rain.
