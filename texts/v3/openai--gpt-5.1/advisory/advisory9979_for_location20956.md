Preparation for visiting this city is less about gear and more about how you let it change the way you move, sense, and think. It is a place where almost nothing is aggressively hostile, but everything is subtly different—physics, ecology, infrastructure, even the way “attention” seems to work. You’ll be safest and get the most out of it if you plan around five themes:

1. **Body & health** – what to wear, breathe, and touch.  
2. **Perception & navigation** – how to see, orient, and not get lost in a slow, shifting machine.  
3. **Behavior & etiquette** – how not to fight the city’s logic (or trigger defensive routines).  
4. **Technology & tools** – what from your home universe will work, fail, or be co‑opted.  
5. **Psychological readiness** – how to handle a place that is quiet, almost sentient, and very patient.

## 1. Body & Health: Clothing, Environment, and Exposure

### Climate & moisture

The city is:

- Warm (but not tropical hot)—think “perpetual humid evening.”  
- Persistently damp: condensation, gentlerains, moist pockets in courtyards and lower levels.  
- Dim, with no harsh sunlight.

Prepare as if for a humid, shaded coastal city with lots of concrete and gardens, but alien physics.

**Clothing**

- **Base layers:**  
  - Light, breathable, quick‑dry fabrics (synthetic or fine merino). Cotton will stay clammy.  
  - Long sleeves and long pants, even though it’s not cold; they manage condensation and protect against surfaces, plants, and microfauna.

- **Outer layer:**  
  - A **thin, non-rustling, hooded shell** that resists both liquid water and fine mist. It shouldn’t squeak or crinkle; sound travels too well here, and sudden noises feel out of place. Softshell, not hard raincoat.

- **Footwear:**  
  - Grippy soles with good traction on slick, smooth composite, wet moss, and shallow mud. Avoid thick clunky hiking boots; you’ll be on terraces and platforms as much as “ground.”  
  - Fast drainage and quick‑dry linings; your feet *will* get damp.

- **Accessories:**  
  - Light gloves with tactile sensitivity (think climbing or lab gloves): you’ll often test unknown surfaces before fully trusting them.  
  - Head covering or hood to manage constant mild condensation and veils of rain.

### Air & respiration

You’re in a place with:

- High humidity.  
- Trace “ozone-like” smells and possibly exotic energetic processes near nodal arrays.  
- Abundant microbial and nano‑scale life/infrastructure.

**Respiratory precautions:**

- Bring a **comfortable, tight‑sealing mask or respirator** with interchangeable filters. You probably don’t need to wear it constantly in open terraces and courtyards, but you should have it:
  - In **lower levels** with luminous phenomena and visible aerosols.  
  - Near **vents** exhaling warm fog.  
  - Around nodal arrays, especially if you feel bone‑deep pulls or odd inner-ear sensations.

- Filters:  
  - **P3 / N100 particulate** plus an **activated carbon layer**. You’re not dealing with industrial toxins per se, but you *are* dealing with unknown nanosystems, spores, and possible charged particles.

### Skin contact & hygiene

Surfaces here are active. Dust reorganizes, water is guided by emergent filaments, cracks heal. You’re stepping into a system that thinks of free matter as feedstock.

**Touch policy:**

- Assume **all city fabric is “mildly alive” or at least responsive**. It’s not out to assimilate you, but it *might* try to clean, rearrange, or incorporate loose material.
- If a surface:
  - Warms or cools under your hand,  
  - Develops a faint texture where it was smooth, or  
  - Shows micro‑filaments emerging near you,  

  remove your hand and break contact *slowly* rather than jerking away. Sudden withdrawals can be read as damage or alarm.

**Skin protection:**

- Barrier cream or light hydrophobic lotion on hands and exposed areas helps keep nano‑scale systems from “tasting” you too deeply.  
- Bring **alcohol wipes** and a **gentle non‑antibacterial soap**. You want to remove particulates and uninvited biology without dumping a biocide that might trigger the city’s repair or defense systems.

**Injuries:**

- Small cuts may attract micro‑filaments or strange coagulation behaviors if they contact active surfaces or water. Clean any open wound with *your own* sterile solution and cover it; don’t let the city “help.”  
- Carry a compact first‑aid kit with sealed bandages, betadine or chlorhexidine swabs, and butterfly closures. Avoid anything aerosolized—you don’t want your medication interpreted as an invading mist.

## 2. Perception & Navigation: Seeing and Moving Through a Living Machine

### Light & vision

The world here is permanently late‑dusk; contrast is low, colors muted, edges soft. Many important cues—fine filigrees, faint glows, barely moving dust—operate near your visual threshold.

**Vision gear:**

- **Low‑light enhancement** (digital viewer, goggles, or high‑sensitivity camera) is more useful than bright flashlights. The city seems to prefer soft gradients; harsh beams might:
  - Disrupt plant behavior.  
  - Interfere with photoreactive infrastructure.  
  - Mark you as noisy and out of sync.

- **If you bring a flashlight:**  
  - Use narrow‑beam, low‑intensity white or warm light.  
  - Red light preserves adaptation but may interact oddly with iridescent veins; test gently.

- A **simple analog compass may misbehave** near nodal arrays or gravitic anomalies. Don’t rely on it for fine navigation.

### Mapping & orientation

Vertical complexity is low (few stories) but horizontal connectivity is extreme: roofs blend into walkways, into terraces, into bridges. Streets are internal hollows, not external gaps. It is very easy to:

- Loop without realizing.  
- Drift gradually downward into denser, stranger layers.  
- Confuse “building interior” with “city interior.”

**Navigation practices:**

- Constantly note **three things**:
  1. Is the air getting *wetter* or *drier*?  
  2. Is natural dim glow *richer* (more reflection from vegetation/water) or *poorer* (bare composite)?  
  3. Are there more or fewer **filament conduits** overhead?

  This gives you a mental map:  
  - Lush + wet + fewer conduits → courtyards/wetlands.  
  - Bare + dry + dense conduits → infrastructural spans / node neighborhoods.  
  - Dimmer + structured glows + vortices → deeper levels.

- Use a **personal beacon with inert tagging** (e.g., passive RFID dots or simple coded markers) for yourself only—not sprayed across the environment. Mark key junctions you pass through. The city does not like random debris; keep markers tiny, non‑reactive, and recover them when leaving if possible.

- Keep a **running mental thread**: “I’m one gently sloping corridor down from the last terrace; I passed three nodal arrays on my left; next opening to the outside goes up.” Narrating your route, even silently, helps.

### Movement style

The city’s subtle awareness reacts to predicted movement. It thickens railings where a hand might be, smooths floors just in time, extrudes barriers when someone nears a dangerous node. This works *with* inhabitants who move according to its expectations.

You are not one of those.

To avoid confusing or triggering the system:

- Move **deliberately and at consistent speed**. Don’t sprint, stop dead, then sprint again.  
- Favor **smooth arcs over jagged detours**; think “walking through a museum at night” rather than “running across a busy street.”  
- When changing direction or speed, give the environment a beat. If a railing is thickening where you *were* heading, let it complete and then walk along it; don’t fight it to go the opposite way at the last second.

**Edges & gravity anomalies:**

- At terrace edges where objects hesitate before falling, never test with your whole body. If a stone hangs for a heartbeat, treat that area as **active gravitational geometry**:
  - Step back two full paces.  
  - Avoid lingering with metal objects or devices there.  
  - Do not lean over or drop anything essential.

## 3. Behavior, Etiquette, and Interacting with a Possibly Sentient System

### Assume the city is watching—gently

Not with cameras and alarms, but with patterns: your weight distribution, your heat, your routine of motion, your persistence in a spot. It seems “attuned to pattern and attention.” It notices *attention* itself.

**This implies:**

- Staring intensely at a nodal array and moving closer and closer may be interpreted as you taking an *active role* in that system. If you do this:
  - Approach slowly; pause at each distance.  
  - Notice if the hum shifts, filaments brighten, or you feel bone‑deep tides.  
  - If it “answers” too strongly—stronger field, thicker hiss, new glows—back off.

- Skimming over surfaces lightly without ever committing weight or touch can confuse predictive systems. Step fully and clearly so the city can “read” your intentions.

### Don’ts: things that break the local logic

- **Do not litter.** Anything you drop becomes raw input to the nano‑infrastructure. It might:
  - Get disassembled.  
  - Prompt structural changes.  
  - Register you as a source of disorder.

- **Do not carve, scratch, or chip surfaces.** The city heals; your gouge will:
  - Close over, possibly incorporating what you bled into it (material, data, or symbolism).  
  - Register as damage and summon corrective processes you don’t understand.

- **Do not plug in or hard‑wire your gear** to any exposed vein, lattice, or active surface. There are no “free outlets” here.

- **Do not aggressively sterilize** any area with harsh chemicals or UV floods. That can look like attack on the city’s own microbiome.

### Do’s: ways to cooperate with the environment

- **Follow existing flows**:  
  - Use paths where plant growth is maintained rather than wildly overgrown; these are “sanctioned” channels.  
  - Walk in corridors that show subtle scuffing or smoothing from prior travel over utterly pristine ones.

- **Submit gently to micro‑adjustments**:  
  - If a surface gently slopes you toward a wider area or more strongly built platform, accept it unless you *must* resist. The city tends to channel movement away from sensitive or dangerous nodes.

- **Observe before entering** any sunk courtyard or lower level:
  - Watch for a full rain‑cycle if you can: does mist collect, does light shift in unusual ways, do filaments extend? Some pockets may be active reaction chambers disguised as gardens.

### Animals & semi‑organic forms

Creatures here have already made arrangements with the infrastructure. They know where not to step and what not to touch.

- Don’t chase, corner, or attempt capture. Their distress could:
  - Bring you into conflict with unseen city processes that maintain them.  
  - Spread their composite tissues onto you or your gear.

- If an animal *chooses* proximity, remain still and let it conduct the interaction. Assume its body has conductive, reflective, or composite properties that can interface with nodes and filaments; you might be standing inside a communication channel.

## 4. Technology & Tools: What to Bring, What Might Break

### Electronics & fields

Energy here moves in unfamiliar ways. Some regions exhibit:

- Micro‑gravity fluctuations.  
- Weak but pervasive fields with odd bone-level sensations.  
- Filament networks that conduct information/energy along paths you can’t predict.

**Risks to your tech:**

- Sensors might show nonsense: gravity meters twitch, magnetometers go wild, GPS equivalents fail.  
- Ungrounded electronics could pick up induced currents from nodal arrays or conduits.  

**Recommendations:**

- Favor **rugged, shielded, low‑complexity gear** over delicate, high‑frequency, high‑voltage equipment.  
- Keep critical devices in **Faraday‑like sleeves** (metal mesh pouches) except when actively using them.  
- Bring some **purely mechanical/optical backups**:
  - Analog notebooks and pencils.  
  - Simple watch with mechanical movement.  
  - Non‑electronic distance/angle tools if you’re surveying.

### Communication & signals

The city may be using its own subsonic, field‑based, and infrastructural “network.” You do not know how it treats foreign signals.

- Use **low‑power, line‑of‑sight** comms between your team (e.g., simple radios at minimal output), and only when necessary.  
- Avoid broadcasting continuous pings or active scanning (no CONSTANT radar/LiDAR sweeps around sensitive nodes).

If you experiment with:

- **Acoustic signals** (tones, claps, low chimes): do so sparingly, and note whether the distant tonal pulses of the city shift in response. Back off if they synchronize too neatly with your patterns.

### Materials

Anything you carry can become “food” or “instruction” for the city’s nanosystems.

- Prefer **inert, non‑shedding materials**:
  - Ceramics, glass, high‑grade stainless steel, stable composites.  
- Avoid:
  - Loose fibers, easily abraded plastics, reactive powders, and porous metals that can corrode quickly.

**Water interaction:**

- Local water has unusual surface tension and climbing behavior. Do not:
  - Mix it casually with solvents from your world.  
  - Store it in pressurized containers—its behavior under confinement may not match expectations.  

If you collect samples, use **small, rigid, sealable vials** and label clearly as “Do not open in base reality” until studied in controlled labs.

## 5. Psychological Readiness: How This Place Will Get Into Your Head

You’ll be in:

- Constant dimness without day/night markers.  
- A sprawling city that feels almost empty of visible people.  
- A soundscape of drips, faint hums, occasional distant pulses.

This can slowly erode your sense of time, urgency, and even reality.

### Time & routine

- Bring a **reliable personal timekeeper** and stick to a schedule:
  - Fixed times to eat, rest, explore, return.  
  - Don’t let the slow light fluctuations replace day/night in your mind; they may not map to anything like a 24‑hour rhythm.

- Set **check‑in intervals** with yourself or companions:  
  - “How long have we been in this level?”  
  - “When did the last rain veil pass?”  
  - “Are we drifting steadily deeper without realizing it?”

### Quiet and low population density

With so few visible inhabitants and so many subtle systems, you may feel:

- Like you’re walking inside the body of a sleeping titan.  
- Like every footfall is an intrusion.

To handle this:

- Travel with at least one companion if possible; humans anchor each other’s sense of normality.  
- Permit low, quiet conversation. Silence can become oppressive; a soft human voice also marks you clearly as “not fauna, not infrastructure.”

### The sense of being observed

Because the city pre‑adjusts railings and floors and sometimes harmonizes hums around presences, you will likely feel:

- Watched, but not in a human surveillance way—more as a factor in a calculation.

Resist paranoia by reframing:

- The city doesn’t appear to target predators with malice. It optimizes processes.  
- If it wanted to exclude you violently, you’d probably see much more obvious behavior—blocking passages, aggressive reconfiguration, intense fields.  
- Subtle accommodation (thickened rails, smoothed floors) is a sign you are being integrated as a low‐priority participant, not as an enemy.

If that still unnerves you, set **personal boundaries**:

- Choose a terrace, define it as a “camp,” and spend time there getting used to background behaviors. Don’t just keep pushing deeper; familiarity with one patch of the city greatly reduces anxiety.

## Practical Itinerary Advice

If you’re stepping in for the first time, consider this progression:

### Phase 1: Perimeter observation

- Stay near edges where the city thins into rough soil and reed‑like plants.  
- Watch how:
  - Channels guide water.  
  - Crystalline films grow and regrow.  
  - The city’s material meets bare ground.

Purpose: learn the “texture” of guided erosion and self‑repair before immersing yourself.

### Phase 2: Open terraces and shallow courtyards

- Move across upper terraces with views of the basin.  
- Visit a **few** sunk courtyards with wetlands:
  - Note how humidity and smell change.  
  - Watch plant cycles: leaf furling during deeper dimness, reopening later.  

Limit yourself to areas where exits are clearly visible.

### Phase 3: Internal corridors and nodal adjacency (not immersion)

- Walk **elongated hollows** that act as streets, just one level into the structure.  
- Stand at a respectful distance from nodal arrays:
  - Feel for bone‑deep tugs.  
  - Listen for shifts in hum.  
  - Observe light pulses along filaments.

Do not linger at nodes that make you dizzy, nauseated, or unusually euphoric.

### Phase 4: Controlled dip into deeper levels

Only when:

- You’re acclimated to the ambient dimness and hum.  
- Your navigation is solid.  
- You’re not exhausted.

Then:

- Descend via broad ramps into lower zones with luminous phenomena.  
- Set a **time limit** before you go back up.  
- Log at least three clear landmarks that you can recognize on the return (distinctive plant, unusual conduit pattern, specific glow geometry).

## Contingencies and Exits

### If the city reacts strongly

Signs of a strong systemic response:

- Rapid reconfiguration—walls or platforms adjusting more than a few centimeters at a time near you.  
- Sudden increase in clicks, chimes, or tonal pulses.  
- Multiple filaments brightening simultaneously or focusing on your location.  
- A pressure change that feels like a rising elevator without visible motion.

Response protocol:

1. **Stop moving.** Let the system finish whatever adjustment it’s making.  
2. **Make yourself predictable.** Stand or sit calmly; avoid fiddling with gear or shining lights around rapidly.  
3. **Retreat slowly** along the path you came when the environment stops changing.  
4. Avoid revisiting that exact configuration of node + depth + activity soon.

### If you get disoriented

- Don’t push forward hoping to hit an “edge.” The city is vast and horizontally uniform in many directions.  
- Instead, **backtrack to your last known courtyard or terrace**—places where the outside basin was visible.  
- Use:
  - Your own physical markers (if you placed them).  
  - Recognition of plant clusters, specific conduit arcs, terraces with distinct views.

If completely turned around, find:

- A **zone with more vegetation** and stay there while you re‑establish bearings. Biological pockets seem to obey more obvious logics than deeper infrastructural spaces.
