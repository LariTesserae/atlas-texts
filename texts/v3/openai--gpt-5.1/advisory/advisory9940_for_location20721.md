Preparation for visiting this drowned high city is mostly about three things: surviving the medium, understanding the slowness, and not disturbing a world that is only just discovering itself.

I’ll break it into practical categories.

---

## 1. Baseline: What sort of place are you entering?

Think of this less as “underwater ruins” and more as “a young, high-altitude seabed that happens to have a city embedded in it.”

Key environmental traits:

- Medium: Fully aquatic, thick but still water, very low current.
- Depth: High enough above a deeper basin to have serious pressure, but not crushing.
- Light: Permanent dim dusk; enough to see with assistance, not enough to rely on unaided human vision long-term.
- Temperature: Likely cold-moderate overall, with localized warmth around geothermal vents and mineral bands.
- Terrain: Fresh, sharp rock; terraces and ledges like a submerged canyon city; lots of verticality.
- Life: Abundant sessile growth; sparse, cautious fauna; possibly a remnant or adapted intelligent species.
- Time-scale: Everything is slow—currents, decay, construction, collapse.

You’re not visiting a ruin that has finished falling apart. You’re stepping into the *middle* of a process.

---

## 2. Body & Survival: How to keep a human alive here

### 2.1 Pressure, breathing, and physiology

You’re “high up” relative to a deeper basin, but still under a world-deep flood. Assume:

- Pressure: Significantly greater than Earth’s typical recreational scuba range, but below bathysphere extremes—call it the equivalent of 40–80 meters depth as a conservative guess, possibly more.
- You cannot go unprotected.

Prepare:

1. **Pressure-rated exosuit, not simple dive gear.**
   - Rigid or semi-rigid shell to:
     - Equalize pressure without needing your tissues to adapt.
     - Protect against sharp rock and slow but relentless impacts from debris.
   - Integrated **buoyancy management**: you’ll need fine control in near-still water to hover, sidle, and hold position in vertical slots.

2. **Closed-cycle life support.**
   - No open-circuit scuba:
     - Bubbles will disturb the environment.
     - Sound and vibration from bubbles carry far.
     - Any surface is probably *very* far away or effectively nonexistent.
   - Use a **rebreather within the suit** or microfusion/advanced chemical oxygen recycler if your tech level allows.

3. **Thermal control.**
   - Ambient water is probably cool; geothermal zones may be warmer.
   - Use active temperature regulation; don’t rely on wetsuit insulation alone.
   - Internal suit climate needs to be consistent: long durations, slow movements, and stress at depth are physically taxing.

4. **Decompression management.**
   - If you move between depths (e.g., exploring down into deeper basins or up toward thinner layers of flood), you must:
     - Ascend slowly.
     - Follow staged decompression protocols—or let the exosuit manage internal pressure so that *your* body never experiences large changes.

Plan for **multi-hour to multi-day continuous submersion** per excursion.

---

### 2.2 Movement & orientation

Water is almost still; vertical surfaces dominate; “up” and “down” are psychologically confusing.

Prepare:

1. **Six-degree thruster pack** built into the suit:
   - Low-thrust, quiet, with good control for:
     - Drifting slowly through frond-curtains without shredding them.
     - Backing away from unstable stone.
     - Holding station in vertical shafts.

2. **Fine-motor manipulators.**
   - Two primary arms with multi-fingered haptic gloves inside.
   - Optionally: small auxiliary manipulators or micro-drones for:
     - Checking stability of blocks or ladders.
     - Sampling growths without moving your main body close.

3. **Mapping and navigation.**
   - LIDAR/sonar is possible but:
     - Sound travels far and will broadcast your presence.
   - Prefer **short-range, low-power imaging sonar** plus:
     - Optical low-light and thermal imaging.
     - Inertial navigation (IMU) for dead reckoning.
     - Real-time 3D mapping that builds a “canyon city” model as you move.

4. **Orientation aids.**
   - HUD indicators for:
     - Actual gravitational down.
     - Local slope of rock faces.
     - Your distance from known fixed landmarks (an anchored beacon line near your arrival point is wise).
   - A visible tether or guide line back to a safe zone is strongly recommended on first visits.

## 3. Gear beyond survival: what to bring

### 3.1 Sensory and recording systems

You’re visiting a place where details matter and change is slow. Bring tools that can see what your eyes and ears can’t.

- **Multispectral cameras**:
  - Visible, low-light, near-IR, maybe fluorescence imaging (some fronds or seedlike luminescent nodes may glow in specific bands).
- **Chemical sensors**:
  - Dissolved minerals, temperature, pH.
  - Detect plumes from vents, chemical gradients that define “ecological neighborhoods.”
- **Micro-samplers**:
  - For silt, fronds, nodules, resin, braided fibers.
  - Non-destructive scraping, suction tubes with extremely small intake to minimize disruption.
- **Acoustic array (passive)**:
  - To listen rather than ping: rock crackle, distant falls, possible calls from fauna or intelligent residents.

All data should be logged redundantly; this environment won’t show you dramatic, cinematic scenes. Meaning emerges in slow, gentle patterns.

---

### 3.2 Structural interaction & safety

You’ll be moving inside a city that’s literally settling under the weight of water and roots.

Bring:

- **Light-touch contact tools**:
  - Extendable probe with force sensor to test:
    - Ledges.
    - Corbelled roofs.
    - Ladders and rails.
- **Micro-anchors & line**:
  - To stabilize yourself or a small block, not to haul down or pry.
  - Avoid any high-tension work; your goal is not to “rearrange the ruin.”
- **Emergency shoring braces**:
  - Light collapsible struts you can deploy if a corridor ceiling or wall shows signs of imminent failure.
- **Dust & debris visibility**:
  - In such still water, one careless fin or thruster burst can blind you with silt.
  - Use minimal thrust; let float time clear things when possible.

## 4. Environmental & cultural etiquette

This world feels young, fragile, and still deciding what it is. Treat it like a living laboratory and a possible inhabited region, not a dead dive site.

### 4.1 Don’t over-write a world that writes slowly

- **No large bubble streams, no loud sonar**:
  - Noise could reach any shy, intelligent residents and mark you as something disruptive.
- **No heavy sediment plumes**:
  - Move slow; avoid kicking up the “dust” that organisms are actively turning into habitat.
- **No rapid thermal or chemical release**:
  - Avoid venting warm suit water or foreign substances.

### 4.2 Respect for possible inhabitants

Evidence suggests:

- Stone-using tool culture.
- Built environment optimized for enclosure even before flooding.
- Adaptation-in-progress to an aquatic world (bipedal, careful silhouettes, repurposed halls).

Assume:

- You are trespassing in someone’s half-abandoned, half-adapted home.
- They might be rare, long-lived, deeply cautious.

Guidelines:

1. **Announce yourself without aggression**:
   - Slow, obvious movements.
   - Lights low, no strobes.
   - No weapons brandished.

2. **Do not occupy or seal their refuges.**
   - If you find:
     - Woven plant barriers sealing “rooms” from open water,
     - Unnaturally clear pools, or selectively planted growths,
     - Warmer, specially tended micro-ecosystems,
   - Treat these as:
     - Private gardens.
     - Sanctuaries.
   - Do not enter unless invited or absolutely necessary for survival.

3. **No relic collecting from meaningful spaces.**
   - Tools, ladders, carved basins, stacked towers:
     - Record in full detail.
     - Leave in place.
   - Only sample:
     - Loose, clearly detached fragments.
     - Biological material in tiny amounts.

4. **Communication approach.**
   - Bring:
     - Basic pictorial displays (projected icons, simple patterns).
     - Soft acoustic beams capable of steady, simple tonal sequences, not loud pulses.
   - If you encounter a thinking being:
     - Stop, hold position or slowly retreat.
     - Display non-threatening symbols.
     - Let *them* control distance and angle.

## 5. How to move through the city itself

### 5.1 Approach and first contact with terrain

When you arrive:

1. **Start at the periphery.**
   - Outer escarpments and broad, sparsely built terraces.
   - Observe:
     - How fronds anchor,
     - Where sediment accumulates,
     - How pressure and light change with slight depth adjustments.

2. **Trace the “streets” sideways, not vertically at first.**
   - Follow ledges horizontally, reading the city’s logic:
     - Clusters of homes.
     - Work yards.
     - Transitional terraces with towers or carved basins.

3. **Map tower fields and ritual features before entering dense quarters.**
   - The slender stacked towers and etched basins likely mark:
     - Orientation points.
     - Social or ritual centers.
   - Understanding these may help in interpreting social layout.

---

### 5.2 Entering denser, interior sections

The inner city is tighter, more vertical, and more choked with life.

Tactics:

- **Go slow through plant curtains.**
  - Don’t slice or tear unless you must.
  - Gently push aside with manipulators, one “doorway” at a time.

- **Treat luminous seed-tipped filaments as “wiring” or “stars.”**
  - They may be:
     - Photoreceptive.
     - Chemically sensitive.
  - Avoid brushing against them:
     - They could be communication structures or critical to the interior micro-ecology.

- **Lower light levels further inside.**
  - Use soft, warm-spectrum illumination.
  - Avoid high-intensity beams that may stun or bleach delicate growth.

- **Steer clear of obviously unstable built forms.**
  - Corbelled roofs with visible sag.
  - Walls bulging with root pressure.
  - Ladders that are more silt than material.

If a route looks unstable but inviting, don’t force it on first visit. This city will change on the scale of decades, not minutes—you can come back through a different path.

## 6. Interpreting what you see (so you don’t draw bad conclusions)

### 6.1 On the city’s original function

Observe:

- No surfaces obviously meant for open sky.
- No soot, no chimneys.
- All architecture assumes enclosure even before the flood.
- Presence of:
  - Steps and ladders.
  - Enclosures and courtyards.
  - Non-habitable towers and carved basins.

So, likely:

- A canyon/slot-world civilization, comfortable with overhead rock, limited open sky.
- Used fluids (water or something like it) and cycles intimately:
  - Carved basins ringed with etched lines: maybe:
    - Measuring changes in liquid level or composition.
    - Ritual vessels tied to tides, temperature, mineral flushing.
- Beginnings of geotechnical manipulation:
  - Right-angle grooves.
  - Channels toward mineral bands and warm vent zones.

When you see incomplete geometry in the cliffs, understand it as:
- A project unfinished by catastrophe.
- A physical record of ambition and interruption.

---

### 6.2 On the flood and adaptation

You will notice:

- Braided plant ropes still intact.
- Resin seals swollen but not fully decayed.
- Ladders collapsed recently enough to be recognizable.
- Life woven into architecture in ways that look selective, not random.

Interpretation:

- The flood is recent in *cultural* time.
- Some inhabitants may have:
  - Died.
  - Fled.
  - Stayed and slowly altered themselves or their practices to this half-lit ocean.

So:

- Expect **hybrid spaces**:
  - Hall half collapsed, half re-walled in biological fabric.
  - Original dwellings now serving as controlled bioreactors or gardens.
- Don’t assume that anything “ruined” is truly abandoned.
  - It may be in the middle of being repurposed across generations.

## 7. Risk assessment

### 7.1 Physical dangers

- **Rockfalls & settling structures**:
  - Slow, but once triggered, stones are massive and inexorable.
- **Entrapment in narrow slots**:
  - Vertical chimneys that once vented air now channel warmer water up:
    - Easy to become stuck.
    - Hard to turn around in a rigid exosuit.
- **Geothermal plumes**:
  - Warm but may carry corrosive or toxic chemistry.
  - Sudden contact with a hot vent surface could damage suit.

Mitigation:

- Never enter spaces narrower than 1.5x your suit width.
- Always keep one safe direction clearly navigable.
- Use chemical and thermal sensors continuously.

---

### 7.2 Biological and sociocultural dangers

- **Unknown fauna**:
  - The turtle-like stones and eel-like figures appear passive, but:
    - They may defend key sites or vents.
    - They may be more dangerous in groups or at breeding times.

- **Potentially sentient locals**:
  - Misinterpreting your actions as desecration or invasion is a real risk.

Mitigation:

- Maintain **distance** from any large, mobile creature until it chooses to shorten that distance.
- Avoid:
  - Approaching from above or behind.
  - Rapidly changing light or direction near them.
- Assume any sealed or unusually organized space “belongs” to someone.

---

## 8. Mission design: how long, how many, how often?

This environment rewards patience, not heroic solo plunges.

- **Team size**: 2–4 travelers at most.
  - Large groups stir up more silt and risk more cumulative disturbance.
- **Duration per sortie**: 6–12 hours inside, with generous safety margins.
- **Campaign length**: Many visits over months or years if you want to truly understand it.

Initial objectives:

1. Perimeter mapping and environmental profiling.
2. Documentation of:
   - Tower fields.
   - Carved basins.
   - Primary terraces and ledge hierarchies.
3. Identification of:
   - Possible refuges or modified “living” spaces.
   - Warm interior chambers.

Only then consider:

- Deeper penetration into communal halls.
- Polite attempts at sustained contact, if residents reveal themselves.

## 9. Psychological preparation

This place will work on your sense of time and self.

Expect:

- **Silence and slowness**:
  - No quick feedback.
  - No busy ecosystems buzzing with obvious life.
- **Persistent ambiguity**:
  - Is this a ruin, a chrysalis, or both?
  - Are you alone, or merely unacknowledged?

Prepare yourself to:

- Accept partial understanding.
- Sit still and watch sediment fall for long minutes at a time.
- Feel height even when you cannot see “down.”
- Be comfortable leaving most questions unanswered after the first few visits.

Consider:

- Rotating observers so that no one spends too long inside on a single continuous deployment; sensory monotony can induce disorientation or depression.

---

## 10. What not to do

In concise form:

- Don’t bring weapons you’re psychologically inclined to use first and think about later.
- Don’t pry blocks, topple towers, or “tidy up” ruin.
- Don’t harvest more than trace samples.
- Don’t flood sealed spaces with outside water.
- Don’t mark the city with bright paint, beacons, or hardware; leave mapping to your own logs and mental models.
- Don’t chase creatures; let them set terms.

---

If you prepare to move slowly, to survive quietly, to observe more than you touch, and to treat every stone and frond as part of an unfinished conversation between world and inhabitant, you’ll be about as ready as any ordinary human traveler can be for this place.
