Preparation begins with scale.

Everything you know about “body size,” “room,” “street,” and “weather” has to be reassessed before you go. The city may be “the size of a room” to you, but to its inhabitants you are a geological force. Preparing as a multiverse traveler means deciding which size you intend to be, what you intend to breathe, and how much you intend to intervene—if at all.

Below is a way to prepare that assumes two possible modes:

- A) You visit at **their scale** (shrunk or embodied to be roughly comparable to the citizens).
- B) You remain **your normal human scale**, treating the city as fragile protected terrain.

I’ll focus on A (the richer experience) but flag when things change for B.

---

## 1. Baseline orientation

**Environmental constants:**

- Cold, saturated air; near 100% humidity, low but livable temperatures.
- Near-no wind; air moves by convection and local temperature gradients.
- Low light; mostly bioluminescent and lamp-based; perpetual dim twilight.
- Everything is wet, soft, and alive. “Ground” is plant; “walls” are plant; “ceiling” is plant.

**Main implications for you:**

- You won’t dry out; instead your clothes and gear will soak and stay soaked.
- Heat loss will be constant via conduction through wet surfaces and evaporation from your skin.
- Slipping and falling will be a more common threat than being “attacked,” unless you are careless.
- Fire, as you know it, is marginal—very little oxygen circulation, everything waterlogged.

---

## 2. Biological compatibility

### 2.1. Air and breathing

The air is oxygen-rich but dense with spores, microbial load, and volatile organics.

**Risks:**

- Spore inhalation (allergic reactions, respiratory infection).
- Novel microbial flora (you are not adapted to this biome).

**Preparation:**

- Bring a **light, low-profile respirator** or a mask with:
  - P3 / N100 filtration or equivalent.
  - Moisture-resistant filter media (ordinary paper respirators will waterlog).
- If going at native scale through tech:
  - Engineer your form to have **higher humidity tolerance in lungs** and robust cilia / mucus clearance.
- Bring prophylactic **broad-spectrum antimicrobials** in your original baseline body, but use them sparingly; expect unknown biochemistry and don’t rely entirely on them.

If you visit at full human size and only lean over the “throat,” you can likely tolerate brief unmasked exposure if you have robust lungs, but a mask is still strongly advised.

### 2.2. Skin and contact

You will be in continuous contact with:

- Plant exudates (saps, resins).
- Fungal mats.
- Biofilms and micro-animals.

**Risks:**

- Contact dermatitis from plant/fungal chemicals.
- Inoculation of foreign organisms through micro-cuts.
- Hypothermia from constant evaporative cooling.

**Preparation:**

- Wear a **full-body, thin, hydrophobic barrier layer**:
  - Something like a breathable waterproof shell, but downscaled: a slick, non-absorbent outer suit.
  - Underlayer: thin wool or synthetic to insulate even when damp; no cotton.
- Gloves: **thin, tactile, but waterproof**; bring spare pairs.
- Footwear: soft, high-traction soles (gecko- or felt-like grip) to negotiate slick root surfaces.

If you go at human scale and don’t step inside, gloves and a good waterproof shell are still sensible when you lean into the hollow.

---

## 3. Thermal regulation

You’ll lose heat continually to cold, wet surfaces and air with almost no radiant warmth.

**Preparation:**

- **Layering:** base (wicks), mid (insulates), shell (waterproof). At native scale, compress this technologically into as few layers as possible to avoid bulk.
- **Active heat source:** small, high-density portable heat—chemical hand-warmers, micro thermal packs, or a compact electric system if you have power. Use sparingly; remember heat rises and vanishes quickly.
- Emergency: a **light, reflective bivy sheet** scaled down—something you can curl in if you become soaked and still.

---

## 4. Movement in a living, wet city

### 4.1. Surfaces and traction

You are walking on:

- Spongy, fibrous root-braids.
- Film-wet moss.
- Scarred, partially hardened plant tissue.

**Preparation:**

- **Footwear with micro-cleats or suction texture.** Flat soles will skate.
- Carry a **light walking probe**:
  - A collapsible pole or hook to test surfaces and stability of ledges before committing your weight.
- Move with **deliberate, three-point contact**:
  - One foot, one hand, one pole in contact at all times when traversing edges or steep, slippery zones.

### 4.2. Respecting the host body

The city is *in* a living organ. Your weight, even at their scale, is significant.

Rules for you:

- Avoid stabbing or cutting into root walls or ceiling unless explicitly guided to; injuries can trigger growth changes and structural shifts.
- Do not stand long on thin leaf or fungal membranes spanning open gaps; these are often partitions, not load-bearing floors.
- Step where they have reinforced:
  - Look for **braided vines**, **scarred/hardened walkways**, and **obvious paths** devoid of new growth.

At full human scale: do not step into the hollow at all. A single misstep could collapse entire neighborhoods. If you must enter, use a **suspended platform or harness**, distributing your weight via ropes anchored outside the basin, and lower yourself carefully between root-columns without touching the city.

## 5. Light and perception

You will mostly be in a spectrum of dim greens, blues, and faint yellows.

### 5.1. Your lighting

You can easily overwhelm the ecosystem with your technology.

**Guidelines:**

- No broad beams. Use **narrow, low-lumen lights**—a tiny, focusable lamp.
- Prefer **red or warm amber** for your own visibility:
  - Less disruptive to local bioluminescence patterns and animal behaviors, and less attracting for predators keyed to the usual blue-green glow.
- Keep lights **shielded and directional**; avoid sudden bright flashes.

### 5.2. Learning local light cues

The inhabitants read the environment through these light patterns:

- **Mottled turquoise walls:** bioluminescent fungi—typically benign substrate.
- **Drifting green pinpricks in water film:** mobile microfauna—food chain base.
- **Intermittent yellow pulses:** often warning colors or predator advertising.

Before serious exploration, spend at least one cycle (equivalent of several external hours) near the central bowl, simply observing:

- Which glows trigger their avoidance?
- Which they harvest?
- Which spread quickly and then vanish (blooms) versus stable glows (persistent symbionts)?

## 6. Communication and social preparation

### 6.1. Sound discipline

Noise travels poorly but anything *loud* is anomalous and dangerous here; it might draw burrowers.

- Avoid raising your voice.
- Learn some of their **soft consonant / click based speech** or basic **drummed root-codes** if you can arrange pre-visit linguistic orientation.
- Bring:
  - A **bone- or wood-resonant tapper** (something small that can send localized knocks).
  - A **haptic communicator** if you have high tech—wrist-to-wrist devices that vibrate rather than ring, to pass messages quietly.

### 6.2. Cultural stance

Their entire existence balances on the health of this plant-body and its microbiome.

Do not:

- Show off fire.
- Boast about cutting, burning, or clearing land in your own world.
- Treat any creature as “vermin” without understanding its role.

Do:

- Show interest in how they guide root growth, how they manage fungal blooms, and what taboos they maintain.
- Ask to observe rather than to do, at least at first:
  - “May I watch your pruning here?”
  - “Can I see where you store your fermentation pods?”

Bring gifts in forms that respect their technological base:

- **Durable cutting edges** that won’t rust in constant moisture (ceramic, treated metal, or high-grade chitin analogues).
- **Waterproof containers** that can withstand pressure and damp: small sealed vials, non-reactive capsules.
- **Fine cordage or knots knowledge**, if you have techniques for stronger or more efficient braids.

## 7. Safety: predators, structural shifts, and water

### 7.1. Predators

Main threats at their scale:

- **Broad-mouthed swimmer** beneath the bowl.
- **Burrowers** that cut through root-flesh.
- Opportunistic small predators/lures in glowing swarms.

Your defenses:

- Constant proximity to locals; do not wander alone initially.
- **Probe water** before stepping into any film deeper than a millimeter at your scale:
  - Use the walking probe; disturb the surface and watch for fast, convergent movements or sudden silences.
- Avoid tight-contact with walls if locals seem nervous; burrowers are detected by subtle vibrations:
  - Learn their **listening postures**: how they press to the wall, what signs trigger alarm.
- Carry a very compact **multi-use tool**:
  - Not a weapon in your terms, but a hook/lever/cutter that can free you from collapsing root or nets and discourage small attackers.

If you retain full human size, most predators cannot harm you, but they can be harmed by you. Your greatest danger becomes:

- Breaking through a layer and plunging into deeper water pockets.
- Introducing pathogens from your own world that devastate this closed system.

### 7.2. Structural dynamics

The plant body grows and shifts:

- New roots can spear through walls or paths.
- Old hollows can slowly constrict or seal.
- Fungal blooms can destabilize surfaces by changing moisture and texture.

Your mitigation:

- Never linger long in **unmaintained, untraveled tunnels**.
- Follow paths with fresh cut marks, woven reinforcements, and recent lamp residue; these are current “arteries.”
- Periodically check your **entry/exit routes**; a few hours may be enough for new growth to complicate your way back.

Prepare an **evacuation map**:

- Note: central bowl, at least two vertical paths toward the upper cracks where scouts interact with the outside wetland, and any lateral tunnels that lead to “safer” zones (nurseries, deeper interior).

## 8. Hydration, food, and nutrition

You are surrounded by water, but it is biologically busy.

### 8.1. Drinking

At their scale, the water film is rich in microfauna and microbes adapted to this niche.

- Unless your biology is custom-adapted, use a **micro-filter**:
  - A very fine portable membrane or advanced nanofilter that can operate even when biofilms clog; you will need to clean or swap frequently.
- If you have advanced technology, carry a **small sterilizer** (UV or equivalent) that works at your scale.

At human scale, a drop from the bowl is essentially a concentrated microbial soup. Treat it as you would any surface freshwater in an alien swamp: boil, filter, or avoid.

### 8.2. Eating

You will be offered:

- Fungi (some luminous, some not).
- Algae films.
- Soft-bodied grazers, chitinous crawlers.
- Possibly fermented plant or animal paste.

**Preparation:**

- Unless you have robust cross-biome tolerance, plan to **bring your own rations**, compact and sealed.
- If you want to participate socially by at least tasting, prepare your body with:
  - A strong, diverse gut flora from your own world (though this may be irrelevant).
  - Sensitive monitoring of your own reactions; sample **tiny amounts** first.
- Do not eat anything that is also clearly critical to their ecological balance:
  - e.g., cleaners that maintain the host plant’s wounds, or keystone grazers.

## 9. Ethics and non-contamination

Entering a micro-ecosystem like this is equivalent to visiting a sealed lab culture.

Key principles:

1. **No casual shedding.**
   - Wear clothing that minimizes your skin/flora shedding into their air and water.
   - If technologically possible, pass through **decontamination** before entry: UV, gas, or chemical sterilization safe for you but lethal to your microbes.

2. **Isolation of brought items.**
   - Everything you bring in ideally has smooth, non-porous surfaces you can later disinfect if they’re to reenter your world.
   - Don’t leave alien trash. Anything you leave may become a substrate or disrupt their cycles.

3. **No drastic demonstration of power.**
   - You are a multiverse traveler: you could burn, cut, or re-engineer this whole throat. Don’t.
   - Stick to **observation, small-scale assistance**, and knowledge exchange.

4. **Reversibility.**
   - Whatever you coerce or coax (e.g., guiding a root), do so in ways locals understand so they can manage or reverse the change over time.

## 10. Mental adjustment

This place will disturb your intuitions.

You must prepare to:

- Think **slow plant-time** and **fast insect-time** simultaneously.
- Accept that:
  - Streets grow narrower unless pruned.
  - Ceilings thicken and send down tendrils seeking the warmth and CO₂ of your bodies.
  - “Stable architecture” is actually a negotiated truce with growth.

Practice before arrival:

- Spend time in dense, damp forests, under logs, and in mossy ravines—not for literal similarity but to acclimate your mind to enclosed, green, breathing spaces.
- Meditate on **small scales**:
  - Watch pond water under magnification for long enough that your sense of what constitutes an “environment” expands to include the microfauna.

## 11. Modes of engagement

Decide in advance what sort of visitor you’ll be.

### 11.1. Observer

You stay primarily in:

- Central bowl rim.
- A few neighboring neighborhoods.
- Under supervision.

Preparation:

- Notebook or recording device designed to handle near-constant moisture.
- Compact camera with light-sensitive sensor and non-intrusive IR/low-light modes.

### 11.2. Participant

You help:

- Prune and re-direct roots.
- Maintain dams and leaf-gutters.
- Harvest mushrooms or tend domesticated grazers.

Preparation:

- Learn their **basic tool forms** and bodily postures (how they brace, how they lift) to mimic them safely.
- Bring a **few exemplar tools** that can be adopted into their existing practices without replacing them outright.

### 11.3. Liaison to the outer wetland

If you intend to travel between this throat-city and the external marsh/log ecosystem:

- Prepare gear for **two distinct scales and climates**:
  - Exposed, wind- and rain-swept, colder outer; sheltered, windless inner throat.
- Learn from scouts:
  - Where cracks open to the outside.
  - What predators in the wetland send their spores or larvae inside.

---

## 12. Exit strategy and time limits

Do not assume you can stay indefinitely.

- Your presence—metabolic heat, CO₂, bacteria—will slowly alter micro-dynamics around you.
- If you arrive via size-shift or embodiment, ensure:
  - Your method of **reversal** is reliable, powered, and not dependent on local conditions.
  - You have a **pre-agreed signal or timeline** to exit, irrespective of how fascinated you become.

Set a **maximum dwell time** on first visit (e.g., equivalent of 1–3 external days), with periods of withdrawal toward the upper cracks or your insertion point to monitor yourself for:

- Respiratory irritation.
- Skin issues.
- Cognitive shift toward complacency about local risks.

---

### In summary

To prepare for this throat-city:

- Wrap yourself, literally and mentally, in barriers thin enough to feel but strong enough to protect.
- Bring light, but little of it; warmth, but only enough to keep you functioning.
- Learn to move as if every surface is both organ and infrastructure.
- Commit to leaving as little as possible behind—other than understanding, and perhaps a few tiny, well-considered gifts.

Imagine stepping into a world where a droplet is a lake, a bruise on a root is a landslide, and your breath is a brief, local storm. Prepare so that, for them, your visit is not a disaster but a story.
