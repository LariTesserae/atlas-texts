Preparation for this place is less about gear weight and more about how tightly you hold onto your assumptions of space, time, and cause-and-effect. You’re not just going high into strange mountains; you’re walking into a half-finished ontology.

Below is a practical briefing, then some deeper notes on how to keep your mind—and body—intact.

---

## 1. Core facts a traveler must accept

- **Space is locally variable.**  
  Distances, alignments, and connections are not stable. A ledge “over there” may also be *this* ledge, depending on how you approach it. Bridges sometimes connect in ways you can’t see until you are already aligned with that version of reality.

- **Time is layered, not uniform.**  
  Processes can run at very different speeds in neighboring structures. A crane may take weeks to finish the swing you’re watching start. Some sounds and lights propagate along non-obvious paths and with significant delays.

- **The city and mountain are co-evolving.**  
  Cliff geometry, cracks, and spires are still “deciding” their final form over months and years, helped along by biological and industrial processes.

- **Nothing is in a hurry—but you can still fall.**  
  Slow industry, patient erosion, and drifting pace do not reduce danger. There are vast voids, young rock, incomplete railings, ontological “thin spots”.

If you fight these facts, you will panic or get hurt. Your safest mindset: **treat this visit as entering a slow, careful machine that is still assembling itself around you.**

## 2. Personal adaptation: body and senses

### 2.1. Vestibular and spatial calibration

You will routinely encounter:

- Streets that tilt up walls as if gravity has reconsidered its job.
- Walkways that “double-expose” for a few steps.
- Bridges that connect differently depending on angle and attention.

Preparation:

- **Vestibular conditioning (if available in your multiverse toolkit).**  
  Spend time in:
  - Variable gravity sims or rotating habitats.
  - Non-Euclidean training environments (maze-rooms where halls loop into themselves, Escher-esque stair modules).
- **Practice walking while ignoring some visual cues.**  
  Train with:
  - Transparent floors over height.
  - AR overlays that distort apparent slope while your body walks on a stable plane.  
  The goal: separate “what my eyes say” from “what my inner ear and foot pressure say”.

- **Balance equipment.**  
  - A **compact dynamic harness** you can clip into rails, cables, or anchor bolts. The city is full of industrial attachment points; treat it like vertical shipyards.
  - **Light climbing gloves** with good grip for metal and damp rock.
  - **Trekking pole or collapsible staff**—not for weight bearing so much as for “testing” ontological stability: tap surfaces before committing your full body, especially near double-exposed segments.

### 2.2. Perception management

Here, **attention steers reality** more than you’re used to:

- Valleys widen or narrow with how much notice you give them.
- Routes “collapse” into being when you commit to them.
- Misplaced focus can cause you to drift into a different alignment than your companions.

Preparation:

- **Meditation and focus training.**  
  Learn:
  - Narrow focus (drilling into a single object, ignoring surroundings).
  - Broad attention (panoramic awareness without fixating).  
  You will use narrow focus to *hold* a route stable, broad awareness to avoid being trapped in just one “version” of the city.

- **Attention discipline in motion.**  
  When walking:
  - Pick a **near anchor** (railing, doorframe, numbered girder) and a **far anchor** (distinct light pool, crane silhouette).
  - Switch gaze rhythmically between them to stabilize your “chosen” path.
  - Avoid obsessively staring at distant spires you’re not actually heading toward. Staring can pull them “away” or shift you elsewhere.

## 3. Gear: what to bring, tuned to this environment

### 3.1. Clothing and protection

Climate: consistently cool, windy, persistent twilight, intermittent mist.

- **Layered clothing**:
  - Base: moisture-wicking, fast-drying.
  - Mid: light but warm (synthetic or multiverse-equivalent of fleece).
  - Outer: wind-resistant shell with a hood; ideally treated to shed fine mist.
- **Color choice**:
  - Avoid clothing that exactly matches bare rock and structural metal; you want to be visible to locals and to your own crew against a grey, matte background.
  - Muted but distinct colors (dull blues, rusty reds, muted greens) work well.

- **Footwear**:
  - Sticky, high-friction soles for metal grating and bare rock.
  - Ankle support, but flexible enough for irregular surfaces.
  - Good performance when damp; you’ll cross condensation-slick surfaces frequently.

- **Helmet / hard hat**:
  - Not optional. You are in a vertical industrial environment: cables, cranes, loose bolts, unusual fauna movement above.
  - Fit it with:
    - A **low-intensity, indirect headlamp** (see light guidance below).
    - Ear slots or bone-conduction gear that let you hear ambient sound clearly (echoes are navigational data here).

- **Gloves**:
  - Two sets: one for grip and climbing; one finer pair for manipulating instruments or local tools.

- **Eye protection**:
  - A visor lightly tinted against persistent dim glare and metal dust, but not so dark you lose subtle shifts of light pooling.

### 3.2. Light and sound tools

Light and sound don’t behave entirely classically. Overpowering them will cause confusion.

- **Primary light**:
  - Low-lumen, diffuse illumination; think “lantern glow” not searchlight.
  - Warm color temperature to distinguish it from the sky’s internal, smoky glow.
  - Adjustable beam, but use narrow, bright beams only for inspection, not navigation—hard shadows might extend in misleading ways.

- **Secondary light**:
  - A tiny, **steady marker-light** you can clip to yourself or gear, to help your team track you in layered twilight.  
  Pick a color uncommon in local biolight (if locals favor certain hues, avoid those).

- **Acoustic tools**:
  - Small **manual chime or clicker**: a consistent, sharp tone of your choosing. You’ll use it to “ping” spaces. Note where echoes emerge from—around corners, down rails, delayed in odd ways.
  - A **personal audio recorder** with a visual waveform display. Useful for:
    - Capturing sounds that reappear later.
    - Verifying if a “voice” is live or a delayed echo from earlier.

Avoid loud, constant noise sources (blaring music, heavy machinery of your own). The city’s acoustic ecology carries information; you don’t want to drown it.

### 3.3. Navigation and recording

Conventional GPS-style tools will fail. Orientation is partly subjective/ontological.

Bring:

- **Local-frame mapping kit**:
  - Field notebook (physical) + pencils waterproof to mist.
  - Sketch maps of *relative* positions: “this balcony is two levels above the main tramline and faces a three-pipe junction.”
  - Simple symbols for:
    - Thin spaces / double-exposed steps.
    - Slow-moving machinery.
    - Light-pooling areas.

- **Breadcrumb system**:
  - Physical markers you can leave:
    - Colored fabric tags.
    - Chalk / removable paint markers for rock and metal.
  - Mark not just the path but your *intention*: e.g. arrows with symbols for “we came from a version where this bridge connects; follow with focused attention.”

- **Pair of synchronized clocks**:
  - One stays at a “base point” in the city; one with you.  
  Note when your subjective journey diverges from the slow rhythm of the place. If your carried clock shows large discrepancies relative to the ambient pace (observed machines, locals’ routines), you may have drifted into a slightly different temporal layer.

### 3.4. Safety and contingency gear

- **Harness, tethers, carabiners** compatible with industrial standards (you will clip into their infrastructure often).
- **Emergency descent / ascent kit**:
  - Compact rope or line with mechanical descender; ideally a system that can also hoist you if a platform “moves” out from where you expected to get off.
- **Compact medical kit**:
  - Focus on:
    - Wound care from falls/scrapes on sharp young rock and metal.
    - Respiratory protection (simple mask) for dust and fine mist near industrial zones.
  - Add treatments against biofilm infections—mosses and epiphytes here are dense, damp ecosystems on metal surfaces.

- **Low-tech signaling**:
  - Flares or bright chemical lights that stand out against the sky’s diffuse glow and the dim, viscous pipe light.  
  Use rarely; sudden strong light might cause confusing shadows or draw curious fauna.

## 4. Psychological and cognitive preparation

### 4.1. Coping with slow-motion industry

Everything industrial here moves, but on cycles you can’t watch to completion within a day.

Prepare to:

- **Abandon your usual productivity sense.**  
  You will see:
  - Cranes frozen mid-arc for weeks.
  - Flywheels turning so slowly that rotation is more inferred than seen.
  - Foundation terraces waiting for structures that are “scheduled” but not urgent.

Instead of asking “why is nothing happening?”, ask “what can I learn from the position of in-progress things?”. The place is legible if you accept its timescale.

Mental exercises beforehand:

- Spend time watching:
  - Tides, glacial timelapses, plant growth under sped-up/ slowed-down video.
  - Very slow mechanical art installations (if available) and practice noticing incremental change.

- Practice **sitting still beside a slow process** (e.g., a pendulum, a long mechanical clock) and observing your urge to interfere. You’ll need that restraint.

### 4.2. Managing existential unease

You are walking in a world where:

- Reality is visibly “deciding itself.”
- Your path is partly a negotiation between your attention and external structure.
- Time and causality blur (voices reappear, light stains linger).

Common reactions:

- Dissociation: “None of this is quite real; I might not be either.”
- Vertigo in time: feeling as if you’re walking both in a future and a past version of the same space.
- Mild paranoia: “Did I cause that bridge not to connect by looking away?”

Countermeasures:

- **Grounding rituals**:
  - Regularly touch fixed things: railings, rock, doorframes.
  - Narrate to yourself: “I am on a metal catwalk. To my left, a glowing slow pipe. Wind from below, steady.”  
  Physical plus verbal confirmation anchors you.

- **Shared reality checks with companions**:
  - At intervals, pause and each describe what you see and hear in a specific direction.
  - Minor discrepancies are normal; major ones (“I see a bridge” vs “I see open air”) are a red flag—someone’s alignment has drifted. Re-synchronize by:
    - Moving together back toward the last mutually-agreed landmark.
    - Focusing jointly on a single stable object: a bolted joint, a pipe junction, a visible tram car.

- **Accept that not all events are about you.**  
  This city has its own internal logic and history. Your attention participates, but you are not the protagonist of its becoming. That humility is stabilizing.

## 5. Environmental interactions: ethics and pragmatics

### 5.1. Fauna

You’re surrounded by species woven deeply into the environment’s physics.

- **Fliers with hinged, plate-like wings**:
  - They leave streaks of disturbed light hanging in air.
  - Do not shine bright beams directly at them; their wing-plates likely play with local light-rules. Startling them mid-flight near a “thin” section could cause unpredictable collisions.

- **Metallic beetle-things and rust-eaters**:
  - They maintain metal by consuming oxidation.
  - Never kill them near critical infrastructure; you’d be removing organic maintenance workers.
  - Don’t touch ones that drink from the glowing pipes; their slow biolight might store unfamiliar energies.

- **Pale quadrupeds with light-storing horns**:
  - They stabilize rock by “grazing” away latent structural tension.
  - Keep distance on narrow ledges; if they step briefly onto not-yet-real surfaces, you do not have their adaptation.
  - Night/low-shift glows from their horns are not lanterns for you—they indicate active geological interaction. Don’t interfere.

- **Translucent floaters from air vents**:
  - They damp and transport sound. Shouting at one is pointless; it may replay your voice hours later somewhere else.
  - Avoid touching them; they might be sensitive to thermal patterns, and your warmth could confuse their “routing” behavior.

Guideline: **observe, don’t “use”** these creatures. Many are likely under recognized local protection, formal or informal. Ask before interacting.

### 5.2. Flora and gardens

Plant life here is precious and adaptive:

- Rock-clinging mats and pocket jungles anchor cracks and hold water.
- Bridge-underside mosses and vines capture engine moisture and support micro-ecosystems.
- Terrace gardens feed people; many species are likely engineered or locally selected over long spans.

Rules of thumb:

- Don’t step into any green patch unless specifically invited.
- Don’t pick fruits or leaves without explicit permission and guidance; some may be:
  - Nutritionally tuned for locals with different microbiomes.
  - Structured to handle particular radiations or ontological gradients that your system isn’t built for.

If you’re offered food from these gardens, accept modest quantities at first and monitor your body’s response.

## 6. Social and cultural acclimatization

You’re entering a society whose everyday habits are shaped by:

- Topological shifts.
- Slow, massive industrial rhythms.
- Multi-layered awareness of time and space.

### 6.1. Built-in nonchalance about reality shifts

Locals:

- Expect anchors to slide a little “sideways in ontology”.
- Install telescoping joints not as emergency fixes but as standard practice.
- Let children dare each other to run through thin-space stairs.

They do not see this as dramatic; they see it as background maintenance.

Your behavior:

- Don’t dramatize every small shift: “The stairs just double-exposed!” is like saying “The elevator dinged.”
- Ask **how to read the warning markings**:
  - Colors or symbols for:
    - Thin zones safe to cross with care.
    - Zones currently unstable and to be avoided.
- Offer help cautiously. Most ad-hoc “fixes” are part of a long, considered design accommodating reality’s habits.

### 6.2. Time etiquette

With processes unfolding over days to seasons:

- Appointments may be phrased in “cycles” or other local measures, not strict minutes.
- Work tasks likely tie to position of certain machines in their big arcs (“When the east crane’s cable is halfway extended…”).

Be prepared to:

- Wait without pushing; impatience will be read as childish or dangerous.
- Explain your own time constraints clearly and early. Locals may adapt if they understand you operate on a faster subjective tempo, but they won’t warp their entire pace to suit you.

### 6.3. Communication customs (light and sound)

Given how sounds and light behave here:

- People may speak into specific **grilles or rails** to send messages along structural echo-paths.
- They may prefer:
  - Soft, distinct tones over shouting.
  - Steady, low-intensity light sources that don’t create misleading shadow bands.

Follow their lead:

- Try not to introduce arbitrary acoustic clutter.
- If taught how, use the same structural speaking-points for messages; don’t invent your own loud “calling method” unless safety demands it.

## 7. Navigation in practice

### 7.1. Moving through the vertical city

Practical protocol for walking:

1. **Before starting a segment**:
   - Identify obvious, built-in features:
     - Rail color/marking.
     - Pipe clusters.
     - The orientation of glowing conduits.
   - Ask: “In local terms, how is this route usually described?” Learn phrases like:
     - “Follow the three high pipes until they bend inward.”
     - “Cross under the horn-lit ledge.”

2. **During movement**:
   - Maintain **three-point awareness**: what’s under your feet, what your hands can reach, what you see at middle distance.
   - Keep your mental picture elastic: acknowledge that the “same” bridge might meet slightly different landing platforms in different alignments, but trust that anchors (pipe junctions, tower bases) remain conceptually stable.

3. **When encountering thin/odd segments**:
   - Slow down.
   - Tap surfaces with your pole.
   - Watch how your own shadow behaves; if it continues in mid-air or diverges from your body’s stance, proceed with extreme care or choose a different route.

### 7.2. Getting lost and getting back

If you realize your environment has drifted away from your plan:

- **Stop walking.** Movement here is a choice-function; don’t keep choosing randomly.
- Take inventory:
  - What stable structures can you see?
  - Are there any city sounds you recognize (a known tramline whine, a particular generator thrum)?
- Check your **clocks** against observed slow processes; if they diverge oddly, your temporal layer has shifted.

To re-anchor:

- Move toward:
  - Densest clusters of infrastructure (more constraints often mean more stable reality).
  - Light-pooling courtyards or major pipe junctions; these tend to be well-known reference points.
- If you have local contacts, **agree in advance** on “absolute” landmarks they expect to exist in all workable alignments (e.g., a major power stack, a specific cistern complex).

## 8. Health and long-term effects

For a short visit (days to weeks), main concerns:

- **Physical strain** from vertical navigation and cautious movement.
- **Cognitive fatigue** from continuous micro-reorientation to unfamiliar physics.
- **Sleep disruption** due to unchanging twilight and slowly shifting noise.

Mitigation:

- Set strict **rest intervals**:
  - Even if you “feel fine,” give your nervous system scheduled breaks where you sit somewhere still and safe and do not try to parse spatial oddities.
- Use **artificial diurnal cues**:
  - Wearable that cycles a soft internal light pattern mimicking day/night for your biology.
  - Fixed sleep schedule regardless of sky, anchored by meals.

For longer stays, there may be subtler adaptation:

- Your attention-habits might tune to local rules, making return to strictly Euclidean, linear-causal worlds jarring.  
  Leave notes to your future self about how navigation and causality work back home.

## 9. How to “be a good guest”

- Respect that the **city is teaching the mountain how to be**, and you are stepping into that relationship.
- Minimize your footprint:
  - Don’t interfere with fauna that modulate geology or infrastructure.
  - Don’t carve your own paths or install permanent equipment without permission.
- Observe before acting:
  - If you don’t understand why something is built in a particular convoluted way, assume it accommodates a quirk of reality or time you haven’t experienced yet.

If invited to watch a process—say, the slow complete swing of a crane, or the condensation of a ghostly twin spire into solidity—treat it as ceremony as much as engineering. For the people who live here, “waiting with” such events is part of belonging.
