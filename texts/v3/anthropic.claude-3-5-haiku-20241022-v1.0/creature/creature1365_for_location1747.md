In this vertical, moisture-laden world, the inhabitants have become profound extensions of the mountain itself. They are not merely residents, but living integrations of stone, water, and organic complexity.

The primary inhabitants are the Seep-Dwellers: a humanoid species with translucent, slightly iridescent skin that shifts color like lichens. Their bodies are lean and incredibly flexible, with extra joints that allow impossible climbing movements. Their skin exudes a thin mucous layer that helps them adhere to wet surfaces and regulate body temperature. Muscular but not bulky, they move with a liquid grace that mimics water's own flow.

Their sensory perception differs radically from humans—they "see" through a combination of moisture-sensitivity and low-frequency vibration detection. Their eyes are large and slightly reflective, with multiple lens structures that capture light from multiple angles in the dim environment. They can detect minute atmospheric changes, sensing approaching weather systems or geological shifts hours before they occur.

Communication happens through a complex language of moisture-transfer, subtle skin color changes, and low resonant vocalizations that echo through stone passages. Their social structures are not hierarchical but networked, with decisions emerging through collective sensing rather than individual leadership.

Their technology reflects their fundamental relationship with the environment. Tools are living constructs—not manufactured, but cultivated. Climbing harnesses are grown from specialized plant fibers that adapt to body temperature. Water collection devices are actually modified plant-animal hybrid organisms that can extract and purify moisture with remarkable efficiency.

Reproduction is a collective, environmental process. Offspring are not born but emerge through careful ecological cultivation, requiring specific moisture conditions, mineral interactions, and communal nurturing. A new Seep-Dweller might take years to fully form, gradually differentiating from a moss-like collective biomass.

Their spiritual and cultural practices are inseparable from geological time. They do not see themselves as separate from the mountain but as its current expression—temporary, fluid manifestations of a much larger, slower consciousness.

Survival depends on profound symbiosis: they shape the mountain, and the mountain shapes them, in an endless, breathing cycle of mutual transformation.
