In this Green Breath Metropolis, the inhabitants are as fluid and complex as the landscape itself. They are not merely residents but integral components of the living urban ecosystem.

The primary inhabitants are symbiotic beings—creatures that blur the lines between animal and plant, between individual and environment. Some appear as walking botanical hybrids: tall, slender entities with skin that shifts between bark-like textures and translucent membranes that pulse with chlorophyll. Their limbs are part tendril, part muscle, capable of both photosynthesis and deliberate movement.

These beings communicate through intricate chemical languages, releasing spore-signals and hormonal messages that ripple through the root networks. Their thought processes are distributed—not centralized in a brain, but spread through their entire biological structure, with decision-making happening through complex biochemical negotiations.

Smaller creatures inhabit the interstitial spaces: swarms of flying spore-beings that look like a cross between insects and dandelion seeds, drifting on humid currents, carrying genetic information and performing microscopic pollination tasks. Some are purely reproductive entities, others serve as sensory scouts for the larger plant-animal hybrids.

Predatory organisms exist here too—mobile orchid-like entities with razor-sharp, photosensitive appendages that can rapidly unfurl to capture prey. These are not simple hunters, but complex beings that integrate their captured nutrients directly into their metabolic systems, their bodies transforming and adapting with each absorption.

Reproduction is a collective, landscape-wide process. Genetic information is constantly exchanged, not through discrete sexual acts, but through continuous chemical dialogues and spore transmissions. New forms emerge not as distinct offspring, but as modulations and variations of existing structures.

Some inhabitants seem almost architectural—massive, slow-moving beings that are part plant, part living structure. They grow pathways through their bodies, create internal chambers that breathe and pulse, serve simultaneously as habitat and inhabitant.

Their consciousness, if it can be called that, is a distributed awareness—less an individual mind and more a collective metabolic intelligence. They perceive through chemical gradients, through the subtle pressures of root networks, through the constant exchange of biochemical information.

These beings do not experience time as humans do—their perception is cyclic, tied to growth patterns, photosynthetic rhythms, the slow pulse of metabolic transformation. A moment for them might be a decade; a conversation might span generations.

They are neither peaceful nor violent in human terms—they are simply part of an ongoing, complex negotiation of survival and transformation, where each interaction is a potential for new becoming.
