In this Underdepths, the inhabitants are themselves liminal beings—creatures of transition and adaptation, shaped by the delicate metabolisms of stone and moisture.

The primary sentient species might be the Lithomorphs: beings that exist between mineral and organic states. Their bodies are part crystalline, part flesh—translucent exoskeletons that capture and refract ambient light, with internal structures that pulse with slow, geological rhythms. They communicate through subtle vibration and chemical exchange, their movements deliberate and measured, like living stones learning to flow.

Their social structures are decentralized, more like mycelial networks than hierarchical societies. Individual Lithomorphs can partially separate and recombine, sharing metabolic information and genetic memory. They cultivate the moss-like vegetation, not through agricultural techniques but through careful resonant interactions—their own biochemical emissions stimulating specific growth patterns.

Smaller symbiotic creatures—the pale insectile-crustacean beings—serve as sensory nodes and information carriers. They move across rock surfaces like living sensors, their translucent bodies picking up microscopic environmental changes, then transferring this data through chemical signals to the larger Lithomorphs.

Deeper in the system live the Aquatic Refractors—soft, jellyfish-like entities that drift through water channels. Their membrane-like bodies constantly shift color and opacity, absorbing and redistributing environmental light. They seem to perform complex filtration and energy transfer functions for the entire ecosystem.

Reproduction here is not binary or sexual in familiar terms, but a complex process of mitosis, crystallization, and environmental resonance. New beings emerge when specific mineral concentrations, moisture levels, and vibrational frequencies align—part biological birth, part geological formation.

Their technology, if one can call it that, is indistinguishable from their biology. The geometric stone formations are both dwelling and extension of their collective body—living architectures that grow and respond, part shelter, part communication network.

They are not aggressive or competitive, but intensely curious. Their entire existence is a continuous process of sensing, adapting, and very slowly transforming—their timescape measured not in years or generations, but in geological increments.
