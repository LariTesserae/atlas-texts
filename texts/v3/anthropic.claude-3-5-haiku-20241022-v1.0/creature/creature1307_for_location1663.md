In this verdant, suspended labyrinth, there are no discrete inhabitants in the traditional sense—rather, the ecosystem itself is a living entity, with emergent forms of intelligence and agency.

The primary "beings" are complex bryophyte collectives, which function more like distributed neural networks than individual organisms. These plant-networks possess a slow, deliberate consciousness—not mammalian or quick, but geological in its thinking. They communicate through hydraulic pulses and chemical signals that travel through their intricate membranes, making decisions at timescales humans would barely perceive.

Trace entities exist at microscopic levels: symbiotic microorganisms that live within the plant tissues, serving as intermediary communicators and metabolic translators. These tiny beings are the true architects of cellular transformation, moving nutrients, restructuring membranes, maintaining the delicate balance of moisture and structure.

Occasionally, something like a "memory" emerges—crystallized in the stone fragments, in the worn technological traces. These are not memories as humans understand them, but encoded environmental information: geological records of past interactions, climate shifts, moments of structural change.

The primitive tool fragments suggest a prior presence—not necessarily human, but something that understood manipulation, that could shape stone and potentially interact with this living landscape. Their absence is as significant as their trace: a suggestion of intelligence that has transformed or departed, leaving only subtle impressions.

The entire ecosystem feels like a single, breathing organism—with its membrane-walls, its liquid pathways, its slow respiratory rhythms. Each tendril, each droplet of condensation, each subtle vibration is both a communication and a transformation.

These are not inhabitants in the conventional sense, but participants—nodes in a complex, living system of perpetual becoming.
