In the Mist Terraces, the inhabitants are as much a part of the landscape as the moss and stone - fluid, adaptive creatures that blur the line between living being and environment.

They are small, perhaps hand-sized, with bodies that shift between soft and semi-rigid states. Their skin - or perhaps external membrane - has a similar texture to the lichen-mortar of their buildings, capable of absorbing moisture and slowly changing color to match surrounding surfaces. When still, they can appear almost indistinguishable from the stone and vegetation around them.

Movement is their most revealing characteristic. They flow rather than walk, their bodies capable of momentary liquefaction that allows them to slip between narrow channels and architectural crevices with extraordinary grace. Their locomotion is more like a controlled ripple than a traditional stride - a continuous, wavelike motion that leaves minimal disturbance.

Sensory perception seems distributed across their entire body surface rather than concentrated in discrete organs. They detect environmental changes through a complex network of microscopic sensory filaments that constantly sample moisture, temperature, and subtle atmospheric shifts. Communication appears to happen through intricate color and texture modulations, almost like a living language written in biological pigmentation.

Reproduction seems less about individual organisms and more about collective adaptation. When environmental conditions are perfect, they seem to simply... divide, with one entity gradually becoming two, the separation happening so smoothly it's barely perceptible.

Their relationship with their built environment is deeply symbiotic. The structures they inhabit are not merely shelter, but extensions of their collective body - living architecture that grows, repairs, and responds as they do. They do not so much occupy space as continuously negotiate and collaborate with it.

Nutrition appears to be drawn directly from atmospheric moisture, the ubiquitous mist, and microscopic organisms embedded in the moss and stone. They are essentially filter-feeders of their environment, consuming and being consumed by the landscape in an endless, almost imperceptible cycle.

They are neither singular nor plural in any traditional sense - more like a distributed intelligence, a living network that thinks and moves and changes as a single, breathing entity.
