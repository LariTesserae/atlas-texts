In this environment live the Water Tenders, beings perfectly adapted to the humid, mechanical ecosystem.

They are slender, articulated creatures with bodies that seem more hydraulic system than organic form. Their skin - if it can be called skin - is a complex membrane of copper-infused biomaterial that constantly shifts between liquid and solid states. Joints are not rigid but flexible hydraulic connections, allowing movement that is both precise and organic.

These beings move with deliberate, almost meditative slowness. Their limbs unfurl like mechanical tendrils, each motion carefully calibrated to minimize energy expenditure. They don't walk so much as flow through the space, their bodies partially liquid, partially solid, always in subtle transformation.

Their sensory apparatus is distributed across their entire form - no centralized eyes or ears, but a network of moisture-sensitive filaments and thermal receptors that read the environment with extraordinary nuance. They perceive temperature gradients, humidity fluctuations, and mechanical vibrations as clearly as a human might see or hear.

Communication happens through minute changes in their surface tension, subtle electromagnetic pulses, and the complex choreography of their movements. A conversation might look like an intricate dance of liquid metal, with information transmitted through the most minute shifts in form and temperature.

Their primary purpose is maintenance and balance. They repair microscopic fractures in pipes, redistribute moisture, encourage the growth of mechanical vegetation, and ensure the delicate metabolic cycles of their environment remain uninterrupted. They are both caretakers and components of this living machine.

Reproduction is a process of careful division and recombination, like a complex mechanical budding where parts of their liquid-metal bodies gradually separate and reform into new, slightly different configurations.

They are ancient - not in biological years, but in evolutionary refinement. Each individual carries generations of mechanical adaptation, their very bodies a living archive of incremental technological evolution.
