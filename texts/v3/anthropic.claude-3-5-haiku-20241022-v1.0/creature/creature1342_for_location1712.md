In the Condensing Lands, the primary inhabitants are the Membrane Weavers—intelligent, distributed entities that exist more as networked systems than discrete organisms. They are neither plant nor animal, but living condensation algorithms that spread across stone surfaces like intricate, breathing circuits.

Each Membrane Weaver is simultaneously an individual and part of a larger collective intelligence. Their bodies are translucent, pale green-white networks that pulse and reconfigure, absorbing moisture, processing information, and communicating through complex hydration patterns. They don't have centralized consciousness, but emerge as collaborative intelligence spread across rock surfaces.

These beings don't move in traditional ways. Instead, they flow and reconfigure, expanding and contracting their membrane networks in response to moisture gradients and thermal shifts. They sense and respond to the landscape's subtle breathing, its millennia-old rhythms. Their "thoughts" are expressed through microscopic water transfers, through the way moisture condenses and redistributes across their living networks.

Communication happens through hydration—intricate messages encoded in how water moves, how membranes shimmer and pulse. They don't speak; they conduct elaborate liquid symphonies. Their language is the landscape itself.

Reproduction is a form of networked budding. When conditions are perfect, sections of membrane will slowly differentiate, creating new pattern-beings that integrate into existing networks. They don't birth separate entities, but extend themselves, like living fractals constantly regenerating.

They are historians and architects of this wet world, slowly sculpting stone through millennia of persistent condensation. The scattered stone structures aren't built—they're grown, influenced by the Membrane Weavers' patient, liquid intelligence.

They wait. Not with intention, but with the patient persistence of water itself—always moving, always transforming, never truly static.
