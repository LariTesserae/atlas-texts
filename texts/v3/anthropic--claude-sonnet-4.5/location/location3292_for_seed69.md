# The Breathing Plains of Umbrathene

In absolute darkness, the world makes itself known through sound, pressure, and the language of water.

This is a place the size of a continent—perhaps larger. No one has crossed it. The indigenous peoples speak of it as having "seven weathers" that move through it in overlapping wheels, meeting and parting like the schedules of distant trains. Stand in one place long enough and you'll feel them pass: the dry-electric wind that makes every hair stand up and causes the grasses to spark against each other with soft blue coronas invisible to human eyes but visible to the creatures here; the moisture-press, when the air becomes so thick with suspended water that breathing feels like drinking; the warmth-wave that brings the scent of fermentation and accelerated growth; the settling-hours when everything drips and drips and the world seems to be wringing itself out.

The ground is ancient highland, worn to its essential form over epochs. These are not sharp mountains—those were here once, perhaps, but they've been here so long they've become something else. What remains are the high rolling backs of the world, endless swells and broad valleys, plateaus that stretch for hundreds of miles, all of it worn smooth, all of it settled into equilibrium. The stone beneath has been shaped by water and wind and the roots of a billion generations of plants until it has achieved a kind of final form, every sharp thing softened, every angle become curve. Rivers have found their grooves and keep to them. The soil—thick, black, ancient—stays where it has accumulated. This is a landscape that has stopped *changing* and started *being*.

But being, here, is loud.

---

The sound comes first from the water. Everywhere, water is speaking.

It rises from below—the water table sits so close to the surface that in many places your foot will sink and the imprint will fill with dark water in seconds. Springs weep from every hillside. The stone here is porous with age, riddled with channels, and water moves through it constantly, emerging in a million small voices. Not the thunder of waterfalls—though those exist too, invisible in the dark, known only by their roar and mist—but the persistent, layered whisper-rush-trickle-drip of water finding its way. In the valleys, true marshes spread for miles, water standing in sheets between reed-forests, each stem adding its note to the sound when wind moves through.

The air itself is so saturated that water condenses on every surface. The vegetation drips continuously. Mist forms and reforms in invisible veils that you walk through, that touch your face like cold silk. The rocks weep. The larger stones are slick with moisture, grown thick with moss and lichens that have been there so long they've achieved extraordinary thickness, cushions of living matter a foot deep, spongy and cool to the touch, holding water like reservoirs.

After rain—and it rains often, great warm rains that sweep across the plains in vast cells of weather, each storm a province unto itself—the world becomes a percussion ensemble. Dripping from every leaf, every branch, every overhang. Streaming across stone. Filling the channels. The marshes rising slightly, their reeds clicking and tapping. This sound can last for days, gradually diminishing but never quite stopping before the next rain comes.

---

The vegetation is lush but not jungle-thick. The darkness has shaped it toward certain strategies.

Grasses dominate the more elevated ground, but these are strange grasses—coarse, tall, some species growing twelve feet high, with hollow stems that drum when rain falls on them, that whistle in certain winds. They grow in dense stands, and walking through them is like moving through an instrument. The stems are slick with moisture, cold to the touch. They bend rather than break, and they've been bending for so long that the paths between them—made by the herds, by the scattered human inhabitants, by time—are permanent grooves, trenches worn into the ancient soil.

Lower, in the valleys and marsh-edges, reeds and sedges create forests of vertical lines. These stands are so dense you cannot see more than a few feet into them, though "see" is the wrong word. The darkness here is total—not the darkness of night, which has stars, moon, the memory of light—but something older. Primordial. The darkness of caves, of depths, but open to the sky. Whatever happened here—and the indigenous peoples have stories, but the stories contradict each other—removed light from this place entirely. It is not blocked. It simply does not arrive.

Yet the plants grow. They've adapted. Many have abandoned chlorophyll entirely, or maintain it only vestigially. They grow toward other things: warmth, moisture, the faint chemical traces of the rich soil, the presence of the vast fungal networks that thread through everything here. Some have become partially mycoheterotrophic, drawing sustenance from the endless underground webs of fungi. Others have developed tissues that can harness the chemical energy of decay directly—they grow best near standing water, near the mulch of their own fallen predecessors.

The trees—and there are trees, though scattered, never forming closed canopy—are broad-crowned things with thick, spongy bark that holds water, that serves as substrate for dozens of species of moss, lichen, ferns. Their branches are irregular, spreading in whatever directions they find most favorable for reasons that have nothing to do with light. They grow slowly, immensely old, their wood dense and dark. When a branch falls—and branches fall often, brought down by their own weight, by the wind, by the decay that works at them constantly—it settles into the ecosystem and begins a decades-long transformation, becoming sponge, becoming soil, feeding the fungal networks, hosting colonies of beetles and worms and things without names.

Between the grasses, in the spaces the water hasn't claimed, grow thick carpets of moss and what might be called moss but isn't quite—something between moss and lichen and fungus, forming cushions and mounds, some glowing very faintly with bioluminescence too dim for human eyes but visible, perhaps, to the creatures born here. These cushions hold astonishing amounts of water. Step on one and it compresses, water welling up around your foot. Release it and it slowly re-expands, breathing like a lung.

---

The fauna have made themselves at home in sound and touch and scent.

The herds are the loudest—vast congregations of large herbivores that have no single equivalent in the lit world. They're built something like bison, something like elk, something else entirely: massive bodies on columnar legs, heads broad and eyeless (or with eyes reduced to useless buds beneath the skin, vestigial receipts of ancestry). They navigate by sound—producing low-frequency calls that travel for miles, that echo back from hills and tree-stands, giving them a sonic map of the world. They have elaborate antlers or horn-structures, but these are not for display; they're tuned like instruments, each individual's configuration unique, producing distinct tones when the animal vocalizes through chambers in its skull. The herds sing to each other, keeping contact, maintaining cohesion. Mothers and calves bond through specific frequencies.

The herds move in rivers through the grass-forests, hundreds or thousands strong, following routes worn smooth over generations. They graze the grasses—which have tough silica-rich stems, requiring powerful grinding teeth—and they browse the lower vegetation. Their hooves drum on the ancient stone, splash through standing water. They leave behind them scent-trails, dung, trampled paths, and a constant low-frequency thrum that's as much felt as heard, that travels through the ground and tells everything else where they are.

Around and among them: the predators. Sleek, powerful, with bodies optimized for explosive speed over short distances. They hunt by sound and scent and something else—they have organs along their flanks that detect pressure changes, air movement, the bow-wave of displacement that precedes a running body. They're social, working in coordinated groups, and they communicate constantly—not with roars but with clicks, chirps, subsonic pulses. A hunt is a symphony of coordination: scouts locating prey, communicators passing information, drivers pushing the target toward ambush points. They take down the young, the sick, the unwary, and they do it with efficiency born of millions of years of refinement.

Birds—though calling them birds is generous—wheel overhead in the darkness. They navigate by echolocation, by sensitivity to the earth's magnetic field, by instincts older than the absence of light. Some are enormous, with wingspans twenty feet across, soaring on the thermals that rise from warmer valleys. Others are smaller, darting between the reed-stems, feeding on insects. Their calls are everywhere: whistles, clicks, harsh cries that echo across the marshlands. At certain seasons they gather in colonies numbering hundreds of thousands, and the sound of these rookeries can be heard—so it's said—from a hundred miles away.

The marshes support their own ecology: fish that hunt by electrical sense, amphibians that have never needed eyes, invertebrates beyond counting. The water is alive with movement—splashing, rippling, the soft percussion of things breaking the surface. At night (though night and day are distinctions that matter little here, marked only by temperature shifts), certain areas of the marshes pulse with bioluminescence—not bright, but enough to create zones of dim blue-green glow, like embers in the dark, where chemical reactions in algae and bacteria paint the water.

And there are the burrowers: mammals (or something close) that have abandoned the surface almost entirely, living in vast tunnel systems in the ancient soil, emerging only to graze or hunt. They've created networks beneath networks, warrens connecting across miles. Sometimes the ground is so undermined that it sounds hollow when walked upon. Sometimes it collapses, creating sudden sinkholes that fill with water and become new marshes, feeding back into the cycle.

---

The human presence here is scattered, adaptive, old.

There are structures, but they're unlike architecture from the lit world. They must be found by other means—by sound (some buildings are designed to sing in certain winds), by scent (markers of specific woods, of the oils used in their construction), by the memory of paths. The buildings are low, blending with the contours of the land, made from the materials at hand: the dense wood of the scattered trees, stone from outcrops, reeds bundled and woven. They're engineered for acoustics as much as shelter—walls that reflect sound in particular ways, rooms that resonate, doorways positioned to channel air and the information it carries.

Many structures are partially underground, dug into the ancient soil, their roofs covered with living moss, indistinguishable from the landscape except by touch. Inside, they're warm (the earth here is warm, heated from below by geological processes, by the slow decay of unimaginable depths of organic matter), dry, arranged not around light but around sound and temperature and the flow of air.

The scattered inhabitants—and they are scattered, living in small groups separated by vast distances, meeting seasonally at traditional gathering places—have developed technologies that work with the darkness rather than against it. They navigate by sound, by scent, by the elaborate mental maps built up over lifetimes. Children learn to read the landscape through their feet, through their skin, through the information carried in every breath of wind.

But there is industry here, and this is where the strangeness deepens.

---

The machines are not factories in the traditional sense. They're more like processes, like systems, like ecologies that include mechanical elements.

Some are harvesters: enormous, slow-moving constructs that crawl across the plains on multiple legs, grazing the grasses like mechanical herbivores, processing the stems, extracting fibers and starches and cellulose, leaving behind pressed blocks of material that are collected later. These machines are old—generations old—maintained and repaired and modified but never fully replaced. They navigate by following subsonic beacons, by detecting the magnetic anomalies in certain rock formations, by algorithms that are part programming and part generational memory. They sound like nothing else: a deep rhythmic thudding, the hiss of hydraulics, the grind of processing drums, all of it echoing across the plains for miles.

Others are pumping stations: vast arrays of pipes and cisterns that manage the water, that draw it from below and move it to where it's needed, that prevent certain areas from flooding while maintaining marshes in others. These stations are powered by the water itself, by the constant flow, by temperature differentials between surface and depth. They throb and gurgle and clank, they release jets of steam at intervals, they create their own micro-climates of warmth and mist. Around them grow dense tangles of vegetation, fed by the excess water, and these become oases, gathering places for fauna and for the scattered human groups.

There are forges, though they burn not wood but something else: methane tapped from deep pockets in the earth, biogas generated from vast composting operations where the endless vegetable matter of the plains is processed. The forges are caves of heat in the cool darkness, places where metal is worked—not steel, exactly, but alloys developed here, suited to the conditions, resistant to the constant moisture. The ring of hammers on anvils carries for miles, a kind of communication, a way of saying: we are here, we are working, we are human.

And there are the listening stations: arrays of sensors, both biological and mechanical, that monitor the continent-sized landscape, that track the herds, that predict the weather cycles, that listen to the vast acoustic information flowing through the world at all times. These stations are tended by specialists who've trained their entire lives, who can interpret the data, who understand the language of the place. They use telegraph-like systems—wires strung between stations, carrying information as vibrations, as pulses—and they use drums, enormous drums that can send messages across hundreds of miles, that form a network of communication spanning the plains.

The technology level is uneven, patchwork: some elements industrial, some almost pre-industrial, some adapted from elsewhere, some invented here in response to the darkness. Engines exist but are used sparingly, their fuel precious. Electrification exists but in limited forms—battery systems charged by the water-flow generators, used for specific applications. Mechanization exists but often in forms that seem semi-biological, that mimic the rhythms of the living world rather than imposing their own.

---

The weirdness is not always obvious. It accumulates slowly.

There are zones where sound behaves strangely—where echoes come back wrong, where certain frequencies are absorbed completely while others are amplified. These zones move, slowly, over seasons or years, and no one fully understands why. Walking into one is disorienting: suddenly you can't hear your own footsteps, or they sound like they're coming from the wrong direction, or there's a persistent hum at the edge of hearing that makes thinking difficult.

There are the singing stones: outcrops of a particular mineral formation that resonate when moisture condenses on them, that produce tones—not loudly, but persistently, a kind of ambient music that rises and falls with humidity, with temperature, with the weather cycles. Some of these formations are enormous, and their songs can be heard from miles away. The scattered inhabitants consider them sacred, or at least significant; the sounds are used for timekeeping, for navigation, for spiritual practices that are hard to articulate to outsiders.

There are places where the ground itself seems to breathe—not metaphorically but literally, exhaling warm moist air at intervals, inhaling cool. These are connected to vast underground chambers, to geological structures that create convection cycles, but standing over one in the darkness, feeling the warm breath on your face, hearing the soft whistle as air moves through stone channels, it's hard not to feel you're standing on something alive.

The bioluminescence is everywhere but subtle, requiring dark-adaptation far beyond what human eyes can achieve in a normal lifetime. Those born here develop sensitivity to it, can navigate by the faint glow of certain mosses, can read the bioluminescent markers that indicate paths, boundaries, significant places. To an outsider, it's invisible. To them, it's a whole layer of information, a kind of not-quite-light.

And there are the storms—not just rain but stranger weather. The dry-electric storms that fill the air with charge, that make every surface tingle, that cause the grasses to glow very faintly with St. Elmo's fire. The fog-banks that roll in with their own internal weather, that contain currents and temperatures and structures. The temperature inversions that bring sounds from hundreds of miles away, that let you hear the ocean (though the ocean is weeks of travel distant) or the songs of herds you'll never see.

Time itself seems different here. Without the cycle of day and night, other rhythms dominate. The temperature cycles, which are roughly diurnal but not exactly. The weather cycles, which operate on longer timescales. The biological rhythms of the herds, the breeding seasons, the migrations. The human inhabitants track time differently, by events rather than hours, by processes rather than arbitrary divisions. A journey doesn't take three days; it takes until you arrive, and that depends on weather, on the condition of the travelers, on what happens along the way.

---

Stand anywhere on these plains and you're immersed in information.

The sound is layered, three-dimensional, constantly changing. Near: the drip of water from the vegetation around you, your own breathing, the squelch of wet ground underfoot, the brush of grass-stems against your body. Middle distance: the calls of the herds, the sounds of water moving, wind through the reed-forests, the percussion of rain on different surfaces, each material singing its own note. Far: the subsonic thrum of distant machines, the echo-distorted calls of the flying creatures, the rumble of weather systems moving across the continent, the singing stones marking cardinal points.

You navigate by all of it: by the acoustic shadows of hills, by the way sound reflects off water, by the temperature differentials that tell you whether you're moving toward lowland marsh or elevated plain, by the scents that flow on the wind—the mineral smell of certain stones, the organic richness of the marshes, the acrid tang of the forge-zones, the subtle markers left by the herds, by other humans, by territorial predators.

The world is in constant motion. Water moving through its cycles, vegetation growing and decaying, herds migrating, predators hunting, weather systems wheeling through their appointed rounds, the machinery clanking and processing, the inhabitants going about their incomprehensible business. Nothing is static. Everything is process.

The darkness is not absence but presence—something that has weight, texture, its own reality. You don't miss light after a while (though it takes a while). You begin to perceive the richness of this unlighted world, the way information flows through sound and scent and touch and temperature, the way the landscape makes itself known without ever being seen.

---

The rules here are different.

Predation operates by different parameters—speed and silence are balanced differently when no one can see. The herds rely on numbers and acoustic awareness, maintaining constant contact. The predators rely on stealth and coordination, on moving through the sound-landscape without creating signatures, on understanding the acoustic behavior of the terrain well enough to approach from directions that provide no warning.

Water is wealth—not scarce, exactly, but its management matters. The marshes are resources, the springs are gathering points, the pumping stations are infrastructure that everyone depends on. Controlling water flow means influencing where vegetation grows, which means influencing where herds gather, which means everything else follows.

Temperature matters more than you'd expect. The warmth rising from below is not uniform—some areas are notably warmer, and these become centers of biological activity, of human settlement. The warm zones change slowly over geological time, but they change, and this shapes history, drives migrations, creates conflicts and alliances.

Sound is both communication and intelligence. The ability to interpret the acoustic environment—to hear the approach of weather, the movements of herds and predators, the signals from distant human groups, the condition of machinery—is survival. The inhabitants develop extraordinary acoustic memory, carrying in their heads detailed sonic maps of territories hundreds of miles across.

Community is dispersed but interconnected. The scattered groups maintain contact through the drum-networks, through seasonal gatherings, through the flow of travelers between settlements. Information spreads through the network: weather predictions, herd movements, technological innovations, stories, disputes, alliances. The network is social and practical and spiritual all at once, a kind of continental nervous system, with the listening stations and the drum towers as nodes.

---

The place has existed in this state for so long—centuries? millennia?—that it has achieved stability. The ecology is in balance: herds and predators, vegetation and decay, water and weather, all cycling through patterns that repeat while never being exactly the same. The human presence is part of this balance now, integrated, their scattered settlements and machines and acoustic networks woven into the larger system.

There are ruins, sometimes. Older structures, different architectures, technologies that no longer work or whose purpose is forgotten. The place has been inhabited for a very long time, through different eras, different approaches to living in darkness. Some groups remember more history than others. Some stories are contradictory. But the ruins—when you find them by stumbling over them in the dark, by noticing how sound behaves strangely around them—speak of long human presence, of rise and fall and adaptation.

The plains feel ancient. Not just old but settled into themselves, like a person who has reached equilibrium with their life. The erosion has done its work. The ecology has found its patterns. The weather moves through its cycles. The herds walk their ancestral routes. The predators follow. The vegetation grows and dies and feeds the soil. The machines process and pump and forge. The humans listen and drum and navigate and maintain.

It's a working landscape—not wilderness, not pastoral, not urban, but something else. A place where human and non-human presences are densely interwoven, where industry and ecology aren't opposed but integrated, where the vast scale creates room for everything, where the darkness has become not a limitation but the defining feature that makes everything else possible.

Stand here long enough and you begin to understand that the darkness is not absence of light but its own positive quality, like cold isn't absence of heat but a real condition that supports its own forms of life, its own kinds of beauty. The world here is rich and full and alive, every cubic meter saturated with presences, with information, with activity.

The sound never stops. The water never stops moving. The herds never stop migrating. The machines never stop processing. The weather never stops cycling. The life never stops living.

The plains breathe in the darkness, vast and ancient and strange and utterly themselves.
