# The Tapping Floors

This is a place of constructed darkness, where something — not quite human, not quite anything with a name — has built a world from stone and has set it into motion.

The floors extend. And extend. And extend. You cannot see their edges in any direction because the darkness is nearly complete, but you know they are there — flat planes of fitted stone stretching outward like vast courtyards that were never meant to see sky. The stones are hand-cut, each one, though the hands that cut them work to no aesthetic you recognize. Some pieces are triangular, some trapezoidal with seven sides, some curved in ways that suggest they were softened by heat and then locked into place while still plastic. They fit together with the obsessiveness of someone solving a puzzle that has no picture on the box. The gaps between stones are filled with a paste made from powdered minerals and something organic that has long since dried to the hardness of the rock itself.

There is no soil here. There was never soil. The stone floors rest on stone pillars, which rest on more floors, which rest on more pillars. It goes down. You can feel it going down when you stand still — a sense of hollow spaces beneath hollow spaces, of architecture that burrows rather than rises. Sometimes through cracks in the flooring you can see the level below, where more stones have been laid in different patterns, where different activities are occurring.

The moisture comes from above and from within. It condenses on the underside of the rare stone ceilings — for most of this place is open to whatever darkness exists overhead — and falls in irregular droplets. The drops are cold. They strike the stone floors with sounds that carry: *tock*, *tock*, *tock*. Not rain, exactly. Individual events, spaced by seconds or minutes, each one clear and separate in the dark. The stones themselves are often damp, slick with a thin film of condensation that never quite evaporates in the cool air. In some areas, water has pooled in the low spots where the floor construction is imperfect, creating shallow lenses of black water, rarely more than an inch deep, that you step through without warning.

There is also water that moves horizontally. Channels have been cut into some of the floor stones — shallow gutters that guide moisture from one area to another according to a logic you cannot parse. The water in these channels flows slowly, almost grudgingly, making soft lapping sounds against the stone edges. The channels sometimes lead to basins, carved deep into the floor itself, where water collects and waits. For what, you cannot say.

The darkness is not absolute but it is severe. There is light, yes, but only barely. It comes from embers — small fires of dried fungal matter or oils rendered from things you do not want to identify, contained in carved stone bowls or crude metal braziers hammered from copper or soft iron. These lights are spaced irregularly across the floors, appearing as distant amber pinpricks in the vast dark. Near them, you can see perhaps ten or fifteen feet. Between them, you navigate by sound and by the faint memory of the last light you passed. The fires make no smoke, or if they do, the smoke rises invisibly into the greater darkness above and disperses there.

The sound is the most constant thing here. It is *loud* in the way a factory is loud — not a single overwhelming noise, but a dense tapestry of separate sounds occurring simultaneously, each distinct but all contributing to a whole that makes your ears feel full, pressured, tired after a while.

The tapping is primary. It comes from everywhere, at all scales. The dripping water: *tock tock tock*. But also the sound of stone on stone — deliberate, rhythmic striking. Something here is always building, always shaping, always working. You hear chisel against rock: *tik tik tik tik*, patient, methodical. You hear hammering: *tang... tang... tang*, metal on metal, the ring of it carrying across the floors and echoing back from surfaces you cannot see. You hear scraping — long, drawn-out grinding sounds as stone is dragged across stone, as heavy pieces are shifted into place. You hear the *shhhhh-shhhhh-shhhhh* of something being sanded or filed.

Beneath this percussion, there are other sounds. Breathing — heavy, labored breathing from exertion. Not human breathing, quite. The rhythm is slightly wrong, the inhalations a bit too long, the exhalations with an odd flutter at the end. Footsteps: bare feet on stone, multiple pairs, walking with purpose. The soft plash of something moving through one of the shallow pools. A distant sound like singing, but with too many voices layered over each other, and the melody follows intervals that make your jaw clench.

Sometimes you hear things falling — the clatter of a tool dropped, the heavier *thoom* of a stone piece toppling. Sometimes there is a sound like laughter, but it is almost certainly not laughter. It is too rhythmic, too patterned, more like a vocalization that serves some social function you do not recognize.

The fauna is rare but present, and strange. There are things here — creatures that move through this constructed landscape as if they belong to it, as if they were made by it or for it.

You see them sometimes near the ember-lights: beings that might once have been something mammalian but have been here so long they have become part of the stone world. They are hairless, their skin the color and almost the texture of dark slate, slightly granular. They move on four limbs or six — you have seen both — and their joints seem to bend in too many places. They are builders. You watch one crouched near a light, using a simple copper chisel to score lines into a stone piece. Its hands — if they are hands — have too many fingers, each one long and thin with a broad, flat tip. It works with intense concentration, tapping precisely, checking its work by touch.

There are also smaller things. Creatures like segmented worms as thick as your thumb, pale white, eyeless, that move through the thin water films on the floor. They seem to feed on the mineral paste between stones, or perhaps on something that grows in it — a microbial film, maybe, or something even simpler. When disturbed, they curl into tight spirals and secrete a droplet of clear fluid that smells like wet metal.

Flying things exist in the darkness above, though you rarely see them. You hear them: a leathery flapping, the sound of something large moving through air. Occasionally one descends close enough for you to catch a glimpse in the ember-light — membranous wings stretched between struts that might be bone or might be something else, a body that is all efficiency, no decoration. They seem to patrol the upper reaches of this place, or hunt the smaller things that also fly there, or perhaps they serve some function in the construction, carrying materials from one location to another.

The weirdness is in the details, in the way things almost make sense but don't quite.

The construction follows rules, but they are not the rules of human architecture. There are areas where the floors spiral — not staircases, but flat surfaces that curve in three dimensions while remaining walkable, creating a topology that hurts to think about. You walk on them and your inner ear reports conflicting information. Water flows on these spirals, following the gradient, but sometimes the water seems to flow upward, and you realize the gradient itself is not what you thought.

There are zones of the floor where the stones are smaller, denser — areas where the pavement becomes almost a mosaic of tiny pieces, each no larger than your thumbnail, fitted together with insane precision. These areas are often damp, always cold, and they hum. Not loudly, but perceptibly. A low frequency that you feel in your sternum more than hear. No one is doing anything to make them hum. They simply do.

There are structures that rise from the floors — not buildings, exactly, but *occurrences*. Pillars that branch. Walls that peter out after a few feet, going nowhere, serving no obvious purpose. Arches made from precisely cut voussoirs that frame nothing but more darkness. Sometimes you find what might be furniture — stone platforms at various heights, arranged in groups — but their proportions are wrong for sitting or lying. The six-limbed builders use them, crouching on them, arranging tools on them, but their purpose remains opaque.

In some areas, the floor itself is in progress. You can see where the pavement ends and raw stone begins — not natural stone, but prepared stone, surfaces that have been ground flat and are waiting for the pavement to reach them. The builders work at these edges, carrying pieces from stockpiles, testing fits, setting them in place with the mineral paste. They work steadily, without haste, without rest that you can detect. The process is both ancient and ongoing. This place has been under construction for a very long time, and it will continue to be under construction for a very long time more.

The technology is simple, almost primitive, but employed with absolute mastery. Copper tools — chisels, hammers, scrapers, all hand-forged, the metal soft enough that it must be resharpened constantly. You see builders pausing in their work to strike a chisel edge against a stone, flattening the mushroomed copper, then grinding it sharp again on an abrasive block. Simple iron exists here too — fire-worked, not smelted in any sophisticated way. The iron is used for wedges and prying bars, for splitting larger stones into pieces.

There are no wheels. Materials are dragged on sledges or carried. The sledges are stone platforms with curved runners, pulled by teams of the builders or sometimes by larger fauna — creatures like the six-limbed builders but scaled up, more heavily muscled, moving with the resigned patience of beasts of burden.

Fire is made with friction or by striking stones, though it is not made often. The ember-bowls are maintained, fed carefully with dried fungoid cakes that the builders produce somehow — perhaps cultivated in the lower levels where you have not been. The metal tools are heated in small forges: stone boxes where embers are concentrated and air is pushed through with leather bellows, crude but effective.

The craftsmanship is extraordinary. Every stone piece is individual, shaped by hand, and yet they fit together into expanses that are level to within a fraction of an inch across distances of hundreds of feet. The builders measure constantly — using cords, using straight edges made from dressed stone, using their bodies as references. They check and recheck. The work proceeds slowly, but what is made is *right* according to the standards of this place.

Water is gathered in the basins and dispensed somehow into channels. You have not seen the mechanism, but there must be one. The water does not flow randomly. It goes where it is needed, or where something needs it. Perhaps it is part of the process of making the mineral paste. Perhaps the builders drink it, though you have never seen them do so. Perhaps it serves some purpose in the lower levels, trickling down through intentional gaps in the floor to places where other work is happening.

The temperature is cool — not cold enough to see your breath, but cool enough that you are always slightly uncomfortable if you stop moving. The stone saps heat from your body. The builders do not seem to notice. They work bare-skinned in the cool air, in the dampness, and show no sign of discomfort. Perhaps their physiology does not register it. Perhaps this is the optimal temperature for them.

The air smells of mineral — wet stone, iron, copper, the dusty smell of stone being worked. Underneath that is a biological smell, musky and slightly sour, the scent of the builders themselves. And underneath that, from the pools and channels, a smell like deep wells, like water that has been underground a long time.

There is no sky. You look up into darkness and see nothing. Sometimes you think you detect a ceiling far above — a sense of enclosure, a subtle echo that suggests a boundary — but you cannot be sure. Perhaps this entire place is within something. A vast cavern, miles across. Or perhaps the darkness itself is the limit, and above a certain height there is simply nothing, a void that sounds and light do not penetrate.

The scale of the place is difficult to judge. You walk for hours and the floors continue. Sometimes you see other lights in the distance — the ember-bowls of work sites far away, appearing as tiny sparks in the black. You walk toward one and it takes so long to reach it that you question whether it's actually where you thought it was. Space seems to behave oddly here. Distances are unreliable. Or perhaps the floors curve in ways that are not apparent, and you are walking along the inside of some enormous geometrical structure.

The density of activity varies. Some areas are thick with builders — dozens of them working in close proximity, their sounds overlapping into a continuous roar of industry. Stone is being cut, moved, placed. The mineral paste is being mixed in wide stone bowls, stirred with wooden paddles that have been worn smooth. Finished pieces are being checked against templates — stone shapes that represent ideal forms, kept as references.

Other areas are nearly empty. You walk across vast pavements where no one is working, where the construction was finished long ago. The stones here are older — you can tell by the way the edges have softened slightly, by the accumulation of mineral deposits in the joints. The pools are more established, their edges stained with salts. These areas are quiet except for the ambient sounds: dripping, the distant percussion of work elsewhere, the occasional passage of a solitary builder on some errand.

The fauna in these empty zones is slightly different. The segmented worm-things are more abundant, creating visible trails through the water films. Other creatures emerge — things like pale crustaceans with too many legs, scuttling across the stones. They eat the worm-things, or eat what the worm-things eat, or eat each other. It is a simple ecology, based on minerals and microbes and the slow, cold chemistry of the damp stone world.

In some pools, there are sessile things — organisms that look like translucent sacs, attached to the stone, pulsing slowly. They might be filtering the water. They might be part of the water management system, engineered by the builders. Or they might be wild things that have colonized this constructed environment and found a niche in it.

The dynamic here is one of relentless process. The construction proceeds. Water falls, collects, flows. The builders work, eat (though you have not seen them eat), perhaps sleep (though you have not seen them sleep). The tools wear down and are resharpened or replaced. Stones are shaped, moved, placed. The floor extends, slowly, outward and downward.

There are rhythms. The work sounds have patterns — periods of intensity followed by lulls. Perhaps shifts change, though there is no light cycle here to mark time. Perhaps the builders rotate through tasks. You notice that sometimes an area will fall nearly silent for a span, and then activity will resume with renewed vigor.

The moisture varies too. Sometimes the dripping intensifies — more water condensing above, falling in a denser rain of individual drops. The pools fill slightly. The channels flow faster. Then it subsides, and for a time the air is drier, the stones less slick.

Something in the darkness above may be breathing — expanding and contracting, warming and cooling, creating these cycles. Or perhaps there is a distant sea whose moisture reaches here through some atmospheric pathway. Or perhaps the builders themselves manage it, controlling humidity through means you do not understand.

You find places where the work has failed. Sections of floor where the stones have settled unevenly, creating a dip or a raised area. The builders do not seem to mind. They work around it, or they incorporate it, turning the imperfection into a feature. Or sometimes they tear it out — you find zones where pavement has been lifted, the stones stacked to the side, and the work is beginning again from the prepared surface beneath.

There are accidents. You find a place where a large stone piece has cracked in half during placement. The pieces remain where they fell, pushed to the side. The builders have continued the pavement around them. Perhaps they will be re-used as smaller pieces later. Or perhaps they will remain as monuments to the failure, reminders of the difficulty of the work.

You find areas that seem to be storage. Stone pieces stacked in organized piles, sorted by size or shape. Tools laid out on stone tables. Coils of leather rope, plaited carefully, stored dry above the dampness. Sacks made from some kind of leather or heavy fabric, containing powders — the minerals for the paste, perhaps. The organization implies planning, logistics, a long view.

And you find areas that serve no purpose you can determine. A section of floor where the stones are all the same shape — perfect hexagons — extending for a hundred feet and then giving way to the usual chaos of forms. A single pillar, standing alone, carved with patterns that might be decorative or might encode information. A basin filled not with water but with small copper spheres, tarnished green, arranged in a pyramid.

These zones feel like experiments, or like artworks, or like religious sites. The builders pass through them without pausing, without acknowledgment, but they do not disturb them either.

The strangeness accumulates. The longer you spend here, the more you notice things that do not fit. A stone piece that is too thin to support weight, yet supports it anyway. A channel that flows uphill for a few feet before resuming its downward course. A shadow that falls in the wrong direction relative to the nearest light source.

The sounds sometimes include voices — not the builder vocalizations, but something else. Syllables that sound almost like words, spoken in the middle distance, impossible to locate. You walk toward them and they recede. You stop walking and they continue, conversational, as if two entities are discussing something just out of earshot.

The darkness itself feels textured — thicker in some places, thinner in others, as if it has weight and can be displaced. You pass through zones where the darkness seems to resist, where moving forward feels like pushing through something, and then you emerge into darkness that is lighter, more yielding.

This is a place that was made. Everything here is deliberate, constructed, ongoing. But it was not made for you, or for anything you understand. The builders work according to plans that exist in their minds or in their culture or in some other form entirely. The purpose of the floors, of the channels, of the basins, of the spirals — all of it is opaque.

You are a visitor here, tolerated but irrelevant. The builders notice you but do not react. You are not part of their system. You do not threaten the work, and so you are ignored.

You walk the floors. You listen to the tapping, the scraping, the breathing, the falling water. You step through the shallow pools. You pass the ember-lights, each one an island of dim amber visibility in the greater dark. You watch the builders at their endless, patient, precise work.

And the floors extend. And extend. And extend.
