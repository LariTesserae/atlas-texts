# The Breathing Manufactory

In a shallow basin where night refuses to fully lift, there stands what was once — or is becoming — a factory complex. The structures rise perhaps three stories at their highest, brick and riveted iron, but the geometry has gone soft and wrong. The buildings lean at angles that shouldn't hold, their walls rippling with a respiration that has nothing to do with wind. The darkness here is textured, granular, as though the air itself has gained mass without quite becoming fog.

The plant life doesn't grow *on* the factory. The plant life *is* the factory, or the factory is digesting into plant, or they are the same substance caught mid-transformation. Massive vines — each as thick as drainage pipes — form the actual structural columns. They pulse visibly, slowly, a rhythm like deep sleep breathing. The brick walls between them are more suggestion than barrier; they phase in and out of solidity, present for several minutes as genuine masonry, then softening to something more like dense moss pressed into the *memory* of bricks. Touch them during the soft phase and your hand sinks in slightly, meeting gentle resistance, the surface warm and faintly damp.

The moisture comes in waves. Every seventeen minutes — you can time it — the air grows suddenly wet. Not rain, but rather as though the entire space decides to exhale humidity all at once. Condensation beads on the iron crossbeams, runs down the breathing vines, collects in small pools that appear in different places each cycle. The pools never exceed the size of a washbasin. They drain away through cracks that seem to open specifically for them, then close again. Some of the pools hold water. Others hold something more like sap, or the clear fluid from inside plant stems, slightly viscous, faintly sweet-smelling.

The light barely penetrates here. It comes from above, but filtered through something — perhaps an uneven canopy far overhead, perhaps the sky itself has grown compromised. What arrives is less illumination than the absence of complete darkness: a deep charcoal dimness with occasional zones where slightly more light concentrates in slow-moving patches, as if beamed through water. You can see perhaps twenty feet with clarity, another ten in vague shapes, then the darkness becomes total. Yet the darkness moves. It flows in currents. Sometimes it presses close, and the visible world shrinks to arm's length. Sometimes it pulls back, and you can suddenly see the full interior of a factory hall that wasn't there a moment before.

The space itself is small but refuses to stay small. You could walk the perimeter in five minutes if the perimeter agreed to be walked. The complex seems to consist of four or five main structures connected by covered walkways where the roofs are solid masses of interwoven vines supporting gardens of ferns and shelf fungi. There's a central courtyard, perhaps forty feet across, where the moisture pools most frequently. There are interior chambers: a boiler room where the boilers have burst open to reveal chambered interiors like cross-sectioned fruit; a manufacturing floor where the assembly line has become a connected organism, machines linked by living tissue; a stairwell that spirals both up and down despite the building having no basement. The stairwell's down direction leads to more factory floor. The stairs are made of wood that grows fresher and more living the deeper you descend, until you're stepping on raw saplings that nevertheless hold your weight.

Walk through any doorway twice and you won't get the same room. The architecture is actively dreaming itself.

The sound environment rests in near-silence, but it's a dense, populated silence. The baseline is a low thrumming hum — barely audible, felt more in the chest than heard — that comes from the vines' respiration. Beneath that: the drip and trickle of moisture, constant but never rhythmic, each drop following its own timing. Occasionally, from deeper in the structure, there comes a mechanical sound: a distant clank of metal on metal, a gear beginning to turn then stopping, the wheeze of a steam valve. These sounds are genuine, produced by actual machinery that still exists somewhere in the complex, still trying to fulfill whatever function it was built for. But they're intermittent, separated by long pauses, as if the factory only remembers it's supposed to operate in brief moments of lucidity.

The animals are everywhere, and they're wrong in the same way the factory is wrong. There are birds — dozens of species, far too many for a space this size — but they perch inside the machinery, nest in the open furnaces, walk along the assembly lines with deliberate steps as though inspecting the work. Their bodies are normal: sparrow-shaped, crow-shaped, warbler-shaped. But they move with an intelligence that's uncomfortable to watch. They gather in small committees around specific pieces of equipment. They manipulate objects — moving a wrench from one location to another, repositioning a fallen bolt — using their beaks with precision that seems tool-using, purposeful.

There are mammals in the walls. Not mice or rats, but things the size and general shape of cats, or otters, or something between. They have too many legs — six is common, eight is not unusual — but the legs are arranged in ways that work, that allow fluid movement through the narrow spaces between real wall and living wall. They tend the plants. You can watch them pruning with their teeth, removing dead leaves, clearing away fungal infections. They harvest small fruits that grow on some of the vines — waxy, purple things the size of crabapples — and carry them to storage areas in the machinery. Whether they eat these fruits or are stockpiling them for some other purpose is unclear.

There are insects, of course. Clouds of them in certain areas, almost none in others. They follow patterns: gathering where the moisture pools appear, dispersing when the darkness presses in, forming living columns between floor and ceiling in the tallest spaces. They're not behaving like insects. They're behaving like flocking birds, like schooling fish, moving with collective intelligence. Sometimes they form shapes: spirals, grids, rotating geometries that hold for several minutes then dissolve. The shapes seem to correspond to the machinery. A cloud of gnats will form a perfect spiral above a coiled spring. Beetles will arrange themselves in a grid that matches the rivet pattern on a nearby wall panel.

And there are the large ones. Three or four creatures — perhaps more; they're difficult to count because they might be the same individual seen multiple times — that are the size of adult humans. Bipedal, but wrong. The body plan is vaguely ursine: thick-bodied, heavy-shouldered, but standing upright in a way bears don't. The limbs are wrong — too many joints, or joints in unexpected places. The heads are worst: mammalian skulls stretched and compressed simultaneously, eye sockets that might hold two eyes or four or six, depending on the viewing angle. They're covered in something between fur and moss, and small flowers grow in their coat, opening and closing in time with the moisture cycles.

These large ones are doing something. They move through the factory with purpose, operating the machinery. One of them stands at a control panel, moving levers in a sequence that might be random or might be deeply meaningful. Another feeds raw plant material into a hopper that grinds and processes it, producing bricks of compressed matter that another creature carefully stacks in a corner. They're manufacturing something. They're maintaining something. The factory is running, after a fashion, under their operation.

They don't acknowledge observers. If you stand in their path, they simply route around you, their movement smooth and uninterrupted, as though you're furniture they've learned to navigate. But they're aware. Sometimes one will pause and turn its head — that terrible head — in your direction, and you can feel the attention, though what it's assessing or recognizing is unknowable.

The world here runs on rules that are adjacent to normal physics. Gravity works, mostly, but objects sometimes rest at angles they shouldn't. A wrench lying on a vertical wall. A pool of water that bulges upward in the middle instead of lying flat. The vine-columns that support the structure are weight-bearing — you can feel them holding the factory up — but they're also hollow. Look closely at the surface and you can see through the plant tissue to an interior space that's larger than the exterior dimensions allow, chambers inside the vines where things move, where smaller animals nest, where the plant stores resources or memories or instructions for its own growth.

Time feels negotiable. Spend what seems like twenty minutes exploring a room, check a mechanical clock on the wall — several still run, their mechanisms merged with living tissue but still functional — and find that forty minutes have passed. Or five. The moisture cycles are the only reliable tempo, arriving like clockwork every seventeen minutes, but even those sometimes double-trigger, two exhalations coming back-to-back, or skip entirely, leaving a gap of thirty-four minutes that feels like a held breath.

The technology level is firmly industrial — this was built during an age of steam power, electrical lighting, mechanical production. Edison-era bulbs still hang from some ceilings, their filaments dark but intact. Generators lurk in side rooms, their flywheels occasionally spinning in lazy rotations. There's a boiler that still produces heat, though what it's burning is unclear; feed tubes run into the plant-walls, suggesting it's consuming the building itself as fuel, or the building is offering fuel, a symbiotic arrangement. The electrification still functions in patches. Sometimes lights flicker on — not the Edison bulbs but bioluminescent clusters that have grown where fixtures used to be, phosphorescent organs that pulse in patterns that might be communication.

The machinery is elaborate: punch presses that rise two stories, their hydraulic rams merged with wooden posts; conveyor systems where the belts are made of overlapping leaves, still moving; intricate clockwork assemblies in glass cases, now terrarium-like environments where tiny frogs shelter among the gears. The factory was designed for precision manufacture at scale. What it manufactured is lost, but the capacity remains, now repurposed by the creatures and the living building itself for production of those compressed plant-bricks, for refinement of sap into different consistencies collected in repurposed oil drums, for processes that have no human analogue.

There's infrastructure everywhere. Pipes run along ceilings and walls, some still carrying water or steam, others serving as habitat — crack one open and dozens of small salamanders pour out, each one perfectly transparent, their organs visible, droplets of water held inside their bodies like pearls. Electrical conduits have become root channels. Ventilation shafts breathe with the building, drawing in air, exhaling that periodic humidity. A network of narrow-gauge rail still crosses some floors, the tracks rusty but present, and occasionally a cart appears, moves along ten feet of track, then sinks into the floor at a junction that shouldn't exist.

The plant matter is overwhelming in its variety and omnipresence. Those massive structural vines are the foundation, but from them grows an entire ecosystem compressed into this small space. Ferns explode from every corner, some delicate maidenhair varieties, others sturdy bracken that crunch underfoot. Mosses coat every horizontal surface in cushions three inches thick, different species in different zones: bright emerald near the moisture pools, dark forest green in the perpetual shadows, and in one corner, a shocking crimson moss that seems to glow slightly in the darkness.

Fungi are everywhere. Shelf fungi project from walls in stepped arrangements, some large enough to use as shelves — and something has used them as shelves, placing objects there: a rusted padlock, a perfect bird skull, a spool of copper wire. Mushroom colonies sprout from the floor in fairy rings, their caps ranging from white to purple to a disturbing flesh-tone. There are puffballs the size of human heads that the large creatures occasionally kick, releasing spore clouds that the insects immediately swarm through, coating themselves, then spreading the spores throughout the complex.

Higher up, visible when the darkness retreats enough, there are flowering vines woven through the iron rafters. They bloom in cycles independent of the moisture waves: small white flowers appearing all at once, staying open for perhaps an hour, then closing and dropping their petals in a brief snow of botanical matter. The petals don't rot. They simply vanish within minutes of hitting the ground, reabsorbed directly by the floor.

The air quality changes from zone to zone. In the central courtyard, it's clean and cool, with that slight dampness and a smell like forest after rain. In the deeper rooms, it grows thicker, more oxygenated — you breathe easier than you should, each breath almost intoxicating. There are pockets where the air is thin and you need to breathe more frequently. There are areas where it smells of machine oil and verdigris, others where it's purely vegetative: chlorophyll and sap and the sweet rot of composting leaves.

The temperature hovers in the cool range, perhaps fifty-five degrees Fahrenheit, though it fluctuates. Near the boiler, it climbs to a more comfortable sixty-eight or seventy. In the stairwell leading down, it drops to perhaps forty-five, cold enough that your breath just barely begins to fog. The moisture cycles don't significantly change the temperature, but they change how it feels — that sudden humidity makes the cool air heavy, makes it wrap around you.

The erosion level is inverse here. Rather than a landscape being worn down, this is a landscape being built up, but uncertainly, in its earliest phases. The factory was complete once — there's enough structure present to sense the original design — but now it's being reconstructed according to different principles. You can find areas where the transformation has barely begun: a section of wall that's still brick all the way through, untouched by the living infiltration. And you can find areas where it's advanced: rooms where there's no brick at all anymore, just dense plant matter shaped into room-form, with perfect corners and level floors grown from continuous organic tissue.

The process is visible. Stand still for ten minutes and watch a brick wall. You'll see veins begin to trace across its surface, thin green lines that darken and swell. Roots probe mortar joints, not destroying but... translating. Where the root touches, the brick softens into something else. Not gone, but incorporated. The wall remains a wall, continues being a wall, but becomes a wall made of different substance while retaining all its wall-properties.

In some rooms, you can see the future of the process. Everything is plant, but shaped into industrial forms. A lathe made entirely of wound vines, hard as wood, with a spindle that rotates when the large creatures turn a crank-growth. A workbench that's a flat-topped fungal shelf eight feet long, its surface as smooth as milled lumber. A gear train grown from concentric wooden rings, each tooth shaped perfectly to mesh with its neighbors, the whole assembly operating when water is poured into a root-feed at its base.

The space rejects the concept of being truly empty despite its vast interior volumes. The density is extremely low — walk into the main manufacturing hall and you could fit a hundred people in the space, with room for movement between them. The ceilings are high, fifteen or twenty feet in places, and most of that volume is just air and dim light. The aisles between machinery are wide enough for two people to walk abreast with space to spare. And yet the space never feels empty because of what moves through it.

Those insect clouds redistribute constantly, so there's always something in motion in your peripheral vision. The birds relocate between perches, maintaining a low background flutter of wings. The darkness itself flows, so the edges of the visible space are never static. And there's the constant sense of activity just beyond perception — sounds suggesting movement in the next room, shadows crossing distant doorways, the feeling of processes continuing everywhere simultaneously.

You could be alone in a vast chamber and still feel accompanied. The space holds presence without bodies, attention without eyes. It's the opposite of emptiness — it's expectant space, charged space, volume filled with potential rather than matter.

The weird permeates everything. It's not localized anomalies scattered through an otherwise normal environment; it's the fundamental condition. Those large creatures with their variable eye-count aren't glitches — they're the inhabitants, the operators, possibly the owners. The shifting architecture isn't malfunction; it's how this place works. The factory-plant hybrid isn't corruption of either category; it's a third thing that's stable in its own right, that's found its equilibrium.

Causality bends here. You'll see one of the large creatures collecting plant matter from the manufacturing floor, feeding it into the hopper, and watch bricks emerge from the press. But later you'll trace the path of those bricks and discover they're building a wall that's already complete, has been complete, that you walked past when you first arrived. The bricks are being made to have always been there. The creatures are manufacturing the past.

Sometimes you'll see yourself. Not a reflection — there are no mirrors. But you'll turn a corner and see a figure ahead that's wearing your clothes, moving the way you move, and as you approach it recedes, staying always at the edge of clear vision, until you realize you're following your own presence backward or forward through time, watching yourself from five minutes ago or five minutes hence explore the same corridor.

Objects appear and disappear. A section of floor clear at one moment holds a coil of rope at the next, but the rope is rusted and ancient, has clearly been there for decades. Time is layered here, multiple states superimposed, and which one is active at any given moment is fluid.

The sound of distant machinery is sometimes actually distant, sometimes coming from equipment that's standing right next to you but existing ten minutes in the past, its sound arriving now through some slippage in temporal flow. Occasionally you'll hear voices — human voices, speaking in fragments. Instructions, conversations, casual remarks about work schedules and lunch breaks. The factory remembering when it was operated by people, playing back recordings written into its structure, audible now that the structure is alive and dreaming.

The dynamic level is high. The moisture cycles every seventeen minutes. The light shifts in its slow currents, brightening and dimming over spans of several minutes. The insects are in constant motion, their formations building and dissolving. The creatures perform their tasks continuously — manufacturing, maintenance, patrol routes through the complex. The plants are actively growing; you can't see it happen, but return to a room after an hour and the vines have moved slightly, the fern clusters are denser, new mushrooms have appeared.

The building breathes. That low thrumming pulse that's always present is respiration, and with each breath — every minute or two — the whole structure shifts slightly. Walls expand outward by an inch, then contract. Floors rise and fall in subtle waves. The movement is organic, coordinated, the entire factory complex operating as a single massive organism maintaining its life processes.

Weather happens, despite being interior space. The moisture exhalations are the primary cycle, but there are others. Occasionally a wind picks up, moving through the halls with purpose, strong enough to stir loose papers (which exist, scattered in corners, their text now unreadable, the language having been absorbed and altered by the plant matter). The temperature will sometimes drop suddenly, five or ten degrees in as many seconds, then slowly recover. Once every few hours, there's an event like a distant thunder — a deep vibration that shakes the structure, rattles the machinery, sets all the insects to sudden flight and all the creatures to a moment of stillness, as though the building is startled by its own dream.

Biological processes are everywhere. The creatures are eating — you find evidence in partially consumed plant matter, in careful collections of empty seed pods. They're defecating — small pellets composed of compressed plant fiber appear in specific corners, which smaller animals then collect and reintegrate into the walls. The plants are photosynthesizing despite the dim light, drawing energy from something, their leaves slowly tracking the movement of the light-patches across the room. They're competing: a moss will overgrow a fern, then the fern will send up new shoots that pierce through the moss layer. Fungi digest fallen plant matter, breaking it down to soil that accumulates in corners until the creatures shovel it into the machinery.

There's predation, but strange. The birds hunt insects, but selectively — they'll take certain species and leave others, as though managing populations rather than simply feeding. The creatures sometimes catch the six-legged mammals, but instead of killing them, they carry them to specific locations and release them, redistributing them through the complex as though maintaining a desired population density in each area.

The conveyor belts occasionally carry bodies. Small things, mostly: a dead bird, a mammal that died of age or accident. But also plant matter that's been pruned away, broken machinery components, anything that needs to be removed from circulation. The conveyors take these to a room at the complex's edge where everything is fed into a hopper that grinds and processes and recycles. Nothing is waste here; everything returns to the system.

Even the dust is active. It doesn't settle permanently. It accumulates on surfaces for a few hours, then something comes along — insects, or one of the mammals, or possibly the surface itself — and clears it away. The complex maintains itself, keeps itself functional, an ecosystem engaged in active preservation of its own conditions.

Stand in the central courtyard when the moisture cycle is about to begin, just as the darkness pulls back for one of its periodic retreats, and you can see the whole system at once. The dim light expands to fill the space, revealing the full extent of the factory-garden hybrid. The massive vines pulse with that slow respiration. Birds perch in committees on every surface. The six-legged mammals move along their pathways through the walls. Insects form their temporary geometries in the air. The large creatures stand at their stations, operating their incomprehensible manufacturing. The moisture beads on every surface, and for just a moment everything glistens.

Then the creature nearest you turns its head — too many eyes in the socket, too many joints in the neck — and looks directly at you with what might be recognition or might be the building itself becoming aware that it's being observed. The machinery in the next room clanks once, twice, then goes silent. A bird on the nearest beam opens its beak and produces not a chirp but a sound like a steam whistle, clear and high.

The darkness flows back in. The visible world contracts. You're standing in a pool of dim light perhaps ten feet across, with the sounds of the place continuing around you: drip, hum, flutter, clank. The factory breathes. The moisture cycle peaks and humidity wraps around you like attention.

Something moves in the peripheral darkness — possibly one of the creatures, possibly just the darkness itself relocating. Seventeen minutes from now the cycle will repeat. Seventeen minutes after that, again. The processes continue. The manufacturing proceeds. Whatever is being built here, in this small space that refuses to stay small, in this darkness that's almost solid, is under construction in layers that don't all exist at the same time.

The place is becoming more itself with every breath.
