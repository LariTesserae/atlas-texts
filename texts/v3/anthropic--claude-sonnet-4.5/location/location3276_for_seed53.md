# The Mirror Quarry

High among the serrated ridges where the world thins to its hardest edges, there exists a basin carved from some ancient, deliberate purpose. Not quite a quarry, though that word approaches what it might have been. The walls rise in faceted angles—not the smooth curves of water-work but the deliberate planes of extraction, each surface catching light like the interior of a vast, fractured geode.

The stone itself is strange. Obsidian, yes, but shot through with veins of something paler, almost opaline. In certain lights these veins seem to move, though they don't—it's the moisture within them, perhaps, or the way cold affects the material's inner structure. The rock face shows the memory of tools: shallow cupmarks where something struck repeatedly, linear grooves where something harder scraped along predetermined lines. These marks are old enough to have softened slightly, their edges no longer sharp but not yet rounded. Time has touched them the way time touches glass—slowly, reluctantly.

The basin itself is perhaps two hundred feet across at its widest point, roughly oval, its floor uneven with the remains of extraction. Blocks lie where they were abandoned, some half-freed from the living rock, others toppled and broken. Between them: water. Not a pool exactly, but a system of shallow catchments in the low places, connected by threadlike channels that appear and disappear depending on recent weather. The water is achingly clear and achingly cold. When you look into it, the bottom seems close enough to touch, though it's deeper than your arm can reach. The obsidian beneath the water gleams like wet ink.

At the basin's heart stands the remnant of something built—a circular structure perhaps twenty feet in diameter, constructed from the very stone that surrounds it. The blocks are precisely fitted, showing that whoever worked here understood the material intimately. The structure rises only five or six feet now, its upper portions gone. Whether it was once taller or was always incomplete remains uncertain. The interior is hollow, and the floor inside is different: flat, worked smooth, with a gentle depression in the center where water collects. Around this depression, carved into the floor, a spiral pattern worn almost to invisibility by something that once moved there repeatedly, or by water that once flowed in that turning path, or both.

The light here is extraordinary. This high, the air is thin and clean, and the sun—when it's visible—strikes with a clarity that feels almost solid. But it's the reflections that transform the place. Every faceted wall catches and throws light across the basin. The wet stones mirror the sky. The water in the central depression becomes a well of luminosity. Even on overcast days, some quality of the stone itself seems to gather and concentrate what brightness exists, so that the quarry glows with a subdued radiance, as if lit from within.

Morning brings fog that doesn't quite descend but hovers at the rim, sending tentative fingers down the walls. These wisps catch in the tool-marks, outlining them in white. As the day warms—a relative term in this cold altitude—the fog withdraws or dissipates, leaving condensation on every surface. Droplets form along the carved grooves, growing until they release and trace the ancient paths downward, feeding the shallow pools. By midday the stone is mostly dry, though the water catchments remain, too deep to evaporate quickly. By evening, if conditions are right, a different mist forms from the water itself, rising a few inches above the pools like breath.

Rain comes infrequently but decisively. When it does, the entire basin transforms into a system of tiny waterfalls and rushing channels. The sound changes from near-silence to a complex percussion—water striking stone at different pitches, overflows, the deeper note of pooling. Within hours it's over, and the quarry returns to its usual state of scattered moisture and stillness, though the pools are deeper, the channels more pronounced for a time.

The scattered artifacts here speak of methodical work suddenly interrupted. Tools, or what remains of them: wooden handles rotted to dark stains in protected crevices, but the worked stones they once held still present. These are simple things—hammerstones with use-patterns worn into their surfaces, wedges of harder stone shaped to fit the veins in the obsidian, what might be picks or pry-bars, the metal portions (copper, perhaps, or very soft bronze) corroded to green stains but retaining their essential shapes. They lie in small clusters, as if work crews set them down at day's end and never returned.

Near the circular structure, partially buried in the fine stone dust that accumulates in the sheltered places, there are objects that might be offerings or might be simply stored: smooth pebbles of non-local stone, arranged in patterns now disturbed by settling and time. A series of thin obsidian blades, each one palm-length, wrapped in something that decayed away, leaving only a dark outline and the blades themselves in a careful row. Small stone cups or bowls, carved from the same material as the walls, sitting in niches cut into the interior of the circular structure.

In terms of life, the quarry holds almost nothing. Occasionally, something visits—you might find tracks at the edge of a pool, three-toed marks pressed into the fine dust, but you'll rarely see the creature itself. It's small, whatever it is, and nocturnal, and whether it's coming for the water or for something else remains unclear. The tracks always lead to the circular structure, circle it once or twice, then depart.

More commonly: ice formations. In the coldest parts of the year, the pools freeze, but not entirely. The ice grows in delicate structures upward from the edges, meeting in the center to form thin bridges and arches. Beneath, the water continues to move minutely, and the ice above records this movement in its layers and patterns. Sometimes these ice formations remain even after the pools beneath have receded through evaporation and seepage, leaving crystal architecture standing free, unsupported, until the next rain dissolves them.

The sound environment is predominantly silence, but a silence with texture. The thin wind at this altitude makes a continuous whisper as it moves across the stone faces—not quite a whistle, more a sustained breath. Water, when present in the channels, adds a delicate trickling that seems magnified by the stone acoustics, so that a flow you can step across sounds like a legitimate stream. Occasionally there's the tick and crack of stone responding to temperature changes: the quarry is always, infinitesimally, shifting. These sounds are sharp in the stillness, startling when they come.

When rain falls, the acoustic transforms completely. Each surface responds differently—the flat stones ring, the angled facets produce overtones, the pools create deep notes as they fill. The circular structure acts as a resonator, amplifying certain frequencies. Standing inside it during rain, you're surrounded by a chord that seems both random and organized, as if the quarry itself is an instrument played by weather.

The air smells of stone and cold and something faintly metallic—perhaps from the trace minerals in the water, perhaps from the corroded tools. After rain, there's a sharpness, almost like ozone but not quite. In certain conditions, when the fog is present, you can smell something older underneath—a mustiness that might be time itself, the accumulated decades or centuries of moisture working slowly into stone that resists it.

At the edges where the quarry meets the surrounding peaks, the stone becomes less worked, more natural, showing the gradual transition from deliberate excavation to the mountain itself. Here you can see how the obsidian formation runs—a thick seam between other layers, and it's clear why this particular place was chosen. The material was accessible, abundant, and of unusual quality. Those who worked here knew what they were looking for.

The blocks that were quarried out vary in size, but they follow certain proportions—roughly rectangular, some small enough to carry, others requiring multiple people. Many show the stages of extraction: the line of holes where wedges were driven, the partial splits, the places where the stone cooperated or resisted. Several blocks lie broken, the obsidian's conchoidal fracture making patterns like frozen waves through them. These failures weren't removed; they became part of the landscape.

Seasons here are subtle. The cold deepens or lessens. The water comes more frequently or not at all. The light changes angle more than it changes duration—even in the depths of winter, the basin receives brilliant illumination for at least part of the day, though the sun tracks lower and casts longer shadows from the rim. In high summer (still cold by any reasonable measure), the sun seems to hover longer, and the afternoon light strikes directly down into the basin, illuminating the bottom of every pool, revealing the carved channels on the floor of the circular structure in perfect clarity.

There's a rhythm here, though not the rhythm of life as we typically recognize it. Water accumulates and evaporates. Stones expand and contract. Ice forms and melts. Light traces its slow arc around the walls, bringing each carved surface into prominence and then releasing it to shadow. The place is in balance with itself, has been for a long time, will be for longer still.

The weirdness is gentle but persistent. Reflections sometimes show details that aren't quite in the original—a figure in the periphery of your vision in a pool's surface, nothing there when you look directly. The spiral on the floor of the circular structure: if you follow it inward with your eyes, there's a moment of vertigo, as if depth exists where there should only be flat stone. The opaline veins in the walls sometimes seem to pulse very slowly, though this is certainly the moisture within them responding to temperature changes, certainly.

Objects left here don't stay quite where they're placed. Not dramatically—this isn't a place of wild forces—but a stone set carefully on one block will be found a hand's width away the next morning. Whether this is settling, or temperature effects, or something else, the result is the same: the quarry slowly rearranges small things according to logic of its own.

And there's the question of the sound at dawn. Not every dawn, but many: a single note, low and sustained, that seems to come from the basin itself. It lasts perhaps five seconds, fades, doesn't repeat. It might be wind finding a particular configuration of gaps and angles. It might be the stone itself, releasing stored stress. It might be something else. Those who've heard it multiple times report that it's always the same pitch, always the same duration, as regular as a bell.

The boundaries of the quarry are clear—the worked walls define it absolutely—but the sense of the space extends somehow beyond those physical limits. Standing in the center, especially inside the circular structure, there's a feeling of being observed not by entities but by the place itself, as if attention is a quality the basin has developed over its long existence. Not threatening. Simply aware.

People came here with purpose, extracted what they needed, built something precise, and departed. Whether they meant to return and couldn't, or completed what they came for and had no reason to stay, the quarry doesn't say. What remains is a space in equilibrium: stone and water and light and cold, processes turning at their own pace, the memory of intention preserved in every tool-mark and fitted block.

At twilight, just before the light fails completely, the obsidian walls glow briefly with deep purple and blue, the last sunlight finding frequencies in the stone that aren't visible at other times. The pools become mirrors of a sky that's shifting through its own spectrum of cold colors. The ice formations, if present, catch fire with reflected light. For perhaps ten minutes, the quarry is a chamber of luminous darkness, every surface contributing to an effect that seems impossible from the simple materials involved.

Then night, and the stars here are extraordinary—hard points in a sky close enough to touch, their light reflecting in the pools, turning the scattered water catchments into an inverted constellation map. The temperature drops sharply. Ice begins forming at the edges of the deeper pools, a rim of white that will either advance or retreat depending on how cold the night becomes.

The basin rests. The stones contract fractionally. Water trickles through invisible channels beneath the worked blocks, following paths established long ago. The circular structure stands in darkness, its interior pool reflecting whatever light the sky offers. The abandoned tools rest in their positions, unchanged. The footprints at the pool edge, if present, begin to soften as fine stone dust settles into them.

Morning will come, and fog, and the cycle continuing. The Mirror Quarry persists in its particular state of completion: not abandoned, not waiting, simply itself, a place where certain properties of material and light and elevation combine into something that feels, without being alive, somehow present.
