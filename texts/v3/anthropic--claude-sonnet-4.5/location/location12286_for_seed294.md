# The Drowned Lattice

The ocean here is not deep in the way of trenches—it is high. You float suspended in cold water at what was once an altitude, where ancient pylons and cross-beams create a three-dimensional city that has become something else entirely. The structures rise from darkness below and continue upward past sight, a vast skeletal geometry of paths, platforms, buttresses, and connecting spans that once held habitation but now hold only water and the ceaseless ballet of turbulent flow.

The water temperature hovers just above freezing. It moves—gods, how it moves—in great churning gyres and sudden reversals, in curtains of descending cold that pour down through the lattice and rising columns of slightly-less-cold that spiral upward. The structures themselves channel and redirect these flows, creating a complexity of currents that never settles, never calms. Thermoclines slice through at odd angles, visible as shimmer-planes where temperatures meet. You feel the push and pull constantly, the water's restless mathematics working on your body, trying to draw you into one current or another.

The construction is everywhere but spare. Imagine a framework—a municipal scaffolding that once held floors, walls, dwellings, markets, industries—with almost everything stripped away except the primary supporting elements. What remains are the bones: vertical pillars ten meters in diameter, horizontal beams connecting them at intervals, spiral staircases going nowhere but providing passage between levels, arched bridges spanning emptiness. The material is something like concrete but denser, more ceramic, colored in gradients of grey and pale blue-green. Centuries—no, much longer—have worn it smooth, rounded every edge, dissolved the sharpest corners. But it has not crumbled. Whatever they built this from has reached its final state of stability, will look essentially like this until the universe grows cold.

The lattice extends for kilometers in every direction. You can see perhaps forty meters through the dim water before forms become suggestions, a hundred meters before they vanish into blue-grey nothing. But the structure continues far beyond sight—you know this from the way sounds carry, from the pressure patterns, from occasionally glimpsing distant verticals that hint at the framework's true scale. It is a city-sized artifact, or perhaps the remnant of something even larger, occupying a volume that might once have been mountainside, valley, and peak combined.

Light comes from above, filtering down through hundreds of meters of water in the palest possible blue. It is barely enough to see by. Surfaces emerge from shadow only as you approach them, and even then they remain muted, drained of contrast. There is no sun visible, only a general brightening in the upper reaches that fades steadily as you descend. The water itself seems to absorb light preferentially, so that even at what must be midday (if day and night still exist), the world remains locked in perpetual dusk. Looking down past the lattice levels below you is like looking into a well of ink that occasionally reveals a beam or platform edge before swallowing it again.

Nothing grows. There are no kelps clinging to the beams, no algae filming the surfaces, no bacterial mats, no bio-luminescent colonies. The structures are clean in the way of deep time, swept smooth by endless water flow and simply too old to support life's fragile chemistry. The cold helps—at these temperatures, organic processes barely function. But it's more than that. The water itself tastes empty. It holds dissolved minerals, salts, trace metals, but none of the dissolved organics or nutrient suspension that would invite even microbial presence. This is water that has been circulating through this lattice for so long that everything that could be extracted or settled has long since finished that process. What remains is just water and stone and movement.

And yet: the place teems.

They drift through the lattice in loosely associated groups—twenty here, fifty there, a single individual rotating slowly past a vertical beam. The fauna of this place are not fish. They are not recognizably derived from vertebrate patterns at all. Most are radially symmetrical: disk-shaped bodies anywhere from hand-sized to several meters across, fringed with delicate appendages that pulse and wave in the current. Jellyfish is the first comparison that comes to mind, but they're far more structured than that, with harder elements visible within the translucent flesh—not quite skeleton but something that gives them architecture. Some have segmented arms that they extend and retract, probing the water or the smooth surfaces of the lattice. Others trail long filaments behind them like thoughts left visible.

They move—some of them—with purpose, though it's hard to say what that purpose is. Most simply allow the currents to carry them, drifting through the geometric spaces between beams with an ease that suggests they evolved for exactly this environment. But occasionally one will pulse harder with its bell or disk or whatever propels it, cutting across the current to reach a particular platform or intersect with another of its kind. They collide gently, appendages intermingling for long minutes, then separate and drift on.

There are smaller creatures too, though sparse: things like articulated worms the length of your forearm that inch along the beams, their segments flexing with mechanical precision. Clouds of tiny polyps—possibly not fauna at all but some simpler organization of matter—that cluster at certain structural nodes before dispersing. Occasionally something larger passes through the deeper shadows: a shape suggesting tentacles, or multiple bodies moving as one, or a rigid form like a swimming cage. They don't come close enough to see clearly.

The creatures are colorless or nearly so—pale grey, translucent white, the faintest blush of pink in the thicker-bodied specimens. They are variations on absence more than presence. But there are many of them. You rarely have a sightline that doesn't include at least a few individuals at various distances, and sometimes currents carry dozens past a particular beam in the span of minutes. They represent a biomass that seems impossible given the lack of any visible food source, any photosynthetic base, any organic substrate. What do they eat? Each other? Minerals filtered from the water? Or have they achieved some state of near-perfect efficiency, moving through this environment with such minimal energy expenditure that they require almost nothing?

The turbulence is the defining fact of this place. Water descends here in a broad column, spreading as it encounters a platform and flowing horizontally until it finds gaps to pour through. There it accelerates, creating local vortices that set up standing waves against the next level down. A meter to the side, current moves in the opposite direction. Three meters above, everything rotates slowly clockwise. The fauna navigate this chaos with apparent ease, their bodies designed for exactly this instability, but you—an interloper with the wrong shape—are constantly correcting, adjusting, fighting not to be tumbled into a beam or swept downward into the darker levels.

The current patterns shift. Not randomly, but according to some complex periodicity you can't quite grasp. A flow that was steady for many minutes suddenly reverses. A calm zone becomes a rushing stream. You begin to suspect the lattice itself is responsible, its geometry creating feedback loops and hydraulic logic gates, so that water flowing through one section affects conditions several platforms away in cascading fashion. The structure doesn't just occupy the water—it computes with it, processes it, creates an endless churning that may be the point rather than an accident.

Sound is mostly the sound of water itself: a low, constant rush like breath that never quite becomes rhythm. Currents sliding past beams produce tones—not whistles but deep thrums felt as much as heard, as if the entire lattice were a vast submerged organ with invisible hands pressing random keys. Sometimes you hear sharp clicks, quick triple-taps that might be fauna communicating or might be thermal stress in the ancient materials. Once, a groan that seemed to come from everywhere at once, as if the entire structure were settling some minute fraction despite its extreme age. Then nothing again but water movement and the occasional brush of current against your ears producing phantom sounds—voices, music, the memory of traffic—that you know aren't there.

The quietness comes from the cold, you think, and the lack of anything organic enough to decay or bubble or ferment. Even the fauna are silent. When two of them collide and intertwine, there's no sound. When something larger moves through the deep shadow, it displaces water without splash or thrash. Everything here has learned to move within the medium rather than against it, to become part of the flow rather than interrupting it.

The place feels old in a way that transcends geology. The beams and platforms show no cracks, no spalling, no deterioration—but neither do they show any newness, any sharpness, any trace of having been made recently. They simply are, in their final form, and have been so long that they've reached equilibrium with water and time and pressure. You understand, floating here, that this lattice might be the last thing remaining from whatever civilization built it, that mountains might have risen and eroded away since its construction, that seas might have covered and uncovered this region multiple times. And through all that, the lattice persisted, holding its geometry while everything around it changed.

Why was it built? The spacing between levels—about four meters on average—suggests they were meant for human-scale occupation. The platforms that remain, worn circular disks twenty meters across, probably held buildings, apartments, workshops, plazas. The connectors between them would have been streets or transit ways. This was a place where people lived in the air, in the high reaches of some mountain or plateau, a vertical city catching wind and light.

But now it catches water and darkness instead, and has done so for so long that the reversal seems permanent, the final state. Perhaps the ocean rose. Perhaps the land sank. Perhaps something stranger happened—a dimensional shift, a reality overlay, a purposeful flooding by descendants who needed the cold more than the air. There are no clues, no artifacts beyond the structure itself. Even the way the platforms were anchored to the verticals has been smoothed away, leaving only the functional geometry.

You drift to a platform edge and rest against it, wedging yourself into a corner where two beams meet so the current can't pull you away. Below, the lattice extends into darkness with no visible bottom. Above, it fades into slightly-less-dark blue. Around you, the fauna continue their slow ballet, appearing from the dim distance, rotating as they pass, vanishing again into the blue-grey murk. The water churns and shifts, never the same pattern twice, and you realize you could stay here for hours and never see the same configuration of currents, never predict what the flow will do next.

A group of the disk-shaped creatures—eight of them, each perhaps a meter across—drifts past in formation, moving as though connected by invisible threads. Their fringe-appendages pulse in synchrony. They maintain exact spacing despite the turbulent water. You watch them disappear around a vertical pillar, their pale bodies fading like thoughts dissipating.

Elsewhere, something is happening. You sense it through pressure changes in the water before you see it: a major current shift several platforms away. The water there begins to spiral, forming a visible vortex that pulls fauna and cold water downward in a tightening gyre. The creatures don't fight it—they spin with it, perhaps gathering food from whatever gets concentrated in the vortex's center. The phenomenon lasts perhaps two minutes, then dissipates as the current pattern shifts again, and the fauna disperse back into their drifting.

This happens constantly—somewhere in the lattice, currents are forming new patterns, breaking old ones, creating momentary zones of calm or chaos. The dynamism is total but local, fractured into hundreds of small events rather than one coherent storm. The lattice breaks the water's movement into smaller and smaller eddies until even the most violent flow becomes navigable, survivable, just another variation in the endless turbulence.

You push off from your corner and let the current take you, flowing horizontally past beam after beam, catching glimpses down through the levels as you cross gaps in the platforms. Far below—impossibly far—you think you see something glowing, the faintest possible bioluminescence or perhaps mineral fluorescence, a hint that the lattice continues deeper than you can imagine. But then you're past that gap and the view is occluded by the next platform, and the current is carrying you toward a spiral staircase that once led somewhere but now leads only up through water and down through water, the same everywhere.

A creature bumps against you—not aggressive, just collision—and you feel its appendages briefly explore your surface before deciding you're not interesting. It has surprising solidity despite its translucence. The touch is cold and slightly sticky, then gone. It pulses away, joining three others of similar size who might be its kin or its meal or its mates or something else entirely.

The water tastes of iron and salt and centuries. Mineral-rich, organic-poor, colder than comfort, moving always. You are inside a machine that processes water into complexity, into chaos, into the sustained turbulence that these creatures need or have simply adapted to beyond any possibility of living elsewhere. The lattice made them, perhaps, or they made themselves for the lattice after it was drowned, evolution finding purchase in this unlikely environment.

Above, the light does not change. Below, the darkness does not lift. The lattice extends in all four horizontal directions farther than exploration would reveal in a day's swimming. You are inside something vast and ancient and purposeless, a monument that has outlived its purpose and found a second existence as habitat for creatures that might not even know it was built.

The quiet persists. The turbulence persists. The cold persists. The creatures drift and pulse and occasionally intertwine and drift apart. The currents shift in their chaotic patterns. The beams and platforms hold their geometry against time and water and pressure with the patience of things that have no other choice, no other form to erode into.

You could leave, probably, swimming upward through platform gaps toward whatever surface might exist hundreds of meters above. Or descend, following the pylons down into darkness to see if the lattice ever ends or simply fades from existence, its lowest reaches too crushed by pressure to matter.

But for now you float here, at this particular level where light barely reaches and cold water endlessly churns, watching pale creatures navigate geometry worn smooth by time, listening to the deep thrums of water flowing through stone, feeling the push and pull of currents that will never settle into stillness.

The Drowned Lattice extends around you in all directions: sparse but vast, empty but inhabited, ancient but constantly renewed by the water's restless movement. It is the final form of what it will always be now, until ocean or stone finally fails.
