# The Pneumatic Steppe

The land extends without meaningful interruption. Not flat — it rolls, buckles slightly, rises into broad swells that take an hour to climb and an hour to descend — but its variations feel like breathing, like the minor adjustments of something vast at rest. This is old shield rock, worn down through spans that make human time feel like a misunderstood concept. The stone itself is dark — basalt, granite, metamorphic compressions that have forgotten their sedimentary origins — and it shows through everywhere, polished by wind and the passage of bodies into surfaces that hold faint sheen even under dim sky.

The cold here is not dramatic. It doesn't announce itself with frost-shatter or ice. It simply *is* — a persistent 2°C, 3°C, sometimes dropping to -1°C in the long dim that passes for night. Water exists in negotiation with this temperature. In the shallow depressions — and there are thousands of them, millions, scooped out over ages by processes both glacial and thermal — water gathers. Thin films. Temporary pools an inch deep and ten meters across. They appear after the condensation hours, when moisture precipitates directly from air too cold to hold it. The pools last hours or days, then vanish into substrate, into air, leaving only darker patches on the stone.

There are systems of narrow channels, not quite rivers. They run in the worn creases of the land, following paths established so long ago that they've become essential geography. Water moves through them irregularly — sometimes a trickle, sometimes nothing for weeks, then a sudden muscular flow when condensation patterns align with subterranean springs that pulse according to pressures no one has mapped. The channels are only centimeters deep but they run for kilometers, occasionally dropping into shaft-like punctures where water vanishes into the porous depths of the shield. You can hear it going down if you put your ear to the stone: a hollow gulping, then nothing.

The vegetation clings to the mathematics of moisture. Where water reliably returns — the margins of the persistent channels, the deepest depressions, certain crack-systems in the bedrock — there are mosses. They form crusts, dark olive and rust-brown, barely raised from the stone surface. In a few places, where multiple water-sources converge or where substrate has accumulated in wind-protected hollows, there are grasses: wire-thin, silver-green, growing in scattered tufts never more than ankle-high. They make a papery sound when the rare wind passes through them. The spacing between plants feels calculated, as if each has measured exactly how much moisture it can claim.

Lichens accomplish more than the grasses. They've colonized vast territories of the exposed rock, spreading in continent-shapes of pale gray-green, white-edged yellow, black-rimmed orange. They grow slowly enough that their boundaries might not change perceptibly in a human lifetime. Some formations are clearly ancient — thick, complex, their centers raised into baroque topographies at millimeter scale. They fracture the stone beneath them, patient and chemical, releasing grains that will eventually become soil in the deeper cracks.

The fauna is the overwhelming fact of this place.

They are called, in the technical documents from the initial surveys, *Pneumatica ungulata* — the pneumatic ungulates — though that naming feels like an attempt to render comprehensible something that resists easy categorization. They are the size of large sheep or small cattle, though their proportions are wrong for either: bodies longer, legs thicker, necks abbreviated. Their hide is dense with short fibers, not quite fur, not quite feathers, in colors that range from pale gray through brown to a charcoal that's nearly black. They have hooves — this much is familiar — but four-toed, splayed, each toe tipped with a worn nub of horn.

What makes them *Pneumatica* is the system of air-sacs distributed through their bodies. The sacs are extensions of their respiratory anatomy, but they serve multiple purposes: insulation, buoyancy in water, communication, and — most distinctly — locomotion assistance. When they move at speed, which is rare, they can partially inflate these sacs, becoming briefly more air than flesh, bounding across the stone in a manner that seems to violate their mass. The sacs create visible swellings along their flanks, shoulders, haunches. You can watch the swellings migrate as air redistributes.

There are millions of them. Perhaps tens of millions. Counting is difficult because they spread across distances that confound survey methods. From any vantage point on one of the higher swells, you can see them scattered to every horizon: dark shapes on dark stone, moving slowly or standing still, distributed in loose aggregations that shift over days. The density varies — sometimes fifty meters between nearest neighbors, sometimes they cluster more tightly in areas where water has recently collected — but they are never truly absent. Their hoofsteps have polished the stone further. Paths appear where many have walked the same route repeatedly, grooves worn incrementally deeper than the surrounding surface.

They make almost no sound. Their hooves on stone are quiet — a dull *tok tok tok* that carries only a few meters. Occasionally one will exhale sharply through its air-sac system, producing a low resonant tone, something between a pipe organ's lowest note and distant thunder. These exhalations seem to be communication, though their meaning is opaque. Sometimes a single exhalation will prompt responses from nearby individuals, creating a brief exchange of long tones that roll across the steppe and then stop.

The herds — if they can be called that; they have no clear boundaries — move according to patterns that operate at landscape scale. A group of several hundred might drift north-northeast for three days, grazing on lichen and moss, drinking from temporary pools. Then, without obvious signal, they turn west-southwest. Over weeks and months, circulation patterns emerge: great slow gyres of animals following water, following growth cycles of the vegetation, following something else that might be cultural or remembered or responsive to stimuli too subtle for outside observation. They walk over and around each other's territories without conflict. They space themselves with unconscious geometry.

They are the dominant processors of this landscape's biological production. They crop the lichen, limiting but not eliminating it, keeping it in a state of perpetual productivity. Their waste returns nutrients to the stone, feeding the mosses, feeding the microbial crusts. They are the mechanism by which diffuse plant growth is concentrated and redistributed. The land without them would be different, quieter, locked in slower cycles.

## The Industrial Remnants

Scattered through the steppe — far apart, visible as dark irregularities from kilometers away — are the remnants of an industrial presence. Not buildings, exactly. Infrastructure. Wellheads where boreholes descend into the shield, capped with housings of rusted metal and degraded synthetic compounds. Pipelines that surface for stretches then dive back underground, following paths unrelated to terrain, their surfaces scabbed with corrosion, their joints weeping stains. Pumpjack mechanisms, skeletal and still, their balance arms frozen at various angles. Compressor stations, box-like structures with louver-walls, silent now but still radiating a faint petrochemical scent.

The installations are old — decades since operation, maybe a century. They were built for extraction: drawing up the mineral oil that still lies in pressurized reservoirs deep in the shield, or collecting the gases that seep from certain geological formations, or processing the trace metals that water can carry up from below. The operations were automated, minimal-footprint, designed to function with infrequent human intervention. When economic conditions or logistical realities changed, they were simply abandoned. No decommissioning, no removal. Just cessation.

The metal corrodes slowly in the cold dry air. The synthetics become brittle, crack, fragment. But the decay is patient. These objects will be recognizable for centuries more. Some still function partially — wellhead valves seeping gas, solar panels on a monitoring station generating microwatts for systems that no longer report to anyone. One pumpjack, for reasons unclear, still operates: it rocks forward and back once every eight minutes, drawing up small quantities of crude that spill into a catchment that long ago overflowed. The oil spreads in a thin black mirror around the installation, poisoning an area roughly fifty meters across where nothing grows. The pneumatic ungulates avoid it, routing around it so consistently that paths have formed at its perimeter.

The industrial objects create acoustic effects. Wind through louvers produces low moans. Thermal expansion and contraction of metal makes sporadic *pings* and groans. When air pressure changes, gases escape from loosened connections with brief hisses. These sounds are rare enough that they punctuate silence rather than fill it, and they carry far across the hard flat surfaces.

The ungulates interact with the infrastructure in unexpected ways. They scratch against pipe supports, wearing grooves into the metal. They lick certain surfaces — perhaps attracted by salt from ancient sweat, or trace minerals in paint, or something in the corrosion itself. One collapsed compressor station, tilted onto its side, has become a regular sheltering spot: fifty or sixty ungulates will huddle in its lee during the worst of the condensation hours, their breath adding to the moisture that drips from twisted metal overhead.

## Light and Atmosphere

The sky is perpetually overcast, though the cloud layer's thickness varies. At brightest — which occurs irregularly, without clear diurnal pattern — the light is like late northern twilight: enough to see by, enough to navigate, but colors are muted, contrasts are low, and distances become ambiguous. At dimmest, the distinction between land and sky softens. The horizon becomes theoretical. You navigate by the feel of stone underfoot and the shape of darkness.

There is no sun visible, though sometimes a brighter area in the overcast hints at its position. This suggests the clouds are not impossibly thick, just persistent. The light that penetrates is diffuse, directionless, creating minimal shadows. Objects on the landscape reveal themselves through silhouette and texture rather than illumination.

The unusual quality of this place — the thing that registers wrong even if you can't immediately name it — relates to scale and gravity. This is a large world. The horizon is farther away than Earth-trained instincts expect: roughly twenty percent farther given the planet's diameter. More subtly, gravity is weaker: 0.86 standard. Not enough to make movement feel floating or strange, but enough that fatigue accumulates differently, that liquids behave slightly wrong, that the pneumatic ungulates' air-assisted bounding becomes feasible in ways it wouldn't elsewhere.

Air pressure is also lower, though still breathable. Speech sounds thin. Sounds in general don't carry as far as they might on denser worlds. This contributes to the silence: the atmosphere itself refuses to sustain vibration.

The temperature varies on long cycles — not day and night exactly, but shifts that might correlate with this world's rotation (which is slow; a full turn takes thirty-one standard hours) or might be driven by atmospheric circulation patterns at continental scale. The coldest periods bring stillness: air that sits heavy and damp, condensing on every surface. The slightly warmer periods — still cold, just less so — bring movement: gentle air currents that flow down from the higher swells, stirring the wire-grass, carrying scents of lichen and animal and old metal.

Precipitation is rare and strange. Every ten or fifteen days, a mist forms — not falling, just suddenly present, a fog so fine that individual droplets are invisible but collectively they occlude vision beyond fifty meters. The mist persists for hours, coating everything in micro-droplets. This is when the temporary pools form. Then the air clears gradually, the moisture redistributed into stone and plant and animal and air.

## The Deep Patterns

The land operates on overlapping time-scales. At the shortest — minutes to hours — there are the movements of individual ungulates, the brief sounds of gas escaping a valve, the gradual dimming and brightening of already-dim light. At slightly longer scales — days to weeks — the animal herds circulate, water pools form and evaporate, mist-events arrive and pass.

At longer scales still — seasons, if this world has them, though they're not obvious in the temperature record — the lichen grows, the industrial metals corrode, the channels shift microscopically as stone grains redistribute. The ungulates follow migration patterns that complete over months, great loops and whorls that statisticians have mapped but not explained.

And beneath everything, at geologic duration, the shield itself shifts. Continents of stone riding mantle currents too slow to measure, mountain ranges worn to gentle swells, basins filled and drained and filled again. This landscape is old not just in absolute time but in cycles: it has reached a configuration that perpetuates itself, that resists dramatic change, that has settled into rhythms which could continue essentially unchanged for ages more.

You could walk here for days without encountering anything that demanded immediate attention. No predators, no steep drops, no sudden storms. The ungulates would move aside if you approached directly, or more likely would simply watch with dark eyes, their air-sacs making subtle adjustments. You could drink from the pools when they formed, though the water tastes of stone and carries traces of whatever minerals the substrate leaches. You could eat the wire-grass if necessary, or certain of the mosses, though they provide minimal nutrition.

The challenges would be cumulative: the cold that never relents, requiring constant energy expenditure. The dampness that infiltrates gear and clothing and lungs. The distances that extend beyond any reasonable walking range, with no shelter except the lee of industrial ruins or the marginal protection of a depression in the stone. The monotony that becomes first tedious, then disorienting, then almost meditative as your mind adjusts to operating in a space where nothing announces itself, where change is incremental, where yesterday and today and tomorrow are distinguished only by whether you've moved to a slightly different section of essentially identical terrain.

The pneumatic ungulates don't seem to mind. They are adapted perfectly to this patient world. They move when movement serves them, rest when it doesn't, eat the slow-growing things, drink the intermittent water, breathe the thin air through their distributed respiratory systems, and make their low resonant tones that might be language or might be something else.

The industrial ruins continue their even slower decay, marking locations on an unmarked plain, proving that someone once thought this place worth the effort of extraction, then decided otherwise.

And the steppe continues, rolling and breathing, extending past every horizon, old and cold and quiet, sustaining its millions through processes so gradual they become invisible, holding its vast patient shape under dim skies that have forgotten brightness.
