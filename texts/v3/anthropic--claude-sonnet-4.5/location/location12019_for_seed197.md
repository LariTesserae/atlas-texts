# The Choir Hollow

There is a place that exists within a single massive tree — or what was once a single tree, before it became something that trees were never meant to become.

The trunk is perhaps sixty feet across at its narrowest point, hollow for reasons no one remembers or that perhaps never had reasons. The cavity extends upward through what might be seven or eight stories of height, though the interior space refuses to hold still long enough to be properly measured. The hollowness is almost total — just the vast cylinder of air, the curving wooden walls, and the things that have learned to live in the space between.

You enter through a split in the bark that has worn smooth, almost door-like, though no tool shaped it. The wood around the opening is dark with moisture and touch, polished by passage. Inside, the emptiness hits you first — that cathedral volume of air — and then immediately after, the *sound*.

## The Acoustic Architecture

The hollow creates a resonance chamber of such perfection that every sound becomes an event. Water dripping from root-tendrils three levels up arrives at your ear as a distinct percussion note. A beetle clicking its mandibles on the far side of the trunk might as well be next to your head. The breathing of sleeping animals creates bass notes that travel through the wood itself. And the wind — the wind never stops finding new ways to voice itself through cracks and knots and the spaces between growing things.

But these are merely the instruments. The inhabitants are the orchestra.

Birds have made this hollow their territory, though they are no longer quite birds in any conventional sense. They have adapted — or been adapted — into something like living sound fixtures, permanent residents who occupy acoustic niches rather than physical ones. There are the Chanters in the upper reaches, crow-sized creatures with heads that seem slightly too large, who produce continuous tonal drones that shift in pitch as they slowly walk the inner circumference. Below them, the Clickers occupy burrows in the wood itself, emerging only to add their percussive contributions — sharp, rapid sequences that sound like speech though they carry no meaning anyone has deciphered.

The hollow amplifies everything into a constant wall of layered sound. There is never silence. Even in what passes for night (the light that filters through the wood dims but never fully disappears), the acoustic life continues. Some creatures here have lost their eyes entirely, navigation and communication having become purely sonic. Others have developed crystalline structures in their inner ears that they can reshape, tuning themselves to different frequencies.

It is deafening. Newcomers often have to retreat after minutes. But those who stay discover that the sound has structure, patterns, something almost like grammar.

## The Botanical Invasion

Every surface inside the hollow teems with plant life, yet the space itself remains empty — a trick of scale and growth habit. Vines no thicker than wire descend from above in loose spirals, creating vertical lines in the air rather than solid barriers. They produce leaves sporadically, small and precise, more like scales than foliage. Where they reach the bottom, they don't root in soil — there is no soil — but instead penetrate back into the wood of the tree itself, connecting to its vascular system in some closed loop that sustains both vine and host.

Mosses coat the inner bark in patches, thick and cushioning, growing in distinct colonies separated by bare wood. Each colony has a different color — sage green, deep emerald, colors that edge toward blue or bronze in certain light. They glisten constantly with moisture, beading and dripping in slow rhythm. The moisture comes from above, condensing on the cooler upper surfaces and migrating downward. It is never rain, but the air is always damp, always on the edge of mist.

Fungi dominate the lower third of the space, growing in shelf formations from the walls. They are architectural in their precision — horizontal ledges in regular intervals, as if the tree itself intended them as floors. Some are large enough to stand on, and indeed, several show signs of use, worn smooth in the centers. The fungi glow faintly, providing most of the direct illumination. Blue-white, soft amber, occasionally a pale green. The light breathes with them, brightening and dimming in cycles that might be respiration or something more complex.

Higher up, epiphytic plants have established themselves in the natural crevices and rot-hollows of the inner bark. They are mostly bromeliads and their stranger cousins — plants that capture water in their centers and create tiny isolated pools suspended in mid-air along the walls. These pools have become ecosystems of their own. Small amphibians live out entire lifecycles in them, never touching the ground. Insects breed there. Some of the pools are connected by vine-bridges that creatures use to travel between water sources, creating highways in three dimensions.

## The Settlement That Isn't

There is evidence of human presence, but calling it settlement would be incorrect. Infrastructure, perhaps. The people who use this place don't live here — the sound would drive them mad within days — but they have made it into something between a shrine and a library.

Platforms of woven vine and salvaged wood span across the hollow at various heights, connected by rope ladders and, in some places, by nothing at all — gaps you must leap or swing across. The platforms are small, just large enough for two or three people to stand, and they serve as observation points or anchor points for the vertical navigation. Some hold objects: bowls of water for visiting animals, collections of shed feathers and insect casings, string instruments that hang untouched but vibrate sympathetically with the hollow's resonance.

The builders — if they can be called that — have worked with what the tree provides rather than against it. Where a fungal shelf was large enough, they simply smoothed it and added a railing. Where vines crossed usefully, they encouraged more growth and wove in stabilizing elements. The construction is light, minimal, distributed. It adds almost no mass to the emptiness.

Around the lower perimeter, carved into the bark at roughly chest height, runs a continuous band of marks. They are not quite writing, not quite tally marks. Some sections are clearly older, the cuts darkened and worn smooth. Others are fresh, still weeping sap. The marks seem to record the sound somehow — graphic representations of acoustic events, perhaps, or a musical notation that corresponds to what the hollow produces. People come to add new marks or to read the old ones, standing with their fingers in the grooves, mouthing along with patterns they've learned to interpret.

There are no doors, no walls, no separation of space. Just these minimal additions suspended in the vast emptiness, swaying slightly in the air currents that constantly circulate through the hollow.

## The Reality Negotiations

The tree hollow should not work as it does. The dimensions are not stable. Sometimes you climb up to a platform and find yourself at the same level you started, but on the opposite side of the space. Sometimes the hollow seems taller than the tree appears from outside, other times barely half the height. Visitors learn not to count steps or trust their internal maps.

The organisms that live here have adapted to this instability, or perhaps cause it. The birds that produce sound are not moving through space in the usual way — they occupy positions that are acoustic rather than physical. You can hear a Chanter above you and also beside you and also far below, all at the same time, because in some sense it is in all those places, or the places themselves are overlapping.

Time is not uniform in the hollow. Growth happens at different rates depending on height and position. A vine at the western curve might grow an inch a day while its counterpart to the east takes a month for the same progress. The fungi release spores in cascades that ripple upward and downward simultaneously, patterns that should be impossible in normal gravity. Some of the amphibians in the suspended pools are aging backwards, growing younger as you observe them, then old again, then young, as if testing out different lifespans.

The acoustic perfection itself is wrong. No natural chamber resonates like this. The sound should dissipate, should be absorbed by the soft plant matter and the animals and the moisture in the air. Instead, it amplifies, it persists, it builds on itself. Visitors report hearing sounds that were made hours ago, still circulating in the upper reaches, mixed with present sounds into a temporal harmony that shouldn't exist.

Yet none of this feels unstable. The hollow has been this way for as long as anyone remembers, and the records carved into the walls suggest much longer. This is not reality breaking down — it's reality that has settled into a different configuration, found an equilibrium that includes the impossibilities as structural elements.

## The Living Ecology

Everything in the hollow is food for something else, but the relationships have become baroque, over-complicated, beautiful in their convolution.

The Chanters eat insects, but not by hunting them. The insects are drawn to specific sound frequencies the birds produce — called to their deaths by what sounds, to human ears, like music. The insects arrive willingly, land on the birds' outstretched wings, and are consumed in a process that looks almost ceremonial.

The Clickers are blind and feed on the fungal matter, but only fungi that have been pre-processed by specific beetles. The beetles themselves eat wood, but only wood that has been softened by particular bacteria, and the bacteria only thrive in conditions created by the moss colonies' water management. Everything depends on everything else in chains so long and interconnected that the removal of any single species would probably collapse the entire system.

The amphibians in the suspended pools are predators, but they cannot leave their water. They have evolved lures — bioluminescent tendrils that they extend into the air, waving them in patterns that mimic insect mating signals. Flying insects approach and are snatched by tongues that can extend three times the amphibians' body length.

Larger animals visit the hollow but cannot stay. There are night-mammals that climb down from the branches above to drink from the lower pools, adding their sounds to the chorus — wet lapping, claws on wood, low breathing. Snakes move through in transit, using the hollow as a corridor between the outer bark and the inner heartwood. They pay a toll — scales that they shed stick to the sap-moist walls and become substrate for certain specialized mites, which are in turn eaten by the smallest of the birds.

The tree itself is alive, though barely. Its heartwood still pulses with sap-flow. Its roots still drink from whatever water sources exist in the ground below. But it grows inward now, not outward. New wood forms on the inside of the hollow, slowly constricting the space over decades or centuries. The vines that penetrate back into it are parasitic and symbiotic simultaneously — they take resources but they also seem to direct growth, to shape where the new wood forms. Some areas of the inner wall are smooth and young, others deeply furrowed with age.

There are creatures no one has fully catalogued. Things that live inside the wood itself, making galleries between the bark and the heartwood, only occasionally visible when they cross an opening. Sounds come from them — grinding, scraping, a kind of humming that might be communication or might be side-effect of digestion. Some people believe these organisms are the tree's immune system, maintaining it, deciding what gets to grow and what doesn't.

## Atmospheric Conditions

The air inside is warm without being hot, heated by the respiration of the thousand organisms and by the slow decomposition processes in the fungi. The temperature hardly varies — day and night make almost no difference, nor do the seasons outside. The hollow maintains itself around a comfortable warmth, like breath, like the inside of a living body.

The moisture level cycles with a pattern that takes about six hours. At the dry phase, the air merely feels humid, and the mosses are dull. Then condensation begins, forming on the cooler upper surfaces and beginning its long drip downward. The sound of dripping increases, adding layers to the acoustic environment. The mosses brighten, swelling with water. The fungi respond, their glow intensifying. This is when the amphibians are most active, when the most insects fly, when the hollow feels most alive.

Then the moisture peaks and begins to recede, drawn back upward by some process that might be simple evaporation or might be something more complex — the vines and mosses actively pulling water up, distributing it where it's needed. The air clears slightly, vision extending a few feet further. The sounds sharpen, losing the softness that moisture adds.

The light that filters through the wood is warm-spectrum, gold and amber, as if permanently late afternoon. It comes from everywhere and nowhere — the wood itself seems to glow faintly, or to transmit light from outside in ways that wood normally doesn't. There are no direct beams, no shafts of sunlight. Just an even illumination that the fungal glow supplements, creating layers of light at different intensities and colors.

The air moves constantly in slow circulation. Warm air rises from the active biological zones, cools near the top where the hollow opens slightly to the sky, and descends along the walls. But the currents are complex, interrupted by the platforms and vines, by the bodies of animals, by temperature variations that shouldn't exist but do. Small eddies form and persist for minutes at a time. Spores and seeds drift in visible spirals. The movement creates some of the sound — air passing through narrow gaps, whistling over resonant structures.

## Material Presence

The wood of the inner wall is not uniform. Where it's exposed, it shows every year of its age — growth rings visible in the vertical surface, patterns that hypnotize if you stare too long. The rings don't always flow horizontally; they curve and twist, suggesting that this tree grew under influences that changed frequently. Some sections are smooth, almost polished, darkened by moisture and time to a rich brown-black. Others are rough, showing cracks and fissures, places where smaller branches once emerged and were absorbed back into the main structure.

The fungal shelves are firm but slightly yielding, like foam rubber, except for their edges which are delicate as paper. Their upper surfaces accumulate dust and debris — fallen moss fragments, insect bodies, plant matter that drifts down from above. This debris forms tiny soil layers in some of the larger fungi, and secondary plants sometimes germinate there — parasites on parasites, life finding niches in the most unlikely places.

The vines have a texture like young skin, smooth and slightly cool. They're stronger than they look, capable of bearing substantial weight despite their thinness. When they're cut or broken, they bleed a clear sap that dries sticky, and they emit a smell like green peppers, sharp and vegetable.

The moss is deep enough to sink your fingers into, and it retains water like a sponge. Pressing it releases droplets that are surprisingly cool. The moss has no apparent structure when you look closely — just an infinite regression of tiny leaves and stems, fractal complexity down to the limits of vision.

The platforms show their age and make. Some are clearly decades old, wood worn smooth and dark, rope replaced multiple times, joints that have been repaired and re-repaired. Others are newer, the wood still light-colored, the knots and bindings still crisp. The variety suggests not a single building project but an ongoing relationship — each generation adding what it needs, maintaining what's useful, letting the obsolete fall away and be reabsorbed by the ecology.

## The Sonic Landscape

You cannot describe the sound in simple terms. It is not loud like machinery or crowds or weather — it is loud like being inside an instrument that is always being played.

The base layer is the drones — continuous tones from the Chanters, usually three or four overlapping at slightly different pitches, creating beats and harmonics. These drones are felt as much as heard, vibrating in your chest and skull. They never stop completely but they do shift, sliding up and down in pitch over the course of minutes, creating slow melodies that might be intentional or might be the aggregate effect of individual birds adjusting their positions.

Above this, the Clickers provide rhythm. Their percussion isn't regular — it's responsive, changing based on what else is sounding. Sometimes they create steady beats, sometimes they work in counterpoint, sometimes they fall silent for long seconds before erupting in complex fills. Multiple Clickers working together can produce polyrhythms so intricate they sound mechanical.

Then there are the incidental sounds, though nothing is truly incidental in this space. Water dripping becomes a pitched instrument — the hollow's resonance gives each drip a definite note, and the different drip locations create melodies. Wing-beats are amplified into rhythmic pulses. The scraping of insects on wood becomes percussion. The breathing of animals creates low sub-bass. Even the growth of plants, imperceptibly slow, somehow contributes a kind of pressure to the acoustic environment, a sense of sustained effort.

Visitors contribute their own sounds, whether they want to or not. Footsteps on the platforms ring out distinctly, and people learn to walk in time with the existing rhythms or risk creating jarring dissonances. Breathing becomes self-conscious — you can hear yourself too clearly, and you can hear everyone else. Speech is almost impossible; the hollow reflects and amplifies voices into incomprehensibility. People who need to communicate use hand signs or wait for the rare moments when the sound dies down to merely loud.

There are periods — they come unpredictably — when the sound locks into something that could be called music. All the elements align, the drones hit perfect harmonic relationships, the rhythms synchronize, and for a minute or an hour the hollow produces a composition of startling beauty. People time their visits hoping to witness these moments. The marks on the walls seem to predict them, or record them, though the system is not fully understood.

And underneath everything, there's a sound that might be the tree itself — a deep thrumming, almost below the threshold of hearing, that resonates in your bones. Some people say it's the sap moving through the vascular system, amplified by the acoustic properties of the hollow. Others believe it's the collective metabolism of all the organisms, their life processes summing into this single bass note. A few insist it's the sound of the reality instability, the frequency at which this place vibrates between possible configurations.

## Patterns of Use

People come for specific purposes, though the hollow doesn't serve any practical function. Some come to listen, to try to understand the sonic patterns, to add new marks to the notation that rings the lower walls. They bring no instruments — adding external sound to this environment would be presumptuous — but they listen with an intensity that is itself a kind of participation.

Others come to study the ecology, to observe the food webs and symbioses, to document the species that haven't been documented before. They bring notebooks but no collection jars — taking specimens from the hollow is taboo, understood to risk destabilizing the whole system.

Some come to experience the reality drift, to feel themselves exist in the uncertain space where the normal rules are suspended. These visitors often sit still for hours on a platform, meditating or simply being, letting the wrongness of the place wash over them.

A few come to feed the animals, bringing offerings of seeds or fruit that they leave on designated platforms. The food is always gone within hours, though no one watches to see what takes it. This might be ritual or ecology or both — a way of maintaining relationship with the hollow, acknowledging its life.

Children are rarely brought here. The sound is too intense for young nervous systems, and the reality instability can be frightening to those who haven't learned to accept impossibility. But adolescents come as a rite of passage, spending a night on one of the platforms, enduring the deafening acoustic environment and the spatial confusion until morning. Those who manage it often return as adults, having gained some capacity to tolerate ambiguity.

The hollow has no custodians, no managers, no official status. The people who maintain the platforms and add to the notation and study the ecology are self-organized, passing knowledge and responsibility through informal networks. There's no need for formal structure — the hollow itself seems to select who can tolerate it, and those people naturally take up the work of understanding and preserving it.

## Temporal Qualities

The hollow exists in its own time frame. A visit that feels like an hour might be three hours by external watches, or twenty minutes. The sound creates a kind of temporal distortion — you lose track of duration because the acoustic environment is too constantly changing and too constantly similar. There are no silence-breaks to mark time, no changes in light level sufficient to track the sun's position.

The ecology cycles at its own pace. The moisture rhythm is six hours, but it doesn't align with any external schedule. The birds' sonic routines change daily but according to no visible pattern. The fungi glow brighter and dimmer over what might be weeks or might be hours. Growth happens at variable speeds, as mentioned, creating the impression that time itself is moving at different rates in different parts of the space.

Some of the marks on the walls are ancient — centuries old, carved when the hollow was younger and smaller. Others were added yesterday. Between them, a continuous record of observation and interaction, growing denser in recent years as more people discover the place and add their notations. The wall has become a palimpsest of time, all periods present simultaneously.

The tree itself operates on deep time. Its growth inward is measured in decades per inch. Its lifespan is already longer than any record-keeping. It might live another thousand years or it might be dying now, its hollow-heart a terminal condition. No one knows and the tree isn't saying, just continuing to exist, to support its impossible ecosystem, to amplify sound into meaning.

## What Happens Here

Nothing happens here in the sense of events or incidents. Everything happens here in the sense of constant process.

Plants grow, continuously and variably, reaching toward light or water or positions in the acoustic field. Animals hunt and are hunted, eat and are eaten, in relationships so complex they appear choreographed. The sound builds and shifts and occasionally achieves transcendence. Water condenses, drips, is absorbed, is released, cycles through the space and the organisms. Light filters in and is supplemented by glow. Air circulates. Spores drift. Time behaves strangely.

The platforms sway in air currents. The ropes creak. The marks on the walls accumulate meaning or lose it, interpretations changing as people's understanding evolves. The visitors come and go, leaving minimal trace, taking only memory and perhaps a slight alteration in how they perceive sound.

The tree continues being a tree while also being this other thing — this acoustic chamber, this ecosystem, this place where reality has negotiated different terms. It stands — or stood, once, before it became hollow — in a forest or a field, rooted in ground, reaching toward sky. But that outside context barely matters. The hollow is its own world, complete and sufficient, empty and full, deafening and profound.

You could stay here forever and never see the same configuration twice. You could leave and return decades later to find it essentially unchanged. Both things are true. The hollow exists in established balance while being in constant motion, ancient while being newborn every moment, empty while teeming with life.

It asks nothing of you except that you accept its impossibilities and endure its sound. In return, it offers you a glimpse of what happens when life finds its way into empty space and fills it with meaning, when sound becomes structure, when a tree decides to become an instrument and plays itself endlessly, perfectly, into forms that shouldn't exist but do.
