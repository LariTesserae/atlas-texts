# The Singing Throat

The city hangs in vertical bands along a throat of stone that rises from cloud-country into thinner air where breathing becomes a conscious act. This is not mountain in the usual sense—not a peak or ridge—but a geological throat, a mile-wide shaft of striated limestone and metamorphic bands that ascends nearly three miles from the moisture-heavy lowlands. The stone itself is the color of old bone and rust-water, marked with horizontal seams where different epochs lie pressed together like pages in a book that no one can read.

Water moves through everything here. The rock weeps. Every surface runs or drips or glistens with a film of moisture that never quite dries. The throat funnels updrafts from the humid valleys below, and these rising currents carry water vapor that condenses on every cold surface in the upper reaches, then trickles back down in perpetual circulation. The stone has been shaped by this patient, endless dampness—not carved dramatically but softened, rounded at edges, pockmarked with shallow solution hollows that collect and release water in rhythms that have settled into something like breathing. The erosion here is old beyond reckoning, everything arrived at its current form so long ago that the landscape feels less ancient than eternal, as though it has always been exactly this and will remain so regardless of whether anyone witnesses it.

The buildings grow from the stone in horizontal bands that follow the natural stratification. They are made of the same limestone, quarried from the throat itself and rebuilt into itself, so that worked stone and living rock blur together. Galleries and platforms project outward into space, supported by nothing visible, as though the architecture itself has partially forgotten the obligation to obey gravity. The structures are dense, layered, built over and into and through one another across centuries—doorways that open onto other doorways, stairs that climb past abandoned dwellings now filled with pooling water, bridges that connect to buildings no longer standing, leaving them arcing into empty air like frozen questions.

There are no straight lines here, or rather, there were once, but they have all softened. Every edge is rounded by condensation and the patient work of calcium-rich water depositing and redissolving stone. The walls glisten. Everywhere you look, surfaces are wet—not with fresh rain but with this constant, condensing exhalation of the throat itself. Water trickles down walls in fingernail-width streams that have, over time, carved shallow runnels in stone that was once architectural and flat. These streams collect in channels that previous inhabitants cut into floors and ledges, creating a branching network that guides water through the city-stone like a vascular system, leading it ever downward until it drips from the lowest platforms back into the cloud-country below.

The vegetation is impossible and undeniable. It shouldn't exist at this elevation, in this much moisture but so little soil, yet it does—densely. Ferns emerge from cracks in walls, their fronds the deep green of things that live in constant shade even though the light here is ordinary daylight, perhaps slightly diffused by humidity but nothing remarkable. Moss blankets every horizontal surface and creeps up vertical ones, thick as fur, holding water like a sponge. There are plants that look like stunted trees but cannot be trees, their trunks the diameter of a human forearm, their roots spread flat across stone surfaces, finding purchase in microscopic irregularities and cracks. They have leaves like platters, designed to catch condensation, and they grow in clusters within ruined rooms and from the tops of walls, creating pockets of green throughout the limestone galleries.

Vines—if they are vines—run along channels and over bridges, following the pathways of water. They are fleshy, almost succulent, storing moisture in bulbous nodes along their length. Some have grown so large and old that they form structural elements themselves, holding up sections of wall that have lost their mortar, or providing railings along platforms where stone has eroded away. The plants here are not decorative; they are part of the architecture, and the architecture is part of the stone, and all of it is suffused with water that moves through the system in circuits that seem almost deliberate.

But the sound. The sound is everything.

The throat sings.

It produces a constant, overwhelming chorus that comes from everywhere and nowhere, that vibrates in your chest and skull, that makes thought difficult and speech impossible except in rare pockets of relative quiet that shift and disappear. The sound begins with wind—the updrafts that rush through the vertical channel create a baseline drone that varies with air pressure and time of day, rising and falling in slow cycles like breathing. This alone would be loud enough to disorient, but it is only the foundation.

Water adds layers. Every drip, every trickle, every small waterfall echoes and resonates within the stone chambers and galleries. The architecture was not designed to dampen sound but to shape it, to redirect it, to create harmonics and interference patterns. Water falling into stone basins rings them like bells. Drips from different heights create rhythms that phase in and out of sync. Streams running through carved channels produce rushing whispers that build and multiply until they roar.

And then there is the stone itself. The throat resonates. The rising air pressure creates vibrations in certain chambers and cavities within the rock, and these produce deep, sustained tones—not quite musical but not quite random either. They are the voice of geology, the sound of stone responding to pressure and moisture and the slow flex of the earth. Some of these tones are below the threshold of hearing but felt in bones and organs. Others are piercing, pure frequencies that lance through the general roar like searchlights through fog.

The built structures add their own voices. Galleries become resonance chambers. Hollow pillars hum. Open doorways create vortices that whistle and moan. Some buildings have channels cut into their walls that cause them to sing specific notes when wind flows through them at certain speeds. These were deliberate, the work of inhabitants who have been gone for time beyond memory, and their notes still sound, mixing with all the other sounds into a dense, layered cacophony that never stops, never settles, never becomes background because it is too loud, too complex, too varied from moment to moment.

Walking through the city-throat is an acoustic assault that requires physical endurance. You feel sound as pressure on your skin and in your lungs. Communication is impossible except through gesture. You cannot hear your own footsteps. The sound fills all available space and then continues past saturation, layering impossibility on impossibility until your mind begins to surrender the attempt to process it as discrete sounds and accepts it instead as a condition of existence, like gravity or air.

And yet: beneath the sound, or perhaps woven through it, there is pattern. Cycles repeat. Certain combinations of tones recur. The throat is not merely loud but speaking, or singing, or performing some function that resembles communication without being communication. You cannot understand it, but you sense structure, intention that has outlived whoever intended it, meaning that continues to be expressed even though no one remains to interpret.

There are no animals. None. No birds despite the platforms that seem designed for nesting. No insects despite the lush vegetation. No small creatures hiding in cracks. The absence is absolute and unexplained. The plants grow and propagate, the water circulates, but nothing else moves except in ways that become increasingly difficult to explain.

Because reality here is not reliable.

The architecture shifts when you are not looking directly at it. Not dramatically—a doorway that was on your left is now on your right. A staircase leads up that previously led down. A wall displays carvings you are certain were not there before, though they are as weathered and old as everything else. The changes are subtle enough that you question your memory, but too consistent to be coincidence. The city rearranges itself according to principles that might be geometric or acoustic or responsive to moisture patterns or something else entirely.

Water sometimes flows upward. Not violently, not in defiance of gravity, but gently, naturally, as though gravity in certain channels has become negotiable. A trickle runs up a wall for three feet before resuming its downward course. A pool's surface tilts at an angle that shouldn't hold water, but does. You reach to touch a dripping surface and find it dry; you touch another that appears dry and find your hand wet.

The light is wrong in ways that take time to notice. Shadows point in directions that don't quite match the sun's position. Some chambers are brighter than their exposure to sky suggests. Others are darker. The light has texture here—you become aware of it as a substance, slightly granular, almost tangible. In some galleries it pools like liquid, denser near the floor. In others it seems to drift, moving independently of the moisture that should carry it.

Plants grow at different rates in different locations without apparent environmental cause. A fern in deep shade flourishes while an identical specimen in better light remains stunted. Some vines grow in loops and knots that could not form through normal phototropism, creating shapes that suggest intention or distress or attempts at forming symbols. When you examine one closely, you realize its growth rings don't make sense—the pattern suggests it grew, then ungrew, then regrew, time moving forward and backward along its length.

Distance becomes uncertain in the longer galleries. A passage that should take five minutes to walk requires fifteen, or three, or some variable length that seems to depend on factors you cannot identify. You see someone—or something—moving on a distant platform and walk toward it, only to find yourself arriving at a completely different location while the figure remains distant, still visible, still moving in ways that suggest purpose.

The city is empty but does not feel abandoned. It feels paused, or waiting, or existing in a state that has no relationship to occupation. The buildings are complete, not ruined but perfected through age, every surface exactly as wet as it should be, every plant exactly where it needs to grow. The sound continues its overwhelming symphony. The water circulates. The stone breathes its slow cycles. Everything functions, everything maintains itself, nothing changes in any meaningful way.

There is no decay here, not really. The weathering reached an equilibrium so long ago that it now preserves rather than erodes. The moisture that should destroy instead sustains. The sound that should be destructive instead holds everything in place through constant vibration. The city-throat exists in a state of permanent completion, every element balanced against every other, the whole system locked into an eternal present that contains its own past and future as collapsed possibilities rather than distinct states.

You climb higher through the vertical galleries, following stairs that curve along the throat's inner surface. The air thins but never quite becomes unbreathable. Your head pounds from altitude and sound in equal measure. The moisture increases—the upper reaches are wrapped in clouds that drift through open galleries and abandoned rooms, reducing visibility to arm's length, then clearing just as suddenly. Everything drips more insistently up here. The plants are smaller but denser, clustering in every available crack, their leaves darkened with moisture.

At certain elevations, the acoustic properties change. You pass through zones where the roar suddenly drops to a heavy drone, where specific frequencies disappear and others emerge. These shifts happen at distinct boundaries, as though the throat is divided into horizontal bands not just geologically but sonically. In one such zone, roughly two thousand feet up, the sound develops an almost syllabic quality—it seems to be saying something, the rushing wind and water forming shapes that could be words in a language that never existed or has not yet come to exist.

The highest galleries extend past the cloudline into thin, bright air where the moisture finally decreases but never disappears entirely. Here the limestone is paler, almost white, and the striations are more pronounced. The buildings in this zone are simpler, or perhaps merely older, their forms so worn and integrated with the stone that they barely register as human-made. The sound here is different—higher-pitched, the wind screaming through narrow channels, the lower frequencies left behind in the denser air below. It is, impossibly, even louder, a sustained shriek that makes vision blur and balance uncertain.

And at the very top—there is no top. The throat simply stops being distinct from the sky. The limestone becomes cloud becomes air becomes something else. The highest platforms extend into space that cannot support them, yet they remain, water dripping from their edges into updrafts that carry it away. The plants here are almost ghostly, pale and semi-transparent, growing in air rather than stone. The sound at this threshold point achieves a kind of white noise purity, so loud and complete that it loops back around to seeming almost quiet, a roar so total it becomes blank.

Looking down from this impossible height, you see the throat descending through layers of cloud and moisture, the bands of city clinging to its sides, the vegetation cascading from every surface, the water circulating in its endless pattern. The whole structure rises from lowlands you cannot see and extends to heights where structure itself becomes questionable. It has been here, exactly like this, for longer than measurement. It will continue, exactly like this, for longer than measurement. Nothing changes. Everything changes constantly but returns always to exactly what it was. The throat sings its overwhelming song. The water moves. The stone breathes. Reality holds itself together through patterns that deny reality.

You realize, eventually, that you cannot remember arriving here. The city has no entrance, or every opening is an entrance, or the distinction between inside and outside stopped applying somewhere in the vertical climb. You might have always been here, moving through these wet galleries, climbing these worn stairs, listening to this sound that makes listening impossible.

The vegetation reaches toward you, or toward the light, or toward something else. Its leaves collect condensation that runs down stems and trunks and returns to the stone, completing another circuit. Everything completes circuits here. Everything returns to where it started while never being still. The city-throat is a machine without moving parts, a song without a singer, a dwelling without dwellers, a reality that has learned to sustain itself through being impossible.

And still the sound continues, battering thought into fragments, overwhelming every attempt at understanding with pure volume. The roar of wind and water and stone and whatever else has been woven into this place, this vertical city, this singing throat that rises through moisture and heat into thin air, holding its impossible gardens in a state of permanent becoming, empty but full, ancient but ageless, deafening but precise.

You descend, or climb, or remain. In the city-throat, these might all be the same action.
