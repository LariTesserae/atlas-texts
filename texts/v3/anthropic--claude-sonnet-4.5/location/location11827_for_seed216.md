# The Woven Altitudes

At first approach, you might think you're seeing a mountain range worn smooth by impossible ages. Then the breathing starts to register.

What stretches across the high plateau—and "stretches" is too small a word, this goes on for what must be thousands of miles, curving with the planet's own geometry—is a construction of living plant matter so vast and so complete that it has replaced geology itself. There are no rocks here. No mineral substrate. The "mountains" that rise in gentle, rounded swells to catch the thin air are composed entirely of compressed vegetable matter: layer upon layer of woody growth, root systems the size of city blocks, cellular structures that have petrified not into stone but into something denser than hardwood, tougher than bark, yet still fundamentally *alive*.

The builders were patient beyond human comprehension. They worked with simple tools—cutting, weaving, guiding, binding—and they worked for so long that their civilization's entire technological history remained arrested at the level of stone blades and bone needles. They never needed to advance. The plants would do anything, given time and the right touch. You can still find the tools sometimes, lying in hollows: simple crescents of sharpened stone, polishing bones worn smooth by ten thousand hands, wooden wedges that have themselves begun to root and join the structure.

The air here is warm but not hot—the altitude sees to that—a perpetual late spring temperature that varies little. Mist condenses constantly from the moisture-laden atmosphere, forming in the cool hours before what passes for dawn, then slowly releasing through the day in a patient exhalation. The water doesn't pool. It can't. Every surface is absorbent. The plant-matter drinks it down through channels and vessels engineered over millennia, routing moisture through the vast hollow chambers inside the mountains themselves.

Because they are hollow, these mountains. Vastly, impossibly hollow.

Stand on the surface and you're standing on something like a membrane, perhaps thirty feet thick in places, perhaps a hundred, stretched across voids that descend for miles. The whole construction is a kind of three-dimensional weaving—an architecture of living cathedral spaces, each one large enough to contain weather. Inside, in the enormous darkness, warm air rises from the planet's interior through carefully maintained vent-shafts grown from specialized root structures. It meets the cooler air that seeps down from above, and clouds form *inside the mountains*, internal weather systems that rain gently on forests that have never seen the sun.

The scale makes navigation meaningless. You could walk for weeks and not be certain whether you'd traveled a straight line or had somehow curved back on yourself. The surface rolls in long, gentle undulations—nothing steep, everything worn to soft curves by the constant work of guided growth. Here and there, valleys run between the swells, and these are waterways of a sort: not rivers but dampness-courses where moisture concentrates, where the plant matter is younger, softer, more actively growing. They glisten in the dim light.

The light itself is strange. The sun exists, somewhere up there beyond the haze, but what reaches the surface is attenuated, softened, scattered by moisture and distance until it becomes a kind of luminous grey. Shadows are gentle suggestions. The brightest hours feel like the interior of a cloud. And yet there is variation—the mist thickens and thins, the light waxes and wanes just enough that you can track time's passage if you pay attention, though the changes are subtle, almost subliminal.

In this dimness, the fauna have adapted in ways that make you question the rules you thought governed biology. They are abundant—everywhere you look, something is moving—but they are largely silent. The air carries almost no sound. Footsteps are absorbed by the spongy surface. The creatures here communicate, when they communicate, through methods that bypass sound almost entirely.

There are things like birds, but they glide more than fly, riding the thermal currents that rise from the breathing vents, their wings stretched wide and membranous. They glow faintly—most of the fauna do—a bioluminescence so subtle you only notice it when several gather together, creating small constellations of pale green or blue light that drift through the mist. They feed on the flowers that bloom in vast, silent profusions: acres of blossoms that open and close in rhythms tied to moisture rather than light, releasing pollen in visible clouds that hang in the still air.

The herbivores move in slow processions across the surface, creatures with wide, padded feet that distribute their weight across the yielding plant matter. Some are the size of elephants, but hollow-boned, light for their volume, moving with dream-like deliberation through the mist. Their bodies are often pale, sometimes translucent, and they feed continuously, mouths working at the ever-present vegetation. They browse on structures that blur the line between plant and building: Gothic arches of living wood, flying buttresses that serve as both architecture and food source, walls that are nutritious.

Because everything here was built. Everything. The builders—whoever they were, and they are long gone now, or transformed beyond recognition—grew this entire landscape as a single, unified construction project. Every ridge serves a purpose in the water management system. Every valley channels air flow. The hollow mountains are positioned to create circulation patterns that keep the interior spaces habitable. Even the plant species themselves were selectively bred over so many generations that they became something new: domesticated beyond any familiar meaning of the word, shaped into living building materials, structural elements, environmental systems.

In some places, you can still see the intentionality clearly: repeating patterns in the surface vegetation, geometric arrangements of vent openings, carefully maintained growth-fronts where newer plant matter is slowly replacing the old. But in other places, the system has been running so long without intervention that it has begun to drift, to evolve, to make its own decisions. The line between constructed and natural has been eroded by time until it's impossible to say what was planned and what simply emerged.

The predators here are the most unsettling. They are silent hunters, and they seem to understand the architecture in ways that suggest memory, or perhaps instinct descended from memory. They know which surfaces are thin, where the hollow spaces beneath are largest. They can sense vibration through the plant matter—the footfalls of prey traveling through the structural network like signals through a nervous system. Some have grown to immense size, but they are stretched, elongated, their bodies adapted to moving through the internal passages where they wait for prey to cross above certain weak points, certain deliberate gaps in the weaving.

You see the evidence of this everywhere: circular depressions in the surface where something has broken through from below, or something above has been pulled down. The plant matter heals slowly, growing new layers across the wounds, but the scars remain visible for decades at least, dark circles in the pale green-grey-brown of the dominant surface vegetation.

The vegetation itself is difficult to parse. At close range, you can identify distinct species: something like moss, but woody and load-bearing; something like trees, but growing in fused masses, their trunks merged into walls and floors; something like grass, but with roots that descend for hundreds of feet, tying the surface layers to the deep structures below. Pull up a handful and you're holding what looks like plant matter but feels like woven fiber, deliberately laid, deliberately integrated into the whole.

And flowers. Endless flowers. This is the detail that seems most excessive, most beyond practical necessity: the flowers. They grow from every surface, emerging from the woody substrate like decorations, like afterthoughts, like prayer. Some are tiny, dense carpets of pale blooms barely larger than pinheads. Others are enormous, fleshy structures the size of a human body that take months to open, emitting warm, humid breaths of air from their interiors. Many are bioluminescent, adding their subtle glow to the dim atmosphere.

The smell should be overwhelming—this much plant matter, this much active growth and decay and flowering—but it's not. The air is clean, almost neutral, with just a hint of something green and growing. The water management must extend to scent somehow, or perhaps the atmosphere is simply so vast, the space between things so great despite the omnipresent vegetation, that smells dissipate before they can concentrate.

Walk for an hour and you might encounter a dozen distinct micro-climates: a depression where mist pools and drips constantly, the plant matter slick and dark with moisture; a rise where the air is clearer, drier, the surface vegetation crispy and pale; a passage between two swells where the air flows like a river, carrying warmth up from some deep source, making the flowers bob and sway in the otherwise still environment.

There are structures that are clearly architectural: domes, arches, spirals of plant matter that can only be deliberate. Most are empty now, their purposes forgotten. Some still function as dwellings for the fauna—the bird-like gliders nest in the high vaulted spaces, hanging their young in membranous sacs from the ceiling beams. The herbivores shelter in the larger chambers during the mist-heavy hours, their pale forms clustering together in the darkness like ghosts.

And then there are the places where reality becomes negotiable.

The builders worked with plant consciousness, clearly. They bred species that responded to intent, to need, to some kind of directed will. And over the thousands of years since—perhaps tens of thousands, perhaps hundreds of thousands, time is hard to read here—those capabilities have evolved, or devolved, or simply continued developing according to their own logic.

There are groves where the plant matter remembers things that never happened. Walk through and you'll see structures forming in front of you—walls, doorways, furniture—that dissolve when you look directly at them but remain solid in peripheral vision. These are places where the vegetable consciousness is dreaming, perhaps, or where the genetic memory of the plants contains blueprints that occasionally express themselves unprompted.

There are sections of the surface that are temporally displaced. Not by much—hours, perhaps days—but enough that you can sometimes see your own footprints appearing in plant matter you haven't crossed yet, or find fruit already eaten from trees you're only now approaching. The plants here were engineered to respond to future needs, to anticipate, and sometimes the anticipation runs ahead of causality in ways that create small loops, small eddies in time's flow.

There are chambers deep inside the mountains where the air itself has become plant-like, where breathing feels like inhaling green life, and visitors report emerging with small flowers growing from their hair, their fingernails, the corners of their eyes—growths that fade over hours, harmless but profoundly unsettling.

The fauna, too, show signs of the reality-breakdown. Some of the herbivores exist in superposition, browsing in two places at once, their bodies stretched across space in ways that should be impossible. Some of the predators hunt in dreams, appearing in the sleep of prey animals and then manifesting physically at the moment of dream-death, creating a feedback loop between consciousness and matter that the original builders may have intended, or may have accidentally enabled.

In the deepest chambers—the ones that descend for miles, the great vertical shafts around which the entire system was built—there are spaces where plant matter transitions into something else entirely. Not death, not mineral, but a state that has no name. The cells remain alive but their organization shifts into patterns that shouldn't be possible in three-dimensional space. Visitors who descend there and return—and most don't—describe walls that recede forever, surfaces that touch you from directions that don't exist, temperatures that are somehow perpendicular to cold and hot.

But these are extremes. Most of the landscape is simply what it appears: a vast, gentle, weathered plant-construction that has reached a kind of equilibrium with itself. The cycles here are biological rather than geological, but they are slow enough to feel eternal. A section of surface might be in its growth phase for a century, then enter a century of stability, then spend another century slowly composting itself while new growth rises through and above it.

The water moves through it all, keeping everything viable. In the cooler hours, the mist is thick enough that visibility drops to a few dozen yards, and the pale forms of the fauna emerge and vanish in the greyness like thoughts. In the warmer hours, the mist thins, and you can see farther—sometimes for miles—across the rolling surface, the gentle swells and valleys repeating into the distance, each one subtly different, each one part of the vast pattern.

There is almost no sound. Sometimes a soft rustling as something moves through the vegetation. Sometimes a distant, low tone—whether it comes from wind through the deep passages or from the plants themselves singing through their hollow structures is unclear. Sometimes what might be rain, or might be the sound of water moving through miles of vascular channels beneath your feet, a patient circulation that never stops.

The fauna make almost no noise. Even the predators kill silently. You might witness a hunt: the slow approach, the sudden movement—and silence. No cry of alarm, no death scream. The prey simply folds into the plant matter, pulled down through a gap that closes after, and the predator descends after it into the internal darkness where feeding happens in private.

Sometimes you find bones, if you can call them that. The fauna here have skeletons made partly of mineralized cellulose, plant-derived structures that are neither quite bone nor quite wood. When they die and decompose, these skeletons often root, sending out shoots, beginning the process of becoming part of the larger structure. There are forests of these rib-cages, scattered across the plateau, their curves forming natural arbors where flowers grow especially thick.

The whole system breathes. You can feel it if you lie down on the surface and wait: a gentle rising and falling, perhaps once every few minutes, as air moves through the vast internal network. In some places, the breathing is more pronounced—specific vents where air rushes out with enough force to create a wind, warm and humid, smelling of soil and growth. The fauna gather at these vents sometimes, basking in the warmth, their pale bodies slowly accreting condensation until they glisten in the dim light.

There must be seasons, though they're subtle. Over months, the light levels shift slightly. The mist becomes more or less dense. Certain flowers bloom while others fade. The herbivores migrate—if you can call it that—moving in slow waves across the surface, following resources only they can sense, taking weeks to cross distances that might seem walkable in days.

And there are the traces of the builders everywhere, if you know how to look. Certain patterns in the vegetation that are too regular to be natural. Tool marks on ancient woody surfaces, though the wood has grown over them, incorporated them. Occasional chambers that are clearly rooms, domestic spaces, though empty now for longer than recorded history. The dimensions are often wrong for human comfort—too tall, too narrow, ceiling-heights that vary in unsettling ways—suggesting the builders themselves may have been something else, or may have engineered these spaces for purposes we can't guess.

In some of these rooms, the plant matter has formed furniture: chairs, tables, beds, all grown rather than placed, their forms emerging from the floor and walls as natural extrusions. Sometimes the furniture is occupied—the fauna use these spaces, clearly, have for generations—but just as often it sits empty, patiently waiting for inhabitants who will never return.

The question of what happened to the builders is one the landscape doesn't answer. Perhaps they're still here, transformed by the same plant consciousness they once controlled, now part of the structure itself. Perhaps they achieved whatever they were working toward and departed. Perhaps they died out, victims of their own success, having created a system so complete it no longer needed them.

What remains is functional, balanced, self-maintaining. The weathering here is the weathering of living systems: constant death and renewal, but at a pace so measured that the overall form remains stable. A mountain might erode, but it erodes by being slowly consumed by the fauna and shat out as fertilizer for the next layer of growth. A valley might fill, but it fills with root mass and accumulated organic matter that becomes the substrate for new surface vegetation.

Walk far enough—and you could walk forever here, the scale is that vast—and you'll find regions with different characteristics. Places where the builders experimented with other approaches, other species, other architectural logic. There are sections where the plant matter grows in spirals, creating landscape-scale nautilus chambers that serve functions no one understands. There are sections where everything is vertical—walls of vegetation rising straight up for thousands of feet, connected by horizontal passages where the fauna move like acrobats.

There are sections that are newer, though "new" is relative—perhaps only a thousand years old instead of ten thousand—where the growth is more active, less settled, and the surface has a spongy quality that makes walking exhausting. And there are sections that seem ancient beyond measure, where the plant matter has compressed into something almost stone-like, dark and hard, and the flowers that grow there are tiny, pale things that seem to feed on time itself.

The whole construction curves with the planet. Spend enough time here and you begin to feel it: the gentle bend of the horizon, the way distances don't quite work the way you expect. Some visitors become convinced that the plant-mountains themselves are slowly rotating, creating their own local geography that doesn't quite match the planet's surface. Whether this is true or a kind of spatial illusion created by the scale and the repetition is unclear.

At night—and night is barely different from day here, just a deepening of the dimness to near-total darkness—the bioluminescence becomes more obvious. The fauna glow like embers scattered across vast dark fields. The flowers pulse gently, attracting pollinators that are themselves luminous. The mist, when it's thick, catches and diffuses the light until the whole landscape seems to glow from within, a soft green-blue radiance that reveals nothing clearly but creates an atmosphere of presence, of awareness.

There are no stars visible. The atmosphere here is too thick, or too strange, or something about the plant-consciousness creates a kind of interference that blocks distant light. The sky is a uniform darkness, occasionally broken by the passage of something high up—whether clouds or vast aerial fauna or some property of the atmosphere itself manifesting visually.

And through it all, that silence. That profound, patient quiet. The sound of your own breathing is the loudest thing. Your footsteps are absorbed into the yielding surface. Even your thoughts seem to grow quieter here, as if the vastness and the age and the settled nature of this place gradually teaches you its pace, its way of being.

This is a landscape that was made completely, that replaced whatever came before with a vision of what could be grown instead of built. And then time wore that vision smooth, eroded the sharp edges of intention, allowed the system to find its own equilibrium. What remains is neither natural nor artificial, but something that has become itself so completely that the distinction no longer applies.

You are walking on the surface of a world-sized thought, a thought that has been thinking itself for so long that it has become real, concrete, alive. And it thinks slowly, patiently, with the rhythm of growth and decay, water and breath, light and darkness, in cycles that will continue long after any individual observer has been absorbed into the structure and become another layer, another root, another flowering in the vast, gentle, ancient body of the woven altitudes.
