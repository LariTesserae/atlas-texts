# The Lung Valleys

You are standing somewhere beneath what was once the floor of the world, in a place that is still learning how to be.

The ground beneath you is not ground—not yet. It's a crystalline lattice of calcium carbonate and silica, shot through with veins of something that glitters wetly in the overwhelming light. This substrate is raw, unweathered, as if it cooled into existence only moments ago, though you know it has been here longer than that. There are no worn edges, no smoothed curves, no dust of ages. Everything is sharp, angular, precise. The stone—if you can call it stone—has the quality of frozen liquid, of interrupted formation. It forms plates and shelves and sudden drops, but nothing has had time to round these features into the vocabulary of landscape. You are walking through geology's rough draft.

But this is not a sterile place.

The vegetation here doesn't grow *on* the substrate—it grows *as* the substrate. Where you might expect soil, there are instead thick mats of plant tissue, rubbery and hot to the touch, that have fused directly to the crystalline base. The plants are vast. Not tall—this is a subsurface realm, and the ceiling (itself a living membrane of root systems from some impossible surface forest) hangs perhaps thirty or forty meters overhead in most places. But vast in extent, in mass, in their complete occupation of space.

The dominant form is something like a succulent, but architectural in scale. Imagine walls of green-white flesh, translucent near the edges, storing water in chambers visible as darker regions within their bulk. These structures rise three, four, five meters high, branching in ways that seem almost deliberate, creating corridors and chambers and plazas in the space below. Their surfaces are smooth, waxy, utterly pristine—no lichen grows here, no moss, no epiphyte. The growth is too new for passengers, or else the heat keeps them away.

Because it is hot. The air sits heavy in your lungs, forty degrees or more, humid but not saturated. The heat seems to come from everywhere at once—from the plant-walls themselves, which are metabolically active and radiate their inner workings; from the substrate, which still holds some generative warmth; from the very air, which has nowhere to go and simply accumulates energy. You sweat, but the moisture doesn't evaporate quickly in this thick atmosphere. It clings to your skin, makes your clothes heavy.

Between the great succulent walls, the space is filled with other things. Vines as thick as your leg spiral upward, leafless, their surfaces covered in breathing pores that open and close with a rhythm just barely perceptible. Patches of what might be moss but grown to the scale of hedges, forming dark green mounds that pulse almost imperceptibly as they draw moisture from the air. Translucent pods the size of cars hang from the ceiling membrane, filled with clear fluid and suspended vegetable matter, purpose unknown. Some have fallen and split open, leaving empty sacs on the ground that are already being overgrown by the next generation of plant tissue.

The light is astonishing.

It comes from everywhere and nowhere. The ceiling glows—the entire surface of it, a sustained bioluminescence that casts the world below in a steady, sourceless radiance. It's not the spectrum of sunlight; it's whiter, colder, more complete. The translucent flesh of the succulent walls filters and refracts this light, creating zones of green-tinted brightness and rare, soft shadows. The overall effect is of being inside something alive and illuminated from within. Which, you realize, is exactly where you are.

In some places, the light is almost painful in its intensity—where the ceiling drops lower, or where certain types of plant tissue have formed natural skylights, concentrating the glow. In other places, where the walls grow particularly thick, the light takes on the quality of deep water, green and attenuated but never entirely absent. You can see for a hundred meters or more in most directions, the landscape unfolding in layers of translucent vegetation and bright mineral substrate.

The scale of the place becomes apparent slowly. You are not in a cave—not exactly. You are in a valley system, a network of elongated depressions in the newly-formed substrate, each one between fifty and two hundred meters wide, running for kilometers in wandering, organic paths. The succulent walls don't entirely block movement between valleys; there are gaps, passages, places where the growth hasn't yet completed its colonization. From higher vantage points—where the substrate forms one of its raw, angular plateaus—you can see the extent of it: valley after valley, each filled with its own particular density and arrangement of vegetable mass, all glowing under that impossible ceiling, stretching away until distance and refracted light blur the details into a general impression of enormity.

There are structures here, but they are sparse and strange.

In one valley, a series of standing stones—except they are not stones, they are compressed plant matter, hardened to the texture of wood, carved or grown into flat slabs three meters high. They stand in a loose circle, twenty of them, their surfaces marked with regular grooves that might be decoration or might be functional. No tool marks are visible. The grooves are too smooth, too perfectly uniform. They might have been grown that way, or shaped by some biological process you don't understand.

Elsewhere, you find a platform of woven vine material suspended between two walls, a few meters off the ground. It sways slightly when you climb onto it, though there is no wind here. The weaving is tight, expert, but there are no loose ends, no places where you can see how it was started or finished. It simply is.

Once, you discover a chamber—a roughly spherical hollow inside one of the massive succulent walls, accessible through an opening that might be natural or might be carved. Inside, the walls are lined with a different texture of plant tissue, something soft and faintly luminescent, arranged in regular patterns that suggest intention. The chamber is perhaps four meters across. It smells of sap and heat. You cannot tell if someone made this space, or if the plant grew it, or if there is meant to be a difference.

These artifacts—if that's what they are—appear perhaps once in every square kilometer. They are scattered, isolated, not connected by any visible pattern or path. They do not decay or weather because nothing here is old enough to decay.

The sound is the sound of slow metabolism.

In the relative quiet, you become aware of the creaking of plant tissue as it grows, as internal pressures shift, as chambers of stored water distribute their contents. A sound like wood settling, but continuous, coming from all directions at once. Occasionally, a deeper groan as one of the ceiling pods detaches and falls, hitting the ground with a wet, heavy sound that echoes briefly between the walls before being absorbed.

There are dripping sounds—condensation forming on the ceiling membrane, accumulating, falling in irregular rhythms. The moisture collects in depressions in the substrate, forming shallow pools that are warm to the touch, sometimes hosting small floating plants, bright green discs the size of dinner plates. The pools evaporate slowly, or are absorbed by the surrounding vegetation, only to be replenished by the next drip cycle.

Very rarely, you hear something that might be an animal. A clicking sound from deep within a wall cavity, irregular, brief. A movement in your peripheral vision that stops when you turn to look. Once, a creature approximately the size and shape of a mantis, but with too many joints, colored the same translucent white-green as the plants around it, moving across the face of a succulent wall with a precise, mechanical gait. It pauses, seems to taste the plant surface with appendages too delicate to see clearly, and then continues upward, disappearing into a fold in the tissue.

The animals here—what few there are—seem to be entirely integrated into the plant ecosystem. You never see one on the bare substrate. They move through the vegetable architecture as if it were built for them, or perhaps they were built for it. A small, limbless thing like a pale eel undulates through the gap between two vine coils, its surface secreting something that makes the plant tissue glisten. A cluster of spherical creatures, each one the size of your fist, translucent, filled with visible organs, clinging to the inside of one of those hanging pods, feeding on or cleaning or tending the suspended matter within.

They are silent, or nearly so. They do not flee when you approach, but they do not remain either. They simply continue their incomprehensible tasks, fitting themselves into ecological niches that are still being written.

The air tastes green, if that makes sense. There is so much oxygen being produced that it's almost dizzying. Your head feels light if you stay too long in one place, as if the atmosphere itself is too rich, too vital for your adapted lungs. You find yourself breathing shallowly, carefully.

Water appears in unexpected ways. Sometimes you'll find entire walls weeping moisture, clear fluid that might be sap or might be something the plant has filtered from the air, running down the waxy surfaces in sheets. Other times, you'll press a hand against the plant tissue and feel it yield slightly, and your palm will come away damp. The humidity varies from place to place—drier in the open centers of the larger valleys, where the air has more room to hold its moisture without condensing; wetter near the ceiling, where the temperature differential causes constant precipitation; and wettest in the narrow passages between walls, where the plant tissue seems to exhale humidity as part of its respiration.

There are no streams, no standing bodies of water larger than the shallow substrate pools. The water is dispersed, atomized, locked in plant tissue or suspended in the thick air. You are always slightly damp here, your clothes never quite dry, a film of moisture on every exposed surface.

The rules of this place are still settling.

You notice it in small ways. A section of substrate that yesterday (if you were here yesterday) had a particular arrangement of crystal planes today shows a different structure, as if something at the molecular level is still deciding what form to take. A succulent wall that blocks a passage; you return hours later and find a gap has opened, the plant tissue having reorganized itself around an internal logic you don't possess.

The growth is not uniform. Some areas feel *finished*, stable, as if they've reached some endpoint of development. The plant tissue there is tougher, more opaque, the colors deeper. Other areas are clearly transitional—the plants translucent, tender, still figuring out their final forms. You can sometimes see internal structures through the walls in these places: veins, chambers, structural elements that look almost skeletal, all suspended in that crystalline green flesh.

There is no day and night. The ceiling glows with constant intensity, never brightening, never dimming. The temperature doesn't cycle. Nothing here marks the passage of time except growth itself, and growth in this heat, with this much light and moisture, is both rapid and imperceptible. You can't watch a plant grow, but you can feel that everything is in motion, that the landscape is actively *becoming*.

Walking through the valleys, you develop a sense of the place's logic, though you could never quite articulate it. The largest succulent walls tend to run roughly parallel to the valley orientations, creating a natural grain to the space. The smaller growth—the vine tangles, the moss-mounds, the hanging pods—fills in the gaps with an almost architectural density, creating layers of space at different heights. The effect is of a three-dimensional maze, but one without dead ends, one where every path leads somewhere and circles back in ways that feel inevitable rather than planned.

The built structures, when you encounter them, don't interrupt this logic—they extend it. The standing stones are positioned to create sightlines through the vegetation. The suspended platform aligns with an unusual gap in the ceiling membrane above it, where the light concentrates. The chamber-inside-the-wall opens toward a spot where three valleys intersect, though you have to travel through the plant tissue to appreciate the geometry.

Who built these things? The question feels increasingly strange the longer you're here. The artifacts have the quality of being *grown* rather than *made*—but grown with intention, with an intelligence directing the process. Or perhaps they're the opposite: perhaps they're made, but made to look grown, to fit seamlessly into this plant-constructed environment.

There are no signs of habitation. No fires, no waste, no worn paths, no discarded objects. If people come here—or came here, or will come here—they leave nothing behind, or else everything they use is absorbed back into the vegetable mass, the way the fallen ceiling pods are absorbed, becoming substrate for the next layer of growth.

The strangeness of the place accumulates gradually. It's not that any one thing is impossible—plants can grow large, bioluminescence exists, subsurface environments are known. It's the combination, the completeness of it. This is an ecosystem with no soil, no conventional rock, no water cycle as you understand it. The plants here don't draw nutrients from the ground—the ground itself is too new, too raw, too sterile. They must be drawing from the air, from the light, from each other, creating a closed or nearly-closed system that sustains itself through some biochemistry you can only guess at.

And there's the heat. Photosynthesis doesn't generate heat at this scale. The plants are *burning* something, metabolizing at a rate that produces these steady forty-plus degrees as a byproduct. The energy budget of this place seems wrong, excessive, as if there's a source you can't see, something feeding the system from outside or below.

You climb to one of the higher plateaus, where the substrate forms a relatively open space, fifty meters across, only lightly colonized by the beginning of new growth. From here, you can see the extent of the valley system, the way it spreads in all directions, valleys intersecting and branching in a pattern that might be fractal, might be organic, might be both.

The ceiling curves slightly—you hadn't noticed before, but from this elevation it's visible. This isn't a flat membrane overhead; it's domed, or rather, it's a series of overlapping domes, each one centered roughly over a valley. The membrane is thickest at the edges, where valleys meet, and thinnest at the centers, where the light is strongest. Those hanging pods cluster at the thin points, as if they're taking advantage of the concentrated radiance.

Looking out across the landscape, you estimate the visible area at perhaps five or six square kilometers, though the refraction effects and the density of vegetation make it impossible to be certain. But the valleys continue beyond what you can see, fading into green-tinted distance. This place is not small. It's regional in scope, a complete under-surface ecosystem spanning the size of a town or small valley system.

And it is—you feel this more than know it—very young.

The rawness of the substrate, the unsettled quality of the growth patterns, the way the light itself seems too pure, too strong, as if it hasn't yet learned to moderate itself—all of this suggests a place that has been in existence for months, perhaps a few years at most. The plants have had time to achieve their current enormous scale, but not time to evolve the full complexity of a mature ecosystem. The animals are too few, too simple. There's no detritus layer, no decay, no accumulation of generations.

You are witnessing—or standing inside—the early stages of worldbuilding. This is what emergence looks like when scaled up to landscape size.

The stability is eerie. Nothing changes while you watch, but everything has recently changed. It's like standing in a photograph of a place that is actually in rapid motion, but moving at a scale where hours pass between visible alterations. The equilibrium is so complete it feels artificial, as if someone or something has set this place into a particular state and it's simply going to remain there, hot and bright and endlessly growing at exactly the same rate it grows now, forever.

But that can't be right. The substrate is still forming, or was recently formed. The plants are still colonizing. Eventually—maybe centuries from now, maybe next month—something will shift. The growth will slow, or accelerate, or change character. The animals will diversify. The heat will find an exit or the system will develop a true cycle. The built structures will multiply or decay or reveal their purpose.

For now, though, it holds. The lung valleys breathe in light and breathe out oxygen and heat, and hold themselves in this improbable state of beginning, this moment of creation frozen just after the first word but before the sentence completes.

You descend from the plateau, back into the dense green corridors between succulent walls, and the light enfolds you again, sourceless and complete.
