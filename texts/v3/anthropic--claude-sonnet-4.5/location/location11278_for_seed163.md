# The Canopy of New Engines

The mountains here are still deciding what they want to be. Young volcanic peaks, perhaps three or four million years in the making, push upward through blankets of moss thick as mattresses. The stone itself—black basalt, rust-streaked andesite—breaks the surface in raw geometric facets that haven't yet learned the grammar of weathering. Boulders sit where they tumbled last century, angular and undomesticated, their faces flat as factory castings. Between them, the earth is more fungal mat than soil, a spongy infrastructure of decomposition that hasn't yet stratified into layers, hasn't yet separated its functions. Everything is inaugural. Everything is first-draft geology.

But onto this embryonic framework, the plants have thrown themselves with the enthusiasm of conquerors who've arrived before the maps were drawn.

The vegetation doesn't grow here so much as *constitute* the place. It forms the actual navigable surface. You cannot walk on ground—there isn't ground in the conventional sense, only a continuous living membrane stretched across the mountain's bones. Moss dominates, but "moss" seems too modest a word for what has happened. These are mosses the size of cushions, of ottomans, of dining tables, bloated with water and impossibly green under the bright mountain light. They've built themselves into architecture: domes, terraces, bridges between stones. The larger formations are species I cannot name, would not know how to name—compact woody plants that have learned to grow horizontally, creating shelves and platforms that jut from cliffs, their surfaces thatched with ferns and smaller mosses in turn. The whole system locks together in a matrix of mutual support, each plant providing substrate for the next, until you're walking on a surface that might be twenty, thirty, forty feet thick in places—a mattress of life suspended above the actual mountain.

Water is everywhere and nowhere you can properly see. The air condenses on every surface constantly. The moss drinks from the air as much as from below. Clouds don't pass through here so much as exhale against the slopes, and the plants catch every molecule of that breath. When you press your hand to the moss—and you must, to climb anything, to move anywhere—water squeezes out around your fingers, cold and clear. Threads of water run beneath the plant-surface in channels worn through accumulated organic matter, the sound of it like whispered commentary from below. Where stone emerges—those raw black faces—it weeps. Moisture films over it, thickens, drips, runs in silver tracks that feed the moss below, which feeds the ferns, which feed the streams you cannot see but always hear.

The light at this elevation is merciless and ecstatic. At perhaps eight, nine thousand feet, the air thins enough that the sun arrives unmediated. There are no tall trees here—cannot be, not on such young stone, not with such shallow rooting—so nothing interposes between the sky and this green ocean. The moss is lit from above like a stage, every shade of green differentiated: the yellow-green of new growth, the black-green of water-saturated patches, the silver-green of lichen collaborations. The light bounces between moisture droplets suspended in air and moisture coating every surface, creating a luminous haze that makes distances uncertain. Shadows are rare and temporary. Even the stones, when they show, gleam wet and dark, picking up the green from everything around them until the whole landscape seems to pulse with chromatic unity.

Scattered across this terrain—breaking through the moss, perched on rare flat expanses of stone—are the remnants and active examples of some kind of industrial presence, though calling it "industrial" might suggest more coherence than exists. These are not factories but single machines, placed with what seems like either great intentionality or complete randomness depending on how you choose to interpret isolation. A pump station built from riveted iron, its metal gone rust-brown and orange, still chugging away with a steady rhythm that you feel in your sternum before you see the structure. A waterwheel—massive, fifteen feet across, wood and steel hybrid—turning slowly in a channel that seems to have been cut through stone and then immediately colonized by moss, so that water and green move together through its buckets. A generator shed, brick and corrugated metal, door hanging open, the sound of electricity humming inside like a sleeping animal. Cable runs between some of these installations, strung on ceramic insulators attached to iron stakes driven into moss, the cables themselves fuzzy with moisture and the first establishment of aerial plants.

These machines are not abandoned—they function, lights blink, gears turn, water is moved or power is generated or something is monitored—but neither are they attended in any conventional sense. Perhaps someone comes monthly. Perhaps these systems are robust enough to want nothing but the water that surrounds them. The machinery has a turn-of-some-century quality, though which century is uncertain. Technologies that might be sixty years old or a hundred and twenty, built when engines were supposed to last, when manufacturing meant casting and bolting rather than integrating and sealing. The machines breathe steam sometimes. They leak oil that the moss seems to metabolize without complaint. They make their sounds—rhythmic, mechanical, patient—and those sounds carry.

Oh, the sound.

The sound is where this place stops being peaceful and starts being something else entirely. It's the water first: not a single stream but ten thousand trickles, a million drips, the constant whisper-rush-patter of moisture in motion. Every surface weeps, every channel carries, every drop falls from some overhung moss-beard into a pool below. Multiply that by every square foot of this valley—and it is valley-sized, this landscape, extending perhaps three miles across, visible in its breadth when you gain any elevation—and the water becomes a roar that never stops, that comes from everywhere, that has no source because everything is source. Then add the wind, which at this elevation and with this much moisture becomes audible as friction—wind through moss makes a sound like brushing fabric, continuous, variable in pitch as gusts strengthen or fade. The wind carries moisture, redistributes it, presses it into new configurations, and talks constantly while doing so.

Then the machines add their voices. Not loud individually, but there are perhaps thirty or forty of them scattered across the visible terrain, each contributing its particular rhythm. The pump stations are loudest—a diesel chug or electric hum that pulses, echoes off stone faces, gets absorbed by moss, emerges somewhere else. The waterwheels creak, wood swelling and shrinking with moisture, the groan of old lumber under constant work. There's a cable somewhere—you can hear it but not see it—that vibrates in the wind, a low thrumming note that changes pitch with air pressure. One structure, far up the northern slope, releases steam on some regular schedule, a hiss that builds and releases every few minutes, audible for miles. And there's something deeper, harder to locate, that might be caverns resonating below, or might be the mountain itself settling, a subsonic presence you feel more than hear, the suggestion that beneath all this green, the stone is still adjusting, still finding its shape.

All of this together creates not cacophony exactly—the sounds are too constant for that, too integrated—but a kind of sonic saturation where silence is not possible, where even thinking happens against this enormous backdrop of water and wind and machinery in patient operation.

Animals exist here but barely. You might see a bird—something small and gray, undramatic, hunting insects among the moss—once in an afternoon. There are insects themselves, of course; there must be, to support the plants, to work the ecology. Small flies, perhaps. Beetles in the damper cavities. But nothing that announces itself. No mammals that I've seen. Perhaps pikas, somewhere higher where stone dominates more. Perhaps something hibernating in the spaces below the moss-surface, in the air pockets that must exist in that thick matrix. But fauna is not the point here, not what the landscape is organized around. This is a plant kingdom with an industrial haunting, and animals—including humans—are permitted as sparse visitors, not residents.

The temperature hovers just below comfort. In full sun, the soaked moss breathes coolness upward; in shade or when clouds push through, the air goes cold enough to see your breath, though rarely cold enough to threaten frost. It's the temperature of highland springs, of water just emerged from stone. You need a jacket but not a parka. Your hands get cold when touching wet surfaces, which is always. The moisture carries heat away efficiently. Nothing ever quite dries. The machines don't seem to mind—they were built for weather, for patient operation in hostile climates. The moss certainly doesn't mind. This is its optimal range, cool enough to slow decay, wet enough to make every day growing season.

Moving through this landscape means accepting the plant layer as terrain. You step on moss and sink three inches, six inches, sometimes a foot before encountering resistance. That resistance might be more moss, compressed. Might be stone. Might be the woody stems of those horizontal shrubs, which can support weight if you find them but also can roll underfoot. The larger moss-formations become landmarks: that dome-cluster by the pump station, this terrace of ferns draped over a boulder field. You navigate from machine to machine sometimes, using their solidity as waypoints. The pump stations in particular, with their concrete pads or stone foundations, offer rare flat surfaces that don't compress, places to rest where water isn't immediately soaking through your boots.

There are paths, but they're strange. Not worn bare—the moss grows too fast for that—but compressed, slightly darker green, following routes between machines or toward some destination I can't identify. Someone walks here. Someone maintains. But infrequently enough that the paths are suggestions rather than scars, a slight difference in spring-back when you step, a sense that others have found this particular route viable.

The built structures, beyond the machines, are minimal. One shelter, maybe two, scattered across miles. Stone walls with corrugated roofs, three walls and an overhang, enough to get out of the weather but not to live in any sustained way. A platform built from metal grating, bolted to stone, providing a vantage over the central valley where seven or eight machines are visible at once. A bridge, very old, brick piers with a steel deck, crossing what must be the valley's main drainage though you can't see the water under its moss covering—you hear it though, louder there, the sound of significant volume in motion below. On the far side of that bridge, a wooden sign, weathered past legibility, marking something that was once considered worth marking.

The infrastructure suggests this place had a purpose, perhaps still has one, though what that purpose might be is not immediately clear. The machines move water, generate power, monitor conditions, but to what end? There are no settlements visible, no towns in the valleys below that you might be supplying. Perhaps this is a testing ground, proof-of-concept installations for high-altitude generation. Perhaps it's a monitoring network, measuring what young mountains do as they settle and accumulate life. Perhaps it was a failed development scheme from decades ago, and the machines simply never got the message that the project ended, so they continue their work in perpetual probation, waiting for instructions that won't arrive.

Or perhaps the purpose is the landscape itself—the machinery serving the plants, helping to move water through this system that's still learning how to hold and distribute it. The pumps might be feeding higher terraces, letting moss colonize slopes that would otherwise be too steep. The waterwheels might be grinding something, producing something, that gets fed back into the soil-that-isn't-soil. The generator might be powering sensors, lights, systems that help the plants through dark months or freeze events, a mechanical midwifery for this infant ecosystem.

Time here moves at the pace of moss expansion, of stone weathering, of cables slowly corroding. A day passes and nothing visible changes. A year passes and that boulder has grown another six inches of green cushion. A decade and the moss might reach a new terrace, establish on stones it couldn't hold before. The machines tick through their rhythms—minute, hour, day—but their impact unfolds across seasons. Water routes itself slowly through new channels. Iron stakes rust and eventually fail, and cables droop, and perhaps someone comes and raises them again, or perhaps the system adapts. The mountain continues its imperceptible rise, millimeters per year, pushing this green platform higher into the sky.

There are moments when the light does something particular—when clouds thin after completely socking in the valley, when sun breaks through and ignites every water droplet simultaneously—and the whole landscape seems to catch fire with brightness. The moss glows. The air becomes visible, thick with suspended moisture turning gold or silver depending on angle. The machines, dark and industrial, throw complex shadows for the first time in days, and you can see their shapes clearly, their purpose written in form. Then the clouds close again or the sun shifts, and the diffuse brightness returns, and everything goes back to being evenly, relentlessly illuminated.

At the edges of the visible terrain, the landscape fades into cloud as much as distance. The valley opens to the west, dropping toward lower elevations you cannot see. To the east and north, higher peaks rise into the permanent cloud layer, their summits invisible, their slopes disappearing into white. South, the terrain seems to level somewhat, extending into a broader plateau where moss gives way partially to low woody vegetation, a transition zone toward whatever ecosystem dominates at this elevation when stone is older, when soil has had time to develop properly. But here, in this valley of new stone and total plants and scattered engines, you are in a threshold landscape, a place still becoming itself, where the rules are being written by moss and water and the patient turning of gears.

The weirdness is in the normalcy of it. This is a place that operates by recognizable rules—gravity, hydrology, photosynthesis, mechanical engineering—but combines them in a configuration that feels like it shouldn't work, shouldn't sustain, yet clearly does. The plants shouldn't be able to build such thickness on such young stone. The machines shouldn't keep running without more obvious maintenance. The water shouldn't be so omnipresent without creating floods or bogs. But walk through it for hours and the impossible becomes familiar. You learn to trust the moss to hold your weight. You stop flinching at the constant sound. You understand that the machines are part of the ecology now, as permanent as the stones, contributing their sounds and functions to whatever strange stability this place has achieved.

This is a landscape of beginnings that has somehow stabilized in its beginning-state, a perpetual emergence that doesn't progress toward maturity but maintains itself in youth. The stone will continue rising, the moss will continue thickening, the machines will continue their work, and perhaps in another million years something different will have developed. But for now, in this particular window of geologic time, you have a high wet valley of total green punctuated by iron voices, bright and loud and cool and alive, building itself one millimeter of moss at a time while water whispers its constant encouragement from every surface, and old engines—patient, unquestioning—tend the garden they were built to serve or monitor or simply witness in its loud, luminous becoming.
