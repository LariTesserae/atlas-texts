# The Drowned Plateau

In the first hours after the ice dam broke—though "hours" may be the wrong word for a rupture whose first wave took three days to crest the continental shelf—the water came not as flood but as arrival. It rose around the plateau's flanks with the methodical patience of something that knows it has already won. The plateau itself stands four kilometers high, a young basalt shield whose formation the water interrupted mid-birth. The rock is dark, dark gray verging on black, and where it breaks—which is often, because the whole edifice is barely twenty thousand years old—the fracture planes catch light like flint.

The water now sits at roughly three kilometers depth across what was once a basin two thousand kilometers wide. The plateau rises through it like a fist, its upper kilometer emerging into air that tastes of iron and cold salt. But "emerges" suggests too much drama. Better to say the rock *persists* above the waterline, because nothing here suggests triumph or defiance—only the bare fact of different densities, stone being lighter than the particular brine that surrounds it.

The scale of it defeats the eye. You can stand at what feels like the edge and see nothing but the geometric line where basalt meets water, and beyond that, water meeting sky in a seam so distant it curves with the planet. The plateau's upper surface runs relatively flat for sixty, sometimes eighty kilometers before the next ridge system interrupts, and those ridges—when they come—are themselves fifty kilometers long, parallel ranks of stone that march toward horizons you cannot see from any single vantage. The emptiness between features is profound. You could walk for two days and encounter nothing but the same dark stone, the same texture underfoot: rough, vesicular basalt, pocked with frozen gas bubbles, each bubble the size of a fist or a head, giving the stone a density that's barely enough.

Because the plateau is young, the stone still has its natal sharpness. Edges have not yet worn down. The columnar jointing—hexagonal pillars that formed as the lava cooled—still shows crisp geometry, each column separated from its neighbors by fracture lines so straight they seem deliberate. In places where the columns have begun to topple, they lie scattered like massive pencils, each one three or four meters across and forty meters long, their fall arrested by the simple fact that nothing here moves fast enough to complete a collapse. The wind worries at them. The thermal cycling worries at them. But after twenty millennia, most of what will fall has fallen, and the rest has achieved a kind of temporary forever.

The water itself is not quite water. It's saline, yes—roughly twice the concentration of Earth's oceans—but the cold has changed its character. At three kilometers depth, the temperature hovers just above freezing, and the pressure does strange things to the dissolved minerals. The brine is heavy, dense, thick in a way that's hard to describe: not viscous, exactly, but *consequential*. Things dropped into it fall slowly. Things floating in it float differently than they should, obeying rules that seem negotiable. The water has a color—a deep gray-green that tends toward black in the depths—but it's not murky. Visibility extends for meters, sometimes tens of meters, before the water simply absorbs all remaining light into its own persistent twilight.

The waterline traces around the plateau's upper reaches in a band of activity that makes the stone above and below it seem even more inert by comparison. This is where the fauna concentrate, where the sound originates, where the few visible signs of the industrial operation appear.

The sound is extraordinary. Constant, layered, a physical presence that vibrates in your chest and jawbone. At its foundation is the *industrial hum*, a bass-frequency drone that comes from machinery working in the depths. You cannot see these machines—they operate at crushing pressures far below—but their sound propagates up through the water and into the stone itself, so that the whole plateau rings faintly, a four-kilometer tuning fork struck and held. Over this foundation: the *biological shriek* of the fauna, which are not quiet creatures. They whistle, they click, they produce sounds like metal scraping metal, like hydraulic pisses, like pressure valves venting. The sounds are not occasional. They are constant, overlapping, a wall of noise that makes conversation impossible at normal volumes and turns human presence into something that must shout to be known.

The fauna themselves are difficult to count because they exist in such numbers that individual identity becomes meaningless. They are, broadly speaking, arthropod-analogs: segmented, exoskeletoned, multi-limbed, ranging from the size of a small dog to the size of a delivery truck. They cluster at the waterline in rafts and masses, piled on and over each other in writhing aggregations that can extend for kilometers along the shore. Their shells are pale—cream, ivory, bone-white—which makes them visible against the dark stone even in low light. They scrape at the basalt with specialized limbs, harvesting something—minerals, perhaps, or bacterial films, or the stone itself—and they do this constantly, day and night (though the distinction barely matters here), producing a subsidiary sound beneath the whistling and clicking: the *grinding*, a textured, abrasive noise like a thousand files working in rough unison.

They are not aggressive toward human presence, but neither are they avoidant. They simply continue. If you stand too close, they will climb over your feet, treating you as they treat any other obstacle: a temporary inconvenience to route around or clamber over. Their touch is cold and hard. They smell like ammonia and salt. They die in place sometimes, adding their shells to the substrate, and others immediately begin to feed on the remains, so that the waterline is also a graveyard and also a feast, layers of shell fragments cementing together into a new kind of stone, pale and composite.

Above the waterline, the fauna thin out rapidly. Some species can survive for hours in air—they drag themselves up onto the dry stone, conduct whatever business they have, then return—but the densities never approach what's happening at water level. In the air, you might encounter scattered individuals, moving with arthritic slowness, leaving trails of moisture that evaporate into the dry cold. The trails catch light. They're one of the few signs of movement on the upper plateau, aside from the human artifacts.

The artifacts are sparse and utilitarian. Every twenty or thirty kilometers, you might find a *station*: a low structure, usually just one or two rooms, built from imported materials—metal, mostly, and some kind of dense plastic—anchored to the basalt with expansion bolts drilled deep. The stations show a design philosophy that prioritizes function over comfort. They are painted in high-visibility colors—orange, yellow, reflective white—because in the emptiness of the plateau, losing track of a station is a real danger. Most are uninhabited. They serve as waypoints, supply caches, shelter from the wind. Inside: minimal furnishings, equipment racks, a heating unit (always running, because the cold here is patient and absolute), maybe a logbook where previous visitors have noted conditions, dates, purposes. The logs are terse. "Seismic array, sector 47, operational." "Resupply run, no anomalies." The handwriting is often shaky—the cold makes fine motor control difficult.

The more substantial structures are the *pumping stations*, which appear at irregular intervals along the plateau's edge. These are larger, multi-story in some cases, built directly at the waterline where they can draw the brine up through massive intake pipes. The stations are loud even by the standards of this place. The pumps are industrial-scale, driven by electric motors fed by cables that snake away across the stone toward the plateau's interior, where—somewhere, far from any edge—the power generation happens. You never see the power plants themselves; they're always over the horizon, always someone else's concern. But you see the cables, thick as tree trunks, insulated in black rubber, running in straight lines across the empty stone, held up every hundred meters by simple posts. The cables hum. Everything hums.

The pumping stations draw the brine up and process it—exactly how is unclear, because the machinery is housed in sealed units marked with warnings about pressure and electrical hazards—and output two things: fresh water, which is stored in tanks or piped away, and a crystalline residue, which is dumped in heaps beside the stations. The heaps are white and glittering, small mountains of salt and other precipitates, growing slowly but steadily. Some heaps are already ten meters high. In time—decades, centuries—they'll become features of the landscape themselves, pale pyramids against the dark stone.

The light here is even and diffuse most of the time. The sky is overcast more often than not, a high, thin cloud deck that turns the sun into a general brightness without clear direction. Shadows are soft when they exist at all. The quality of illumination is what you'd call "flat," which makes depth perception difficult and adds to the sense of scale confusion—it's hard to judge distances when everything is evenly lit and there are no familiar reference objects. On the rare clear days, the sun is small and cold, and the shadows it casts are long and sharp and somehow wrong, because the light is coming from a sun that's the right color but the wrong intensity, as if someone has turned down its dimmer switch.

The atmosphere is thin. Not so thin you need equipment, but thin enough that you're always slightly short of breath, and exertion becomes costly fast. The air is dry—phenomenally dry, because the water locked in the brine below isn't evaporating much, and what moisture does rise is immediately swept away by the wind. The wind is constant, if not always strong. It comes from the direction of the water, flows over the plateau, and continues toward... what? More plateau? Mountains? Another edge? No one you talk to has circumnavigated. The wind tastes like salt and metal. It carries no pollen, no spores, no smell of life except the ammonia-tang from the fauna colonies, and even that's faint once you're a few hundred meters inland.

There's no vegetation because there's nothing for vegetation to root in. The basalt is intact and largely unweathered—erosion has barely started its work—and what little sediment exists is just basalt sand, ground down by the wind and the thermal cycling. No soil. No organics except what the fauna deposit, and even that's minimal; the creatures seem to be remarkably efficient, leaving little waste. You can find bacterial mats in some of the shallow pools that form in the stone's depressions, where water collects from the rare rain or from spray blown up from below. These mats are rust-red or sulfur-yellow, and they're the only thing like plant life here: primary producers, maybe, or chemoautotrophs feeding on the minerals in the stone. They don't grow fast. They don't spread far. They just persist, one more thing that's achieved stasis.

Because almost nothing changes here. The water level hasn't varied more than a few centimeters in decades—wherever the inflow and outflow are happening, they're balanced. The fauna populations are stable; whatever they're eating is regenerating at exactly the rate they consume it. The pumping stations run year after year without visible expansion or contraction of operations. The stone doesn't crumble. The wind doesn't intensify or slacken. Even the cloud deck seems fixed, the same thin overcast day after day, month after month.

Time feels negotiable in this place. You can leave a piece of equipment on the stone, walk away for a week, and return to find it exactly as you left it—no rust, no displacement, no sign that anything has noticed or cared. The stations' logbooks sometimes have gaps of months between entries, and reading them gives the impression that each visit is essentially identical to the last: same conditions, same operations, same terse notes. The human presence here is less an inhabitation than a maintenance routine, a set of actions performed because they were performed before, will be performed again, in an environment that neither welcomes nor resists them.

At night—and the nights are long, because this place sits at a high latitude, or something analogous to it—the darkness is nearly complete. No moon. The stars are visible, but they're faint, dimmed by the thin haze that never quite clears even when the cloud deck breaks. The horizon glows faintly where the fauna concentrations are thickest, a pale phosphorescence from their shells catching starlight and from bioluminescent organisms in the water. The sound doesn't diminish. If anything, it becomes more noticeable, more oppressive, because without visual distractions, your awareness focuses on the industrial hum, the grinding, the whistles and clicks that continue without pause.

Walking the plateau at night is an exercise in trust. The stone is dark enough that even adapted eyes struggle to read its surface. The emptiness becomes absolute. You navigate by the faint line of a cable, by the distant glow of a station, by memory of the terrain you crossed in daylight. You move slowly. You stop often. The cold settles into your core, and the wind works at any exposed skin, and the sound fills every space around and inside you until you can't remember what silence was like.

There are places where the plateau's surface fractures into small canyons—not true canyons, really, just places where the columnar jointing has created parallel cracks deep enough to climb down into. These are quieter, relatively speaking, because you're sheltered from the wind and below the direct line of the fauna's noise. The walls are close and dark and angular. Sometimes you find water at the bottom, seepage from the brine below, pooled in the geometric channels between columns. The water is still, cold, so saline it's almost syrupy. Nothing lives in it. Nothing grows on the walls. It's just water and stone, a pocket of isolation in a landscape that's already isolated.

In the depths—in the water, where the machinery operates—there are lights sometimes. You can see them from the plateau's edge at night: deep glows, amber or pale green, moving slowly or holding position. These are the submersibles, the autonomous drones, the manned capsules that service whatever infrastructure exists at pressure. They're working the seabed, or they're maintaining the cables and pipelines that connect the surface operations to the deep ones, or they're doing research, sampling, monitoring. The operations are coordinated—that much is clear from the pumping stations' evident functionality—but they're not explained. The people you encounter on the plateau's surface are mostly technicians, mostly focused on their specific tasks: maintain the pump, check the array, resupply the cache. They're not hostile, but they're not informative either. There's a sense that everyone here knows only their piece of the operation, and maybe that's intentional, or maybe it's just the natural result of scale—no one can hold the entire picture of something this large.

The plateau's edges are the most dramatic features, though "dramatic" is relative in a place this austere. Where the stone meets the water, it usually does so in cliffs—not tall, perhaps thirty to fifty meters, but sheer, with that sharp columnar structure making them look like deliberate fortifications. The waves, when there are waves, are small and tired. The brine is too dense for energetic wave action, and the weather patterns don't generate significant storms. Instead, the water just laps at the stone, a rhythmic pulse that adds another layer to the sound: a low, wet slap, repeated, echoing slightly in the spaces between columns.

The fauna use the cliff faces as habitat. They cling to the vertical stone, piled into crevices, sometimes hanging in masses that seem structurally impossible—hundreds of kilograms of shell and flesh clinging by what? Adhesion, maybe. Friction. There's no obvious glue, no webbing. They just hold on. And if they fall—which they do, occasionally, whole clusters detaching and dropping into the water below—the sound is a muted splash, and then the water churns briefly as others converge to feed or investigate, and then equilibrium returns.

You can climb down the cliffs, if you're careful. The stone offers plenty of holds—those hexagonal columns create natural hand- and footholds—but the fauna make the descent complicated. You have to push through them sometimes, or wait for a section of cliff to clear. They don't react aggressively, but they also don't move aside. You're just another thing in their environment. At the waterline, where the stone is slick with spray and biological film, the footing is treacherous. People have fallen. The water is cold enough that immersion is immediately debilitating. Rescue protocols exist, but they depend on proximity to a station, on communication equipment working, on someone noticing. The emptiness here extends to safety margins. There's space for error, but there's also space for consequence.

Below the waterline, the cliff faces continue down into the murk. In the better-lit shallows—if "shallow" is the right word for the first hundred meters—you can see that the fauna extend down, densities thinning gradually but never disappearing. And beyond them, in the dimness, there are larger shapes. These are the fauna's adult forms, or apex predators, or something else entirely. They're vehicle-sized, some of them, and they move with slow, powerful strokes, their limbs working in concert to propel them through the dense brine. They ignore the surface activity. Their concerns are deeper, darker, out in the cold pressure where the machinery hums and the lights sometimes glow.

The industrial operation's purpose is unclear from the plateau's surface. Desalination, obviously—the pumping stations make that much explicit. But at this scale? Pumping stations every thirty or forty kilometers, all operating continuously, all feeding their output into a distribution network that must be vast? Who's using this much fresh water? Where is it going? The pipelines run inland, elevated on posts like the power cables, and they disappear toward the plateau's interior, toward the same unreachable horizon. You can follow them if you want. People do. But the journeys are long and the landscape doesn't change—just more stone, more cables, more pipelines, more emptiness—and eventually you turn back, because the stations you've passed are the same as the stations ahead, and the purpose remains opaque.

There's a theory, mentioned in some of the logbooks, that this is a terraforming operation, that the water is being moved and processed to create habitable zones somewhere else on the planet. Another theory: industrial input, the water feeding into manufacturing processes that require enormous quantities of specific-purity brine or fresh water. A third theory: the operation is its own purpose, a self-sustaining system that exists because it was built and now continues because stopping would be harder than continuing. No one seems certain. No one seems concerned, either. The work continues.

At the plateau's highest point—a subtle rise, only fifty or sixty meters above the surrounding surface, but enough to matter in this flat landscape—there's a monitoring station larger than the others. It has a tower, a skeletal metal structure thirty meters tall, festooned with instruments: anemometers, atmospheric sensors, cameras on motorized mounts. The station itself is a cluster of prefab modules, insulated and heated, with actual sleeping quarters, a common room, a kitchen. This is a staffed facility, one of the few. Three or four people live here on rotating schedules, maintaining the equipment, compiling data, managing the logistics for the more remote stations.

Inside, the warmth is shocking after the exterior cold. The air is dry and smells like machine oil and coffee. The common room has a window, a large triple-pane thing that looks out over the plateau's expanse, and during the day, you can sit and watch the light not change across the stone. At night, the glass just reflects the interior, and the outside becomes a theory. The people who live here are polite but distant. They've adapted to the isolation in ways that make casual interaction feel slightly off—conversational rhythms that expect long pauses, physical proximity that's either too close or too far, eye contact that lingers or avoids. They've been here months. They'll be here months more. They know the plateau in a way that surface visitors never will, know the subtle variations in wind patterns, the slight seasonal shifts in fauna behavior, the specific sounds that indicate a pump malfunction versus normal operation.

They talk about the silence, sometimes. Not the actual silence—there is none—but the *conceptual* silence, the absence of change, the way days blur together until time feels less like a sequence and more like a condition. They talk about the dreams they have, which are often about motion: rivers, traffic, crowds. They talk about the way the sound gets inside your head, how you hear it even when you're wearing hearing protection, even when you're in the insulated interior of the station, how it becomes a kind of tinnitus, a baseline hum against which all other sounds are measured.

And they talk, occasionally, about the *strangeness*. The plateau is young, geologically speaking, but the industrial operation feels ancient, even though the records show it's only been running for—what? Fifty years? Eighty? There's a disconnect between the evidence of recent construction and the sense that everything here has been in place forever. The stations weather too slowly. The machinery shows no wear. The fauna have no parasites, no diseases, no signs of population pressure despite their numbers. It's all *slightly wrong*, not enough to point to and say "there," but enough that people who spend time here start to notice, start to question.

The weirdness parameter sits around 0.9, which means it's present but not dominant. The place doesn't violate natural law; it just bends it in ways that are hard to articulate. Water that's too dense. Fauna that's too numerous and too stable. Stone that's too unchanged. A scale that doesn't map onto familiar planetary templates—the plateau is the size of a continent, the water around it is apparently global or near-global, and yet the curvature you see from the high points suggests a planetary radius that's... off. Too small for this much water? Too large for this gravity? The math doesn't break, but it doesn't quite add up, either.

And underneath everything: the sound. The deafening, omnipresent, multi-layered sound that makes this landscape not just something you see or walk through, but something that occupies you, fills you, becomes part of your internal state. The industrial hum. The fauna's shrieking clicks and whistles. The grinding. The wind adding its own steady white noise. The slap of waves. The creak and ping of cooling stone in the nighttime cold. You can't escape it. You can't adapt to it, not fully. It's always there, always demanding your nervous system's attention, always adding a layer of stress that accumulates slowly, imperceptibly, until you realize you haven't relaxed in days, weeks, and the thought of true silence—of a quiet room, a still forest, an empty desert at night—becomes almost painful in its desirability.

The plateau persists. The water surrounds. The fauna grind and whistle. The machinery hums. The stone remains sharp and dark and empty. And the horizon stays distant, curved, unmarked, offering no promise that anything beyond it is different from what's here: just more basalt, more brine, more sound, more stasis, extending to scales that defeat comprehension and meaning both.
