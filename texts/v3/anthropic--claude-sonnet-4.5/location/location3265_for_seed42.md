# The Hanging Gardens of Stillwater

This is a drowned city that has become a forest suspended in water.

The place exists at what was once a high plateau — the stone foundations suggest elevation, the way certain peaks still break surface during the lowest tides of the dry season, but now the plateau is a shallow sea, a lens of fresh water held in an ancient basin. The water stands at perhaps twenty to thirty feet deep across most of the district, greenish and dense with suspended life, clear enough to see through but never truly transparent. You can stand on what were once rooftops and see down to the street level below, see the doorways and the worn steps, but the details blur into soft geometry after the first few fathoms.

The buildings remain. Stone walls, timber frames, clay brick — a settlement that was substantial before the water came. The architecture speaks of centuries: a fishing town, perhaps, or a monastery complex, something between a hamlet and a small town that grew according to its own logic rather than grand design. The walls are thick, built for permanence. Windows and doors are irregular, placed where they were needed rather than for symmetry. Some structures have collapsed — you see rubble piles softened by time, walls that stop mid-height, but most still stand, their upper stories emerging from the water surface like the heads of patient animals.

And through every broken window, every doorway, every crack in every wall, the plants have come.

## The Vegetation as Architecture

Kelp, but not kelp. Freshwater plants that have evolved or adapted to fill the drowned buildings as if the buildings were designed for them. The primary growth is a species — or perhaps several species braided together by time — that resembles giant waterweed, Elodea grown to impossible scale. Stems as thick as cables rise from the submerged floors and streets, branching in the water column, reaching for the light above. The leaves are long, ribbon-like, flexible, appearing in whorls along the stems. In color they range from deep emerald to yellowish-green depending on depth and age, and they move constantly with the water's minimal current, a slow breathing motion.

These plants don't just grow in the buildings — they have become structural. Their root masses grip and hold the old stones. Their stems weave through window frames, wrap around support posts, bridge from one wall to another. In places they've clearly prevented collapse; you can see buildings that lean at angles that should be impossible, held together by a internal scaffolding of plant matter. The vegetation has turned the architecture into a kind of coral, a hybrid structure where stone and plant share the work of standing.

Above water, the stems continue, but the character changes. Here the plants develop thick, waxy leaves that can handle air exposure, and produce woody structures — not quite true wood, but something between stem and trunk, lightweight and fibrous. These aerial portions form platforms, terraces, walking surfaces between buildings. Some of the growth is clearly very old: trunks three feet across, bark-like surfaces that flake and peel, the kind of gnarled thickness that speaks of decades at minimum. The plants have made bridges, causeways, a network of passages through what is now a three-dimensional space.

And everywhere, smaller plants fill the niches. Mosses coat every surface that spends any time above water. Floating water lilies cluster in the calmer zones between buildings, their leaves overlapping like roof tiles. Small flowering plants — bright yellow blossoms, tiny white clusters — root themselves in the accumulated debris on platforms and rooftops. Algae paints the partially submerged walls in bands of color marking the waterline's subtle variations. Mushrooms and shelf fungi extend from the trunks where the air-adapted wood begins to decompose. It's not a monoculture but an entire ecosystem structured vertically through the water column and into the air, with the drowned buildings serving as substrate and armature.

In certain buildings, the interiors have become grottos of pure plant. You can swim or walk through doorways into rooms where the vegetation has filled the space so completely that you move through a solid mass of green, parting stems and leaves like curtains. Other structures are hollow cores where the plants have lined the walls but left the center open, creating chambers of still water surrounded by living green walls that pulse slowly with filtered light.

## Water States and Qualities

The water is cool but not cold — perhaps 60 degrees Fahrenheit, comfortable for extended immersion if you're moving, a bit chill if you're still. It's fresh or nearly fresh, with just a slight mineral taste, suggesting this is rainwater and runoff collected in the basin, not seawater risen. The coolness speaks of altitude, of nights that pull heat away, of depths that shelter water from the strongest sun.

The surface is usually calm but textured. Small movements from fish and fauna create dimples and rings. The emergent vegetation breaks the surface tension in a million places, so there's no single plane of water but rather a complex boundary where liquid becomes air gradually, through layers of floating leaves and partially submerged stems. When it rains — and it rains often here, short daily showers that come from the altitude and the moisture held in all this vegetation — the surface becomes a stamped metal sheet of overlapping circles, and the sound changes from the ambient trickle-and-drip to a sustained hiss that fills the basin.

Below, the water has layers. Near the surface, it's most active — warmer, lighter, rich with oxygen and suspended particles of life. Deeper, near the old street level, it's cooler and darker, and here the water can become nearly still on calm days, holding its temperature and its secrets. You can feel the layers as you dive: warmer, cooler, warmer again where a submerged courtyard holds sun-heated water in a pool. The plants create these micro-environments, channeling currents, creating shade, releasing oxygen in visible streams of tiny bubbles that spiral upward in the light.

## Light Conditions

The light is substantial but filtered. On a clear day, full daylight reaches through the water to the old street level, though weakened and greened by its passage through water and plant. It's the quality of light you'd find in a forest, dappled and moving, but here the dappling comes from water movement rather than wind, a slower, more liquid kind of shifting shadow.

Above water, on the platforms and rooftops, the light is open but not harsh — the constant moisture in the air softens it slightly, and the larger leaves provide scattered shade. The quality suggests latitude and altitude: strong enough to support all this growth, but with a gentleness that comes from cooler climate or morning sun rather than midday desert glare.

At depth, in the interiors of buildings, the light becomes cathedral-like: shafts penetrating through doorways and windows, illuminating suspended particles, creating columns of visibility in otherwise dim spaces. These light shafts move through the day, sweeping through the drowned rooms as the sun arcs overhead, revealing first one corner then another. Some buildings face fortunate directions and hold light for hours; others only catch brief morning or evening rays that turn their interiors momentarily golden-green before darkness returns.

At night, there's moonlight and starlight enough to navigate by in the upper levels, and sometimes bioluminescence from small organisms in the water — not constant or bright, but occasional sparks and glows where something disturbs the micro-life, a ghostly blue-green trace that follows movement and then fades.

## The Fauna and Their Presence

The animals are everywhere, abundant but not overwhelming — they fill the space without crowding it, each species occupying its particular niche in the vertical habitat.

Fish are the most obvious: schools of small, silver-green fish that move through the submerged streets like flocks of birds, wheeling in unison around corners, flowing through windows. Larger, solitary fish — something like bass or carp but adapted to this environment — patrol territories, their bodies marked with patterns that have evolved to match the light-and-shadow stripes of sun through water-weed. Very large fish, the elders, lurk in the deepest chambers, rarely seen except as shadows or the sudden dispersal of smaller fish when they move through.

Birds are abundant in the aerial zone: herons and egrets that wade through the shallows at roof level, standing motionless on platforms waiting for fish to pass beneath. Cormorants dive from perches on the higher ruins, slicing down through the water column to hunt. Smaller birds — something like warblers or reed-birds — nest in the dense tangles of vegetation, and their calls create a constant background chorus. Ducks and other waterfowl paddle through the channels between buildings, upending to feed in the shallows, their tails marking the water surface like strange flowers.

Amphibians claim the boundary zones: frogs the size of your fist sit on lily pads and platforms, their territorial calls resonant and frequent, a deep rhythmic croaking that pulses through evening and morning. Smaller frogs and newts hunt insects along the waterline. Salamanders slip through the submerged moss forests on the lower walls.

Insects are everywhere the plants are, which means everywhere. Dragonflies the size of small birds helicopter through the air above the platforms, their wings catching light like stained glass. Damselflies in iridescent blues and greens perch on stems. Water striders dimple the calmer surfaces. Midges and mosquitoes form clouds — not thick enough to be maddening but present, a fact of the place. Butterflies visit the flowering plants, and various beetles work through the decomposing matter on the platforms.

Turtles bask on the logs and floating debris, slipping into water when approached. Snakes — water snakes, harmless or mostly harmless — swim through the underwater passages or sun themselves on the aerial platforms. Otters, sleek and quick, have made homes in some of the half-submerged buildings, and you hear them more often than you see them: splashes, chirps, the sounds of their play and hunting echoing through the stone chambers.

The animals create layers of sound and movement. In the water, it's mostly visual — the flash of silver, the slow glide of a large fish, the frog-kick of a turtle descending. Above, it's sound: bird calls, the splash of a diving cormorant, the croak of frogs, the buzz of insect wings. Together they make the place feel attended, inhabited, alive in a way that goes beyond the plants, though plants and animals here are so interdependent it's hard to separate them.

## Sound Character

This is a loud place, but it's a living loudness rather than a mechanical one.

The baseline is water sounds: dripping, trickling, the gentle lapping against stone walls, the hollow glug-glug of water entering and leaving semi-submerged chambers with the minimal tide or wind-push. Every platform drips. Every wall weeps. Water running off leaves, water channeling through stems, water finding its level through a thousand small passages. It's not rushing water — there are no waterfalls or rapids — but the accumulated sound of water in constant small motion.

Layered over this are the animals: the frog chorus at dawn and dusk, a rhythmic pulsing that can build to surprising volume, dozens of individuals calling and answering. Bird calls of many kinds — the harsh croak of herons, the sharp cry of diving birds, the melodic warbling of small songbirds, the quacking of waterfowl. Fish generally don't contribute sound, but large fish surfacing create significant splashes. The otters are vocal, chirping and squeaking to each other.

Insects add their portion: the hum of dragonfly wings when they pass close, the high-pitched whine of mosquitoes, the buzz and click of beetles, and in the evening, if this place has them, the electric drone of crickets or similar creatures calling from the vegetation.

The plants themselves make sound: stems creaking against stone as they grow or shift, the rustle of leaves in the minimal breeze, the occasional crack and splash when an old growth drops dead material into the water. When wind does come, it creates a complex sighing through the aerial foliage, different from forest wind because the plants are more vertical, creating a deeper, more organ-like tone.

Human or human-made sounds add their part: if there are people here — and the maintained qualities suggest there are — then footsteps on wooden platforms, the splash of swimming or wading, voices calling to each other across the water, the clunk of poles or oars against stone, the creak of ropes and boardwalks. Tools at work: cutting, building, repairing. Not industrial noise, but the sounds of hand-work, wood on wood, stone on stone.

Rain, when it comes, transforms everything: the hiss of drops hitting water surface, the patter on leaves, the deeper drum on platforms, creating a white noise that obscures other sounds but has its own rhythm and variation.

At night, the sound character changes. The diurnal birds quiet. Frogs become more vocal. Night birds and insects take over. Water sounds remain constant. The overall effect is not silence but a shift in the acoustic register, less bright, more pulsing and rhythmic.

## The Human Layer

The "urban" and "built" qualities with primitive tech suggest a settlement that exists not in a single time period but in layers, the old stone town beneath and the adapted living space above and within.

The original architecture is vernacular and durable: stone foundations and lower walls, perhaps volcanic or quarried from the plateau itself, dark grey to brown in color. Upper stories in timber frame with wattle-and-daub or brick infill. Roofs long gone or collapsed, though a few structural beams remain visible above water, now wrapped in plant growth. The buildings suggest varied use — residential structures, workshops, storehouses, perhaps a central hall or temple whose larger footprint still reads in the layout. Streets between buildings are narrow, twelve to fifteen feet wide, creating a dense network that now forms underwater canyons between walls.

The current inhabitation is adaptive and light-touch. Wooden platforms built across the rooftops and between buildings, using the emergent plant growth as support where possible, supplemented with poles driven into rubble below. Boardwalks and bridges connect the accessible areas, creating a neighborhood of maybe thirty to fifty usable buildings with open space between. The construction is simple but competent: notched joints, rope lashings, wooden pegs. Some areas have rope railings. Ladders lead down to lower platforms or directly into the water.

A few buildings have been partially roofed again, using thatch or woven reed mats laid over simple frames, creating dry shelter. These seem to be communal spaces — a gathering hall, a workshop, storage. Most of the platforms remain open to sky, covered only by the natural plant growth.

Evidence of daily life: drying racks for fish and plants, stretched between poles. Fishing nets hung for mending or storage. Woven baskets. Clay pots. A firepit on a secure stone platform, carefully maintained, the smoke rising through the open air. Simple boats tied to posts — dugout canoes or plank boats, primitive in construction but functional. Garden plots on the larger platforms, where soil has been built up and vegetables grow among the wild plants.

The technology is early but sophisticated in its simplicity: stone tools for cutting, bone hooks for fishing, wooden spears, woven fiber cordage, pottery, basketry. Some copper or bronze — a knife blade, a fishhook, an awl — but mostly stone, bone, wood, plant fiber. Nothing mechanized, nothing that requires complex manufacture. Everything that can be made here is made here; anything else is either very old or traded from elsewhere.

The arrangement suggests a community of maybe twenty to fifty people, perhaps a few family groups who have adapted to this place or returned to it after the flooding, finding in the drowned town a unique resource rather than a ruin. They live vertically, using the water as a transportation network, the platforms as living space, the submerged areas as storage and fishing ground. They've made an accommodation with the plants, pruning some areas, encouraging growth in others, understanding that the vegetation is maintaining the structures they depend on.

## The Temporal Character and Dynamics

Everything here moves slowly. This is a place in balance, in an established state that changes on the scale of seasons and years rather than hours or days.

The flooding happened long ago — decades at least, perhaps a century or more. The water level has stabilized. The plants have reached mature equilibrium, growing and dying at balanced rates. The ruins have settled into their current configurations. Erosion continues but gradually: stone slowly softening under constant moisture, brick slowly dissolving, wood slowly rotting and being replaced by plant tissue. This is not a place in catastrophic change but in long accommodation.

Daily cycles exist: sun rises, temperature increases slightly, animals shift their activity patterns, plants orient toward light. Evening comes, temperature drops, different animals emerge, some flowers close, others open. But these are gentle rhythms, not dramatic shifts. The temperature range is narrow — cool nights, mild days, rarely hot, never freezing given the water's thermal mass.

Seasonal changes are more pronounced but still moderate: wet season and dry season rather than summer and winter. The water level varies by perhaps three to five feet between seasons, exposing different bands of wall, changing which platforms are accessible, forcing adjustments in where boats can go. Plant growth pulses with the seasons — more vigorous in the wet and warm, slower in the cool and dry. Fish breeding cycles. Bird migrations or movements. But again, these are expected changes, part of the balance rather than disruptions to it.

Longer cycles play out invisibly: a building slowly weakening until one season it settles another foot into the water. A plant scaffolding taking twenty years to fully replace a collapsed roof section. The accumulation of sediment in the lowest areas, slowly raising the floor level. Trees — because some of the aerial plant growth has become tree-like — that may live fifty or a hundred years, forming the deep structure of the canopy platforms.

Human time here is tied to these natural rhythms. Daily fishing rounds. Seasonal harvests of particular plants. Annual repairs to platforms before the wet season storms. Multi-year projects of building or reconfiguration. The pace of life matches the pace of the place — patient, observant, responsive rather than driving.

Disturbances are minor and local: a storm that damages some platforms. A large fish that tears a net. A section of plant growth that dies back and must be cleared. Nothing catastrophic, nothing that fundamentally changes the character of the place. It repairs itself or is repaired, and continues.

## Atmospheric Qualities and Rules

The air is humid but not oppressive. The altitude keeps it from becoming tropical thickness. Instead it's a fresh coolness with moisture present, the kind of atmosphere that feels alive, that's comfortable against skin, that carries scents clearly.

Smells: fresh water, green plants, the earthy rot of decomposition, fish, bird guano on the rocks, wet stone, wood smoke when fires are lit, occasionally the richness of mud when the water is disturbed. These are clean smells, organic, the result of living processes rather than pollution or stagnation. The water doesn't smell swampy or foul because it's fresh, because it flows slightly, because the plants keep it oxygenated.

The atmosphere has visible moisture: mist in early morning rising from the water surface, fog banks that sometimes settle into the basin on cool nights, the slight haze of humidity that softens distant views. Light takes on quality because of this — halos around the sun, deeper colors at dawn and dusk, that particular saturation of green that only happens when there's water in the air and everywhere you look.

Physics here are normal but the water changes everything. Sound travels differently — muffled when submerged, echoing strangely between buildings above water. Movement requires different strategies: swimming, paddling, wading rather than just walking. Weight and buoyancy become constant considerations. Time feels different because travel is slower, because you can't simply walk in a straight line but must navigate three-dimensionally around obstacles, over and under and through.

The rules are biological and physical: plants grow toward light, fish swim, water finds its level, stone erodes, wood rots, life feeds on life. The place is unusual but not weird — it follows natural laws, just in an uncommon configuration. The flooding created a unique environment, and life has filled it, adapted to it, made it work.

## Boundaries and Scale

This is a small place, a distinct district or neighborhood within what might have been a larger town, but isolated now by the flooding. The accessible, inhabited area spans perhaps five hundred feet by three hundred feet — large enough for multiple buildings and complexity, small enough that you can see across most of it from a high point, small enough that voices carry, that you recognize each person and boat, that everywhere is within a few minutes' travel.

The boundaries are natural: deeper water surrounds this shallow plateau, and while you can swim or boat beyond the settled area, the buildings and accessible plant platforms end. Beyond the margins, the water deepens to fifty, a hundred feet, becoming a true lake or reservoir. The drowned town continues below in the depths, but those areas are inaccessible, too dark, too cold, beyond easy reach.

Vertically, the place spans perhaps forty to fifty feet from the deepest building floors to the highest platforms and treetops. This creates a compressed but complex three-dimensional habitat, everything reachable, everything negotiable with the tools available — breath-hold diving, climbing, walking, swimming.

The density is sparse in the sense that there's space between things. Buildings don't connect in solid blocks; there are gaps, channels, open water courts between structures. Platforms don't fill every available surface; there are areas of just plants, just water, just broken stone. The fauna is abundant but not crowded — you see animals frequently but they have room, territories, space to move. Human structures are scattered, placed where they're needed rather than filling every possible location.

This sparseness gives the place a quality of balance, of not being overwhelmed by any single element. There's room to move, to see, to notice details. The abundance of life exists within spaciousness, creating richness without claustrophobia.

## Details Emerging

As the image settles, certain specifics become clear:

A heron stands on a half-submerged wall, one leg tucked, completely still except for the slight sway as water laps the stone. Its shadow on the underwater wall moves more than the bird does.

Inside a building, visible through a glassless window, the interior is a vertical garden: stems rising from the flooded floor, leaves layering the water column, small fish suspended among them like ornaments, and at the top where the old ceiling would have been, the plants have formed a living roof, leaves spreading horizontally to catch the light that comes through the empty window frames.

A bridge between two platforms sags slightly in the middle, built from a single log that still has bark on parts of it, with rope railings on either side. The ropes are fuzzy with green algae growth where they pass through the splash zone.

On a larger platform, someone has built a drying rack: poles supporting a woven mat, and spread across the mat are dozens of small fish, butterflied and laid flat, silver and pink in the light, flies occasionally landing and being waved away by a person sitting nearby weaving a basket.

Below, in the submerged street, a large fish moves slowly past, dark green on top, pale beneath, perhaps three feet long. Small fish scatter ahead of it. It seems unconcerned by human presence above, following its own business through the underwater canyon.

A lily pad cluster fills the space between two buildings, the round leaves overlapping like scales, each one slightly different in size and shade of green, some with ragged edges where something has eaten them. A turtle hauls itself onto the cluster, water streaming from its shell, and settles to bask.

The aerial plant growth in one area has developed thick, woody trunks wrapped around the stone walls of a building, and someone has cut steps into the trunks, notches for feet, creating a living ladder that you can climb to reach the roof platform.

Rain begins: first a few drops that make rings on the water surface, then a steady patter that builds to a sustained fall. People move under the thatched shelters. Birds shake their feathers and continue fishing. The frogs begin calling immediately, as if rain were a signal. The sound of rain on leaves, rain on water, rain on platforms creates a layered hiss-and-drum that fills the basin, and the air takes on the sharp green smell of rain on plants.

In the evening, as light fails, small cooking fires appear on several platforms — orange lights reflected in the water, smoke rising straight up in the still air. Voices carry across the water, a call and response between platforms. Somewhere, someone is singing, a rhythmic melody that fits the sound of the place, that rises and falls with the frog calls and the evening birds.

At night, under stars, the water becomes black except where moonlight catches it silver. The platforms are dark shapes. The plants are shadows. Bioluminescence traces a path where something large moves through deep water, a ghost-light streak that fades behind it. The frogs reach their evening crescendo, then slowly fall quiet. The place settles into its night configuration, still alive, still sounding, but slower, darker, deeper.

---

This is the Hanging Gardens of Stillwater: a drowned town become a vertical forest, a small plateau under twenty feet of fresh water, where stone and plant have merged into hybrid architecture, where people live on platforms among the greenery, where fish swim through old streets and birds nest in the walls, where everything is cool and moist and slow, where life is abundant but spacious, where the sound of water and animals fills the air, where light filters green through plant and water, where the old world below supports the new world above, and where balance has been found between what was and what is.
