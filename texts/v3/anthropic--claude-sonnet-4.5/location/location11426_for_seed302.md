# The Shatter-Fields of Tesh Qamar

High on the shoulder of the world, where the air thins to a brightness that seems to polish the very atmosphere, there stretches a vast plateau of broken crystal — not gemstone, but something between mineral and glass, as if the mountain's ancient bones had once been liquid and cooled into transparency. The plateau extends for perhaps thirty kilometers in its longest dimension, a basin cupped between peaks that rise even higher, their upper reaches lost in the pure assault of altitude light.

The crystal is everywhere. It forms the ground itself — not in smooth planes but in a dense, chaotic jumble of angular fragments, none larger than a child's torso, most the size of fists or fingers. They lie packed together with almost no space between, creating a surface that is simultaneously solid and fractured, a pavement of a million shattered pieces that have settled into an accommodation with gravity and each other over uncountable years. The fragments are primarily clear, or nearly so, though some hold subtle colorations — pale amber, smoke-grey, the faintest blue of distant ice. They have been here long enough that their edges, while still angular, have lost the knife-sharpness of fresh breakage. Time has gentled them into a rougher texture, faces slightly pitted, corners fractionally rounded.

Water moves through this crystal desert in a way that seems almost impossible given the density of the mineral pack. It rises from below — the plateau sits atop some deep aquifer or network of seeps that finds its way upward through invisible channels and microscopic spaces. In the cool air, particularly in what passes for morning here (though the light changes little), moisture condenses on every crystal surface. Each of the countless millions of fragments becomes slicked with a thin film of water, and the entire plateau surface gleams as if freshly washed. Small pools collect in the rare depressions where fragments have settled into shallow bowls, never more than a hand's-width deep, never quite evaporating even in the day's brightest hours. The water is extraordinarily pure — filtered through kilometers of crystalline matrix until it carries almost nothing but itself.

The sound is what overwhelms first-time visitors, if visitors this place ever had. 

The wind here never stops. At this elevation, in this configuration of peaks and valleys, something in the shape of the land creates a perpetual atmospheric current. Not a gale — the wind speed is not particularly violent — but constant, tireless, moving perhaps at the pace of a person walking briskly. And when this wind moves across the densely-packed crystal field, across billions of angled surfaces and tiny gaps and minute channels, it generates a sound that is less like wind and more like the plateau itself is singing, screaming, speaking in a language of pure tone.

It is deafening in the truest sense — not painfully loud in most places, but so pervasive, so layered, so complex and unceasing that it fills every possible space sound can occupy. High frequencies keen and whistle where air moves across sharp edges. Low frequencies hum and moan from larger resonant chambers in the crystal pack. Mid-tones warble and oscillate as wind pressures shift microscopically. The sound has harmonics and disharmonies, moments that almost resolve into chords before scattering again into noise. In some areas where the crystal fragments lie at particular angles, the sound intensifies into focused beams of pure tone that can be physically felt as much as heard. In other zones, frequencies cancel and reinforce in patterns that create rhythmic pulses, though the rhythm never quite settles into predictability.

There is no silence here. There is no possibility of silence. Even in the nominal shelter of the rare boulder-sized crystal formations that rise above the general surface, the sound permeates everything. It enters through bone conduction, through the chest cavity, through the skull itself. Those hypothetical visitors would need to speak in signs, though even thought becomes difficult in the sonic saturation.

The light completes the assault on the senses. At this altitude, with so little atmosphere to soften it, sunlight arrives with an intensity that seems almost malevolent. The sky is not the comfortable blue of lower elevations but a darker, harder blue that hints at the void beyond. And this fierce light strikes the crystal field and multiplies. Every surface reflects, refracts, scatters the light into new trajectories. The entire plateau becomes a field of stars, of blinding points and gleaming lines, of caustics that crawl across surfaces as the sun moves through its arc. The moisture film intensifies the effect — water and crystal and light entering into a collaboration that produces brilliance bordering on intolerable.

Shadows here are strange things, multiple and conflicting. A single piece of crystal might cast five different shadows at five different angles, in five different intensities, as light finds different paths through and around it. The shadows are never dark — reflected light ensures that — but rather zones of relative dimness, blue-grey havens in the blaze. Looking across the plateau is like looking into a field of broken mirrors, of shattered prisms, of light made solid and then smashed into infinite pieces.

The temperature hovers in the cool range, never warm despite the intense sunlight, never cold enough for frost despite the altitude. The thin air cannot hold much heat, and what warmth the sun provides dissipates quickly. The crystal field itself seems to maintain a temperature slightly below comfort, neither ice-cold nor pleasantly cool, but persistently drawing heat away from anything that touches it. The water pools, surprisingly, never freeze — perhaps there is something in the water's chemistry, some mineral content just sufficient to depress the freezing point, or perhaps the constant percolation from below brings up water at a temperature that keeps the system just above freezing's threshold.

The plateau exists in a kind of equilibrium that feels ancient beyond reckoning. The crystal does not grow here — whatever process created it finished long ago. The fragments do not noticeably break down further; erosion works on them but at a pace so glacial as to be effectively static on human timescales. Water comes up from below in amounts that almost exactly match what evaporates from the surface and what little trickles away at the plateau's distant edges. The wind does not vary in its essential character, though it may gust or slacken fractionally. The sun traces the same paths season after season, producing the same fields of light and shadow in endless repetition. Nothing is happening here, in the sense that nothing is changing. Everything is happening here, in the sense that the wind and light and water-movement never cease.

Life clings to this place with the most marginal of grips. There is no vegetation — how could roots penetrate the crystal pack, and what would feed such life in any case? The stone contains no nutrients; the water carries nothing to nourish. But there is motion beyond wind and water. Occasionally — perhaps once an hour, perhaps once a day, patterns differ — something moves among the crystals.

They are difficult to describe, these creatures, if creatures they are. Most often they appear as disturbances in the light-patterns, zones where the reflections don't quite resolve as they should. Sometimes they seem more substantial: pale, segmented things with too many legs, transparent enough that they nearly vanish against the crystal background, moving with a careful, articulated delicacy as if afraid to disturb the eternal balance. They might be insects, though that word feels wrong. They might be something else entirely, something that has evolved or emerged in this weird high place where ordinary life cannot reach. They make no sound that can be distinguished from the wind's constant symphony. They do not appear to eat, or if they do, they consume something invisible — perhaps mineral traces in the water films, perhaps something even more minimal.

Occasionally, something larger moves across the plateau. A shape the size of a dog, perhaps, or a large bird, though it moves on too many legs and its body is the same translucent, crystalline-textured material as the smaller creatures. It picks its way across the packed crystal with what seems like immense care, never disturbing the arrangement of fragments. These larger presences are rare — a person standing watch might see one every few days. They appear at the plateau's edges, cross to the other side over hours or days, and disappear again into whatever landscape lies beyond the crystal fields. Whether they are hunting, migrating, or engaged in some activity beyond these categories is impossible to determine. They leave no trace. The crystal fragments they pass over show no sign of displacement.

The weirdness here is not in any single dramatic feature but in the accumulation of strangeness, the way familiar elements combine into something that sits just outside the comfortable. Water, stone, light, wind — all normal things. But here they achieve a configuration that feels alien without being fantastical, unsettling without being impossible. The transparency of everything creates a visual confusion where surfaces are uncertain, where distance becomes difficult to judge because the eye cannot find clear boundaries. Walking across the plateau would require constant attention to footing, as what appears solid might be a shallow pool, and what looks like water might be perfectly dry crystal.

The fragments themselves, examined closely, show no obvious origin. They are not shattered versions of something clearly whole — no one could fit these pieces together and reconstruct the original form. It's as if they were always fragments, as if they precipitated into their broken state directly, without a prior wholeness to have lost. Some faces are flat, some concave, some convex. Some fragments are simple geometric shapes — rough tetrahedrons, partial cubes. Others have more complex, irregular forms. But none show the kind of surface markings that would suggest they were once part of a larger crystalline structure with cleavage planes and growth patterns.

At what might be called the plateau's center, though it has no clear geometric center, there is a zone where the fragments are slightly larger on average, and where several massive formations rise from the general surface. These are not spires or columns but irregular masses, baroque accumulations of crystal that might have formed in place or might have been exposed by some ancient removal of surrounding material. They reach perhaps three meters at their highest points. Around and through these formations, the water is more present — not pools now but actual small streams that emerge from gaps in the crystal pack, flow for a few meters across the surface, and disappear again into other gaps. The sound here is different too — the wind-song combines with water-sounds, trickles and drips and the peculiar hollow tone of water falling into hidden chambers.

This is where the creatures seem most active, or at least most visible. The distortions in the light gather here. Occasionally, one of the translucent things will pause at a water stream and lower its front portion into the flow, remaining motionless for long minutes. The larger forms seem to avoid this central zone, or perhaps they pass through it at times when observation would be unlikely.

The edges of the plateau are gradual transitions rather than cliff-edges. The crystal fragments become less densely packed, with actual gaps visible between them. Other minerals begin to appear — darker stones, finally some soil in thin pockets. The sound begins to lessen, or rather begins to compete with other sounds as the wind finds surfaces that don't sing. The light becomes merely bright rather than overwhelming. These transition zones might extend for a hundred meters or more, fringe territories where the crystal plateau grades into whatever surrounds it — presumably actual mountains, actual rock faces and scree slopes, though that is beyond the scope of this description.

Looking outward from the plateau's interior, the surrounding peaks create a distant horizon of snow and dark stone. They are higher still, some perhaps by another thousand meters of elevation. They seem to provide wind-blocks at certain angles, though the wind across the plateau itself never stops. Perhaps there are passes, gaps in the mountain ring, that channel the wind through. Perhaps the entire plateau is itself a kind of massive wind instrument, shaped over geological time into a form that produces this constant tone-field.

Time moves strangely here, not in any magical sense but in the psychological effect of the unchanging. With no sun movement clearly visible in the glare, with the sound never varying in its essential quality, with no reference points that shift or alter, minutes and hours lose their distinctiveness. The water films evaporate and reform in cycles, but the cycles are too subtle to track without instruments. The light has a quality at dawn and dusk that differs from midday, but the differences are minimal at this elevation where the atmosphere provides so little filtering. A person alone here would lose time sense quickly, might stand watching the light-fields play across crystal for what feels like minutes and discover hours have passed.

There is no shelter. No caves present themselves in the crystal pack — the fragments are too small and too densely arranged to leave habitable voids. The large formations in the center area have gaps, but they are too small and too shaped by the random crystal geometries to provide real refuge from wind or light. To survive here would require brought structures, tents or artificial breaks, and the complete absence of building in the landscape suggests no one has ever chosen to stay. This is a place to cross, perhaps, or to visit briefly from more hospitable bases, but not to inhabit.

Yet it is inhabited by those translucent creatures, suggesting either that they require very little — perhaps only the water and some trace mineral, perhaps only the particular quality of light at this elevation — or that they are not quite biological in the usual sense. Their transparency makes them almost non-present even when visible, as if they occupy the borderland between creature and phenomenon.

The plateau's stability is its most defining feature. Nothing is building here, nothing is destroying. The crystal doesn't grow or break at any rate that would be noticed in a human lifetime or a hundred lifetimes. The water seepage neither increases nor decreases. The wind pattern, surely established by the mountain configuration, will remain until those mountains themselves erode into new shapes millennia hence. Even the creatures seem part of the equilibrium rather than agents of change — they neither consume the landscape nor modify it, but rather exist within its constraints as perfectly adapted components of a system that does not alter.

Walking across the plateau would be an exercise in sensory endurance. The footing would be treacherous despite appearing solid — millions of separate fragments means millions of potential instabilities, places where weight might cause unexpected shifts, where a turned ankle becomes likely with every step. The sound would make communication impossible and thought difficult, a physical presence pressing on the mind as much as the ears. The light would force eyes to slits, would create a constant need to look away, look down, find some relief from the relentless brilliance. The cool air would leach away body heat through exposure, while the thin atmosphere would make every exertion feel insufficient, leave lungs hungry for more oxygen than exists.

And yet the plateau possesses a terrible beauty. The light-fields are genuinely magnificent, patterns of caustics and reflections that shift with every slight movement of the viewer's position. The sound, despite its overwhelming volume, has a kind of complex musicality, moments where harmonics align into something almost intentional. The transparency of everything creates an aesthetic of dematerialization, as if the landscape is attempting to achieve invisibility, to become pure light and air with only the minimum of substance necessary to give those elements a place to interact.

In certain light conditions — perhaps at the exact moment of sunrise, or in the deep evening when the sun approaches the horizon — the entire plateau might briefly become something transcendent. All those millions of crystal fragments, all that water-film, all that particular angle of light combining to create an effect that would burn itself into memory, that would seem worth every difficulty of reaching this high, harsh place. A moment of alignment where the crystal field becomes a single instrument, where the light resolves into something that feels meaningful even if no meaning can be articulated.

But such moments, if they occur, would be brief. The plateau would return to its equilibrium state: bright, loud, cool, packed with crystal and crossed by wind. Essentially unchanged since whatever ancient event created it. Essentially unchanging until whatever future event unmakes it. A permanent present tense, a landscape that exists in a kind of mineral eternity.

The translucent creatures pick their way across the fragments, leaving no trails. The water seeps up and evaporates. The wind sings its endless, complex note. The light multiplies itself into a field of brightness that approaches the unbearable. And the plateau remains, settled into its own being, asking nothing of the world but to be left alone to continue its peculiar existence at the elevation where air grows thin and the sky begins its change from atmosphere to void.
