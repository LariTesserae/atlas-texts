# The Drowned Bells

The water here is not sea but sky made liquid — a cold, weightless suspension where clouds have fallen and remain, never quite deciding between states. You move through it as if through dense fog, but you are fully submerged, breathing something that is neither water nor air but the compromise between them. The temperature hovers just above freezing, cold enough that your breath — if it could be distinguished from the surrounding medium — would fog, would become one more wisp in the already-thick particulate drift.

This is a highland plateau, drowned. The elevation persists in the geography even if the atmosphere has transformed: you are perhaps two thousand meters above what was once sea level, but the distinction has lost most of its meaning. The plateau stretches for kilometers in all directions, a broad basin ringed by suggestions of higher peaks that fade into the grey-green murk at the limits of vision. The scale is that of a small region, a country of interconnected valleys and rises, large enough that you could walk — or drift — for days and still be within its particular conditions.

The stone beneath shows its age. Metamorphic rock, schist and gneiss, folded and refolded until the layers run in sweeping curves like frozen music. Erosion has had centuries here, perhaps longer, but the weathering is strange — softened by the liquid medium, the rocks have rounded edges and dimpled surfaces where slower currents have worked at them. They are not sharp-edged and young, nor are they the ancient, nearly-dissolved bones of truly old mountains. They are settled, established, at equilibrium with the forces that shape them. Some formations rise fifteen or twenty meters, sculptured into asymmetrical pillars and arches. Most of the plateau sits at a steady grade, gently undulating, neither dramatic nor flat.

## The Forest That Remains

Vegetation grows here with an insistence that borders on defiance. Not kelp — this is not ocean — but something stranger: trees that were, trees that have adapted, trees that now exist in a form that questions the category. Their trunks are thick and fibrous, bark softened into something more like sodden paper, layered and peeling. The wood inside has transformed, become something between plant matter and coral, shot through with chambers that once held air and now hold the liquid atmosphere. They grow slowly, these trees, but they do grow.

The canopy — if it can be called that — spreads twenty to thirty meters above the plateau floor, branches extending horizontally before gently rising, as if still remembering which way was up when up meant something different. The branches are widely spaced, each tree giving its neighbors room, and between them stretches nothing but the grey-green medium, that thick water-air, that liquid sky.

Leaves persist, but transformed. They are thick and rubbery, dark green verging on black, and they move constantly in currents too subtle for you to feel directly. Some species have leaves the size of dinner plates, slow-waving like the ears of enormous, patient elephants. Others grow fine, feather-like fronds that shimmer and blur in the flow. The vegetation is dense where it exists, but it exists in clusters, in groves separated by stretches of bare rock or expanses of low, mat-like growth — something like moss but more robust, forming carpets of deep green and occasional rust-red across the stone.

In clearings and along the edges of the groves, other plants have emerged: tube-like structures that rise from the substrate, some thin as fingers, others thick as your torso, reaching upward two or three meters before opening in cups and funnels. They are a pale, translucent grey-green, and they pulse. Slowly, rhythmically, they contract and expand, drawing the liquid medium through their interiors. This is not respiration as terrestrial plants know it — this is something else, some mechanism for extracting what they need from this cold, thick atmosphere.

## The Sound of Bells

The sound is what defines this place as much as the water or the cold.

It is loud, not deafeningly so, but constant and pervasive, a presence that presses against you from all directions. The water conducts sound strangely, flattens it and stretches it, so that everything seems to come from nearby and far away simultaneously.

The primary sound is the bells. Scattered across the plateau, hanging from the adapted trees, suspended on corroded frames of what was once metal — iron or bronze, now crusted with accretions, softened at the edges — hundreds of bells ring in the currents. They are of all sizes: small ones that produce high, crystalline notes, large ones the size of a person that intone deep, resonant gongs. They do not ring randomly. The currents that move through this place have their own rhythms, their own patterns, and the bells respond, creating a music that is almost structured, almost composed, but never quite resolves into melody.

The currents themselves make sound: a constant, low rushing, like wind through narrow spaces, like water over stones. But thicker, more textured. You can hear the particulate matter in the medium, the suspended sediment, the spores and fragments and microscopic debris all hissing and whispering as they move.

The trees creak. Their wood, softer than it was, presses against itself, and the branches sway and rub, producing groans and sighs that echo through the medium. The tube-plants add their own contribution: as they pulse, they create small pressure waves, subtle bass notes below the level of easy hearing but felt in the chest, in the bones.

And there are sounds with no clear source: knockings and tappings, distant impacts, as if somewhere always just beyond sight, something is falling, something is striking stone. Occasionally — perhaps once an hour, perhaps more frequently — there is a deep thrumming, a vibration that seems to come from the plateau itself, from the rock, as if the mountain is adjusting its weight, settling itself.

## The Architecture of Aftermath

The built environment is sparse but present, scattered across the plateau in a way that suggests this was once more populated, or differently populated, or that those who built here did not build densely.

There are towers — or what remain of towers. Simple stone construction, using the local schist stacked without mortar but with considerable skill. Most have collapsed, leaving only the foundations and perhaps a few courses of stone, corners jutting from the mat-plants that have overgrown them. But some still stand, rising ten or fifteen meters, open to the medium, windows empty, interiors filled with drifting sediment. The stone has weathered, edges softened, but the structures have achieved a kind of stability. They are not actively crumbling; they have crumbled to the degree they will crumble, and now they persist.

Attached to these towers, strung between them, hanging from outcroppings and from the stronger trees, are frameworks of metal. This is where most of the bells hang. The metal itself is simple iron or bronze, worked with hand tools, forged and hammered, jointed with pins and crude welds. It has corroded extensively — the iron blooming into rust formations that themselves have become structural, the bronze developing thick patinas of green and blue that blur into the surrounding colors. But the frameworks hold. They flex in the currents, they sway, but they do not break.

On some of the flatter stretches of stone, there are arrangements of smaller stones, walls that never rose higher than waist-height, enclosing spaces that might have been dwellings, workshops, pens for animals that no longer exist. These are half-buried now, overgrown with the mat-plants, or in some cases, swallowed by formations of the tube-plants, which seem to have no regard for the distinction between natural and built substrate.

In one location — a slight valley between two rises of the plateau — there is something larger: the suggestion of a plaza or gathering space, stone paving still visible beneath a thin layer of sediment. Around its edges, more towers, more bell-frameworks, and in the center, a larger bell than any other, mounted in a heavy frame. This bell is silent. It is so large that the currents cannot move it, and whatever clapper it once had is gone. It stands mute, a dark shape two meters tall, surrounded by the ringing of its smaller kin.

Scattered everywhere, barely visible unless you are looking for them, are smaller artifacts: hand tools, simple iron implements half-dissolved, ceramic vessels mostly shattered, their pieces rounded by erosion. The technology these represent is basic — farming tools, construction tools, vessels for carrying and storing. No mechanisms, no complexity beyond the lever and the wedge. Whoever built here built with their hands and simple metal.

## The Quality of Light

Darkness is too strong a word; dimness is more accurate, but even that doesn't capture it. The light here is diffuse, sourceless, as if the medium itself glows very faintly, or as if light filters through it from somewhere very far away.

There is a direction that is brighter — call it up, for lack of better terminology — and a direction that is darker. The gradient is gentle. At the level of the plateau floor, visibility extends perhaps thirty meters in the clearer areas, less in the denser groves or where sediment concentrations are higher. Everything is seen through a faint greenish haze, a tint that makes judging distances difficult.

The light does not change dramatically, but it changes. Over what might be hours — it's hard to track time in this place — the dimness deepens slightly, and the green tint shifts toward grey. The bells continue regardless. Then, gradually, the light increases, the green returns. There is no sun visible, no moon, no stars. Just the slow cycling of slightly-less-dim and slightly-more-dim, a rhythm that might be day and night, might be something else entirely.

Occasionally, there are flashes — brief, distant illuminations from the direction of brighter, as if lightning is occurring somewhere above the liquid sky. But no thunder follows, or if it does, it is indistinguishable from the general acoustic chaos of currents and bells.

Some of the tube-plants produce their own light. In the darker periods, they glow very faintly from within, a pale blue-white luminescence that barely penetrates their translucent walls. It is not enough to see by, but it is enough to mark their locations, to create fields of dim stars across the plateau floor.

## The Living System

There are no animals. No fish adapted to this medium, no swimming creatures, no crawling things on the rocks. The ecosystem consists entirely of the plants and whatever microscopic life must surely exist to support them.

Yet the place is not still. It is active in ways that suggest a complete system, a cycle, processes turning over.

The currents are the primary dynamic force. They move in patterns: broad, slow circulations around the major rock formations, tidal-like pulses that seem to correspond to the light cycles, and unpredictable surges that arrive without warning, setting all the bells ringing frantically for a few minutes before subsiding. The currents carry sediment, carry spores, carry dissolved nutrients, carry waste products. They are the bloodstream of this place.

The tube-plants pulse, as mentioned, but in aggregate, their pulsing creates secondary currents, small local circulations. Where they grow densely, they can shift the flow patterns, create eddies and slow zones.

The trees shed. Not leaves, usually, but pieces of bark, small branches, fragments of that coral-like wood. These drift down slowly, accumulating in depressions in the rock, where they break down, where the mat-plants and smaller growths can access their nutrients. The mat-plants themselves grow in waves — one area will be lush and full, then gradually thin, then begin recovering, while adjacent areas go through their own cycles.

There is a respiration to the plateau itself. Periodically — every few days, it seems — the medium becomes more turbulent. Currents strengthen, the bells ring more insistently, sediment churns up from the substrate. The tube-plants pulse faster. This lasts for what feels like half a day, then subsides. In the aftermath, the water — the air — the medium is clearer, cleaner, and the plants seem more vigorous.

Occasionally, something rises from below. Bubbles — but not air bubbles. Pockets of some other fluid, lighter and less viscous, silvery, wobbling upward through the green-grey medium until they reach some threshold and dissipate, their contents diffusing. They emerge from cracks in the rock, from vents scattered across the plateau. They are more common near the foundations of the old structures, as if something about the stonework or the disturbance of building has created pathways for them.

And there is the phenomenon of the chimes. At irregular intervals — sometimes twice in what seems like a day, sometimes not for what feels like weeks — all the bells fall silent at once. The currents do not stop, but they shift, moving in a different mode that does not set the bells ringing. The sudden quiet is profound, oppressive. It lasts for minutes, sometimes a quarter-hour. In this silence, you can hear things you didn't know were there: subtle clickings from the mat-plants, a high-pitched whine from the tube-plants, distant groaning from deep in the rock. Then, all at once, a current surges from a new direction, and the bells resume, often in a different tonal pattern than before.

## The Strangeness

The weirdness of this place is not in singular impossible objects but in the accumulation of wrong details, in the way familiar elements combine into something that should not be.

Trees should not grow underwater. But these are not quite underwater, and they are not quite trees anymore. The medium should be water or air, but it refuses to choose. You should not be able to breathe it, but you can, and you do not question this because the alternative is unthinkable, so your mind accepts it and moves on, even as some part of you recognizes the impossibility.

The bells should corrode and fail, should have failed centuries ago. But they persist, ring on, as if the act of ringing protects them, as if sound itself has become structural.

Time feels negotiable here. How long have you been drifting through the groves? How long since the last light-cycle? The absence of animals removes one of the usual ways of marking time's passage — no circadian rhythms of birds or insects, no patterns of feeding or sleeping except your own. The bells ring constantly, which should provide a rhythm, but they are always changing, never quite repeating, so they become more like a noise that cancels rhythm rather than establishes it.

There are moments when the medium seems to thicken, when moving through it becomes more effortful, and then moments when it thins, when you drift too easily, when stopping becomes difficult. These changes have no apparent cause.

In certain locations — usually near the old structures, but not always — there are zones where the direction of down becomes ambiguous. The rock is still below you, the brightness still generally above, but your inner ear insists that down is at an angle, that you are standing on a slope even when the stone is level. The feeling passes if you move away, returns if you come back.

The largest bell, the silent one in the plaza, has an interior surface that does not match its exterior. From outside, it appears to be perhaps two meters tall and proportionately wide. But if you drift beneath it and look up into its interior, the space seems larger, the curve of the metal receding farther than it should. It is possible to enter it fully, to curl up inside it, and the sense of enclosure is wrong — too much space, not enough metal between you and the outside. No sound penetrates from outside when you are within, but you can hear something else: a very deep, very slow vibration, a note below the threshold of hearing, felt rather than heard, a pulse that comes once every few minutes.

Some of the trees, when you examine them closely, have rings in their wood — growth rings. But they are too close together, too many of them, suggesting the trees are far older than their size would indicate, or that seasons here are far shorter, or that the rings mean something else entirely.

The spores or seeds that drift through the medium — visible as occasional motes in the dim light — sometimes move against the currents. Not often, and not far, but enough to notice if you watch long enough. A small spiral trajectory that cannot be accounted for by flow dynamics, a brief acceleration toward a tube-plant, then resumption of normal drift.

## The Experience of Being

To be in this place is to be suspended in more than the physical sense. You drift more than you walk, even when your feet touch stone. Movement is slow, considered, half-swimming and half-climbing through the thick medium.

The cold is constant but not unbearable. It makes you slow, makes you want to curl up in protected spaces — inside the bell, against the leeward side of a tower, within a dense grove of trees where the mat-plants create a marginally warmer microclimate. But it does not kill, does not even threaten. It is simply the background condition, the neutral state.

The absence of animals creates an absence of certain fears and certain comforts. Nothing will hunt you, but nothing will share this space with you in a way you can relate to. The plants are alive, are active, but they do not regard you. You are neither prey nor threat nor companion to them, merely another object in the medium, another obstacle to grow around or through.

The sound makes silence impossible but also makes specific sounds difficult to hold onto. You cannot have a quiet thought here; the bells intrude. But neither can you focus on any single bell's voice; they blend, they chorus, they create a texture rather than a music. After a time — hours? days? — you stop hearing them as individual sounds and they become like the temperature, like the dimness: a condition of the place rather than an event within it.

Hunger and thirst become strange concepts. The medium provides something, or the plants do, or the very act of breathing the thick water-air sustains in ways normal breathing does not. You can eat the tube-plant flesh if you wish — it is bitter, fibrous, but edible. You can scrape mat-plant growth from rocks and consume it. But the urgency of these needs is muted, stretched out, as if metabolism itself has slowed to match the pace of the place.

Sleep comes in the darker periods, curled in sheltered spots, but dreams are vivid and often indistinguishable from waking perception. You dream of bells and wake to bells. You dream of drifting through groves and wake to find you have drifted, that you are not where you thought you were when you closed your eyes.

## Hypothesis and History

You develop theories about this place because theories are what minds do when confronted with strangeness.

Perhaps there was a flood, not of water but of something else, something that changed the air itself. The elevation suggests this was once above whatever catastrophe transformed the lowlands. Perhaps the people who built here thought they were safe, thought altitude would protect them. But the change reached even here, and they had to choose: adapt or leave. The bells suggest ceremony, suggest they stayed long enough to create culture in the new conditions, then vanished in ways that left their artifacts intact.

Or perhaps the medium has always been here, and the towers and bells are far older than they appear, persisting in this preserving fluid for centuries or millennia, and the "weathering" is not erosion but accretion, a slow building up of mineral deposits and corrosion products that makes everything seem more recent than it is.

Or perhaps this is not Earth at all, not anywhere related to familiar biology or physics, and the tree-shapes and bell-shapes are convergent forms, structures that emerge in any world where certain conditions apply, and the strangeness you perceive is only strangeness from a human perspective, while to something native here, this would be the natural order.

The tubes-plants trouble the last theory. They seem too purposeful, too much like designed irrigation or filtration systems. But designed by what? For what purpose? They connect to nothing, serve no visible function except their own growth. Unless their function is the medium itself, unless they are maintaining the conditions that allow everything else here to exist, including themselves, a self-stabilizing system that has found an equilibrium and holds it.

## Deeper In

The plateau extends farther than you have explored. In the direction of less-bright, the land rises slightly, and the vegetation becomes sparser, the groves smaller and more widely separated. The bells continue, but they are fewer, their tones deeper, as if only the larger ones survive the harsher conditions of higher altitude.

In the direction of more-bright, the plateau breaks into a series of stepped terraces, descending gradually. Here the vegetation is denser, the tube-plants growing in tight clusters, some of them branching, connecting their tubes into networks. The terraces were carved, you think — too regular to be entirely natural — and there are remnants of stone channels, waterways that once directed flow but now simply interrupt it, creating local turbulences.

To what might be called north, there is a section of the plateau where the rock has failed, where a bowl-shaped depression has formed, fifty meters across and perhaps ten deep. It is filled with a denser growth of the feather-fronded trees, a submerged grove so thick that the light barely penetrates. The bells that hang here are all small, creating a higher, more frantic music. Something about this place feels active, feels like a heart or a lung, though it is no more animated than anywhere else.

To the opposite direction, the plateau extends to a cliff edge — the boundary of the highland, where the stone drops away into deeper dimness. You cannot see the bottom; visibility fades to grey-green long before any floor becomes apparent. A few bell-frameworks extend out over this edge, their bells hanging in the emptiness, ringing in the updrafts that seem to rise from the depths.

Standing at this edge, looking out into the unseen below, you can hear a different quality to the sound. The bells here are joined by echoes, by reverberations from the cliff face, by harmonics that seem to come from the void itself. It is the loudest place on the plateau, a wall of sound that is almost tangible, and it draws you forward, makes you want to step out, to drift down into whatever is beneath. 

But not yet. The plateau still has places unexplored, bells unheard, groves unvisited. The medium holds you suspended in all ways — in space, in time, in decision. You can move when you choose, but choosing is difficult when all directions seem equally valid, equally strange, equally settled into their wrongness.

So you drift, you listen, you touch the cold stone and the soft bark, you breathe the impossible medium, and you let the bells mark time or fail to mark it, and you exist in this place that has found its balance between water and air, between life and emptiness, between sound and silence that can never quite arrive.
