# The Plateau City of Teln Avaar

The city sits upon a limestone shelf that thrust upward from the surrounding hills some few thousand years ago—recent enough that the edges remain knife-sharp, the escarpments still vertical where they haven't been carved by intention. The plateau's surface is perhaps three miles across at its widest point, shaped like a hand with fingers extended northeast. From below, the white-gray cliffs rise three hundred feet, their stratified layers clean-cut and geometric, showing the bones of the earth with unusual clarity.

What strikes you first is the *sparseness*—not of life, but of how life arranges itself. The city doesn't sprawl. Buildings stand separate from one another, connected by packed earth paths that wind with apparent randomness until you realize they're following the slight depressions where water once pooled, channels that still collect the mist-rain when it comes. Between structures lie gardens, but not dense ones. A fruit tree here, its branches guided along a wooden frame. A plot of herbs there, stone-edged. Patches of wild grasses. Empty courtyards with a single well. The architecture breathes.

The buildings themselves are limestone block—quarried from the plateau's own flesh, you can see the geometric wounds where sections of cliff-face were removed, clean-cut and recent, the stone flesh-pink where newly exposed, weathering to dove-gray. Structures are built two or three stories high, flat-roofed, with walls that taper slightly inward as they rise. No mortar between stones; instead, each block is cut so precisely it seats into place with only friction, the joints nearly invisible. Door and window frames are dark wood—juniper, brought up from the valleys below—fitted into spaces left during construction.

The cool air settles here differently than in the lowlands. At this elevation, temperature holds steady in a narrow band—cool enough for woolen layers, never quite warm even in full sun, never truly cold. The air has a weight to it, a presence. You become aware of your breath as visible phenomenon on certain mornings, how it hangs briefly before dissipating.

Water comes in particular ways. Not rain, exactly, but something between rain and fog—what the people here call *vesh*, a fine moisture that drifts in on the prevailing wind from the east, where distant lakes sit in bowl-shaped valleys. The vesh arrives in the early morning hours, always the early morning hours, and by mid-afternoon has evaporated or soaked into the stone. During vesh-time, sound dampens. Footsteps become intimate. Voices carry only a few yards. The limestone darkens to slate-blue.

Every building has a cistern—some carved into the foundation, others built as separate structures of cemented stone. From the roof corners, channels guide the vesh-water downward through a system of copper gutters (the only metal used with any regularity, worked from ore found in fissures along the plateau's southern edge). The gutters have that green patina age gives copper, but the underlying metal shows red-bright where joints have been recently repaired. You hear water trickling into cisterns during vesh-time, a distributed sound across the city, dozens of small streams finding their vessels.

The vegetation grows in a manner that seems, at first, like underachievement—until you recognize it as abundance carefully distributed. Along the cistern walls, moss establishes itself in thick pelts, kept deliberately, harvested in sections for medicinal uses and then left to regrow. In the gardens between buildings: chamomile, thyme, sage, mugwort, establishing themselves in soil that's half limestone grit, half composted matter brought up in baskets from the valleys. The herb-smell mixes with the mineral scent of wet stone.

Trees are positioned like punctuation. A cedar at a plaza's edge. A twisted pine where three paths converge. The fruit trees—apple, pear, a few plums—are espaliered against south-facing walls, their branches trained along wooden lattices into geometric forms: triangles, half-moons, ladder shapes. The fruit hangs sparse but large, the trees' energy concentrated. In certain courtyards, grape vines climb on stone pillars, creating partial canopies that drip during vesh-time.

Beyond the cultivated spaces, native grasses grow in the fissures and low places: tufted, silver-green, moving in wind like water. These grasses seed in the cracks of unused stone, in the spaces behind buildings that face the cliff-edges. Where the plateau surface dips into shallow basins—nowhere more than a few feet deep—sedges establish themselves, creating zones of deeper green that hold moisture longer into the afternoon.

The fauna is everywhere but never crowding. Stone martens slip along the bases of walls, hunting the abundant mice that nest in the grass-tufts. You see them in peripheral vision, rust-brown streaks. The mice themselves are bold, sitting upright on their haunches to observe passersby from a distance of a few feet, whiskers forward. Kestrels nest on the cliff-faces and hunt over the plateau's surface, their sharp *kee-kee-kee* calls the most common sound puncturing the general quiet. Ravens move between buildings, walking more than flying, overturning small stones with their beaks to expose insects.

The insects are abundant in ways that become apparent only through attention: iridescent beetles on the moss, damselflies near the cisterns, moths that emerge at dusk in clouds from beneath roof-tiles. Bees work the herb gardens, and you see woven skeps in some courtyards—dome-shaped straw hives sitting on stone platforms. Dragonflies patrol paths, hovering at eye level to investigate movement before darting away.

In certain shaded corners, salamanders—small, dark orange with black spots—emerge during vesh-time. They cross paths in unhurried lines, seeking the damper moss patches. No one disturbs them; they're understood as indicators of water quality in the cisterns, their presence or absence read like an instrument.

The urban layout follows a logic that becomes clear slowly. Buildings cluster loosely into neighborhoods of eight or twelve structures, each cluster separated from the next by fifty yards of garden and pathway. Within clusters, buildings face inward toward a shared open space—not quite a courtyard, more like a commons that no one finished defining. These spaces contain communal elements: bread ovens built of cemented stone, their domed shapes rising chest-high, with chimneys that lean slightly (indicating recent construction, before the builders fully understood wind patterns at this elevation). Textile frames where wool is stretched and worked. Stone tables—single pieces of limestone carved with shallow basins for grinding.

The city's infrastructure reveals itself through what connects the clusters: stone-lined channels for water distribution, brick-paved paths slightly raised above the surrounding ground, tall poles at intersections where oil lamps hang (unlit during day, their copper reflectors catching sun). Everything is maintained but nothing is new—the equilibrium of constant repair, where each element is perpetually at the same stage between construction and deterioration.

At the plateau's edges, where the cliff-faces drop away, buildings stand back twenty or thirty feet. In this liminal zone, stone markers have been placed—pillars about waist-high, too regular to be natural, their tops carved with geometric patterns that might be decorative or might be linguistic. Between markers, paths approach the edges but don't quite reach them, stopping at wooden rails. The rails are simple—juniper posts with cross-beams—but positioned with clear intentionality, marking where standing is permitted, where the edge becomes too dangerous.

From these edge-points, you see the landscape below: rolling hills covered in oak and ash forest, darker green than the plateau's silver-grass. Smoke rises from valley settlements invisible beneath the canopy. The hills extend in waves toward a horizon that's always slightly hazed, the moisture in the air softening distances. To the east, the land rises again—far mountains carrying snow on their peaks even now, white against pale sky.

The light here is notable for its evenness. The plateau sits high enough that haze never quite occludes the sun, yet the air's moisture diffuses the light into something softer than harsh highland sun might suggest. Shadows are present but not dramatic—blue-gray extensions of objects rather than dark voids. During vesh-time, light becomes nearly directionless, as if rising from the wet stone itself. By afternoon, when the moisture evaporates, the light gains a slight amber quality, warm-toned despite the cool air, making the limestone glow.

Buildings cast shadows that move with absolute predictability across packed earth. The same corner falls into shade at the same moment each day. The same path is half-shadowed, half-sunlit in the same pattern each afternoon. People arrange their work rhythms around this constancy: grinding grain in the shaded morning courtyards, weaving textiles in the afternoon-lit southern exposures.

The soundscape is defined more by what's absent than present. No running water, only the intermittent trickle during vesh-time. No wind-roar—the plateau's elevation is high but not mountainous, and the surrounding peaks block the strongest winds. Voices carry but there are few of them, and they're not raised; conversation happens at close range. Footsteps on packed earth are nearly silent; on the stone-paved paths they create a hollow tap that echoes slightly against building walls.

What sounds exist arrive in discrete events: the *clank* of a bucket against cistern edge. The *thud* of wood being stacked. The *scrape* of stone being worked—you can hear masons reshaping blocks, the rasp of chisel on limestone, the grittier sound of grinding stone for mortar. Bird calls. The bleating of goats being led through the city (kept in valley pastures but brought up periodically, their hooves clattering on paved sections). A wooden flute, sometimes, in early evening, someone practicing scales that hang in the air then cease.

The atmosphere holds a peculiar stillness—not stagnation but equilibrium. Nothing rushes. The vesh comes, the vesh departs. The sun crosses. Shadows wheel. The kestrels hunt. The martens emerge. The pattern repeats with the precision of mechanism, yet nothing feels mechanical because the pattern includes abundance: mice moving through grass, moths circling lamp-posts, herbs flowering then setting seed then flowering again.

This is a city in a steady state. Not unchanging because frozen, but unchanging because perfectly balanced—enough construction to counter the slow work of weather, enough harvest to match regrowth, enough use to justify maintenance. You sense no trajectory here, no development toward or away from any condition. The sharp cliff-edges don't round. The buildings don't age. The gardens don't expand or contract. Everything exists in present tense.

The weirdness of the place emerges gradually. First you notice how the stone blocks fit—too precisely, the joints too perfect for hand tools. Then you observe that the trees, though clearly old, show no lightning scars, no storm damage. The paths, though earth, never muddy during vesh-time; the water drains immediately into the limestone, as if the stone is actively drinking. 

The animal behavior has regularities that seem almost coordinated: the mice emerging at dawn, the martens at dusk, the ravens at midday. Not trained, not domesticated, but moving through the city as if following an agreed-upon schedule. The salamanders always travel from northeast to southwest during vesh-time, never the reverse. The kestrels nest on the same cliff-face sections, leaving other equally suitable sections unoccupied.

Certain buildings have no doors—open archways that lead to dim interiors where stone tables hold arrangements of objects: small carved figures, polished stones, bundles of dried herbs. No one enters these buildings; you watch for hours and see people pass by, glance in, continue walking. The objects inside remain undisturbed, dust-free despite the open entrances.

The stone markers at the plateau's edges: their geometric patterns are not repeated. Each marker carries a unique configuration of carved lines—some angular, some curved, some dense with detail, some nearly blank. No two are similar enough to suggest they're part of a shared system, yet they're clearly intentional, and someone maintains them, re-carving lines that weather threatens to smooth.

In certain low basins where sedges grow, the water that pools during vesh-time has a faint blue tint—not reflected sky, but something in the water itself, or something the sedges release. The blue fades as the water evaporates, the basins returning to moist earth by afternoon, dark brown with no trace of azure. People collect this water in copper vessels, careful not to touch it directly, and pour it into specific cisterns marked with spiral symbols carved into their rim-stones.

The moss on the cistern walls grows in vertical stripes—bands of different species, you assume, except the stripes are too regular, too evenly spaced. Dark green, light green, almost yellow-green, repeating around the cistern's circumference in sequence. When harvested, the pattern remains; the moss regrows into the same configuration, as if the stone itself determines which variety grows where.

At night—and you stay long enough to see this—the city doesn't darken completely. The lamps are lit at dusk by lamplighters who move through the city with practiced efficiency, carrying flame from one post to the next. But there's also a subtle luminescence from the limestone itself, barely perceptible, a faint blue-white glow that's only visible when your eyes fully adjust. It's strongest where vesh-water has soaked deepest, fading to nothing on stone that's been dry all day. The glow persists through the night hours, fading only as dawn approaches, as if the stone is exhaling light it absorbed from water.

The people who live here move through the city like another fauna species—abundant, following patterns, creating small sounds, going about necessary tasks with minimal wasted motion. They wear wool and linen, undyed mostly, in creams and grays and soft browns. Leather aprons for work. Head-coverings when the vesh comes, woven hoods that shed water onto shoulders.

They work primarily with stone and wood, using tools that seem simple until you watch them employed: bronze chisels that ring with particular tones against limestone, indicating to the practiced ear how the stone will split. Wooden mallets with heads that show the compression of thousands of strikes but no splitting—whatever wood this is holds together under repetitive force. Measuring cords made from braided wool that don't stretch. Levels that use water in sealed wooden tubes, the bubble's position indicating true horizontal.

The more sophisticated technology appears in water management: copper alloys that resist corrosion, ceramic pipes that conduct water through building walls, filtration systems in the cisterns—layers of gravel, sand, and charcoal through which water percolates before reaching the reservoir below. These systems are maintained by specialists who you see entering buildings, emerging later with small tools, making minor adjustments that keep everything flowing in its eternal pattern.

Food preparation happens in the shared ovens, communal spaces where bread bakes, grains are roasted, vegetables are charred. The ovens are always at the same temperature—you observe someone approaching, opening the clay door, inserting a flat wooden paddle with bread, closing the door, walking away. No testing, no checking, no uncertainty. The bread emerges precisely browned. This happens identically each time you watch, with different people, different breads.

The city's economy seems gift-based or perhaps need-based—you see no money change hands, no bartering. Someone carries a basket of apples from a tree to a communal table and leaves it. Others take apples, sometimes leaving herbs, sometimes a carved wooden bowl, sometimes nothing. The basket is always full when you look. The table is never empty but never overcrowded.

Textiles are worked outdoors on the stretching frames: wool cleaned and carded and spun and woven in a progression you can observe by walking between clusters. Here, raw fleece being washed in copper basins. There, carding with wooden paddle-brushes. Further on, drop spindles rotating, suspending from hip-held wool. At another cluster, small frame looms producing cloth in undyed natural colors, the warp threads perfectly parallel, the weft beaten tight with bone implements.

The city contains perhaps three hundred people, though you never see them all at once. They exist dispersed through the clusters, working individually or in pairs, occasionally in small groups. Children move between clusters, carrying messages or items, learning tasks by observation. Elders sit in doorways or on stone benches positioned to catch afternoon sun, working on detail tasks—carving, sewing, sorting dried herbs.

No one performs the same task continuously. You watch someone grinding grain until a raven calls, then they set down their grinding stone and walk to a garden plot, working there until the vesh-moisture begins, then they enter a building. This fluidity extends across the city—everyone doing everything in rotation, or in response to subtle cues you can't quite identify, maintaining the whole through distributed attention rather than specialization.

At the plaza spaces where clusters connect, stone benches form rough circles. Here, in late afternoon, people gather without apparent coordination. They sit. Sometimes they speak quietly. Sometimes they're silent. Children play at the circle's center—simple games with sticks and stones, creating temporary structures that are dismantled before evening. No one leads these gatherings; they emerge and dissolve with tidal regularity.

The temples or shrines—if that's what the doorless buildings are—receive attention in passing rather than visitation. Someone walking by pauses, removes a stone from the arrangement inside, replaces it in a different position, continues walking. The next day, the stones are back in their original configuration. You never see anyone return them; it happens during hours you're not watching, or it happens in ways too subtle to observe.

The city's relationship with the cliff-edges is complex. People approach them regularly but briefly—standing at the rail, looking out at the valley forest, looking toward the distant mountains, then stepping back. No one lingers. The edges are not feared but respected, treated as boundaries that define the possible extent of the city without tempting expansion beyond it.

Where the plateau surface has been quarried, creating those geometric wounds in the cliff-face, the voids are now something else. Some have become gardens, their floors terraced, their walls moss-covered. Others are workshops where the vertical surfaces provide attachment points for tool racks and material storage. The quarry voids integrate into the city seamlessly, as if they were always meant to be interior spaces, the act of removing stone a kind of revelation of potential use.

The seasons don't change here, or change so minimally that you'd need years to detect their turning. The temperature holds. The vesh comes with the same frequency. The plants maintain their greenness or silverness without dramatic growth spurts or die-backs. Fruits ripen in steady progression—always some trees bearing, some flowering, some bare—but the overall abundance remains constant. This is not a place where spring arrives dramatically or winter strips everything bare. This is continuous mid-season, perpetual growth exactly matched by perpetual harvest.

Time feels different here. Not slower, not faster, but somehow beside the usual flow. Days pass but they're identical, each one a precise copy of the last, yet the copying doesn't create monotony because abundance makes each moment locally rich. The specific mouse you see is not yesterday's mouse. The specific kestrel call is shaped by today's air. The bread emerging from the oven is unique while being exactly like every other bread.

You realize, eventually, that you're observing something like a living frieze—a city in three dimensions that contains all the elements of urban life (construction, maintenance, work, rest, social gathering, solitude, art, utility, edge, center) but arranged in perfect stasis. Not frozen, because everything moves—people walk, birds fly, water trickles, light shifts—but moving within boundaries so precise that the overall pattern never deviates.

This plateau city exists at a peculiar intersection: high enough to be separate, accessible enough to be viable, abundant enough to be sustainable, sparse enough to be spacious, built enough to be urban, primitive enough to be comprehensible, stable enough to be eternal, weird enough to be unsettling. Everything in balance. Everything held at exact ratios that prevent change while allowing life.

The white limestone glows in afternoon light. The paths wind between buildings. The cisterns collect the vesh. The gardens grow precisely enough herbs, precisely enough fruit. The martens hunt precisely enough mice. The people move through their tasks with the fluidity of long practice. Nothing breaks. Nothing wears out. Nothing improves. Nothing decays. The city breathes at the same rate, forever, the inhalation of moisture each morning, the exhalation of light each night, the rhythm unbroken, unbreakable, perfect.
