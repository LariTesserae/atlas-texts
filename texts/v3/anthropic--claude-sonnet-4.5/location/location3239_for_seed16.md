# The Resin Quarter

In the deep hollow of a crater—so old its rim has become a low earthen bulge barely higher than the surrounding plain—there exists a settlement built entirely within, around, and from the bodies of immense fungal towers.

These are not mushrooms as you might imagine them. Each structure rises twenty, thirty, sometimes fifty meters: vast cylindrical columns of dense, woody fungal matter, their surfaces bark-like, deeply fissured, the color of old bone stained with rust and amber. They grow in clusters, sometimes fusing into one another to create walls, archways, chambers. The air here is thick and hot—oppressively so—a wet heat without water, the atmosphere heavy with volatile oils and organic esters that the fungi exhale. Breathing feels like inhaling the inside of a cedar chest left in an attic through a hundred summers.

The settlement is called the Resin Quarter, and it exists in the spaces between and within these towers.

## The Architecture of Absence

There are perhaps three hundred inhabitants here—you would have to count carefully, moving through the interconnected chambers, and even then you might miss the solitary craftspeople working in rooms carved into fungal heartwood. The place feels emptier than it is. Passages stretch long and hollow. Rooms stand cathedral-vast but contain only a single workbench, a sleeping mat, a clay oven. The fungal towers create natural enclosures thirty meters across, and the residents have divided these spaces with walls made of compressed fungal fiber—thick, papery stuff that has been beaten and pressed and dried into structural panels. But they divide sparingly. Space is abundant here. Why crowd when you can spread?

The streets, if you can call them that, wind between the tower-bases in curves that follow no grid. Some are broad enough for ten people to walk abreast; most see only two or three people in a day. The ground underfoot is packed fungal debris—centuries of shed spores, sloughed skin, fallen fragments—compacted into a surface that has the texture of compressed sawdust, warm to the touch, slightly springy. When you walk, your footsteps make a soft, dense thump, like stepping on a book.

Overhead, where the towers lean toward one another or where branches have been laid between them, there are second and third levels: platforms, bridges, whole rooms suspended in the dim space. Rope ladders dangle. Wooden stairs carved from fungal heartwood spiral up the exteriors of towers. But many of these elevated spaces sit empty. The population hasn't filled this place. It has nested in it, leaving vast territories dormant.

## The Substance of Things

The towers themselves are alive—or were alive—or exist in some state between those conditions. They grow, but so slowly that a person might live their entire life without seeing visible change. They no longer reproduce; the last spore-fall that anyone remembers was three generations back, and those spores never took. What stands here is established, settled into its final form, a forest that has reached its climax state and simply... continues.

The residents harvest the towers carefully, surgically. They cut doors and windows into the walls using bronze saws with teeth like small chisels—hard, patient work. The fungal flesh is dense, layered in annual rings like a tree, and when you cut into it, it weeps. Not water—there is no water here—but thick resinous sap, amber-colored, viscous, smelling of pitch and cinnamon and something darker, more bitter. This resin is everything here. It is waterproofing, adhesive, preservative, fuel, medicine, and art material. It pools in carved channels, is collected in ceramic vessels, is refined in clay distilleries heated by burning the dry outer bark of the towers.

The resin never fully hardens. Even after years, it remains slightly tacky, slightly mobile. Buildings sealed with it seem to breathe, their surfaces always faintly glistening in the dim light. Touch a wall and your fingers come away with a thin film, smelling of that eternal summer-attic heat.

## Light in the Hollow

The light here is a problem that has been solved poorly and accepted. The crater rim blocks the horizon in all directions—not dramatically, just enough that the sun rises late and sets early. The fungal towers themselves block still more light, their massive bodies creating a perpetual understory dimness. By mid-morning, you can see well enough to work, but you're always squinting slightly, always adjusting. By mid-afternoon, the light is already failing.

So the residents burn lamps. Hundreds of them. Small clay vessels filled with refined resin, burning with small, hot flames that produce surprising light for their size—clear and steady, almost white. They hang from hooks driven into the towers, sit in niches carved into walls, cluster on workbenches. At night, the Resin Quarter becomes a constellation, each light a small defiant star pushing back against the darkness. The air shimmers with heat-ripples. The smell of burning resin grows thicker, sweeter, almost narcotic.

Walking through the settlement at night, you move through zones of light and shadow. A workshop might be blazing with twenty lamps, the craftsperson inside working with bronze tools on some object you can't quite identify. Twenty meters away, a residential chamber sits with only a single lamp, someone sitting motionless near it, perhaps reading, perhaps simply watching the flame. The spaces between are dark—not frightening, but very dark, the kind of darkness where you must shuffle your feet and feel ahead with your hands.

## The Work

The settlement makes things. This is its purpose, its reason for existing in this remote hollow. The primary craft is the working of the resin itself, but around this central industry cluster others: the casting of bronze tools, the making of ceramic vessels, the weaving of fungal fibers into rope and cloth, the carving of the heartwood into furniture and structural elements.

The workshops are loud. This surprises you at first—you expect a place so empty, so hollow, to be silent. But sound travels strangely here, reflecting off the curved walls of the towers, focusing in unexpected ways. A bronze hammer striking a chisel in one workshop creates a sharp crack that seems to come from everywhere at once. The rhythmic thump of fiber being beaten echoes through the streets. The hiss and pop of resin boiling in clay retorts fills entire chambers. Someone splitting heartwood with wedges and mallet produces concussive reports that roll through the settlement like slow thunder.

And beneath these working sounds, constant and almost subsonic, the towers themselves produce noise. They creak. They groan. They make sounds like ships settling in a harbor, deep woody complaints as their massive bodies shift infinitesimally, adjusting to the heat of the day, the cooling of the night, the eternal patient pressures of their own weight. Sometimes a tower will produce a sound like a sigh—a long exhalation of gases from some internal pocket, lasting thirty seconds, smelling strongly of fermentation.

The residents don't react to these sounds. They are the background, the ambient condition. Only visitors startle, look up, search for the source.

## The Absence of Water and Its Consequences

There is no water in the Resin Quarter. Not in the conventional sense. The residents drink a thin oil pressed from certain fungal tissues—clear, slightly sweet, nutritious enough to sustain human metabolism though it sits strangely in the stomach. They wash—when they wash—with dry fungal powders that absorb oils and dirt, then brush off. Most don't wash often. The heat makes everyone sweat, but the sweat evaporates almost immediately in the arid air, leaving salt-patterns on skin and clothing.

Clothing here is simple: wrapped garments made from fungal-fiber cloth, undyed, the color of old canvas. People tie them in different configurations for different tasks. The cloth absorbs the resin-smell from the air, so everyone carries that scent with them—sweet, slightly medicinal, woody.

Food arrives from elsewhere. Every eight days, a caravan appears at the crater rim—you can see it from below, silhouetted against the sky, pack animals descending the gentle slope. They bring dried goods, salt-preserved meat, grain, fruits dried to leathery strips. They take away the products of the Quarter: refined resin in stoppered jars, carved heartwood boxes and furniture, bronze tools, woven rope. The exchange happens in a large open area near the crater's western edge, one of the few spaces that isn't dominated by towers. Voices rise in negotiation, argument, greeting. Then the caravan ascends again, and the Quarter returns to its work.

## The Ecology of Function

With no animals, no conventional plants, the ecology here is strange—not wildly so, not disturbingly so, but skewed, off-center. The fungal towers host other, smaller fungi: bracket-like growths on their surfaces, threadlike strands hanging from their branches, flat crusts that spread slowly across their bark. These secondary organisms form relationships—some seem to damage the towers slowly, creating pits and hollows, while others may protect, though no one understands the mechanisms fully.

Insects would normally complete certain cycles, but there are none here. Instead, the residents have taken on some of these roles without quite realizing it. When they cut into a tower, they expose interior layers to air, allowing certain fungi to colonize wounds that would otherwise seal. When they transport heartwood from one area to another, they carry spores. They have become the inadvertent vectors of the ecosystem they inhabit.

In corners, in unused chambers, in the spaces beneath platforms, you find gardens of sorts—though no one planted them deliberately. Small fungi fruit: tiny structures the size of a fingernail, clusters of thread-like filaments, soft cushions of growth. They appear, exist for a time, then collapse back into the substrate. The residents mostly ignore these unless they become obstructive, then scrape them away.

The towers themselves shed constantly—not dramatically, but continuously. Tiny flakes of outer bark, wisps of fiber, dust of broken-down tissue. This material drifts through the air, especially when disturbed by movement. In the lamplight, you see it: a constant slow snow of organic particles, falling, settling, accumulating. The residents sweep sometimes, pushing the debris into corners or out into the streets, where it eventually compacts into the walking surface.

## The Heat That Is and Isn't

The temperature here is carefully balanced in a way that took generations to achieve. The towers, alive in their slow way, generate metabolic heat—not much per unit of mass, but they are massive, and the heat accumulates. The crater hollow traps this warmth. During the day, the sun adds more. The residents burn resin constantly, adding more still.

It should be unbearable. Instead, it hovers just at the edge of comfortable, tipping into uncomfortable during the hottest hours of mid-afternoon, but never becoming dangerous. The residents have learned this balance through careful management: how many lamps to burn, which chambers to seal and which to leave open, where to position ventilation shafts carved vertically through the towers' cores. Hot air rises through these shafts and exhausts somewhere high above, drawing cooler air in from the crater rim.

Still, you are always warm here. Your skin is always slightly damp with sweat that vanishes as soon as it appears. You feel constantly on the edge of thirst, and you sip that strange fungal oil more often than you'd like. The heat makes you slow, makes you want to sit rather than stand, lie rather than sit. The residents have adapted their rhythms: work in the early morning, rest during the hot hours, work again in the evening, continue by lamplight into the night when the heat finally ebbs to something pleasant.

## The Strangeness That Isn't Quite

There is a wrongness here, or almost a wrongness—a sense that things don't fit together quite as they should, but you can't identify the misalignment. The scale is part of it: towers so large, population so small, the ratio off in some way that troubles the eye. The silence beneath the noise is part of it: all those empty chambers, all that hollow space, the settlement somehow too big for itself.

And there are details that nag. The resin sometimes moves when it shouldn't—you're certain you left that pool in a particular shape, but returning an hour later, it has flowed slightly, changed configuration. Some of the carved chambers have surfaces too smooth, too regular, as if something other than bronze tools shaped them. Occasionally you'll find an object—a tool, a vessel—in a place where no one remembers leaving it, and when you ask, people shrug, assume someone else placed it there, but no one claims responsibility.

The residents don't talk much about the towers' origins. They know the place is old, that the towers were here long before the first craftspeople arrived and carved their workshops. But no one speaks of what made them, or why they stopped spreading, or whether they might wake again. There's a sense of precariousness that no one acknowledges—that all this depends on the towers' continued dormancy, their patient tolerance of being slowly carved away.

Sometimes at night, in the deepest dark between lamp-pools, you might see something that isn't quite bioluminescence but isn't quite not: a faint glow deep within a tower's surface, visible only in peripheral vision, gone when you look directly. The residents have no explanation. They've stopped looking for one.

## The Active Stillness

For a place that seems so static—ancient towers, slow craft, established patterns—there is constant activity, though it runs at different tempos. The fastest is human: the daily work, the conversations, the movement through streets and chambers. This is the rhythm you notice first, the one that orients you.

Slower is the work of the towers themselves: that endless shedding, the gradual seeping of resin, the century-scale growth. You don't see this directly, but you sense it in the accumulation of dust, the way carved chambers slowly heal at their edges, the thickening layers of resin-glaze on old surfaces.

Slower still is the erosion of the towers—not by water or wind, but by the patient work of the residents, cutting doorways and chambers, harvesting heartwood, drilling ventilation shafts. The settlement is gradually hollowing the forest that contains it, but at a pace so measured that it might continue for a thousand years before the structure fails.

And beneath all of this runs something else, a rhythm too slow to perceive but somehow present: the life-cycle of the towers themselves, whatever process governs their existence. They are in balance, neither growing nor dying but maintaining, existing in some steady state. The entire settlement is built on the assumption that this balance will hold, that the towers will neither expand to crush the carved spaces nor collapse to leave the residents unhoused.

This assumption has held for seven generations. No one knows if it will hold for eight.

## The Sound of Your Presence

You walk through a street between two massive towers, their bases twenty meters apart. Your footsteps thump softly on the compressed fungal ground. From a workshop to your left comes the steady tink-tink-tink of someone working bronze—shaping a tool, perhaps, or repairing one. The sound bounces off the curved tower walls, arrives at your ears from three directions at once.

Above, invisible in the dim light, someone is walking on a platform. You hear the footsteps, hollow and wooden, moving away. A rope creaks. Then silence from that direction.

Ahead, a woman emerges from a doorway carved into the right-hand tower. She carries a ceramic jar of resin, heading somewhere with purpose. She nods at you—greeting or acknowledgment—and turns down a side passage. You hear her footsteps receding, the sound distorted by the tower-architecture into something that seems to come from above rather than ahead.

The towers groan. First the left one—a deep, slow complaint like wood under pressure—then the right one answers with a higher creak, almost conversational. This continues for several seconds, then stops.

You pass an open chamber where an old man sits cross-legged near a single lamp, working on something small with bronze tools. He doesn't look up. The light from his lamp illuminates fungal walls carved with shallow relief—geometric patterns, spirals, interlocking curves. The walls gleam with old resin, the patterns catching light, losing it.

Another turn. This passage is darker—only one lamp visible far ahead, everything else shadow. You slow, let your eyes adjust, shuffle forward with careful steps. The air here is thick with the smell of resin, almost overwhelming, sweet and sharp at once. Your hands find the tower walls on either side—warm, slightly tacky, fissured with deep cracks.

The lamp ahead resolves into a workshop where two people are lifting a large panel of compressed fungal fiber, positioning it as a wall. They move in the practiced silence of people who've worked together for years. The panel thuds into place. One of them—you can't tell if it's a man or woman in the dim light—reaches for a vessel of resin-glue, begins sealing the edges with careful strokes.

You emerge from the passage into a larger space—one of the natural plazas formed where towers grow in a rough circle, leaving an open area perhaps forty meters across. Here there are more lamps, more activity. A group of children—five of them, rare sight in this place—are playing some kind of game involving small bronze tokens, arguing about rules in loud voices that echo and re-echo until the space seems full of argument. An elderly woman sits on a bench carved from heartwood, watching them with an expression you can't read.

The ground here is swept clean, showing the packed fungal matter beneath, slightly orange in the lamplight. Around the plaza's edge, multiple doors lead into tower-chambers, and from several come the sounds of work: sawing, hammering, the scrape of fiber being combed, the bubble of boiling resin.

You stand for a moment, absorbing this—the center of the settlement, such as it is. The towers rise around you, their tops invisible in the darkness above. The air shimmers with heat. The smell of resin is everywhere, inescapable, starting to seem normal. The children's voices fill the space, then suddenly stop as the game resolves somehow, then resume at a lower volume as a new round begins.

This is the Resin Quarter: a pocket settlement carved into an ancient fungal forest in a forgotten crater, producing refined resins and crafted goods through patient manual labor, existing in the spaces between giant organisms that tolerate their presence or perhaps don't notice it at all. It feels both established and precarious, both bustling and hollow, both strange and unremarkable.

You could live here. People do. Three hundred of them, spread through empty chambers and narrow streets, breathing resin-thick air, drinking fungal oil, working by lamplight, sleeping on platforms suspended in the warm darkness. They have built a small urban density in this low place, filled it with the sounds of craft and argument and footsteps, and left vast territories of it empty, as if waiting for something to arrive or return, though no one speaks of what that might be.

The towers groan again, a sound like agreement or warning. The lamps burn on. The work continues.
