# The Rootworks

You descend through engineered shafts cut into bedrock, following the cough and wheeze of ventilation engines, and emerge into something that shouldn't exist: a city growing downward into the earth, constructed entirely from living plant matter in a place where no plant should survive.

The scale reveals itself slowly. The chamber extends for miles—not a single cavern but a networked complex of grottos, galleries, and manufactured voids carved from stone that still bears the fresh scars of recent excavation. Dust hangs in the cooler air, mineral particulate that catches in your throat. The rock faces show drill marks, blast patterns, the geometric violence of industrial boring. This place is perhaps thirty years old, maybe less. In some galleries, you can still see where the stone was cut away last season, raw faces weeping mineral seepage, not yet colonized.

And into this wound in the earth, the Rootworks grows.

## The Architecture of Growth

The city—and it is unmistakably a city—constructs itself from an engineered plant species that has adapted, or been adapted, to subsist on something other than water. The primary structural organism resembles bamboo crossed with mangrove, but the analogy fails quickly. The trunks, if you can call them that, are segmented cylinders of woody tissue that exude a waxy, petroleum-derived coating. They smell of turpentine and rendered fat. When you touch them—and you will, because they form the railings, the bridges, the walls—they're cool and slightly tacky, like leather that's been sealed with dubbin.

The plants grow from substrate beds of crushed mineral supplemented with what appears to be industrial nutrient slurry delivered through ceramic pipes. Pumping stations clank and hiss at regular intervals throughout the warren, maintaining pressure. The slurry is viscous, greenish-gray, and smells of ammonia and metal salts. It is not water. The plants have been engineered to metabolize this chemical soup, processing it through root systems that dangle exposed in many places, pale and segmented like intestines, pulsing slowly as fluids move through specialized tissues.

Buildings—warehouses, residential blocks, workshops—are not built so much as *grown into templates*. Metal frameworks rise first, geometric skeletons of riveted steel and iron struts, and then the plant matter is induced to fill the spaces. The result is a hybrid architecture: industrial bones sheathed in living wood that continues to thicken, to add layers, to repair itself. The structures are oddly rounded despite their metal cores, swollen with vegetable growth. Some buildings are clearly older, their metal completely encased, the original framework visible only where panels have been cut away for doors and windows.

The windows themselves are a minor marvel—thin membranes that certain cultivars produce, translucent sheets that can be peeled away and mounted in frames. They admit the carefully managed light but remain slightly opaque, giving interiors a clouded, aquarium quality.

## The Illumination Problem

Light comes from three sources, each contributing to the strange, layered visibility of the Rootworks.

First: industrial fixtures. Electric lamps mounted on gantries and walls, powered by generators whose diesel rumble provides a constant, felt-more-than-heard baseline to the soundscape. The lamps produce harsh white light, focused and intentional, creating pools of absolute visibility surrounded by comparative darkness. You navigate from pool to pool.

Second: bioluminescent cultivars. Not the primary structural plants, but smaller varieties—vines, mostly, and clusters of bulbous growths that fruit from the main trunks. These emit a cool greenish glow, far dimmer than the electric lamps but more pervasive. They trace the edges of walkways, outline doorframes, provide just enough light in residential areas to navigate by. The glow pulses faintly, responding to some internal rhythm—perhaps circadian, though there is no sun here, or perhaps keyed to the chemical composition of the nutrient slurry. The pulse is slow enough that you don't consciously notice it until you've been watching for several minutes.

Third: reflected and scattered light from the surface, delivered through deliberate shaft systems. Not sunlight directly—the shafts are too long, too angled—but a diluted, twice-reflected approximation that maintains some connection to the day-night cycle above. This light is warm-toned, amber and gold, and arrives in shafts that pierce down through specific galleries, creating pillars of dusty radiance around which the city organizes itself.

The combination produces a landscape of contrasts—bright industrial work zones, dim green residential tangles, and the scattered amber columns of surface-light that serve as landmarks and gathering points. Your eyes adjust constantly. There are no true shadows, only gradations of visibility.

## The Fauna

Animals move through the Rootworks with an opportunistic confidence. They were not planned for, precisely, but neither are they unwelcome. The ecosystem is young enough that it hasn't stabilized—species appear, establish themselves, sometimes fail, sometimes thrive.

Most common are the bark-miners: insect analogues the size of your thumb that bore into the living wood, feeding on something in the plant's strange sap. They're segmented, chitinous, a dark iridescent purple. Their mandibles produce a quiet clicking, a sound like distant typing, that fills the spaces between mechanical noises. The plants tolerate them, perhaps even benefit—the insects distribute some element of the nutrient slurry, or perhaps their tunneling aerates the dense wood. There are theories. No one is certain.

Larger animals hunt the miners. Lizard-things that shouldn't exist this deep but have found their way down through the ventilation shafts and adapted with shocking speed. They're pale, their pigmentation fading over generations, eyes growing larger. They move in stop-motion bursts, utterly still and then suddenly somewhere else, clinging to the vertical surfaces of plant-walls with adhesive toe-pads. Watching one hunt is like watching a glitch in time—absence, presence, absence again, and a bark-miner vanished.

There are birds. How they arrived is unclear—perhaps brought deliberately, perhaps migrants that found the entrance shafts and discovered an ecosystem of flying insects around the lamp-heat. Small brown things, almost sparrows, but their songs are wrong. The acoustics of the caverns have changed their calls. They sound mechanical, like damaged instruments, patterns of notes that spiral and repeat. They nest in the dense upper tangles where vines cross and recross the iron frameworks.

And there are the nutrient-seals. This is what the workers call them, though they're not seals—they're not anything from the surface. They're native to here, to this new ecosystem. Quadrupedal, smooth-skinned, the size of large dogs. They gather around nutrient-slurry access points and the exposed root-systems, and they drink. They lap at the chemical soup with specialized tongues, metabolize it somehow, convert it to body-heat and motion. They're almost friendly, habituated to human presence, but they watch with black eyes that reflect the bioluminescence in unsettling ways. Sometimes they make sounds—low, resonant moans that carry through the galleries. The acoustics amplify these calls, make them omnipresent and directionless. You hear them and cannot tell if the creature is nearby or miles distant.

## The Sonic Landscape

The Rootworks is not silent, but it is fundamentally quiet. Sound exists in layers here, most of it distant, much of it mechanical.

The base layer: the generators. Diesel engines running in sealed chambers, their vibration transmitted through the stone itself. You feel this more than hear it—a subtle tremor in the floor, in the walls, a presence in your bones. When the generators cycle or shift load, the quality of the vibration changes, and the entire city seems to shift its weight.

Above that: the pumps and ventilation systems. These produce rhythmic sounds—the suck and wheeze of air being forced through ducting, the hydraulic pulse of slurry delivery, the occasional hiss of pressure release. These sounds are spaced widely enough in time that they don't create a continuous roar but rather a punctuation, a sentence spoken by infrastructure. Hiss. Pause. Clank. Pause. Wheeze. The pauses matter as much as the sounds.

Then: biological sounds. The click of bark-miners, the wing-flutter of birds in the upper tangles, the low moan of nutrient-seals. The plants themselves make sounds—the creak of growing wood, the occasional crack as a trunk shifts or a branch splits. These sounds are unpredictable, arriving without warning, sometimes loud enough to startle.

Finally: human activity. Footsteps on wooden walkways produce a hollow percussion. Voices carry strangely here—the acoustics are uneven, unpredictable. Conversations held in one gallery can be perfectly audible three chambers away, transmitted through some accident of stone geometry, while people standing ten feet apart might need to raise their voices. The residents have learned which spaces are private and which are acoustic commons. Factories and workshops produce the expected industrial symphony—hammering, sawing, the whine of lathes—but these sounds are localized, contained. The city is organized to keep the loudest activities in specific galleries, leaving residential zones relatively untouched.

What's striking is the space between sounds. In a surface city, there's a constant ambient roar—wind, traffic, the accumulated noise of thousands of activities. Here, there are actual silences, moments when the generators are between cycles, when no one is walking nearby, when even the bark-miners pause. In these moments, you can hear your own breath, your heartbeat, the whisper of blood in your ears. The silences are unnerving at first. You adjust.

## The Industrial Heart

The Rootworks exists because of industry, and industry remains its pulse. This is not a garden that happens to have workshops—it is a production center that has been overgrown by engineered vegetation, and the two systems have become entangled in ways that serve both.

The primary industries are:
- Mineral extraction and processing. The ongoing excavation of the caverns produces raw ore and stone that is sorted, crushed, refined. The refineries are the loudest places in the Rootworks, where rock is broken down, metals extracted, slag disposed of through deeper shafts. These operations are contained in galleries specifically grown for heavy industry—the plant structures here are reinforced, thicker, more resinous to withstand heat and chemical exposure.

- Bio-engineering and cultivation. The plants themselves are a product, constantly refined. Laboratories occupy entire buildings, filled with specimen tanks, genetic documentation, trial grows. This is where new varieties are developed—plants that grow faster, produce more useful materials, tolerate different nutrient compositions. The work is part agriculture, part industrial chemistry, part something entirely new.

- Manufacturing. The plant material is harvested, processed, shaped. Some of it becomes construction material—beams, panels, structural elements. Some becomes textiles—fibers extracted and woven. Some becomes fuel—dried, pelletized, burned in modified furnaces. The workshops are scattered throughout the city, integrated into the residential areas. The smell of sawdust and plant-oil is omnipresent.

The factories operate on shifts. The workforce is substantial—hundreds of people, perhaps over a thousand now, though the population fluctuates as contracts begin and end, as new excavations open and old ones are sealed. The shift changes create the closest thing the Rootworks has to rush hour—periods of increased movement and noise, though even these are muted compared to surface cities.

## The Living Infrastructure

The relationship between the industrial systems and the plant life is deeply reciprocal, almost symbiotic, and increasingly difficult to parse.

The plants require the nutrient slurry, which is manufactured from mineral extracts and industrial chemicals. The slurry production requires the refineries, which require power, which requires fuel, some of which now comes from processed plant matter. The plants provide structural material for expansion, which houses more workers, which enables more extraction, which produces more minerals for slurry production.

But it's not a simple loop. The plants are doing things no one entirely understands. The root systems extend far beyond their nutrient delivery points, penetrating deep into the bedrock. Are they mining minerals directly? Possibly. Chemical analysis of the plant tissue shows trace elements that don't appear in the slurry. The roots are getting something from the stone itself.

And the plants are spreading beyond the planned zones. In older galleries, you find volunteer growth—plants sprouting from cracks in the stone where no one planted them, no nutrient lines run. They're smaller, twisted, half-starved, but they're alive. Growing on what? Mineral seepage? Airborne spores from the main growth? Something in the rock dust itself? The bio-engineers study these volunteers intensely. They represent either contamination or evolution, depending on your perspective.

Some of the volunteer growth has been incorporated deliberately. A residential block might plan for four stories, but a volunteer trunk sprouts against the back wall, and rather than remove it, the residents train it, shape it, eventually integrate it into the structure. These unplanned additions give the architecture an organic irregularity that the engineered growth lacks. The city looks less like a settlement and more like a reef—planned in sections but growing beyond intention.

## Materials and Textures

Everything here has a particular feel, a material quality that becomes familiar:

The primary structural wood is dense and slightly oily to the touch. It ranges in color from pale amber to deep brown, often with darker streaks where nutrient concentrations vary. It doesn't feel like surface wood—it's heavier, less inclined to splinter, more inclined to dent. It ages in strange ways, developing a patina that's more like leather than weathered timber.

The stone of the cavern walls is primarily limestone and shale, gray and beige, layered and fractured. In new excavations, the stone is clean and sharp, almost sterile. In older sections, it's begun to develop a coating—mineral deposits from the humid air, perhaps, or some interaction with the plant exudates. The coating is thin, translucent, slightly sticky. It catches dust, accumulates into patterns that resemble water stains but aren't.

Metal is everywhere—structural frameworks, pipes, railings, tools. It's industrial-grade stuff, thick and practical, usually painted but the paint doesn't last. The humid air and the plant chemicals corrode it in distinctive ways. The rust here is less orange, more purple-brown, and it forms in fractal patterns that look almost crystalline. The residents treat metal as a temporary material, expecting to replace it every few years.

Textiles are strange. Some are plant-fiber, woven from harvested material, and these have a rough, canvas-like texture. They smell faintly of turpentine. Others are fungal—someone discovered that certain fungi grow well on the plant waste, and their mycelial mats can be processed into a felt-like material. This fungal-felt is soft, slightly damp always, sound-absorbent. It's used for bedding, room dividers, acoustic panels.

The nutrient slurry itself is viscous, slightly translucent, the color of infected wounds. It leaves residue on everything it touches, a film that has to be cleaned regularly. The cleaning is done with mineral spirits, which adds another layer to the smell-scape. The combination of turpentine, mineral spirits, diesel exhaust, and plant oils creates an odor that is the signature of the Rootworks—industrial and botanical at once, artificial and organic, not unpleasant exactly but definitely strange.

## The Geometry of Settlement

The city's layout is partially planned, partially emergent. The major galleries were excavated according to design—long chambers connected by passage shafts, with specific functions assigned. But the plant growth has softened these geometric certainties.

Main thoroughfares are wide galleries where the plant-buildings line both sides, leaving a central space for movement. These feel almost like streets, though the ceiling is living wood and stone rather than sky. The light here is brightest—multiple lamp gantries overhead, bioluminescent vines trained along the walls. These are the commercial and administrative spaces, where workshops face the pathway, where notice boards are posted, where the nutrient-seal gather to beg scraps from the food vendors.

Residential areas are denser, more tangled. Buildings grow together, sharing walls, connected by aerial walkways where vines have been encouraged to form bridges. The interiors are smaller, more irregular. Rooms are often cylindrical or ovoid, following the natural geometry of the plant growth. Furniture is built-in, grown as part of the structure or fitted into the spaces between structural trunks. Privacy is acoustic rather than visual—the fungal-felt hangings, the careful choice of which rooms to use for what purpose.

Industrial zones are separated by empty buffer galleries, spaces where the plant growth is minimal and the stone is left mostly bare. These serve as firebreaks and contamination barriers. The factories themselves are massive—multi-story buildings where the plant framework is reinforced with extra metal, the wood treated with flame-retardant compounds. The floors are stone, easy to clean, resistant to chemical spills.

And everywhere: the vertical dimension. The Rootworks is not a single level but a three-dimensional maze. Galleries stack above and below each other, connected by grown-wood staircases that spiral through vertical shafts. Some sections have five or six levels of habitation, each with its own character, its own micro-climate. The upper levels, closer to the ventilation shafts, are cooler and dryer. The lower levels are warmer, more humid, where the heat from the generators accumulates.

The deepest levels are still being excavated. You can descend to the working face—the frontier of the city, where drilling and blasting continue, where the stone is fresh and the air is thick with dust. Here, the plant growth is minimal—just starter beds, frameworks waiting to be filled. This is the Rootworks in its embryonic state, industrial and raw, not yet softened by biology. The contrast is stark. You descend from a space that feels like a forest-city and emerge into something that's purely mining operation. Both are true. Both are the same place at different stages of becoming.

## The Rules of This Place

Certain facts govern life in the Rootworks:

Water is precious. The plants don't use it, but humans do. All water is imported, delivered through surface lines or collected from condensation. It's rationed carefully, recycled obsessively. The bathhouses use a closed-loop system, filtering and retreating the same water indefinitely. Drinking water comes in sealed containers. Nothing is wasted.

The air is breathable but regulated. The ventilation systems maintain oxygen levels, extract carbon dioxide, filter out the worst of the mineral dust. But the air is never quite fresh. It tastes of stone and chemicals, recycled breath and plant volatiles. Respiratory infections are common. Most long-term residents develop a chronic cough.

The plant growth must be managed constantly. Left untended, it becomes invasive, blocking passages, splitting structures. Teams of cultivation workers prune, train, harvest, and redirect growth daily. It's like gardening at urban scale. The city stays functional because someone is always trimming vines, cutting back unauthorized sprouts, maintaining the distinction between architecture and wilderness.

Fire is catastrophic. The plant matter is oily, burns hot and fast. Fire suppression systems are extensive—stored chemical suppressants, sand hoppers, emergency flood channels that can be filled with slurry to create barriers. There are fire drills. Everyone knows the evacuation routes, the sealed refuge chambers. Actual fires are rare but remembered for generations.

Time is altered. Without day-night cycles, without seasons, chronology becomes artificial. The city maintains a clock system, shift schedules, designated rest periods. But your body doesn't entirely believe it. Sleep becomes irregular. People drift between private and public time, following work demands and personal rhythms that don't always align. There's a social acceptance of temporal flexibility—if someone is asleep at midday (by the clock), no one questions it.

## The Strangeness

The Rootworks is functional, industrial, practical. But strangeness accumulates in the margins:

The plants sometimes grow in patterns that seem intentional. Vines will arrange themselves into geometric forms—spirals, grids, radiating spokes—without training or guidance. The bio-engineers insist it's emergent behavior, response to light and nutrient gradients. But the patterns are striking, almost deliberate. Some residents attribute meaning to them, read them as omens or messages. Others ignore them entirely.

The nutrient-seals exhibit behaviors no one expected. They're found in places they shouldn't access, behind locked doors, in sealed chambers. They're not small enough to fit through gaps, not clever enough to operate mechanisms. Yet they appear. And they watch. They're particularly drawn to the laboratories, will sit outside the doors for hours, patient and intent. What are they sensing? What do they want?

Sounds sometimes repeat. You'll hear a sequence—a particular cough, a dropped tool, a snatch of conversation—and then hours later, in a completely different gallery, you'll hear it again. Exactly the same. Perfect acoustic recall. The engineers say it's echoes, peculiar rock formations channeling and storing sound energy, releasing it later. But the delays are too long, the reproductions too perfect. It feels like the city itself has memory.

People report dreams of plant-consciousness. Not frequently, not universally, but enough that it's a known phenomenon. Dreams where they're experiencing the slow time of vegetable growth, the strange satisfaction of nutrient absorption, the geometric pleasure of putting out new leaves in optimal patterns. The dreams are not disturbing, exactly. They're too alien for that. But they're vivid, memorable, and many people have them. Is it something in the air? The plant volatiles? Suggestion and culture? No one knows.

And there's the depth-thing. The sense, growing stronger the longer you stay, that the Rootworks is not just below the surface but beneath something else, some conceptual membrane. That the excavation didn't just go down but through. That this space occupies a slightly different position in reality than the world above. It's not a belief, exactly—most people would deny feeling it if asked directly. But it influences behavior. The way people sometimes pause at the border between an old gallery and a new excavation, as if crossing a threshold. The way certain deep sections are avoided without formal prohibition. The sense that you're not just underground but somewhere else entirely.

## The Life Cycle

The city is growing. Not just expanding but maturing, developing emergent properties, becoming more itself.

New galleries open every few months. The excavation continues, following ore veins, creating new space for workshops and housing. Each new gallery is initially sterile—stone and metal and harsh light. Then the cultivation teams arrive. Nutrient lines are installed. Starter growth is planted. The frameworks go up. And over weeks and months, the space transforms. The plants fill in. The buildings swell. The bioluminescence establishes. The fauna arrives. The new section becomes part of the city, indistinguishable from older areas except by the thickness of the growth, the depth of the patina.

The oldest sections, the first excavations, are now nearly a decade mature. The plant growth there is massive—trunks as thick as your torso, canopies that completely obscure the stone ceiling, root systems that have penetrated deep into the floors. These areas feel less like architecture and more like cave-forests, engineered ecosystems that have achieved a strange stability. The original metal frameworks are almost invisible, completely encased. The buildings have grown together into complexes, warrens of interconnected spaces where finding specific addresses requires local knowledge.

And people are born here now. The first generation of native Rootworks citizens—children who have never seen the surface, who think the smell of turpentine-plant and mineral dust is what air smells like, who navigate by bioluminescence and lamp-pools, who consider the nutrient-seals as normal as dogs. What will they become? What will they make of this place that their parents built? The question is open, still developing.

## The Relationship With Above

The Rootworks is not isolated. It depends on the surface for water, for certain materials, for human population. And the surface depends on the Rootworks for minerals, for processed materials, for the products of its unique bio-industry.

The shaft-connections are numerous. Major freight elevators for moving ore and materials. Personnel lifts for workers. Ventilation tunnels. Emergency egress routes. The surface settlement is modest—a mining town, warehouses, administrative buildings. The real city is below.

But there's a cultural separation developing. Surface workers who descend for shifts but live above. Permanent residents who consider the Rootworks home, who go to the surface only for annual leave or medical needs. And increasingly, those who simply don't go up at all. The surface is bright, loud, chaotic—overwhelming after the regulated environment below. Some residents have developed a quasi-agoraphobia about the open sky, the unbound horizons.

Trade flows through the shafts. Raw ore goes up. Water and electronics come down. But also: culture. Books, recordings, news. And in the other direction: reports from the Rootworks, scientific papers about the plants, recordings of the strange acoustic phenomena, samples of the new textiles and materials. The Rootworks is being studied, documented, theorized about. It's becoming known.

But known as what? A mining operation? An experimental settlement? A new form of urban ecosystem? A preview of how humanity might inhabit non-Earthlike environments? All of these. None completely. The Rootworks is still defining itself, still becoming.

## The Atmospheric Whole

Stand in one of the main galleries during a shift change. The electric lamps are at full brightness. Workers move through the space, their footsteps hollow on the wooden walkways. Conversations echo unpredictably—you hear laughter from somewhere, an argument, someone singing. The generators throb below, constant. The air smells of plant-oil and stone dust and human presence. A nutrient-seal watches from beside a doorway, its eyes reflecting green in the bioluminescence. Above, in the canopy, a bird makes its wrong-sounding call. Somewhere distant, a pump cycles, hissing.

The space around you is massive but bounded, open but enclosed. The plant-buildings rise on both sides, their surfaces irregular, slightly swollen, organic. The metal frameworks show through in places like bones under skin. The stone walls curve away into shadow. The light is layered—electric and biological and the warm shaft-light from above, creating pools and gradients, places to be seen and places to hide.

Everything is cool—the air, the stone, the wooden surfaces. Not cold, but definitively not warm. A temperature that requires layers, that makes your breath just barely visible in the dimmer sections.

You hear the bark-miners clicking in the walls. You smell food cooking somewhere—someone's dinner, the smell of oil and spices and protein. You feel the subtle vibration of the generators through your feet, a rhythm that you've already started to tune out but that's always there, always present.

This is the Rootworks. A city that is also a garden, an industry that is also an ecosystem, a human settlement that is becoming something new, something that doesn't have a name yet. It's being built and grown simultaneously, engineered and evolved, industrial and organic in the same breath.

It is strange but functional. Alien but inhabited. Not natural, not entirely artificial. Something else. Something emerging.

You descend the grown-wood stairs to the lower levels. The light dims as you go. The sounds of the upper gallery fade. The temperature rises slightly—the generator heat accumulates down here. You emerge into a quieter space, more residential. Doors are closed, marked with personal signs. Fungal-felt hangings mute the acoustics. A nutrient-seal sleeps curled against a wall, its flank rising and falling slowly.

This is someone's home. This is a city. This is the Rootworks, still becoming, still growing, still figuring out what it is and what it will be. Young. Strange. Alive in ways that exceed the merely biological.

Still being born.
