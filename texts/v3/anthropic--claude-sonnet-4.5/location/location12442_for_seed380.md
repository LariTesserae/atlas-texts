# The Drowned Switchyard

Beneath the surface, where the old rail junction once sorted freight between three cities now gone to grass above, the water has found its own level. Not flooding — that would suggest violence, sudden change. This happened slowly, over decades perhaps, the water table rising or the land subsiding by imperceptible degrees until the equilibrium settled here: eighteen inches of standing water across the entire basin, still as black glass in the main yards, gently flowing where the old drainage channels remember their purpose.

The coolness is constant. The temperature of deep earth, of water that hasn't seen sun in its recent history. Fifty-two degrees, perhaps fifty-three. The kind of cool that doesn't bite but gradually extracts warmth from anything that enters. Breath doesn't quite plume but close. Metal sweats. Concrete weeps in long dark streaks where moisture perpetually condenses on the vaulted ceilings, twenty feet above the waterline, pattering down in an arrhythmic percussion that might be mistaken for silence until you stop and listen.

The space has the proportions of a cathedral, but the aesthetic of industry. It's a sorting floor, a transfer hall, a place where railcars once rolled in from three different levels and were shunted, reorganized, reassembled into new configurations. The tracks remain, iron rails surfacing from the black water like the backs of sleeping creatures, running in parallel and convergence, dividing and rejoining. Where they curve, they catch what little light exists in gleaming arcs. Where they run straight, they disappear into the darkness at the boundaries of perception, perhaps sixty yards away, perhaps eighty — the space has definite edges but they're obscured, suggested rather than seen.

The water smells of mineral and metal, of limestone and rust, of things dissolved and held in solution. Not unpleasant. Ancient, patient, chemically balanced. It has had time to reach agreement with everything it touches.

---

The infrastructure is everywhere but spare. This was a functional space, not an aesthetic one, and it remains committed to that honesty. Massive concrete pillars rise from the water at regular intervals — every thirty feet in a grid — square in cross-section, three feet on a side, their surfaces aged to a mottled gray-green-black. The weathering is profound. Edges that were once sharp have rounded. Surfaces that were once smooth have become textured, porous, accepting. Rebar shows through in places, blooming rust like iron flowers, but structurally everything holds. This place has found its equilibrium with decay.

Steel I-beams crisscross overhead, supporting the vaulted ceiling. They've rusted to a deep orange-brown, but it's the rust of surfaces at rest, not the rust of active deterioration. Where water drips regularly, stalactites of iron oxide have formed, some as long as a foot, hanging like the pipes of a scattered organ.

The machinery remains. Not all of it, not the valuable parts that would have been salvaged, but the infrastructure too heavy or too integrated to remove. Switch mechanisms rise from the water like mechanical flowers — all wheels and levers and hinged plates, frozen in their last positions. Some still hold paint in protected creases: yellow safety warnings, red indicators, numbers stenciled in white. Track junction boxes, their lids missing, their copper innards long harvested, sit at intervals like opened skulls. Sections of elevated platform run along one wall, a steel grating catwalk ten feet above water level, corroded through in places but mostly intact.

One piece dominates: a overhead crane trolley system, its rails running the length of the space some fifteen feet up, the chain hoists hanging down at intervals, their hooks just kissing the water's surface. Twelve of them, spaced along two hundred feet of track, motionless. One hook has something caught on it — fabric, maybe, or some organic material that's become leather with time — and it moves slightly when air currents shift, the only moving thing attached to all that frozen machinery.

---

The vegetation is there, but it's not conquering. This is too dark, too cool, too mineral for exuberance. What grows does so with patience and specificity.

Moss, primarily. It's found every surface that stays damp, which is most of them. On the north wall — if there are directions here, and perhaps there are only depths and distances — a vertical garden of moss runs forty feet long and twelve feet high, a hanging tapestry in shades of gray-green to black-green, following the path of a permanent seep. It's thick, established, ancient-feeling. The kind of moss you could press your hand into and leave an impression that would slowly, over minutes, erase itself.

Bracket fungi emerge from the wooden elements that remain — old railroad ties that were left behind, sections of platform planking, a forgotten pallet turned vertical and wedged between machine parts. The fungi are pale, shelf-like, luminous in the dim light. Not bioluminescent, but so white they seem to generate brightness. They grow slowly enough that any individual specimen might be years old, decades old. One cluster on a tilted beam shows sixteen individual shelves, stacked like plates, the lowest nearly a foot across.

At the margins, where the water is shallowest — around pillars, along the base of walls — there's a plant that might be a fern but isn't quite. The fronds are smaller, darker, almost succulent in their thickness. They grow in rosettes, each plant separate from its neighbors by a foot or more, scattered rather than colonial. Their spores, when they release, which must be rarely, leave faint dust patterns on the water's surface, geometric ripples that persist for hours in the stillness.

One corner has a willow. Not a tree — the ceiling wouldn't permit it — but a willow shrub, growing from a crack where the floor has been breached by some old upheaval. It's perhaps six feet high, its thin branches hanging down to trail fingertips in the water. The leaves are sparse, yellowed, but unmistakably alive. How it survives in this light is unclear. Perhaps roots run deep, finding cracks that lead upward. Perhaps it lives on a different timeframe than surface willows, photosynthesizing so slowly that the faint light suffices. It moves slightly, constantly, responding to air currents too subtle to feel, a slow waving like kelp in a gentle current.

---

The fauna is the strange part. The abundant strange part.

The water is full of axolotls. Not the pet-store kind — these are larger, darker, more varied. They range from six inches to nearly two feet long, and they come in colors that seem wrong for animals: deep purple, midnight blue, a greenish-black that's almost iridescent, pure white with pink eyes, mottled gray-and-rust like the walls. They drift more than swim, hovering in the water with their frilled gills extended like elaborate collars, their small legs spread, their faces locked in that expression that looks like a smile but isn't, not exactly. They're everywhere. Dozens visible at any moment, hundreds probably in the space total. They congregate around the pillars, they rest on submerged rails, they gather beneath the hanging crane hooks for reasons unclear.

They make sounds. This seems impossible, but it's undeniable. Little clicks and chirps, just at the edge of hearing, produced somehow in their throats or gills or through some mechanism that doesn't have a name. The sounds echo strangely in the space, bouncing off water and stone and metal so that it's impossible to locate the source. A clicking might come from your left or straight ahead or behind. A chirp might come from the water at your feet or from the darkness sixty yards away. The sounds are infrequent enough that long silences stretch between them, but frequent enough that you're always waiting for the next one.

They feed on something. The water must have a population of smaller creatures — insects, larvae, crustaceans perhaps. Sometimes you see an axolotl turn its head with sudden precision, snap forward, and you know it's caught something though you never see the prey.

There are fish, too, but fewer. Pale things, some eyeless, some with eyes that are too large. They move in small groups, never more than five or six together, navigating between the rails with precision. They've been here long enough to go blind, or their ancestors were already adapted to caves. They're longer than they should be for their body depth, stretched-looking, like they've been pulled through water resistance for generations.

On the platforms, on the rails above water, on any dry surface, there are crickets. Thousands of them, perhaps tens of thousands, but so spread out across the space that you only ever see a handful at once. They're the regular brown-black kind, nothing modified or strange, but their presence here is itself strange. They chirp occasionally, but softly, and never in chorus. One cricket on a rail twenty feet away. Another on a pillar base. A third on the edge of a rusted gear housing. Individual voices in the dark, scattered, like stars.

And bats. They roost in the highest corners, in the darkest recesses of the vaulted ceiling. You hear them more than see them: the ultrasonic clicking just at the edge of perception, the occasional wing flutter, the movement of air when one drops from its perch and swoops low over the water, taking something — an insect, a cricket — with such precision you barely register the event. They're small bats, not the dramatic kind. Practical. Efficient. They've made a working relationship with this place.

The strangest fauna is the crabs. Pale, almost translucent, the size of a hand, they emerge from gaps in the concrete, from spaces between machine parts, from underneath tilted panels and inside empty junction boxes. They're hermit crabs, but down here they've made shells from human debris: bottle caps, sections of pipe, small gears, the bent bowls of industrial light fixtures. One wears what appears to be the bell housing of an old telephone. Another has somehow wedged itself into a section of brass valve. They move slowly, deliberately, across any surface above water, scavenging what the crickets leave, what the bats drop, what the water deposits. They don't flee when you approach. They stop, pull slightly into their industrial shells, wait. Patient as the stones.

---

The light comes from apertures. This isn't a natural cave — it's a human-made space that's been partially sealed. There are ventilation shafts, too small and too distant to be escape routes, that let in threads of daylight from the surface. The light comes down at angles, in shafts when conditions are right, in diffuse glows when they're not. It's enough to see by, barely. Enough that your eyes adjust and the space resolves into shades of gray and darkness rather than pure black.

The light changes across the day, but slowly, indirectly. Morning might be marginally brighter than evening, or maybe it's your imagination. The shafts move, imperceptibly, as the sun tracks overhead in a world you can't see. Sometimes the light catches water vapor in the air and becomes momentarily visible as a column, a solid thing you could almost touch, full of floating motes and drifting insects.

There are other lights, artificial but rare. Some emergency fixtures remain on the ceiling, their casings intact even if their bulbs are long dead. But three of them — just three, spread across the entire space — still function. Weak fluorescent tubes, probably on some battery system or trickle-fed by panels on the surface, flickering on for a few hours each day at random times, or perhaps on a schedule that no longer corresponds to human logic. When they light, they cast a greenish-white glow that reaches maybe thirty feet, illuminating circles of water and machinery before fading back into darkness. The axolotls seem to avoid these lights when they're active, or perhaps that's imagination too.

The water itself sometimes seems luminous, but it's not — it's just reflective. When light hits it at the right angle, it mirrors the ceiling, creating the illusion that the space extends downward as far as it extends upward, a perfect symmetry, two vaults meeting at a common surface. This is disorienting when you're wading through it, trying to place your feet on submerged rails or flat concrete.

---

The sounds deserve more attention than they've received. The place is quiet, but it's not silent, and the distinction matters.

The water dripping is constant but scattered. Plink, plink, plonk, plip — a thousand different voices as drops fall from different heights onto different surfaces. Water into water is a soft sound, easily overlooked. Water onto metal is sharper. Water onto stone is somewhere between. The echoes blur together into something like white noise, but quieter, more textured. You can listen to it and hear patterns that might be there or might be your mind organizing chaos.

The axolotl sounds are the most frequent animal presence. Those small clicks and chirps, unpredictable, directionless. Sometimes one will vocalize and another will answer, or seem to answer, from across the space. Communication or coincidence?

The crickets add their slow voices. Never a full chorus, never that rising and falling wall of sound from a summer night above ground. Just individual crickets, serenading the darkness at their own pace. One will chirp for thirty seconds, then stop. Silence for three minutes. Another begins, from a different quarter. They create a sense of space, of distance, of geometry. You can map the room by listening to where the crickets are.

The bats are nearly silent. The flutter of wings when they move. The faint impact when they take a cricket. Once or twice an hour, a vocalization — not a screech, but a small sound, social, checking in with the others perhaps.

Water moving is rare but noticeable. The flow through the old drainage channels creates a soft rushing, but so quiet you can only hear it if you're within ten feet and nothing else is happening. Sometimes a fish breaks the surface — a small splash, immediately swallowed by the space. Sometimes something falls from above — a piece of rust, a fragment of concrete — and the plunk echoes for seconds.

Your own sounds are intrusive. Footsteps on a steel grating. The splash of wading through water. Breathing. The creak of wet fabric. You become conscious of yourself as the loudest thing present, and you move more carefully, trying not to disturb the acoustic equilibrium.

Once a day, maybe more often, maybe less — time is hard to track here — there's a deep sound. A groan, a creak, a settling. It comes from the infrastructure itself, the building breathing, metal expanding or contracting with temperature shifts too small to feel, loads redistributing across beams, water finding new channels through concrete. The sound is low, subsonic almost, felt in the chest as much as heard. It lasts five seconds, ten seconds, then fades. The first time you hear it you freeze, instinctively uncertain whether the ceiling is about to collapse. But it always passes, and nothing changes, and you realize this place has been making this sound for decades, a century maybe, breathing in and out with patient respiration.

---

The rules of the world, as they reveal themselves:

Water seeks its level, but the level is not uniform. There are high spots where platforms remain above the surface, connected by the steel grating catwalks. There are low spots where the floor drops and the water is deeper — three feet, four feet, uncertain. The deepest spot is probably near the center, where the largest sump would have been, but it's impossible to know without diving, and who would dive into that cold darkness?

Time moves differently here. Not faster or slower, but denser, more textured. Minutes expand. Hours compress. You can't tell if you've been here twenty minutes or two hours. The light doesn't change enough to mark time. The sounds are irregular. Your body's clock becomes unreliable in the constant cool and dimness. You could wade from one end of the space to the other and feel like you've made an expedition, or you could sit on a platform for what feels like ten minutes and discover you've been still for an hour.

The air is breathable, obviously, since you're breathing it. But it's thick somehow, rich with moisture and minerals. It coats the inside of your lungs. You can taste it slightly — stone and metal and the almost-sweetness of the moss. If you stay long enough, you wonder if you might begin to rust from the inside out, or grow moss in your lungs, or adapt like the fish with their stretched bodies and too-large eyes.

The space is finite but difficult to exit. The entrances are there — ramps that lead upward, old service corridors, the shaft where the trains once descended. But they're at the edges, in the darkness, and finding them requires navigation through the forest of pillars and machinery. The water makes everything more difficult. Rails that seem solid turn out to be tilted or broken. Equipment that looks stable shifts when you put weight on it. The floor is uneven in ways you can't predict. You develop a walking rhythm: slow steps, testing weight, watching for submerged obstacles.

The ecosystem is balanced. The crickets feed on something — detritus, fungus, each other. The bats feed on the crickets. The axolotls feed on smaller aquatic life. The crabs feed on whatever falls or dies. The plants grow on light and minerals and patience. Nothing is excessive. Nothing is starving. The abundance is spread thin, but it's real abundance, not the edge of scarcity. This system has had time — decades, generations — to find its working arrangement.

The strangeness is not supernatural. Everything here can be explained by adaptation, by time, by the peculiar conditions of this specific place. But the explanations don't make it feel less strange. The axolotls with their colors and their clicks. The crabs with their industrial shells. The willow in the corner, living on starlight and stubbornness. The space itself, a human place that has become something else, not quite reclaimed by nature but no longer serving human purposes. A place in between, in balance, in its own state.

---

You stand on a platform, looking out over the submerged rails. An axolotl the color of deep purple drifts below, its gills fanning slowly. A cricket chirps from somewhere to your left. Water drips — plink, plonk, plip — and the echoes blur together into almost-music. The hanging crane hooks sway slightly in a current you can't feel, and one of them has that piece of leather or fabric attached, moving like a slow pendulum, marking time in a place where time has become negotiable.

Above, in the darkness of the vaulted ceiling, bats roost and rust blooms and water condenses on cold concrete. Below, in the black water, fish navigate by senses you don't possess and axolotls hang suspended like strange angels. Around you, the machinery sleeps its standing sleep, rusted but not ruined, aged into a new kind of purpose: being habitat, being structure, being the bones of this underwater cathedral.

The building groans, that deep settling sound, and you feel it in your ribs. The sound of a place being itself, being in balance, being established. The sound of equilibrium.

In the far distance, barely visible, one of the fluorescent tubes flickers to life, casting its greenish glow over a circle of water and metal and moss. It will burn for three hours, maybe four, and then it will go dark again, and the dim natural light from the ventilation shafts will be enough, just enough, the way it always is.

You breathe the thick air. You listen to the scattered sounds. You feel the cool settle into your bones. You stand in the Drowned Switchyard, and it stands around you, patient and strange and utterly itself.
