# The Porcelain Ranges

The mountains rise in tiers that defy immediate comprehension—not because they are impossibly tall, though they are extreme, but because they seem to have been *settled* into their verticality over such vast spans of time that height itself has become a kind of patience. These are the Porcelain Ranges, and they extend across a territory large enough to generate their own atmospheric phenomena: thermal columns that spiral upward in the warm, dry air; pressure differentials that create zones of perfect stillness beside zones of subtle, persistent movement.

The material is calcite, gypsum, and something else—minerals that formed in the absence of water as we commonly know it, through processes involving sublimation and vapor-phase chemistry. The mountains are white, cream, pale grey, with occasional strata of rose quartz and bands of translucent selenite that catch the light. Over uncountable years, these peaks have weathered into smooth, rounded forms punctuated by sharp vertical fractures. The erosion here is not the work of water but of thermal cycling, chemical weathering from dry atmospheric compounds, and something like wind—though the wind itself is odd, carrying suspended particles that ring faintly against the mineral surfaces, almost like the sound of distant bells made from fingernails tapping porcelain.

Between the peaks lie vast basins and plateaus. These spaces are *sparse*—there is a breathing quality to the emptiness, a sense that the distance between things is not absence but a kind of presence in itself. Scattered across these reaches are the remnants and current workings of an industrial civilization that has adapted to extreme elevation and aridity.

## The Built Presence

The settlements cling to plateau edges and nestle into the fractures between peaks. They are urban in organization—you can see the logic of streets, the layering of construction, the infrastructure of a society that has learned to build vertically and sparingly. But there is no sprawl here. Each settlement is a compact node of habitation, connected to others by thin railways and cable systems that span the gaps between plateaus like the work of mechanical spiders.

The architecture is primarily mineral-brick and cast ceramic, sometimes reinforced with metal frameworks that have oxidized into pale greens and rust-browns. Buildings are typically three to five stories, with deep-set windows to manage the thermal environment. The warm air here can be surprisingly comfortable—the elevation creates a temperate zone despite the latitude and the lack of moisture. But temperatures swing between day and night, so walls are thick, and many structures have adjustable louvers made of thin stone panels.

The industrial elements are scattered but unmistakable. There are processing facilities where minerals are refined—you can see the tall, narrow smokestacks that release heat-shimmer and occasional pale vapors into the air. The machinery tends toward the mechanical rather than the electronic: great grinding mills turned by geothermal engines, cable-ways that haul mineral loads up impossible grades, presses and kilns that transform raw stone into building material. The sound of these operations is usually distant, a low rhythmic grinding or the occasional clank of metal on metal, but the settlements themselves are mostly quiet. Footsteps on stone streets, the creak of cable systems, voices that carry in the thin, dry air but seem to dissipate rather than echo.

## The Fauna

The creatures here are abundant and deeply strange.

Most obvious are the *thermal gliders*—organisms that might be animals, though their biology is uncertain. They resemble enormous, translucent kites or manta rays, with bodies made of something between chitin and mineral lattice, stretched into vast, membranous wings that can span ten meters or more. They soar on the thermal columns, sometimes alone, sometimes in loose congregations of twenty or thirty. Their bodies are nearly transparent, with internal structures visible as delicate shadows—branching filaments that might be circulation systems or something else entirely. They feed by filtering the air itself, drawing in the suspended mineral particles and the microscopic crystalline structures that proliferate in this environment. When they pass overhead, they make a sound like wind through a very fine mesh, and occasionally a high, glassy clicking.

In the valleys and on the plateaus, there are the *stone-walkers*—quadrupedal creatures with bodies encased in segmented mineral shells that they have somehow grown or accumulated. They are roughly the size of large dogs, and they move with a peculiar, deliberate gait, each footfall producing a soft *tick* against the ground. They consume the thin crusts of chemical precipitate that form on rock surfaces overnight, scraping with specialized mouthparts. Their shells are beautiful—layered in bands of color, sometimes incorporating translucent crystals that catch the light.

Then there are the *vertical swimmers*—perhaps the strangest of all. In the deep fractures between peaks, where air pressure and chemistry create unusual conditions, these organisms navigate as though the air were a denser medium. They are eel-like, long and sinuous, perhaps two meters in length, with bodies covered in fine, hair-like projections. They undulate through the air in the fracture zones, feeding on something—energy differentials, perhaps, or chemical gradients. Locals largely ignore them, but visitors often stop to watch them weave through the vertical spaces, their movements hypnotic and impossible.

There are smaller creatures too: colonies of crystalline growths that might be alive in some sense, spreading slowly across rock faces in geometric patterns; things like insects made of articulated mineral plates that process stone dust and excrete tiny, perfect spheres of waste; and in the settlements themselves, a kind of symbiotic organism that lives in the walls of buildings, consuming structural stress and depositing microscopic reinforcements. Whether this last was engineered or naturally occurring, no one seems certain anymore.

## Atmosphere and Light

The light is clear but not harsh. The sun—or whatever provides illumination here—casts distinct shadows, and the air is dry enough that visibility extends for vast distances. You can see storms forming on the horizon, though "storm" here means something different: slow-moving fronts where temperature and pressure shift, creating zones of intense thermal activity. The gliders gather at these fronts, riding the turbulence in great spiraling masses.

The sky is usually a pale blue-grey, sometimes fading to almost white near the horizons. At certain times, when the angle of light is right, the selenite bands in the mountains glow from within, and the whole range seems to emit a soft, warm luminescence. This effect can last for hours during what might be called evening, though the day-night cycle here is gradual, the light fading and returning in slow, patient sweeps.

The air smells of hot stone and something faintly acrid—not unpleasant, but mineral, like the interior of a cave that has never known moisture. When the wind picks up, it carries a fine dust that doesn't irritate so much as it accumulates, giving everything a matte patina.

## The Rules of This World

Water is not entirely absent—there must be some moisture in the air, locked in the biology of the creatures, perhaps cycled through industrial processes—but it is never visible. No rivers, no lakes, no rain. The inhabitants drink something processed from mineral substrates or distilled from atmospheric chemistry in the industrial facilities. It has a faintly chalky taste and leaves no residue.

The ecology runs on chemical potential and thermal energy. The sun provides warmth, the mountains release geothermal heat from below, and the temperature differential drives everything. The creatures have metabolisms based on mineral processing, crystallization, and some form of energy harvesting that might involve electrostatic phenomena or chemistry that borders on alchemy.

Gravity feels slightly lighter than expected for the scale, but the thinness of the air at these elevations balances it—you can move easily, but exertion brings rapid fatigue. Locals tend toward slow, deliberate movements. There is a cultural patience here that mirrors the geological patience of the landscape.

The settlements are old—not ancient, but established. Generations have lived here, adapted, refined their industrial processes. The economy seems to run on mineral extraction and refinement, though what they trade and with whom is unclear. There are cable-stations that suggest connections to lowlands far beyond the horizons, but the distances are so vast that travel is measured in weeks or months.

## Specific Locations

In the basin called *Clarity*, a settlement of perhaps five thousand people clusters around a geothermal vent system that powers a ceramics industry. The kilns run constantly, and the smell of hot clay and glaze permeates the streets. At night—when the light gradually diminishes to a deep, soft twilight—the kiln fires glow through their ventilation slits, and the settlement looks like a constellation settled onto the plateau. The stone-walkers are common here, moving between buildings, tolerated by the residents who leave offerings of mineral-rich dust in shallow bowls.

On the highest accessible peak, called *The Threshold*, there is a single structure: a tower built from translucent ceramic blocks. It is thirty meters tall, and its purpose is unclear—perhaps astronomical, perhaps religious, perhaps simply a marker. Thermal gliders roost on its upper sections at certain times, their bodies glowing faintly in the twilight, their clicking song audible for kilometers.

In a fracture known as *The Descent*, vertical swimmers are so abundant that they form shimmering curtains of movement. A small group of researchers (or perhaps philosophers) have built a platform system here, suspended on cables, and they spend their days watching the swimmers, recording their patterns. They believe the swimmers are navigating information—that the chemistry in the fracture is a kind of language, and the swimmers are reading it. Whether this is science or mysticism is uncertain, possibly both.

## The Strangeness

The weirdness accumulates gradually. At first, the landscape seems merely unusual—dry mountains, adapted life, resourceful people. But the longer you remain, the more you notice the things that don't quite align with expectation.

The way the thermal gliders sometimes hover motionless for hours, as though suspended in time rather than air.

The geometric precision of the crystalline colonies, which suggest intention but grow at rates that seem impossibly slow for conscious design.

The occasional finds of structures in the deepest valleys—ruins, perhaps, but made from materials that don't match the local geology, and weathered in ways that suggest they predate the mountains themselves, which is temporally impossible unless the mountains are much younger than they seem, or the ruins are much older, or time here is more flexible than it should be.

The residents speak of *the Underneath*—not with fear, but with a kind of respectful acknowledgment. There are ventilation shafts in some settlements that draw air from below, and sometimes the air carries sounds: rhythmic pulsing, like vast machinery, or breathing. No one has ever descended far enough to find the source. The common wisdom is that the mountains are not simply geological—that they were *made*, though by what and when is a matter of theological debate rather than historical knowledge.

And then there are the *silent hours*—periods, unpredictable but occurring every few weeks, when all sound dampens. The machinery continues to run, but you cannot hear it. Footsteps produce no sound. Voices emerge as whispers even when shouted. The thermal gliders descend to the ground during these hours and remain perfectly still, their bodies pressed flat against the stone. The effect lasts anywhere from ten minutes to several hours, and then sound returns gradually, like a volume control being raised from zero.

## The Living Pattern

Despite the strangeness, or perhaps because of it, there is a stability here. The settlements have found their balance with the environment. The fauna have their niches and cycles. The industrial processes hum along, extracting and refining, producing building materials and trade goods and the mysterious processed water that sustains life.

People live entire lives here, generation after generation. Children play in the sparse streets, their games adapted to vertical geography—climbing competitions on designated cliff faces, glider-counting contests, collections of the perfect spheres excreted by the mineral insects. There are schools where geology and thermal dynamics are core subjects, where the biology of the local fauna is studied with reverence and incomplete understanding.

There are festivals timed to the glowing of the selenite bands, when entire settlements gather on viewing platforms to watch the mountains illuminate themselves. Music is rare—the mostly-quiet culture doesn't favor loud instruments—but there is a tradition of stone chimes, carefully tuned pieces of mineral hung in arrangements that ring softly in the thermal winds.

The urban infrastructure continues to expand, slowly. New buildings rise at the rate of perhaps ten per year across all settlements combined. New cable lines are strung across gaps that previously could only be crossed by the most dedicated travelers. The industrial facilities become more efficient, their processes refined through generations of accumulated knowledge.

But the expansion is careful, almost tentative. There is a sense that this civilization knows it exists in a landscape that tolerates rather than welcomes it—that the mountains, the fauna, the strange physics of this place have their own priorities, and human presence here is contingent on maintaining a certain respectful distance.

## Erosion and Time

The weathering here tells its own story. The rounded peaks, the smooth basin floors, the way fractures have widened and then stabilized—all of it speaks of processes that have reached equilibrium. This is not a young landscape still finding its form, nor an ancient one crumbling into entropy. It is in its own state, its own time.

The buildings weather too, their surfaces slowly smoothing, their colors fading into the palette of the mountains. Old structures become difficult to distinguish from natural formations. Sometimes, when surveying for new construction, builders discover that what they thought was a promising rock formation is actually a building from centuries past, so thoroughly integrated into the landscape that only the presence of right angles gives it away.

The thermal gliders are sometimes found deceased, their bodies crumpled in remote locations. Over time, they mineralize completely, becoming part of the stone. Cross-sections of certain rock layers reveal glider fossils—but from how long ago? The geology suggests millions of years, but the gliders are clearly thriving now. Either the species is impossibly ancient and unchanging, or the mineralization process is vastly accelerated, or the rocks are younger than they seem. No consensus has emerged.

## The Sound of It

Stand on a plateau edge in midday. The wind is a soft, constant whisper, carrying that faint mineral chime—the particles striking stone. From a settlement several kilometers distant, the periodic *clank* of a mill, muffled by distance and air. The clicking of thermal gliders overhead, scattered and arhythmic. Your own breathing, audible in the quiet. The creak of your boots on stone. Occasionally, a stone-walker passing nearby, its shell segments making a soft *tik-tik-tik* as it walks.

At night, the sounds diminish further. The mills often shut down, the cable systems fall still. The gliders settle into their roosts and silence their clicking. What remains is the thermal wind—almost subsonic, more felt than heard—and the occasional distant voice from a settlement, carrying with improbable clarity across the empty spaces.

During the silent hours, even these fade, and you are left with the experience of sound's absence—not silence, exactly, but a negative space where sound should be, a void that presses against your ears.

## Ecology Without Water

The chemistry here operates in gas phase and solid phase, with only trace liquids involved. The fauna process minerals directly—grinding them, dissolving them in internal solvents that aren't water, restructuring them. The excretions are often purer than the intake, creating slow cycles of refinement and concentration.

The crystalline colonies grow at interfaces where two different mineral surfaces meet, or where atmospheric chemistry creates unusual conditions. They might be the base of the entire ecological system—primary producers that extract energy from thermal and chemical gradients, structuring it into forms that other organisms can utilize.

The vertical swimmers seem to feed on the crystalline colonies in the fractures, or perhaps on something even more ephemeral—voltage potentials, pH gradients, information encoded in molecular distributions. Their biology is frustratingly difficult to study; captured specimens mineralize almost immediately, as though their existence depends on the precise conditions within the fractures.

The thermal gliders are filter feeders, but what they filter is itself strange—microscopic crystalline structures that might be spores from the colonies, or might be inorganic particles that happen to carry trace nutrients, or might be something else entirely. Their digestive systems, when examined, contain chambers filled with what appears to be a dry, static electricity that somehow processes the filtered material.

There are no plants because there is no water-based photosynthesis, but the crystalline colonies perform an analogous function, capturing energy from the environment and structuring it into forms that support a food web. The system is low-energy compared to water-based ecologies, which may explain the sparse distribution and the slow, deliberate pace of life here.

## The Industrial Integration

The settlements don't fight the environment—they integrate with it. The geothermal vents that power the machinery are natural, and the technology simply taps into them. The materials for construction are local, processed through kilns and mills but fundamentally unchanged in their nature. The cable systems mimic the flight paths of thermal gliders, following the same atmospheric corridors.

Some industrial processes seem to have become ecological processes. The waste heat from refineries creates micro-climates where certain crystalline colonies thrive more abundantly. The vibrations from mills crack rock surfaces in ways that accelerate weathering, creating fresh mineral deposits for stone-walkers to feed on. The settlements have become part of the landscape's metabolism.

There is a factory in the settlement of *Perseverance* that produces something—no one seems able to explain exactly what—that is essential to maintaining the cable systems. The process involves electrifying mineral dust in vacuum chambers, creating microscopic structures that are then embedded in the cable fibers. These structures apparently self-repair, or repair each other, extending the life of the cables from years to decades. The organism in the walls that consumes structural stress and deposits reinforcement may be related, or may be a natural analog, or may be something that escaped from the factory generations ago and naturalized.

The line between technology and ecology, between built and grown, between industrial and organic, has blurred here. Everything participates in the same slow, patient processes.

## Weather and Climate

The scale of the Porcelain Ranges creates its own atmospheric dynamics. Thermal columns rise from the sun-warmed peaks and the geothermal basins, creating predictable updrafts that the gliders exploit. Where these columns meet cooler air from the higher elevations, you get shear zones where the air itself becomes visible—not through moisture condensing, but through suspended particles organizing into flowing patterns that catch the light.

Temperature inversions are common, trapping thin layers of warmer air between colder strata. During these inversions, sound carries differently, and you can sometimes hear settlements or industrial operations from impossible distances—voices and machinery from hundreds of kilometers away, crisp and clear as though nearby.

The storms are slow and vast. A pressure front might take three days to cross a basin, during which the wind gradually builds from whisper to steady blow, the temperature shifts by ten or fifteen degrees, and the light changes quality—becoming more diffuse, or sharper, or taking on a faint chromatic aberration as atmospheric density varies. The gliders become agitated during storm fronts, gathering in huge congregations and performing what might be mating behaviors or might be simple exuberance—diving and soaring in complex, interweaving patterns.

There are no seasons in the traditional sense, but there are climate cycles that operate on longer timescales. Every few years, the average temperature rises or falls by several degrees for a period of months, and the ecology responds. The gliders become more or less abundant, the crystalline colonies advance or retreat, the vertical swimmers change their distribution in the fractures.

## Verticality as Culture

Living at extreme elevation shapes everything. The people here think in terms of altitude as naturally as others think in terms of distance. Settlements are located not just by their coordinates but by their elevation, and social identity is partially tied to whether you live in the high plateaus (cooler, thinner air, closer to the glider roosts) or the mid-level basins (warmer, more geothermal activity, better for industry).

Architecture is three-dimensional in ways that flatten landscapes never require. Buildings connect at multiple levels, with bridges and tunnels linking structures that appear separate when viewed from any single perspective. Streets spiral and switch back, and there are entire districts accessible only by climbing or by cable lift.

Children learn climbing before they learn to read. Safety systems—ropes, harnesses, anchors—are as common as furniture. The culture has an elaborate etiquette around vertical space: who has right-of-way on a climbing route, how to signal when you're above someone, the proper way to pass on a narrow ledge.

There are professions unique to this place: cable-walkers who inspect and maintain the transportation systems, thermal-mappers who chart the updrafts for glider-watchers and industrial planners, fracture-guides who lead research expeditions into the deep cracks where vertical swimmers congregate.

Death from falling is common enough to have its own funeral traditions. Bodies are placed on designated platforms where thermal gliders will eventually claim them, carrying them upward on the thermals until they are lost from sight. Whether the gliders actually consume the remains or simply transport them elsewhere is unknown, but the platforms are always empty by the next day, and this is considered proper.

## The Question of Origins

There are debates, ongoing and unresolved, about how this place came to be. The religious faction believes the mountains were raised by intentional design—that the Underneath is the machinery of creation, still operating, maintaining the landscape according to some ancient plan. The rationalists argue for exotic geology and natural processes that simply aren't fully understood yet.

There are the ruins in the deep valleys, which neither faction can adequately explain. And there are the occasional objects that turn up during excavation or erosion—artifacts made from materials that don't exist here, or that shouldn't exist anywhere: tools made from solidified light, containers that have volume but no mass, fragments of text in languages that look almost familiar but can't be deciphered.

Some scholars believe this place is a remnant—that it was once wetter, once supported different life, and what exists now is the adapted survivors of some ancient catastrophe. Others believe it is emergent—that the conditions here are creating new forms of life, new types of matter, that this is an origin point rather than an ending.

The majority of people don't concern themselves with these questions. They live their lives, work their industries, raise their children, and accept that the Porcelain Ranges are simply what they are—vast, strange, stable, and home.

## The Temporal Quality

Everything here moves in slow time. Even the abundant fauna are not *busy*—they glide, they walk deliberately, they swim through air with hypnotic patience. The construction of new buildings takes years. The expansion of a settlement is measured in decades. The erosion processes that maintain the landscape operate on timescales of millennia.

This seeps into human behavior. Speech is slower here, with longer pauses between thoughts. People stand still more often, simply observing. Decisions are made after extensive consideration. The culture values persistence over speed, endurance over intensity.

Even the industrial machinery, despite being mechanical and purpose-driven, operates at measured pace. The mills grind slowly. The kilns heat gradually. The refineries process materials in batch cycles that run for days. There is no sense of urgency, no drive for efficiency beyond what is sustainable.

This makes the silent hours even more uncanny—not because they disrupt frantic activity, but because they interrupt the slow, steady rhythm that is the baseline state of existence here. When sound vanishes, the awareness of time itself becomes uncertain. Without the ambient sounds marking the passage of moments, you can stand for what feels like minutes and discover that an hour has passed, or vice versa.

## The View from Threshold

If you climb to the top of the translucent tower on Threshold peak—the highest accessible point in the surveyed Ranges—you can see approximately three hundred kilometers in every direction. The mountains extend to the horizon in all directions, tier upon tier of pale stone and vertical fractures, basins and plateaus scattered with settlements whose buildings catch the light like scattered crystals.

The atmosphere is clear enough that you can distinguish individual thermal columns rising from the distant peaks, and if you watch long enough, you can see the slow migration of glider congregations from one thermal to another, like schools of fish moving through a very slow ocean.

At this elevation, you are above most of the suspended particles, and the wind carries almost no sound. You can feel it, but not hear it. The vertical swimmers occasionally rise to this height, emerging from the fractures to glide through the open air before descending again, and they pass close enough to touch, their movements creating pressure waves you can feel against your skin but that make no sound.

From here, the settlements look both permanent and temporary—established enough to have architectural identity, but so sparse against the vast mineral landscape that they seem like they could be erased by a single storm, though storms here have no such power.

You can see the cables connecting settlements, thin dark lines against the pale stone, geometric and purposeful. And beyond the furthest settlements, where no cables reach, the mountains continue, unexplored or long-abandoned, holding whatever they hold—more ruins, more strange materials, more questions that accumulate at the same patient rate as the weathering of stone.

The thermal gliders roost on the tower around you, dozens of them, their translucent bodies glowing faintly in the warm, clear light. They make their clicking sounds, arhythmic and individual, and collectively it becomes something like music, or like the sound of the landscape thinking to itself.

You can stay here for hours, and time passes differently at this elevation, in this clarity, surrounded by the patient accumulation of strangeness that is the Porcelain Ranges—a place that exists in its own state, on its own terms, weathered into balance between the industrial and the impossible, the urban and the utterly alien, the established and the eternally uncertain.
