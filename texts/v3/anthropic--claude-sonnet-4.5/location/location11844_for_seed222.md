# The Suspension Fields of Qen Mora

The plateau exists at an altitude where breathing becomes a practiced thing—not starved exactly, but deliberate. Around three thousand meters, perhaps more in places where the land buckles upward into broad, worn domes. The air is warm despite the height, heated by something below or beyond, maintaining a constant temperature that would be comfortable at sea level but here feels luxurious, almost improbable.

The ground is ancient basalt, weathered down to smooth undulations, dark gray verging on charcoal, shot through with veins of rust-orange and pale green where metals have oxidized over immense spans. The rock has been here so long it has found its final form—no sharp edges remain, no recent fractures. Everything curves. The terrain rises and falls in long, patient swells like a petrified ocean, each rise taking minutes to crest on foot, each shallow valley opening into the next with geometries that feel inevitable rather than random. In the distance, perhaps fifteen or twenty kilometers away depending on which direction you face, isolated buttes stand like weathered teeth, their bases eroded into impossible thinness, their tops still broad and flat.

Water arrives but does not stay. Every third or fourth day, rain comes in from the east—you can watch it approaching for an hour, a gray veil moving across the plateau. The rain is warm and brief, twenty minutes at most, falling in fat drops that strike the stone with soft percussion before gathering into temporary streams. The basalt has been worn into subtle channels, ancient watersheds that guide the rainfall into particular pathways. For an hour after rain, these channels run with clear water that vanishes into cracks and fissures, drawn down into the plateau's interior. By the time the sun returns, only scattered pools remain in the deepest depressions—puddles that sit like curved mirrors, slowly evaporating, leaving behind crusts of salt and mineral that build up over seasons in delicate cauliflower formations.

In places where water lingers longest, condensation gathers overnight. You'll find it in the morning clinging to certain stones, stones that seem to be positioned deliberately though they likely aren't—dark rocks that cool faster than their surroundings, becoming collection points for airborne moisture. Small ecosystems exist in the radius around these stones, dependent on those few milliliters of water.

The vegetation knows its place here. Sparse doesn't mean absent—it means considered, strategic. There are tussocks of wire-grass, thin as fiber optics, growing in circular patches where water predictably pools. The grass is a dusty silver-green, waxy, capable of folding itself nearly flat against the stone during dry periods. Between tussocks: five meters of bare rock. Ten meters. Twenty. The spacing feels musical, rhythmic without being regular.

In the channels where water runs most reliably, there are other things. Low shrubs, barely knee-height, with thick, segmented stems that look more like stacked vertebrae than wood. Their leaves are tiny, almost scale-like, deep green-black, oriented to catch moisture from the air rather than sunlight alone. These shrubs grow alone—you never see two within touching distance. Each has claimed its territory, and the pattern holds across the plateau.

There are also the suspension structures. This is where strangeness enters, where the landscape shows its hand.

Growing from certain rocks, always basalt, always at least two meters across, are formations that might be plants or might be something else. They begin as a circular node, a disk the size of a dinner plate, pale gray and fibrous. From this node extend threads—hundreds of them, thousands as the structure matures—stretching outward and upward at angles. The threads are thinner than spider silk near their tips, thick as braided cable near the base. They're nearly invisible until the light hits them right, then they flash prismatic, colors that don't quite match the spectrum you expect.

These threads extend for meters, sometimes five or six meters from the central node, creating roughly spherical zones of suspension. And suspended within these zones are objects.

Small rocks, caught somehow in the thread-field. Seeds. Dead insects. Occasionally something larger—a piece of wood, though wood is rare here. The objects don't rest on the threads; they hang in space within the sphere, rotating slowly, suspended by principles that aren't immediately apparent. When rain comes, water droplets enter these suspension fields and stop, hanging like beads of glass, sometimes for hours after the rain has ended. The suspended water slowly migrates toward the central node, traveling along paths you can't quite see, feeding the structure.

The threads hum. Not audibly, but if you place your hand near one—not touching, they're fragile—you feel it in your bones, a sensation like standing near high-voltage lines, but gentler, stranger. Some frequencies you hear as sound, very soft, overlapping tones that create interference patterns. Walking through an area where multiple suspension structures grow creates layers of near-silence punctuated by these whispered harmonic clusters.

Around the suspension structures, fauna gathers. The plateau supports surprising animal life given its apparent austerity.

Birds, first: small raptors with long, narrow wings built for the thin air, feathers dark on top and pale underneath. They ride thermals that rise from the warm basalt, hunting in slow spirals. Their calls are sharp and infrequent—this is not a place of bird chatter. When they vocalize, it's single notes, declarative, carrying across kilometers. They nest on the flat-topped buttes, but they hunt here among the suspension fields.

There are also beetles. Abundant beetles. They emerge around the water-stones at dawn and dusk, black-shelled and palm-sized, moving with surprising quickness across the basalt. The beetles are harvesters—they visit the suspension structures and somehow extract material from them, taking away tiny fibers which they weave into cases for their larvae. You find these cases in rock crevices: small, translucent packets that shimmer like the threads themselves, each containing a single white grub.

Herd animals cross the plateau in groups of seven to twenty. They're roughly deer-sized but heavier-built, with broad feet adapted for stone rather than soil. Their hide is thick and gray, wrinkled like elephant skin, good for retaining moisture. They move between water-points with purpose, arriving at pools shortly after rain with timing that suggests sophisticated awareness. They ignore most vegetation but specifically browse the shrubs, taking only the oldest leaves, maintaining rather than destroying each plant. They're silent animals—you hear their footsteps, the click of hoof on stone, but they make no calls, no social sounds. Communication happens through gesture, through the tilt of a head or shift of weight.

Predators follow the herds. Low-slung, six-legged, built like elongated badgers but larger, perhaps wolf-mass. Their fur is tawny on the back, white underneath, and they move with a flowing gait that uses all six limbs in rotation. They hunt alone or in pairs, patient, waiting for an animal to separate or weaken. A kill happens once or twice a month in any given area—the predator-prey balance here is exact, evolved into equilibrium. After a kill, beetles arrive within hours to claim what the predator leaves.

And there are the observers. This is where the advanced technology reveals itself, though "technology" might be the wrong word.

Scattered across the plateau, never closer than a kilometer apart, are objects that could be machines or could be life-forms or could be something that makes that distinction obsolete. They appear as smooth shapes, roughly two meters tall, form-language somewhere between sculpture and crystal growth. They're made of something that looks like dark metal but responds to touch like warm clay for just a moment before firming again. Their surfaces show patterns—not writing exactly, but information, configurations that change slowly, cycling through states over hours or days.

These objects don't move, but they're not inert. Around each one, approximately three meters out, physical laws soften slightly. Gravity remains, but friction becomes negotiable. Thermodynamics continues but with margins for dialogue. Small objects left in these zones sometimes rearrange themselves into configurations of lower entropy rather than higher. A scattered pile of stones might, over the course of a week, gather itself into a cairn. Water in a shallow depression might spontaneously separate into warm and cool layers rather than equalizing. These effects are subtle, quiet, never dramatic—nothing levitates, nothing bursts into impossible flame. But the local physics is being gently rewritten according to aesthetics that include function but aren't limited to it.

The objects seem to watch. Not with eyes, but with something like attention. Birds perch on them without fear. Beetles navigate around them with slight detours. The herd animals pause when passing, not fleeing, not approaching, just acknowledging.

Human visitors—and there are occasional human visitors, evidence of their presence if not their current proximity—leave small built structures. Stone shelters, always simple: three walls and a roof made of flat rocks carefully balanced. These shelters sit in the lee of larger formations, positioned to block the easterly rain. Inside, you find marks: scratched dates, names, small cairns of stacked pebbles. Fire rings, rarely used, the stones barely blackened. A sense of pilgrimage rather than habitation. People come here, stay briefly, leave little.

The light is remarkable. At this elevation, in this warm air, with moisture always present but never dominant, clarity becomes profound. Distances collapse—the buttes twenty kilometers distant look near enough to touch, their every detail visible. Shadows are sharp-edged and very dark against the sun-bright stone. The basalt itself reflects heat-shimmer in the afternoons, creating zones where the air bends vision, but this happens in predictable locations, can be anticipated and navigated around.

The sun moves through the sky with presence. You're aware of it constantly, not because it's harsh—it's not, though you'll burn if careless—but because it's the primary dynamic here. Shadows wheel around the rocks like slow clock-hands. The temperature stays warm but shifts ten or fifteen degrees between sunlit stone and shadow, creating microenvironments, determining where beetles emerge, where condensation forms.

Clouds build in the afternoon sometimes even when rain doesn't come, towering cumulus that rises to altitudes beyond the plateau, brilliant white against blue so deep it approaches violet. These clouds move west to east, casting vast shadows that sweep across the terrain like spotlight beams in reverse, dimming whole sections of the plateau for minutes at a time. When a shadow passes, temperature drops just enough to feel, and the suspension structures' harmonics shift slightly, responding to some variable of pressure or light intensity.

Night comes with stars in such numbers that constellations become difficult to distinguish. The Milky Way is a luminous road. The plateau continues to radiate stored heat long after sunset, and this creates gentle updrafts that carry scents—stone dust, the occasional sweetness from shrub flowers so tiny you'd never notice them in daylight, a mineral tang from evaporating pools. The predators hunt in darkness, their movements silent except for the very soft pad of feet on stone.

The suspension structures glow faintly at night. Not bright—you wouldn't use one for reading—but enough to navigate by if several are nearby. The glow is bioluminescence or something like it, pale blue-green, pulsing almost imperceptibly. The objects suspended in their fields rotate slightly faster at night, as if responding to the absence of solar radiation, maintaining some balance of accumulated energy.

The mysterious observer-objects don't glow, but they become even more present in darkness—negative spaces against the stars, and within the zones they influence, stranger things happen. Sounds occasionally reverse, playing backward for just a moment. Loose pebbles sometimes arrange themselves into spirals. Once or twice a night, across the whole plateau, there are moments of perfect silence, five or six seconds where even the wind pauses, where every process stops simultaneously. Then everything resumes as if nothing happened.

The wind itself is generally light, a presence rather than a force. It comes from different directions depending on time of day—upslope from the valleys to the east in the morning as they warm, downslope in the evening, channeling between the buttes with soft sighs. The wind carries sound from far away—you'll hear a rockfall that happened three kilometers distant, hear the wing-beats of a single bird passing overhead, hear the footsteps of the herd animals long before you see them.

There are no running rivers here, but the network of channels and fissures suggests ancient hydrology, a time when water was more constant. The channels connect in patterns that would form watersheds if water flowed continuously—you can trace them across the plateau, following their logic as they gather smaller channels into larger ones, all eventually leading to particular holes in the basalt, throats that descend into darkness, into some aquifer system below. 

Drop a stone down one of these holes and you never hear it land.

The ecology operates in layers. At ground level: beetles, shrubs, water-stones, the herd animals moving through. At the mid-layer, roughly one to six meters up: the suspension structures with their hanging gardens of small objects and water droplets, the spaces the birds navigate. And at the upper layer, in the sky: the thermals, the clouds, the raptors in their high spirals. Each layer touches the others at interfaces—beetles climb into suspension fields to harvest material, birds sometimes snatch suspended objects for reasons unclear, clouds determine when water descends to ground level.

Time moves strangely here, or rather, you become aware of multiple tempos operating simultaneously. The deep time of the weathered basalt, unchanged for epochs. The seasonal time of rainfall patterns, four-day cycles repeating endlessly. The daily time of sun and shadow, heat and cooling. The hourly time of animal movement, predator and prey. The minute-by-minute time of beetle activity, each individual pursuing its tasks. And the slower-than-slow time of the observer-objects, cycling through their pattern states, each full cycle taking months or years.

The rules seem to be these:

Water is precious but arrives reliably. Space is abundant—claim what you need, no more. Energy flows from sun to stone to life, efficient systems only. Waste nothing; every death feeds something else. Adaptation is complete—everything here is exactly what it needs to be, no excess, no deficiency. The suspension structures mediate between physical and something-else, providing resources no standard biology could. The observer-objects maintain conditions, perhaps, or witness, or slowly accomplish some task incomprehensible in human timeframes.

Violence exists but is rationed. A predator kills rarely. The herd animals defend themselves with horned heads when necessary but otherwise submit to the statistical requirements of predation. Even the weather is measured—rain arrives, delivers its water, departs. No floods, no droughts to extremes that break the system.

Human presence is tolerated as long as it remains peripheral. Build your small shelter, leave your marks, take your wonder, and depart. Stay too long and the plateau's indifference might become uncomfortable—not hostile, but simply unaccommodating. This isn't a place that bends to human timescales or human comfort. It has found its equilibrium without needing us.

Walking here, you become aware of your own sounds. Footsteps. Breathing. The rustle of clothing. These feel intrusive at first, then gradually acceptable—you're just another animal crossing the plateau, neither more nor less entitled than the others. The silence between sounds grows comfortable. You stop needing to fill it.

At the plateau's edges, the land drops away—not in cliffs exactly, but in accelerated slopes where the gentle swells steepen over the course of a kilometer into angles too sharp to walk. Below, perhaps a thousand meters down, are different ecosystems, greener, denser. But up here, at this specific altitude, in this specific configuration of warmth and sparseness and ancient stone, the Suspension Fields of Qen Mora exist in their particular state.

The place feels discovered rather than made, even accounting for the observer-objects, even accepting the suspension structures' biotechnology. It feels like conditions came together—elevation, erosion, temperature, the specific chemistry of the basalt, the arrival of seeds and spores from elsewhere, the introduction of creatures adapted by evolution or design—and produced an equilibrium so exact that it persists without intervention.

You can walk for days here. The scale allows it—the plateau extends far enough that horizons remain consistently distant, always another swell of stone ahead, always another butte in the distance growing fractionally closer. Supplies are the limiting factor, not terrain. Water if you time your travel with rain, or bring your own. Food from elsewhere; the plateau offers nothing humans can digest except perhaps beetle meat, which requires knowledge to prepare and tastes of minerals and faint electricity.

At night, in your stone shelter or under open sky, the warmth rising from basalt that has absorbed sunlight all day, you hear the small sounds: beetle movement, the distant pad of a predator, the almost-inaudible hum of suspension structures creating their fields. Very occasionally, a herd animal coughs, a soft sound like surprise. Wind moves around stones with breathy tones. A bird calls once, twice, then silence resumes.

The stars rotate. The Milky Way sets and rises according to season. Satellites sometimes cross the sky—human technology from another context, looking down on this place, probably recording it, data streaming to databases that try to categorize and understand what's happening here. Those satellites see the heat signature of warm basalt, the infrared dots of animal life, perhaps even the electromagnetic anomalies around the observer-objects. But whatever data they collect doesn't capture this: the way presence feels here, the sense of being inside a system that is both natural and beyond natural, wild and quietly managed, ancient and somehow contemporary.

Morning comes with the sun rising from behind distant mountains, light moving across the plateau from east to west like a revelation, touching each stone, each suspension structure, each sleeping animal. The temperature rises. The beetles retreat into crevices. The herd animals begin moving toward the next water-point, walking in their silent file. Birds wake and take flight, riding the first thermals. 

And somewhere, everywhere, the observer-objects continue their slow cycling, maintaining or witnessing or simply being, their purposes as established and inscrutable as the basalt they stand upon, their functions woven into the plateau's equilibrium so completely that removing one might collapse something essential, some balance that makes this place exactly what it is:

A high, warm, weathered stone garden where water arrives in mercy and life prospers in careful ratios and something beyond current understanding keeps its patient vigil across distances both spatial and temporal that humans measure in kilometers and days while the plateau itself measures in horizons and eons.
