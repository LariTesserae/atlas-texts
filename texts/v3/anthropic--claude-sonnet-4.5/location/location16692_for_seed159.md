# The Nursery Caves of Isthal-Ven

You descend through a throat of volcanic basalt, black and glassy where ancient heat kissed it, and the world opens beneath you into something that shouldn't exist—not yet, not like this. The air thickens as you go down. Warm. Blood-warm. The kind of warmth that suggests digestion, transformation, something being made from something else.

The caves extend for miles in every direction, a honeycomb warren that sprawls beneath the surface in chambers that connect and reconnect, passages that branch and merge with the logic of lungs or coral. This is not a carved space. It's an *emerging* space. The walls are still deciding what they are—some sections glisten with fresh mineral deposits, calcite and selenite growing in real-time, adding millimeters while you watch. Other areas remain raw wound-rock, exposed stone matrix that hasn't yet committed to a final form. In the dim peripheral zones, you can see where new chambers are breaching through, stone membrane thinning, ready to rupture into connection.

The light comes from above, mostly—from vertical shafts and fissures that punch through the ceiling at irregular intervals, some wide enough to admit full columns of daylight, others mere cracks that bleed illumination in knife-thin lines. The light ricochets off the glassy obsidian veins that thread through everything, off the water, off the bodies. It pools in some chambers and fails entirely in others. Where it reaches, it reveals everything. The darkness, where it exists, conceals nothing that makes sound or moves—which is to say, conceals almost nothing here.

Because the caves are *full*.

## The Inhabitants

They are called nithka in the shallow trade-tongue of the surface settlements, though what they call themselves—if they call themselves anything—remains unknown. They look like someone started to invent crustaceans but forgot to finish, or got bored halfway through and started adding mammalian details at random. Chitinous plates cover portions of their bodies, but not all portions, not consistently. Some have hard carapaces over their backs and soft, furred bellies. Others reverse the pattern. They range in size from hand-length juveniles to specimens as large as draft horses, and they are *everywhere*.

They pack the chambers in layers. The floors writhe with them. They cling to walls in clusters, stacked three and four deep, their various limbs—some ending in pincers, some in digits, some in naked sensory palps—interlocking in casual intimacy. They drape across each other without apparent hierarchy or discomfort. Where moisture collects in stone depressions, they gather in heaps that pulse like a single organism, their breathing synchronized. The density is such that in many passages you cannot see the actual stone floor—only the moving carpet of carapace and fur, shell and skin.

They are not aggressive. They simply *are*, and there are so many of them that their existence becomes the primary fact of this place.

They move constantly. Not with purpose exactly, but with an unceasing low-grade restlessness. One will detach from a wall-cluster and skitter across the ceiling. Another will rear up on its posterior limbs (however many it has—the count varies) and test the air with its palps before settling back down. They investigate each other endlessly, antennae brushing, claws clicking, bodies rearranging in slow waves across the available surfaces. The overall effect is like watching a time-lapse of a coral reef, but warm-blooded, but louder, but faster.

## The Sound

The caves roar with them.

Not a single roar—an accumulation of sounds that builds into something between a roar and a static hum. Clicking. So much clicking. The tap-tap-tap of countless chitinous feet on stone, the sharper *tik* of mandibles working, the hollow knock of shells bumping shells. Underneath: breathing. Thousands of respiratory systems moving air, some whistling through spiracles, some panting through mammalian throats, creating a base layer of susurration that never stops.

And then the calls. The nithka vocalize—chirps, squeaks, low thrumming sounds that resonate in chest cavities or shell-chambers. Not language, probably. Communication, certainly. One will make a sound and thirty others will respond, creating cascades of call-and-response that ripple through entire chambers. Sometimes a juvenile makes a high, keening cry and the adults closest to it throb in apparent sympathy, or irritation, or acknowledgment.

The acoustics amplify everything. Sound bounces off the curved walls, the low ceilings, the water surfaces. It layers and doubles back on itself. In some chambers the noise reaches a threshold where individual sounds become indistinguishable—just a solid wall of *presence*, of biological activity made audible. In others, the strange geometries create pockets of relative quiet where you can hear the specific: the scrape of a single claw, the wet sound of something being eaten, the drip of condensation from above.

And the water adds its voice. Not a major voice—not a river's rush or ocean's thunder—but a persistent minor key. Drip. Drip. Trickle. The soft collapse of small pools being disturbed. The caves weep moisture that collects in networks of shallow channels worn into the floor, finger-width streams that wander downslope toward collection points. Some areas bloom with damp—walls slick with condensation, air heavy enough to taste. Other sections run dry, dusty stone between the wet zones. The water comes and goes with no apparent schedule, emerging from cracks in the ceiling, from pores in the walls, gathering and draining through fissures that lead deeper still.

## The Architecture of Accretion

Scattered through the warrens are things that were made. Not by the nithka—or probably not by them, though the question hangs open. Simple structures. Walls of stacked stone, drystone technique, no mortar, most of them partial now—collapsed or never completed. They divide spaces that divide themselves naturally, suggesting someone once tried to impose additional order on the chaos and gave up, or died, or left.

Stone platforms, knee-height, purpose unclear. They're too large to be seats, too small and irregular to be effective floors. Some have shallow depressions worn into their surfaces—by tools? By bodies? By weather that never reaches here? The nithka ignore them mostly, occasionally incorporating them as climbing structures or resting surfaces without apparent awareness that they're different from natural stone.

And tools. Scattered, lost, forgotten. Simple things: stone hammers with the handles rotted away, leaving just the shaped heads. Bone awls, the bone itself yellowed and brittle, some cracked, some whole. Clay vessels, unglazed, thick-walled, primitive coil construction—most broken but a few intact, tucked into niches or half-buried in silt deposits. The metal is even rarer: a copper knife, green with patina, bent. Iron nails, rusted to fragility, staining the stone around them. 

Whoever made these things worked by hand and firelight. They shaped the world with direct force, stone against stone, fiber twisted into cord. The technology speaks of early solutions to basic problems—how to cut, how to hold water, how to fasten materials together. The artifacts don't cluster—they're truly scattered, as if dropped over years or generations by people passing through rather than living here. Though some of the stone platforms suggest longer presence. It's unclear. The nithka have outlasted any answer.

## Biological Weather

The nithka are not the only living things here, but they are the dominant fact that makes the presence of other life possible. Their waste creates microenvironments. Their dead create others. They are a mobile, breathing ecosystem infrastructure.

In the damper zones, where water persists and the nithka congregate most densely, films of slime grow on the stone—not quite bacterial, not quite fungal, something that spreads in colors ranging from bone-white to rust-orange. It processes whatever the nithka leave behind. It thickens until it develops texture, almost a skin, then liquefies and spreads again. The nithka walk through it, track it across dry zones where it dries to crust and flakes. In some chambers the slime has accumulated into actual *thickness*—spongy layers several inches deep that squelch underfoot, that hold moisture, that host even smaller things.

Because there are smaller things. Pale creatures the size of rice grains that might be nithka young or might be something else entirely—they swarm through the slime, creating minute ripples in its surface. Worm-things, translucent, thread-thin, miles of them accumulated in the moist crevices. They knot into balls of themselves, then unknot and disperse. Flying things, rare but present—insects or pseudo-insects with paper wings that make clicking sounds in imitation of the dominant species, camouflage through mimicry. They don't last long. The nithka eat them.

The nithka eat *constantly*. They eat the slime. They eat each other's shed shells. They eat whatever smaller things they can catch. They eat the eggs they themselves lay in moist crevices—or sometimes they don't, and juveniles emerge, already moving with that same ceaseless restlessness. The caves smell of this eating, this processing: rich, mulchy, organic decay in constant transaction with new growth. Not putrid—too active for putrid. Just *dense* with biological exchange.

## The Physics of Motion

The turbulence is not atmospheric. The air moves sluggishly, warmed by bodies and stone, stirred only by the collective respiration and movement of thousands of organisms. The turbulence is *biological*—it's the storm of the nithka themselves, their numbers and their restlessness creating a state of constant, chaotic change.

A chamber can be navigable one hour and impassable the next, not because the stone has changed but because the nithka have decided collectively, unconsciously, to congregate there. The carpet of bodies thickens from ankle-deep to waist-deep. They pile atop each other, driven by imperatives invisible to human understanding. Are they thermoregulating? Mating? Simply drawn by the density itself, the comfort of numbers? They swarm and separate and swarm again. 

Waves of motion pass through the congregations—sudden coordinated movements triggered by nothing obvious. A thousand nithka will simultaneously shift three feet to the left, climbing over each other in a cascade of clicking shells and scrambling limbs, then settle as if nothing happened. Minutes later, another wave, different direction. The effect is like watching murmuration, but earthbound, but made of hard edges and organic noise.

This creates danger of the non-malicious kind. They don't attack, but they can overwhelm. An isolated human can be simply buried, not by intention but by the thoughtless accumulation of bodies seeking whatever it is they're always seeking. They can crush by forgetting you're there. They can exhaust you by making rest impossible—there is nowhere without them, no space that remains clear for long enough to sleep without vigilance.

And yet there's an intelligence to their patterns, or an accidental optimization that looks like intelligence. They avoid blocking the water channels—the shallow streams flow through the forests of their legs mostly unimpeded. They don't congregate where the stone is most unstable, where new chambers are breaching. As if they sense the weakness, or as if the stone's vibration discomforts them. They map the space through use, carving pathways of heavier traffic through their collective movement, creating a kind of living infrastructure.

## The Geography of Making

The caves are still becoming. This is what the raw stone tells you—the exposed faces that haven't yet acquired the patina of age, the fresh cracks webbing across ceilings, the places where mineral-heavy water is actively building new formations, drop by drop. This is not an ancient, settled place. Geologically, it's young. Still plastic. Still negotiating its final form.

The scale is regional but not endless. If you could somehow map the full extent—and you can't, not really, not with the nithka making accurate surveying impossible—you'd find it sprawls across what might be three or four miles, give or take. An area the size of a small town, but entirely subterranean, entirely chambered and split by stone partitions that are themselves full of holes, creating a three-dimensional maze of more-or-less-connected spaces.

Some chambers are vast, cathedral-volume, ceilings thirty or forty feet above, floor space that could hold hundreds of people if it weren't already holding thousands of nithka. Others are intimate, barely body-width, vertical squeezes or horizontal crawls that connect the larger nodes. The vertical variation is significant—you might climb up twenty feet through a fissure to reach a higher gallery, or descend through a slumping passage into a lower bowl. But you're never on the surface. The deepest points might be a hundred feet down, the shallowest perhaps thirty. You exist in the upper crust, the realm where surface water can still percolate down, where light can still find ways in.

The light shafts are the vertical organizing principle. The largest chambers tend to center on them—the places where full sun reaches down, creating pools of brightness that the smaller chambers lack. The nithka moderate their density around these, not avoiding them but not clustering as thickly. Perhaps the direct light is uncomfortable. Perhaps it makes them visible to predators that don't exist. The illumination creates gradients of visibility—zones where you can see clearly, zones of half-light, zones of darkness where your eyes adjust and still can't penetrate. The obsidian veins help, flashing and reflecting wherever light touches them, creating secondary gleams in unexpected places.

The overall structure suggests lava tubes, maybe, or limestone dissolution, or something that started as one and shifted to the other. The stone is not uniform—basaltic here, sedimentary there, intrusive igneous cutting through in dark dykes. It suggests multiple geological events overlapping, creating this space through addition and subtraction, flow and fracture. And it's *still happening*. Not fast enough to watch in real time—except for the mineral deposits, you can watch those—but fast enough that the caves in a hundred years will not be the caves today.

## The Logic of Presence

There's a wrongness here, but it's the wrongness of timing rather than nature. Everything present makes sense—caves, stone, water, life—but they make sense in the wrong *order*. This feels like a space that should be ancient, that should have reached equilibrium centuries ago. Instead it's raw and populous, teeming before it's finished forming. It's as if someone accelerated the biological clock without touching the geological one, creating a fundamental desynchronization.

The nithka themselves embody this. They're not cryptids, not monsters—they're recognizable as organisms following organic imperatives. But their variety, their density, their dominance of a space this young and unfinished suggests they were introduced, or that they evolved under pressures that don't match this environment, or that they're taking advantage of something temporary, a window of opportunity that will close when the caves finish becoming whatever they're becoming.

The human artifacts compound the mystery. The tools and structures are too primitive to suggest people who understood what this place was. They're the leavings of visitors, maybe, or of a very early settlement that failed. But why here? What drew people to an unfinished cave system full of creatures that, while not hostile, make sustained human presence nearly impossible? What were they building, or studying, or running from?

The clay vessels—those especially nag at understanding. Unglazed, simple, but *made*. Someone sat somewhere (here? nearby?) and shaped wet clay and fired it and carried the finished vessels into these caves for purposes that remain opaque. To hold water, presumably, but there's water here already, unreliably but present. To store food? To mark territory? To serve some ritual function? The vessels themselves offer no answers. They're just silent, scattered, occasionally cradling a small pool where condensation collects, serving accidental purposes generations after their makers vanished.

## The Experience of Traversal

Moving through the Nursery Caves—the name comes from the surface traders who buy juvenile nithka from the braver delvers; whether the nithka actually breed here more than elsewhere is uncertain—is a full-body negotiation with density. You cannot walk freely. The floor is bodies. You step on shells, feel them shift under your weight, hear them click in protest or indifference. You wade through them. You climb over accumulated layers when they mound too high, hands finding purchase on stone wall or stone platform while nithka flow around your legs like a living tide.

They touch you constantly. Not grasping—investigating. Antennae tap your clothing. Palps brush your skin with damp, seeking pressure. Smaller specimens climb your legs, your back, treating you as mobile terrain. You learn not to react, not to flinch. Sudden movements trigger nothing aggressive but cause local chaos—nithka scatter, creating domino effects through the packed bodies, noise spiking, then settling. Better to move slowly, to telegraph your intentions through steady pressure, allowing them to flow around you like water around a stone.

The warmth builds on your skin. Your own heat adds to the collective heat. Sweat comes quickly in the more crowded chambers, in the zones where thousands of respirating bodies and warm stone create a greenhouse thickness to the air. The damp zones offer relief—cooler, the condensation on the walls inviting to touch. You can drink it, sometimes, where it collects in cleaner depressions. It tastes mineral, slightly metallic, but safe enough. The water that runs in the floor channels is less certain, contaminated by whatever the nithka leave behind, though in the upper reaches where it first emerges it sometimes runs clear.

Navigation is by landmark and light. You remember the chamber with three light shafts forming a triangle on the floor. The passage with the collapsed stone wall blocking the right-hand fork. The gallery where an iron nail rusts on a natural shelf at chest height. The cavern where the nithka thin out around a copper knife—do they dislike the metal? fear the memory of it? The orientation is three-dimensional; you must think in terms of up and down as much as forward and back. The mental map becomes topological rather than geometric, a network of nodes and connections that exist in relation to each other but resist conventional direction.

Sound becomes navigation too. The acoustics are distinctive enough that you can recognize chambers by their echoes, by the specific quality of how the roar builds and reflects. And sometimes, rarely, you hear other sounds—the groan of stone settling, the crack of a new fissure opening, sounds that make you pause and listen hard, trying to determine if the floor will remain solid, if the ceiling will hold.

## Edges and Questions

At the margins, the caves simply... stop. New chambers peter out into dead ends, into stone too solid to have breached yet. Or they narrow to cracks too small even for the smallest nithka, passages that might widen eventually or might remain impassable. Some edges are vertical—open shafts that descend into darkness beyond what light reaches, beyond what mapping has explored. The nithka avoid these mostly, or the juveniles that venture in don't return. What's below? Older, deeper caves? Groundwater? Stone with no cavities?

And at the upper margins, the caves approach the surface without quite reaching it everywhere. The main entrance passage is obvious, human-navigable. But there are other points where ceiling stone thins to mere feet, where you can hear the surface world muffled through basalt—wind sounds, animal calls, the different acoustic signature of open air. Could you breach through? Probably, with tools and time. Would you want to? It depends on whether you're trying to enter or escape.

The nithka population fluctuates, or seems to. There are times—reports vary on whether it's seasonal, lunar, random—when the density increases even beyond baseline overwhelming. The caves become impassable, every chamber packed solid. And other times when they thin slightly, when you can see actual floor in places, when the roar drops to merely loud. Where do they go? Are there other cave systems connected through passages too small for humans? Do they emerge onto the surface somewhere, somehow, at night when no one sees? The delvers argue about this. No consensus exists.

And the question of the slime, the films of almost-life—are they native? Are they a food source the nithka cultivate unconsciously? Or are they processing the caves themselves, breaking down the fresh stone into something more habitable, serving as a living interface between mineral and animal? In the oldest-looking sections, where stone has acquired that smooth, worn quality that suggests centuries, the slime is thinnest or absent. In the newest sections, where rock still shows fracture edges, the slime is thickest. As if it's part of the finishing process. As if the caves digest themselves into completion through biology.

## The Sensation of Time

Time moves strangely here. Not mystically—there's no time dilation, no supernatural thickness. But the constancy, the lack of day/night transitions in most areas, the unchanging warm temperature, the endless motion of the nithka that provides no clear markers of progression... hours become elastic. You think you've been moving through the passages for twenty minutes; it's been two hours. Or the reverse—subjective eternities that collapse into brief intervals when checked against objective measures.

The light shafts provide some temporal anchor. You can watch the sun's movement through them, see the angle and quality of light change from morning brightness to afternoon gold to evening's fade. But in the chambers without direct light, without those celestial references, you drift. The nithka never sleep as humans understand sleep—they rest, go dormant in cycles, but not in unison, so there's never a quiet hour, never a moment when the caves sleep too.

This timelessness suits the place, somehow. It enhances the sense of witnessing something embryonic, something pre-temporal, that hasn't yet been caught in the flow of proper history. The caves exist in the before-time, still gestating, still becoming. The nithka seem appropriate to that—they swarm with the energy of creation, the chaos of things coming into being without yet settling into final patterns.

And perhaps that's the deepest strangeness: not any single element but the combination, the conjunction of rawness and plenitude, of geological youth and biological overflow. The caves feel *early*. Not in human history—human history here is shallow, probably recent. But in some other scale. As if you've descended into a place where the world is still trying things out, still experimenting with combinations, still adjusting the ratios of stone and water and life. And the nithka, overwhelming in their numbers, are the current iteration, the current answer to the question of what fills this space before it finishes being born.

Whether they'll still be here when the caves mature, when the stone finally settles—whether they're permanent residents or temporary occupants of an embryonic moment—remains as unknown as everything else. For now they are the fact, the dominant presence, the sound and motion and warmth that makes the caves alive in every sense except the one that matters for stone.

And the stone grows anyway, millimeter by millimeter, indifferent to what scuttles on its surface, building toward a future that may or may not include the overwhelming life that currently defines it.
