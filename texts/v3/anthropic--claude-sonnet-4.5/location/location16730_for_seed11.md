# The Thundering Gardens of Koreth

The mountains here have been mountains so long they have forgotten the shape they were born to. These are peaks worn not to hills but to something else — vast, patient bulges of stone that rise in slow curves against the sky, their surfaces polished by eons of wind until they gleam like the inside of shells. They climb to enormous heights, the air thin and sharp, but their slopes are gentle, almost maternal, as if the earth has been breathed smooth by time itself. Crevasses split them, yes, but these are ancient wounds long since healed into rounded edges, and the stone around them has the texture of poured wax, of melted candles frozen mid-drip.

The mountains are not bare.

Where you would expect scree and ice, there is green. Not the tentative green of alpine meadows, but *green*, riotous and layered and thick as carpet — moss of a hundred varieties, some finger-length and soft as animal fur, others forming cushions dense enough to bear weight, still others hanging in beards and curtains from every overhang. Ferns spring from cracks, not delicate but robust, waist-high, their fronds curling in spirals that seem almost geometric. Small trees twist from impossible angles, growing horizontal from cliff faces, their roots visible as they probe the stone for any pocket of ancient soil. They are bonsai-proportioned but full-sized, gnarled things with bark like alligator hide and leaves that cluster in rosettes at branch-ends.

The moisture comes and goes like breathing. For hours the air is merely close, thick, waiting. Then mist materializes as if the mountains are exhaling — not rolling in but *appearing*, suddenly present in every direction, reducing the world to arm's length visibility. It clings. It beads on every surface. Water runs down the smooth stone faces in sheets, in temporary rivers that merge and split and vanish into crevices, only to emerge minutes or hours later from some lower opening. Then, just as suddenly, the mist burns away — not from sun but from some other process, some exhalation reversed — and the air is breathable again, merely humid, and everything drips.

But the water never quite leaves. It collects in the ten thousand depressions the ancient erosion has left behind — shallow bowls in the stone, some small as skulls, others large as houses, all of them holding a hand's depth of water that never fully evaporates. These pools are everywhere, countless, scattered across the slopes like mirrors fallen from the sky. They reflect the changing light. They host their own miniature ecologies — filaments of algae, water beetles that appear and disappear with mysterious synchronicity, tiny translucent things that might be tadpoles or might be something else entirely.

The sound is what overwhelms first-time visitors. The sound is *constant*, multi-layered, immense. Water is the foundation — the gurgling rush of temporary streams, the deeper thunder of underground rivers audible through thin places in the stone, the drip-drip-drip multiplied by millions into a susurrus that never stops. But there is more. The wind, channeled through the crevasses and hollows worn into the mountains over ages incomprehensible, produces tones. Not random whistles but *tones* — deep thrumming notes that last for hours, higher harmonics that flutter and warble, occasional shrieks when the wind finds just the right configuration of stone. The mountains sing, constantly, a composition that never repeats but maintains certain themes, certain recurring intervals.

And under and through and around all of this: the vegetation itself makes sound. The moss, when mist condenses on it, releases drops that patter against stone in their billions. The ferns, moved by wind, rustle like paper — and there are *so many* ferns. The small trees creak, their wood stressed by growing at angles, and sometimes — not often, but often enough to notice — a root will shift, seeking better purchase, and the sound of stone grinding on stone will briefly punch through the water-song and wind-music.

To walk here is to walk through chambers without walls. The spacing of the pools, the placement of larger outcrops, the way vegetation thickens and thins, creates a sense of rooms, of passages, of territories defined by no visible boundary but absolutely distinct. Here is a place of open stone where four shallow streams converge before plunging into a cleft. There, twenty paces away, is a zone where the moss grows in such profusion that the ground bounces underfoot, and the ferns create a canopy overhead, and the light turns green-gold. Beyond that, a field of stone eggs — boulders once angular, now worn to ovals, some house-sized, scattered across a slope as if giants played marbles and left the game unfinished.

The scale is *regional*. You can see, when the mist permits, for miles — the slopes rolling away in every direction, the distant suggestions of peaks that might be separate mountains or might be other faces of this same complex. There are landmarks: a particularly tall stone pillar, improbable survivor of erosion, draped in hanging moss like a monument to patience. A lake, or perhaps a very wide river, visible in glimpses far below, its water the color of green glass. Patches where the stone is darker, richer in some mineral that has allowed vegetation to grow so thickly it obscures the ground completely.

The structures are easy to miss. A stone wall, knee-high, following a contour for fifty meters before subsiding back into the mountain — its stones shaped by hand but fitted without mortar, and so worn they look almost natural. A circle of posts, or what remains of posts, just stubs now, wood long since rotted to punk, but arranged with clear intention around one of the larger pools. What might be a hearth, blackened stone in a shallow wind-shelter, though the charcoal is so old it crumbles to dust at a touch.

And there, tucked into a fold in the mountainside, a small building. Or buildings — it's hard to say if this is three structures or one structure with additions. The walls are stacked stone, unmortared, the gaps chinked with moss that seems deliberately placed. The roof is stone slabs, massive ones, resting on walls that look too thin to bear them but have borne them for so long the question is moot. No door, just an opening. Inside: darkness, the smell of water and stone, the sense of space extending back into the mountain. Scratches on the walls that might be tally marks or might be something else. A stone shelf. The impression — powerful but unverifiable — that someone was here, not long ago, though "not long ago" might mean decades in a place like this.

Other marks: a path, visible only as a slight depression in the moss, a route where feet have passed enough times to compress the vegetation, though the moss is recovering, slowly reclaiming. Cairns — small stacks of stones on prominent overlooks, though their original height is uncertain; the wind and time have reduced most to stumps. A series of notches cut into a vertical stone face, a ladder for climbing, though the notches are worn shallow and would be treacherous to use.

The tools, when you find them, are simple. A stone hammer head, hafted with wood so old it's nearly petrified, wedged in a crack in the rock as if abandoned mid-task. Cutting edges of flaked stone, beautiful in their simplicity, some still sharp, others dulled. Fiber cordage rotting in a damp hollow, its weave still visible. A basket frame, the basketry itself mostly gone, but the structure surviving to show the maker's skill. These things appear rarely, scattered across the landscape without pattern, as if the people who left them walked from place to place and simply set things down when they were finished.

The light is filtered, diffuse, even when the mist is absent. The sun is often visible as a bright smear in the clouds rather than a distinct disc. When it does break through clearly, the wet surfaces explode into brilliance — the stone faces become mirrors, the pooled water throws up dazzling reflections, the moss glows as if lit from within. Rainbows are common, sometimes multiple, arcing across gorges or standing vertically in waterfalls. The light has texture here, you can see it in rays when the mist is right, and sometimes it seems almost as substantial as the water.

Animals are sparse but present. Birds — some small and drab, moving through the fern understory in quick nervous bursts, feeding on insects. Others larger, long-legged, wading in the pools and stabbing at the water with sharp beaks, taking fish or amphibians or whatever else lives in those depths. Their calls add to the soundscape: whistles, croaks, sharp warning cries that echo off stone. Occasionally, something larger moves through the vegetation, visible only as motion — fur or scale-flash, gone before you can focus. Tracks in mud at the pool edges suggest creatures of various sizes, but they are seldom seen.

Insects are more common. Clouds of tiny flies dance over the pools when the light is right, each individual almost invisible but the clouds themselves like smoke. Beetles with iridescent shells crawl on the stone, their paths seeming purposeful. Things that might be moths, or might be something moth-adjacent, cling to the underside of fern fronds, their wings folded into origami patterns. And in the water: movement, constant, darting shapes that never quite resolve into recognizable forms.

The strangeness is subtle, a matter of small discrepancies that accumulate. The moss, for instance — some varieties seem to orient themselves, all their fronds pointing the same direction, and that direction shifts, not with the sun but according to some other logic. The pools: their water levels don't always correlate with recent rain. Some are full when they should be dry, others low when mist has been condensing for hours. The sound of the mountains, the deep tones produced by wind through stone, occasionally resolves into patterns that seem almost intentional — not quite music, not quite language, but something that makes the mind lean forward, listening, certain that meaning is just barely out of reach.

And there are the blooms. Rarely — perhaps once every few days if you're traveling through — you come across a patch of vegetation where something is flowering. But these are not flowers in any familiar sense. They're structural, geometric, built of repeating elements that shouldn't work organically but do. One is a spiral of thin petals, each petal a perfect triangle, the spiral ascending a foot into the air before simply stopping, no visible means of support for the upper petals. Another is a cluster of spheres, each sphere constructed from what look like scales or maybe leaves, the whole arrangement hovering — actually *hovering*, a hand's breadth above the moss, roots or stems or whatever should connect it to the ground simply absent. They last hours or sometimes a day, then collapse, dissolve, return to the moss layer as if they never were.

The dynamism is everywhere, constant, a restlessness built into the fabric of the place. The water is always moving, always finding new paths, old channels filling with debris and forcing the flow elsewhere, creating new patterns of erosion even in stone this ancient. The mist appears and vanishes with no warning. The vegetation grows *quickly* — you can almost watch the moss expand, tendrils creeping outward, claiming bare stone, and in areas that have been cleared (by rockfall, by wind, by whatever), the recolonization is aggressive, competitive, different species racing to establish dominance.

The pools change. Not just their levels but their positions, slightly, as if the stone beneath them is still settling, still finding its final form despite eons of opportunity. New pools appear in depressions you're certain weren't there yesterday. Old ones drain suddenly, their water vanishing into newly opened cracks, leaving behind a film of algae that dries and blows away.

The wind is inconsistent — still for an hour, then suddenly gusting from a different quarter, then still again. It brings sounds from far away: the crash of water falling from some distant height, the crack of stone giving way, the weird harmonics of other caves and hollows tuning themselves to its pressure. It brings scents: the iron smell of exposed mineral, the green smell of crushed vegetation, occasionally something else, harder to identify, almost sulfurous or perhaps organic-decay, suggesting hidden processes in the mountain's depths.

Walking here requires constant adjustment. The moss is treacherous when wet, which is almost always. The smooth stone offers no friction. The ferns hide depressions. The temporary streams change course, and a route that was passable an hour ago is now blocked by rushing water. The sound makes communication difficult — you must raise your voice to be heard, even at close range, and often resort to gestures. The air pressure changes cause ears to pop, a constant reminder of the altitude.

And yet the place feels *inhabited*, not by what you see but by presence, by history layered so deep it becomes atmosphere. Every stone has been rained on uncountable times. Every pool has reflected the sun for eons. The wind has played these stone pipes longer than minds can hold. The vegetation has crept and claimed and died back and crept again in cycles beyond number. The few human marks — the walls, the buildings, the tools — seem less like impositions and more like acknowledgments, small gestures of participation in something vast and ongoing.

There are areas where the ancient buildings cluster slightly more densely. Not a village, exactly, but a node, a place where multiple structures stand within sight of each other. The largest is perhaps fifteen feet on a side, its interior a single chamber with stone benches built into the walls and a central depression that might have held fire. The walls are covered in marks — not writing but patterns, grooves worn into the stone by repetitive scraping, swirls and lines that follow the natural contours of the rock face. Outside, a series of terraces, small flat areas supported by low walls, once perhaps planted with something, now wild with moss and fern.

From this cluster, you can see other structures scattered across the valley — solitary rectangles of stone against the green, most roofless now, their walls subsiding. They were never numerous, these builders, or they were numerous but spread thin, each structure serving one person or a small group, the wider community maintained through walking, through traveling the moss-paths from node to node across the singing mountains.

The sky overhead is usually obscured, but when it clears — and it does, in moments, in rare hours of pure transparency — it is startlingly blue, intense at this altitude, and the clouds that race across it move *fast*, visible in their motion, torn and reformed by winds that don't quite reach the surface. Those moments of clarity don't bring silence; if anything, the mountain-song intensifies, as if the stone is responding to the direct light, adjusting its tones, celebrating the temporary absence of mist.

At the edges of the pools, where water meets moss, tiny ecosystems perform their dramas. A beetle approaches, hesitates, drinks. A strand of moss releases spores that drift like dust in a shaft of light. Something in the water breaks the surface, creates rings that spread and die. A fern frond uncurls by increments, its movement imperceptible unless you watch for long minutes, patient, learning the place's slower rhythms underneath the chaos of sound and wind and water.

The place teaches patience and movement in equal measure. To survive here, you must be still enough to notice the details — which moss is too slick to trust, which pools are shallow enough to wade, which stone-songs indicate wind about to gust. But you must also keep moving, because stillness for too long means getting soaked, means losing warmth, means losing track of the temporary paths through the water-flows. It is a landscape of eternal motion suspended in eternal form, change occurring constantly within a structure that has finished changing, the final state proving to be not stasis but this — this endless, deafening, gorgeous turmoil of green and stone and water and wind, singing its complex song to itself, mostly unheard, mostly unconcerned with hearing.

The temperature is comfortable, warm enough that the moisture doesn't chill, but the altitude keeps it from being hot. When you exert yourself climbing the gentle slopes, you warm quickly; when you rest, the evaporation cools you. The cycle maintains balance. Nights — though you sense them more than see them, the light fading to grey rather than black in the omnipresent mist — bring coolness but not cold. The stone retains warmth. The moss insulates. The sound continues unchanged, so that sleep comes fitfully, in pieces, the consciousness never quite letting go of the need to track the auditory landscape for changes, for warnings, for meaning.

And through it all, the sense that this is a place that has achieved something. Not peace — there is no peace in this tumult. Not completion — the erosion continues, grain by grain, the pools fill and empty, the vegetation advances and retreats. But something like *rightness*, as if the mountains have arrived at the form they were always becoming, and that form is this: a garden of stone and water and green, singing to itself, marking time in its own measures, mostly empty of witnesses but fully itself regardless, ancient beyond reckoning and yet, somehow, forever in the exact moment of transformation, perpetually young in its oldness, alive in its mineral patience.

The air tastes of stone-dust and green growth and water. The light shifts as clouds pass. A bird calls, barely audible. Water finds a new path. The mountains sing on.
