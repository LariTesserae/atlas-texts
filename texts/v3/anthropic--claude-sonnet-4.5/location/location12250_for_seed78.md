# The Scaffolding Gardens

The mountains hang in permanent dusk, their peaks lost not to clouds but to something stranger — a ceiling of their own making. At this elevation, where air grows thin and cold bites constantly at exposed stone, the vegetation has built upward for so long that it has created a second landscape entirely, a living architecture that floats two, three, sometimes five hundred meters above the actual ground. You walk beneath forests that never touch earth.

The structural plants — no one who sees them calls them trees, though that's the nearest word — grow in a way that defies lowland logic. Their trunks are hollow lattices, cross-braced internally like the bones of birds, rigid enough to support immense weight while remaining porous to wind. They rise in stands that span kilometers, their bases rooted in high valleys and ridge-lines, their canopies interlocking far overhead into continuous mats of living tissue. Between trunk and canopy: mostly空 emptiness, cold air, the occasional drifting seed-sail catching what little light filters down from above.

The light itself is never direct. By the time it penetrates the upper canopy layers, it has become something blue-grey and aquatic, a permanent twilight that makes distance hard to judge. The canopy is so vast — stretching across entire mountain ranges, bridging valleys a kilometer deep — that it generates its own weather. Moisture condenses on the underside of the leaf-ceiling, gathers, and falls in erratic interior rains that have nothing to do with the sky above. You can stand in these dripping chambers while the actual weather, the mountain weather, howls somewhere far overhead, entirely separate.

The ground is sharp, young stone — broken plates of slate and schist, angular scree that hasn't had time to smooth. These mountains are new in geological terms, still rising, still breaking themselves apart as they grow. Frost shatters stone every night. The fragments lie in loose, clinking drifts that shift underfoot. Nothing grows down here except a thin film of moss on the more stable surfaces, dark as liver, damp to the touch. The moss doesn't photosynthesize — there's too little light. It feeds on what falls from above: the constant rain of organic debris, the slow dissolution of the canopy, the droppings of animals that never descend.

Those animals move through the lattice forest, not the ground. They are scaffolding creatures, adapted to three-dimensional space and cold air. There are climbers with eight limbs and gripping pads that secrete anti-freeze compounds. There are gliders with membranous wings stretched between elongated fingers, their bodies insulated with dense fur that traps air in layers. There are burrowers that tunnel through the actual substance of the structural trunks, excavating spiral galleries in the living wood — and the trees tolerate this, seem even to expect it, their internal architecture accounting for these hollowings. The sound of these animals is constant: the scratch and tick of claws on bark, the breathy calls that echo in the empty spaces, the boom and rustle of wings catching air.

But the loudest sound is the forest itself. The lattice trunks are instruments. Wind moves through them — and there is always wind at this elevation — and plays them like flutes, like organ pipes. Each trunk has its own pitch depending on its diameter, its degree of hollowing, the specific pattern of perforations that climbing animals have added. A stand of structural plants becomes a chord. A whole valley full of them becomes a continuous, droning symphony that shifts with wind direction and intensity. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. Low frequencies travel through stone, through your bones. The mountains hum.

The canopy layer — far overhead — is where the actual light-gathering happens, and where most of the biological mass concentrates. Up there, it's nearly solid: leaves packed in shingling layers, epiphytic plants growing on structural plants, vines threading everything together, the whole mass cross-braced and interlocking like a tensegrity structure. It's so dense that large animals can walk on top of it, and do. You see them sometimes as shadows moving across the underside of the canopy, backlit by whatever dim light makes it through from the true sky. Herds of something heavy, their footfalls creating ripples in the leaf-surface, causing small waterfalls of condensation to release and patter down.

The water situation is strange. It never rains properly at ground level — the canopy intercepts everything. Instead, you have condensation: constant, pervasive dampness that forms on every cold surface. Metal rusts. Stone weeps. The air tastes of wet bark and cold. Then, periodically, the canopy sheds its accumulated water in sudden interior storms. These aren't predictable by looking at the sky (which you can't see anyway) but by reading the canopy itself: when the underside begins to bulge downward, when you hear a certain creaking in the lattice trunks, when the animals suddenly ascend. Then the water comes down in curtains, in buckets, in great sloshing releases that turn the ground temporarily into a network of rushing streams. These floods last minutes, sometimes an hour. Then they drain away through the broken stone, and the slow process of condensation begins again.

Scattered through this landscape, always near the structural trunks, are the built things. Stone circles at ground level, never more than a few meters across: cleared spaces where the loose scree has been moved aside, where flat slabs have been arranged as floors or seats. Small hearths, though the fuel sources aren't obvious. Sometimes a wall, waist-high, stacked without mortar, serving as windbreak or boundary marker. The structures are always simple, always stone-on-stone, and they're old enough that moss has begun to claim them but new enough that their edges are still sharp, their purposes still legible.

Higher up, built into the lattice trunks themselves, are platforms. These require closer inspection — you have to crane your neck, squint into the gloom. They're made of wood, but not the wood of the structural plants. Something else, something brought from lower elevations perhaps: planks and beams that have been lashed or pegged into place, creating flat surfaces in the vertical maze. Some platforms have walls, partial enclosures. Most are just stages, resting places, lookout points. There are rope bridges too, strung between platforms, between trunks, spanning the empty spaces. The ropes are made from plant fiber, thick as your wrist, and they're failing. Some bridges are intact. Others have fallen, their remnants hanging like dead vines.

You find tools sometimes, always in the built places. Stone blades, still sharp. Bone awls. Wooden shafts that might have been spear handles or digging sticks. Everything is functional, made with clear purpose but no decoration. The metal is rare and simple: copper mostly, sometimes bronze, shaped into hooks and pins and blade-tips. These pieces are corroded green, but they were never complex to begin with. No forging, no alloys beyond the basic. Someone knew how to make tools but hadn't moved beyond the fundamentals.

The ecosystem is all about verticality. Ground level is a desert despite the dampness — almost nothing lives there. The life is in the canopy and in the trunk-space between ground and canopy. The scaffolding creatures move through this middle zone in complex patterns. They're not quite migratory, but they're not sedentary either. They follow the interior weather, the condensation cycles. They follow the fruiting seasons of the canopy plants, which happen asynchronously in different areas. There are predators: long-bodied things with gripping tails and binocular vision, ambush hunters that wait in the trunk-hollows. There are prey animals: smaller, faster, moving in loose groups that scatter and reaggregate. The fauna is abundant but spread across an enormous three-dimensional volume, so you can go hours seeing nothing, then suddenly find yourself in the middle of a mass movement — hundreds of climbers flowing past you through the lattice, heading somewhere, driven by instincts or information you can't access.

The weirdness is in the relationships. The structural plants should compete, should shade each other out, should fight for resources. Instead, they seem to coordinate. Their spacing is too even. When one dies — and they do die, sometimes from lightning strikes in the canopy, sometimes from internal failure — the gap closes in a way that feels orchestrated. Neighboring trunks lean slightly inward. New shoots emerge at precisely the right locations. The canopy reseals. It's as if the forest is a single organism, or as if the individual plants are communicating in ways that leave no visible trace.

And sometimes, in the deepest twilight, you see lights in the canopy. Not bioluminescence — this is cold light, blue-white, lasting for minutes then disappearing. It might be some kind of electrical discharge, a byproduct of the living canopy's metabolism. It might be something else. The lights don't correspond to any animal movement you can detect. They don't follow any pattern. They just occur, scattered and random, like stars trying to happen in the wrong place.

The temperature is always cold, but it's a specific kind of cold: dry and constant, not bitter but numbing, the kind that seeps in gradually and makes you realize you've been shivering for the past hour without noticing. Frost forms every night on the ground stone, on the structural trunks, on your skin if you're still enough. The cold preserves things. Bodies left on the ground don't decay in the usual way — they mummify, freeze-dry, become part of the stone drift. You find them occasionally: scaffolding animals, perfectly preserved, their fur still soft. Once, a person, curled on their side on a stone circle, hands still gripping a tool, frozen in the act of working.

The scale makes everything relative. A formation that seems close — a particular stand of trunks, a distinctive canopy bulge — can take days to reach. The distances are high-mountain distances, where the air tricks your depth perception, where "over there" might mean a day's walk or three days'. The lattice forest continues across entire ranges. From the rare high points where the canopy is thin enough to see through, you can look out and see it extending to every horizon: the same twilight, the same hollow architecture, the same humming sound, going on apparently forever. Whether it's actually endless or just vast beyond your ability to traverse doesn't particularly matter. The effect is the same.

Navigation is by trunk-pattern, by the sound of particular formations, by the shape of canopy overhead. The people who built the platforms and stone circles must have had mental maps of immense complexity, three-dimensional charts that tracked vertical space as carefully as horizontal. Some of their route-markers remain: stones stacked in specific ways, notches cut into trunk-bases, arrangements of bone that might be directional indicators. You can sometimes follow these paths, though you're never sure where they're leading or whether you're reading them correctly.

The dynamic quality is the ecosystem's constant motion. The interior weather moves in slow cycles: condensation building, releasing, building again. The canopy plants fruit on schedules that vary by location — there's always somewhere in the forest where seed-cases are splitting open, somewhere else where flowers are releasing pollen into the cold air. The scaffolding animals follow these events in waves, their populations rising and falling in response to available food. Predators follow prey. Prey relocate. The whole system pulses, shifts, reorganizes itself continuously.

And the structural plants themselves are always growing, always adjusting. They grow slowly at this elevation — cold limits metabolism — but they're never static. A trunk you used as a landmark might sprout new lateral branches. A platform you marked on your mental map might be ten meters higher the next season because the tree beneath it added a growth ring. The forest is patient and it's active, and it operates on time scales that make human planning almost irrelevant.

The built spaces feel like arguments against this timelessness: attempts to create fixed points, permanent markers, places that say "here, this matters, this stays." But the forest absorbs them. Moss grows. Water drips. Wood rots (slowly, because of the cold, but inevitably). The stone circles remain longest, but even they shift as the ground frost-heaves, as the scree underneath redistributes itself. Nothing is permanent except the forest, and even the forest is temporary — it's just temporary on a scale you can't personally witness.

You can climb if you're careful. The lattice trunks have enough perforations, enough textural variation, to offer handholds. The scaffolding animals don't object to your presence — they regard you as just another moving thing, neither threat nor opportunity. Ascending is like climbing inside a vast, cold, living building. The wood is solid under your hands, but it's wood, not stone — it has give, it responds slightly to weight. The higher you go, the louder the wind-song becomes. You can feel the vibrations, the whole trunk resonating around you.

At a certain height — maybe fifty meters up, maybe a hundred, it varies by location — you enter the inhabited trunk-zone. This is where most of the scaffolding fauna lives. You start seeing nests: woven structures in the hollows, lined with shed fur and plant fiber. You see territorial markings: claw-scrapes, scent-posts, the scattered remains of kills. The animals themselves watch you from higher positions, curious but not alarmed. Some of them make sounds: hoots, clicks, a strange purring rumble. The sounds echo in the trunk-spaces, layer over each other, create a continuous acoustic texture.

If you keep climbing, you eventually reach the underside of the canopy. This is a surface, almost solid, made of compressed vegetation. It's wet — constantly dripping — and slightly warm compared to the air below. The warmth is metabolic: all that plant-mass generating heat as it photosynthesizes and respires. You can sometimes push through, force your way up into the canopy interior, but it's difficult. The vegetation resists. It's not hostile, just dense, packed, occupying every available space. If you do break through, you enter a different world: green light, close humid air, the sound of wind in leaves rather than through hollow trunks. But most travelers don't go up that far. The under-canopy is the practical ceiling, the roof of the world they move through.

The stone circles at ground level often contain evidence of fire. Charcoal, heat-cracked stone. But where did the fuel come from? The structural plants are alive and damp. Deadfall is rare — the canopy catches most fallen material, composts it internally. The answer might be that the fires burned compressed plant material from the canopy itself, carried down and dried. Or they burned animal fat. Or they burned something else, some fuel source that isn't obvious. The fires themselves must have been important: sources of light in the permanent twilight, sources of heat in the constant cold, centers for gathering. You can imagine people clustered around these small blazes, their backs to the vast cold darkness, the hum of the forest surrounding them.

What were they doing here? Why build platforms in a place so inhospitable, so far from conventional resources? The tools suggest long occupation, not just passing through. The platforms suggest infrastructure, planning, community. Maybe they were mining something, though there's no evidence of excavations beyond the natural stone-breaks. Maybe they were harvesting something from the canopy — some fruit or fiber or chemical compound that justified the difficulty. Maybe they lived here because somewhere else was worse.

Or maybe they came because of the forest itself, because this weird architecture of life and emptiness and cold drew them. The forest does have a quality that makes you want to understand it, to map its patterns, to find the logic in its structure. The more time you spend here, the more it seems like there *is* a logic, just one that operates outside familiar frameworks. The spacing of the trunks. The timing of the canopy-sheds. The movement patterns of the fauna. It all fits together in ways that suggest deep organization, but organization of a type that human minds aren't shaped to recognize immediately.

The darkness isn't absolute but it's pervasive. Even when you know intellectually that it's daytime — that somewhere above the canopy layers, the sun is shining — the twilight makes it feel like eternal evening. This affects perception. Colors desaturate. Everything becomes shades of blue-grey and brown-grey and black. The world loses its edges. You find yourself navigating more by sound than by sight: the hum of the trunks, the drip of condensation, the movement of animals, the way your footsteps sound on different surfaces.

At night, the darkness deepens but doesn't fundamentally change. There's no moon — the canopy blocks it. There are no stars. The only light sources are occasional bioluminescence from canopy-dwelling organisms (faint, scattered, unreliable) and those strange electrical discharges that flicker and vanish. During deep night, you can lose all sense of space. You know intellectually that the trunks are there, that the canopy is overhead, that the ground is beneath you. But you can't see any of it. You can only hear the wind-song and feel the cold and trust that the world is still arranged the way you remember. People must have learned to sleep through this, or learned to not require the visual confirmation that most humans need to feel secure.

The sound of the place is layered, complex, never quite monotonous despite its constancy. The base layer is the wind-song: that deep thrumming of air through hollow trunks, rising and falling with the wind itself but never stopping entirely. Over this, the condensation: drip, drip, the patter of water hitting stone or wood or water. Over this, the animals: calls and movements, the rustle of bodies through vegetation, the sudden louder sounds of chases or fights or matings. And occasionally, breaking through everything, the crack of a trunk splitting — whether from frost or internal failure or lightning strike — a report like a gunshot that echoes through the forest and leaves silence in its wake, as if everything paused to acknowledge the death of part of the structure.

In the platforms and stone circles, you sometimes find objects that suggest ritual or art, though the line between tool and symbol is unclear. Stones with holes drilled through them, threaded on rotted fiber — counting devices? ornament? something else? Bones arranged in patterns — natural accumulation or deliberate design? Small carvings in the platform wood: spirals, cross-hatching, simple geometric forms. These could be idle scratchings, or they could mean something. The objects are ambiguous enough that you project meaning onto them, and the act of projection makes you realize how much you're trying to find pattern, find story, find human purpose in a place that might have had different priorities entirely.

The scaffolding creatures — the most common ones, at least — have a peculiar relationship with the human-built platforms. They use them, clearly. You find their nests on platforms, their droppings, signs of habitation. But they don't damage the structures. They don't gnaw through the support ropes or dislodge the planks. It's as if they understand these things as different from natural features, as things to be respected or at least accommodated. This suggests intelligence, or at least behavioral flexibility. Or it suggests that the platforms were built with the scaffolding creatures' nature in mind, designed to be usable by both species.

There's an ecology within the trunks themselves. The hollow structures host entire communities: fungi growing on the inner walls, insects feeding on the fungi, small vertebrates feeding on the insects, all of it happening in the darkness of the trunk interior. The structural plants tolerate this — more than tolerate, they seem to incorporate it. The internal hollowing that animals create allows better air circulation, which prevents rot. The droppings provide nutrients. The system is mutualistic on scales from millimeters to hundreds of meters.

As you spend time in the forest, you start noticing patterns in the canopy-shed cycles. They're not random. There are areas that shed frequently — every few days — and areas that might go weeks between sheds. The frequent-shed zones tend to have younger vegetation, more vigorous growth. The infrequent zones are more stable, older, the canopy plants there having achieved some kind of equilibrium. The animals know these patterns. They avoid the frequent-shed zones during shedding times, move through them afterward when the ground is temporarily rich with fallen organic matter.

You also notice that the stone circles are positioned with relationship to shed-patterns. They're usually in moderate zones: places that get enough water to collect dripping condensation for drinking, but not so much that they're regularly flooded during sheds. This suggests the builders understood the forest's hydrology, could read its patterns well enough to site their camps optimally. That knowledge must have taken generations to accumulate.

The temperature varies a little — it's slightly warmer after a canopy-shed, slightly colder when frost is actively forming at night — but it never varies much. The forest maintains a narrow range, cold but not lethally so, stable but not comfortable. This constancy is part of what makes the place feel timeless. There are no seasons in the usual sense. The canopy blocks seasonal light changes. The cold prevents seasonal temperature swings. The only cycles are the internal ones: the condensation, the fruiting, the animal movements, the slow growth of the structural plants.

Everything happens at the pace of cold. Growth is slow. Decay is slow. The mummified bodies can last years. The built structures can last generations. Even the scree shifts slowly, one frost-heave at a time. If you could watch the forest at time-lapse — centuries compressed to seconds — you might see it flowing, adjusting, the trunks rising and falling like breathing, the canopy spreading and contracting. But at human speed, it's nearly static. Nearly, but not quite. There's always some small change: a new shoot, a fallen rope, a stone moved, a creature born or dying.

The vastness means you're always small. Even climbing high into the trunks doesn't change this. You gain altitude, but the forest gains with you. The scale defeats ambition. You can't map it, can't encompass it, can't hold it in your mind as a completed thing. You can only experience the part you're in, and trust that it continues beyond your perception. The builders of the platforms must have felt this too — that sense of working within something too large to fully comprehend, making small structures in a vast architecture, marking routes through infinite space.

And yet the forest is also hollow, empty. For all its biological richness, most of any given volume is air and space. The trunks are porous. The canopy is far away. The ground is bare stone. The fauna is abundant but dispersed. You can stand in the middle of everything and still be essentially alone, surrounded by emptiness that happens to be structured by living pillars and roofed by living ceiling. This combination — biologically total and spatially empty — creates a specific kind of loneliness, or maybe peace. You're never truly alone (there's always something moving, something growing, something alive nearby) but you're never crowded either.

The place doesn't care about you. It's not hostile, but it's not accommodating. It operates according to its own principles: the hydraulics of sap and water, the mechanics of hollow-tube structures, the ecology of cold-adapted organisms, the slow physics of high-mountain air. You can learn these principles, can adapt to them, can find the edges where human needs overlap with forest function. The builders did. But you can't change them, and they won't change for you. The forest was here before humans found it, and it will be here after the last platform rots away.

Which makes the built things — those simple stone circles, those deteriorating platforms — somehow more significant. They're not just shelters or work-sites. They're arguments: claims that human presence matters, that the forest can be inhabited, that meaning can be made even in a place so fundamentally other. Whether those arguments succeeded depends on why the builders are gone. Did they leave? Die? Become something else? The evidence is ambiguous. The structures remain, slowly failing, slowly being absorbed back into the larger patterns of growth and weather and time.

You can walk for days and never leave the forest. The twilight continues. The humming continues. The cold continues. And gradually you stop expecting it to change. You adjust to permanent dusk, to navigational uncertainty, to the constant low-level presence of the scaffolding creatures and their calls. You learn to predict the canopy-sheds, to find good stone-circles for camping, to read the subtle differences between trunk-stands. The place begins to make sense, not through understanding exactly, but through accommodation. You become one more moving thing in the vertical maze, neither builder nor scaffolding creature, but something that can exist here, for a while, making your small marks and following your small purposes beneath the vast living roof.

And always, the sound. The wind through the hollow places. The drip of condensation. The scratch of claws. The crack of splitting trunks. The calls echoing. Your own breathing. Your own footsteps on the loose stone, on the solid platform wood, on the occasional patch of dark moss. The sound of being here, in this cold, high, nearly-dark forest that is everywhere and hollow and alive and indifferent and magnificent and strange. The sound that reminds you, constantly, that you're inside something—not a room or a building or even a wilderness in the usual sense, but an architecture of life itself, scaffolding that reaches toward light you can't see, rooted in stone you can barely perceive, existing in the narrow space between growth and cold and emptiness where somehow, improbably, everything thrives.
