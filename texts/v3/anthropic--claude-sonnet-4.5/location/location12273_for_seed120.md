# The Throat Garden

There is a place being born inside a mountain that should not exist.

You reach it through a vertical chimney, climbing worn handholds that someone—many someones—carved into stone that hasn't finished cooling. The rock is blood-warm even where your palm rests. The higher you climb, the hotter it gets, which is the first wrong thing, the first sign that you're entering a pocket of space where the ordinary sequences have been interrupted.

The chimney opens into a cleft no larger than a modest room, but the room curves in ways that resist measurement. It is a throat-shaped hollow in newborn volcanic stone, a cavity that formed as gases escaped from cooling magma perhaps months ago, perhaps still forming now, perhaps forming continuously in a time that doesn't progress the way time should. The walls are basalt and obsidian and something between the two—rock that can't decide what it has become, that shows swirls of both textures, that gleams black in some light and shows the frozen ripples of liquid stone in others.

Every surface has been worked.

Every handspan of wall has been carved, built upon, inlaid, pressed with marks, fitted with objects, wrapped with cordage, studded with purpose. There is no natural stone left exposed. What you're seeing is layer upon layer of human—or human-like—intervention, so dense that the additions have become a second geology, a made-stratum as thick as your forearm in some places, paper-thin in others. Clay tiles fitted together with hair-fine precision. Woven grass mats pressed into the heat-soft stone and now permanently fused there. Fragments of obsidian knapped into fish-scale patterns that cover entire sections of wall. Bone fragments—small bones, bird-sized, fish-sized—mortared with some paste that has hardened to the consistency of stone, creating a fossil bed that never formed naturally, that was *constructed* as though someone were making a memory of sedimentary process rather than allowing it to occur.

The air is wrong.

It should be stifling—the heat radiates from every surface in waves you can see, ripples of thermal distortion that make the walls seem to breathe. But somehow there's water. The atmosphere is so humid it clings to your skin instantly, soaking into fabric, condensing in your hair. You breathe it more than air. Droplets form on the obsidian scales, running down in channels that have been carved specifically to guide them, and where they fall they splash into containers, into carved depressions, into small pools someone has built from fitted stones no larger than teeth.

The water comes from nowhere you can identify. It weeps from the rock itself, from stone too hot to touch comfortably, beading and running and dripping with a sound that fills the space like rainfall, like a hundred small drums, like the inside of a mouth swallowing. The acoustic is deliberate. Each surface has been shaped not just visually but sonically. The drops hit different materials—taut hide stretched over bowl-shaped hollows, clay vessels tuned to different pitches, stretched sinew that rings when struck by falling water, wooden stakes driven into cracks at angles that make them hum when water runs down them.

The result is layered percussion, dense and arrhythmic and almost overwhelming. Nothing is silent. The space *sings* with water-sound, and under that is the tick and ping of cooling stone, the occasional sharp crack as thermal stress breaks something, and something else—a sound like breathing, like slow inhalation and exhalation, coming from deeper in the mountain or from the mountain itself.

Vegetation grows in ways that should not be possible.

In the cracks between constructed surfaces, in the spaces where water pools, in the deposits of ash and organic matter that have been deliberately placed and are being deliberately cultivated, plants grow at a speed you can almost see. They are mostly ferns—small, dense ferns with leaves like dark green hands, fronds that uncurl visibly over the course of minutes. And mosses, thick carpets of moss in shades of green so deep they're nearly black, moss that grows on the hot stone, that seems to thrive in the heat-and-wet, that covers surfaces like fur, like living upholstery.

But there are other things. Root-tangles hanging from the ceiling where no soil exists, drawing moisture directly from the dripping air. Vines thin as wire that have been trained along the carved channels, guided into specific patterns, woven through the bone-and-obsidian surfaces like green veins through manufactured flesh. Some of them flower—tiny white flowers that smell of fermentation, of brewing, of organic chemistry in progress. The flowers open and close on rhythms that don't match the dim, inconsistent light.

The light itself is another wrongness.

It comes from openings that shouldn't exist, that weren't there a moment ago, that appear to shift position when you're not looking directly at them. Shafts of exterior light—mountain daylight, hot and tropical—lance through gaps in the rock, but the gaps don't connect logically to the mountain's outside. You climbed up, but the light comes from angles that suggest you're somehow at the mountain's base, or in its middle, or that the mountain's position relative to the sun is changing, or that there are multiple suns, or that the light is older than it should be, showing a sky from a different time of day than the one you left behind.

Where the light falls, things grow faster. Where it doesn't, there's a dim phosphorescence from some of the mosses, from certain pale fungi that someone has encouraged in shadowed corners, from the water itself which seems faintly luminous, as though it carries dissolved light the way seawater carries salt.

Every surface is occupied. Every cubic inch of this throat-shaped hollow serves multiple purposes. A section of wall might be: carved basalt substrate, inlaid with clay tiles, those tiles inscribed with marks, moss growing in the carved marks, a lattice of vine trained across the surface, obsidian flakes hanging from the vine on plant-fiber cord, small shell beads threaded on the cord, water running down the shells, the water collecting in a bone cup wedged into a crack, a fern growing from the cup, its roots visible through splits in the bone.

Nothing is single-purpose. Nothing is simply present. Everything is part of systems within systems, functions layered so densely that parsing them is like trying to read a book where every letter is also a word in another language, where every sentence contains encrypted messages in its spacing, where the paper itself is made of ground-up pages from earlier books.

The construction is entirely made by hand and simple tools. You find them lying in niches, resting in the small spaces left between denser accumulations: stone blades still sharp, the edges showing use-wear; bone awls polished by palm-friction; wooden stakes with fire-hardened points; lengths of cordage made from plant fibers so carefully processed they're nearly as fine as thread; clay vessels in various stages of completion, some fired hard, others still damp and pliable; mortars and pestles carved from dense stone; drills made from hardwood and sand.

The people who work here—who are still working here, who have always been working here—appear occasionally, briefly, incompletely. You don't see them so much as see evidence of their immediate presence: a hand withdrawing from a crack where it was placing something, a shadow crossing one of the light-shafts that doesn't correspond to any body you can see, the sound of breathing from a corner that appears empty, the definite sense that someone just passed behind you in a space too narrow for passage.

Once, you glimpse a figure crouched in an alcove that wasn't there before, pressing something into the wall with absolute concentration, fingers moving in patterns that look like prayer and surgery combined. The person is small, compact, skin dark with stone-dust and damp, wearing something that might be woven plant fiber or might be moss cultivated directly on the body. You blink and the alcove is empty, but whatever was being pressed into the wall is now there, permanent, part of the accumulated surface.

The fauna is rare but intensely present when it appears.

A small lizard, brilliant green, moves across the carved surfaces eating something—insects too small to see, or possibly the flowers, or possibly the moss itself, or possibly the meanings embedded in the carvings. It moves in stop-motion bursts, perfectly camouflaged against the vegetation, then impossibly visible against the black stone, then gone.

Something flies through one of the light-shafts—you register wings, a body the size of your thumb, an iridescent flash—and it's gone before you can identify it as bat or bird or insect or something with no name yet.

In one of the water pools, you see movement. Something swimming, something segmented, something with too many legs or not enough, something that might be aquatic or might be a land creature that has adapted to the permanent wet, or might be something that was made here, constructed piece by piece from other organisms and organic materials and grown in the hot wet dark until it became independently viable.

The weirdness is total. It's not an addition to the space—it's fundamental to its existence.

The mountain is active, still forming, but this hollow within it is more finished than anything outside it, has been worked on for longer than the mountain has existed, contains evidence of habitation and construction that predates the stone that contains it. This is impossible, but the impossibility is visible, tangible, built into the structure of the place.

Time moves differently in different parts of the chamber. A fern in one corner goes through what looks like a week's growth while you watch. A drop of water falls from the ceiling and takes subjectively minutes to reach the floor, refracting light in slow motion, and when it hits it makes a sound that echoes forward and backward simultaneously, that you hear before and after the impact.

The inscriptions on the walls are in multiple languages, multiple writing systems, some of which are clearly older than others but all of which are equally fresh, equally recently carved. Some of them move, lines reshaping themselves when you're not looking directly at them, or possibly when you are looking at them, your observation causing them to update, to revise, to correct themselves toward some truer version.

There are maps carved into certain surfaces, but they don't map this place or anywhere near this place. They show coastlines that don't exist, mountain ranges arranged in patterns that don't match any real geology, rivers that flow in impossible directions. Yet they're rendered with absolute precision, with the confidence of survey and measurement, as though someone traveled these nonexistent places and recorded them faithfully.

One section of wall contains what looks like a calendar, but it has too many months, or not enough, or the months are different lengths, or they overlap. Marks are being added to it continuously—you can see fresh marks that weren't there when you entered, though you haven't seen anyone make them.

The heat fluctuates. Sometimes the air is merely hot, tropical-hot, rainforest-hot. Other times it surges to something nearly unbearable, the stone surfaces shimmering, the water flash-evaporating before it can drip, your breath coming in gasps. Then it eases again, cycling without pattern, or with a pattern too complex to perceive in the short time you're there.

The moisture responds to the heat in ways that suggest purpose rather than physics. When the temperature spikes, the humidity increases proportionally, impossibly, as though the water is being generated in response to the heat rather than evaporated by it. The air becomes soup-thick, nearly unbreathable, but somehow it carries more oxygen than thin air, keeps you conscious and functional even as you feel like you're drowning in humidity.

Plants respond immediately to the cycles. Fronds uncurl during hot-wet phases, then curl tight during brief dry moments. Flowers open and close. Growth accelerates or pauses. The vegetation is attuned to the space's rhythms in ways that suggest long co-evolution, or deliberate breeding, or that the plants themselves are partly responsible for the rhythms, generating them through some collective metabolism.

The processes layer and intersect. Water falls on moss, which grows, which holds more water, which attracts insects (you see them occasionally, tiny moving dots), which feed the lizard, which defecates, which fertilizes the moss, which someone harvests and uses to make paste, which they apply to the walls to hold other materials, which changes the wall's texture, which changes how water flows, which determines where new moss grows, which...

The cycles are so tight, so interconnected, that the whole space operates like a single organism, or like a machine made of living parts, or like an ecosystem compressed to room-size but maintaining full complexity, all niches filled, all roles occupied, predation and decomposition and growth happening in visible, audible, tangible proximity.

You realize gradually that you're not observing this place—you're inside something. The throat-shaped hollow isn't a container for these processes; it's a organ performing a function. The mountain is growing this space the way a body grows tissue, and the people who work here aren't decorating or inhabiting it—they're midwifing it, participating in its formation, necessary to its existence.

What they're making with their careful placement and carving and weaving and planting isn't art or architecture or agriculture, though it resembles all three. They're making the place remember itself forward. They're constructing the conditions for the space to exist in the first place, working backward through causality, creating the past that will allow the present to cohere.

The sound reaches a crescendo periodically—all the water-music, all the thermal pinging, all the breathing-mountain sounds combining into something that feels like language, like meaning on the edge of comprehensibility. You almost understand it. You can almost parse the message in the rhythms, the information encoded in which surfaces water hits in which sequence, the semantics of thermal expansion and contraction.

Then it eases, drops back to the constant moderate roar, the acoustic texture that fills the space like light fills a room, omnipresent and defining.

In the densest sections—where the accumulation of made-things is thickest, where layer upon layer of human effort has built up into masses that no longer resemble construction and start to resemble geology—the boundary between natural and made becomes meaningless. You can't tell if you're looking at stone that has been carved or stone that grew in those shapes because it was carved. The obsidian scales might be knapped or they might be how the obsidian cooled because someone was there to witness it cooling, their observation shaping the crystal structure. The bone fragments mortared into patterns might be arranged or they might be fossils of patterns that will exist in the future, embedded now as evidence, as prophecy.

There are sections where you can't see the wall at all—only vegetation, only the green living layer, moss and fern and vine grown so thick they're architectural, they're load-bearing, they hold up sections of ceiling, they form the structure the stone merely supports. The plants are the real construction. The stone is scaffolding.

In other sections, the geometry is clearly impossible. Angles don't add up. Surfaces connect in ways that violate Euclidean space. You can follow a carved line with your finger and end up somewhere you weren't expecting, having traversed more or less distance than the visible length of the line. The space isn't larger on the inside than the outside—it's differently sized, sized according to rules that apply within it but nowhere else.

You find food growing in certain places, though you didn't plant it and can't imagine how it got there. Small fruits, dark and glossy, growing directly from the moss. Edible mushrooms emerging from the paste between materials. Roots that can be pulled from cracks and eaten raw, tasting of minerals and heat and something else, something that might be the mountain's flavor, its essential taste.

Someone wants you to be able to stay here. The whole construction includes provisions for human presence, human survival. The water is fresh, the food is real, there are alcoves sized for sleeping, surfaces that could be sat upon, spaces that invite habitation.

But you're also certain that you shouldn't stay long. The time-strangeness, the impossibility layered into the structure—too much exposure might make it difficult to leave, or might make you too adjusted to this pocket reality, too unable to function in the normal world outside where time moves predictably and space behaves itself and plants grow at reasonable speeds.

The builders—the people you sometimes almost see—have adapted to it. They exist partly outside normal causality now. They flicker in and out of visibility because they're not quite synchronized with regular time anymore. They've become continuous with the space, part of its processes, their lifespans stretched or compressed or made into loops, their bodies partially constructed from the same materials they work with.

You see a hand placing clay tiles and the hand is covered with moss, or possibly made of moss, or possibly human flesh that has learned to photosynthesize. You see someone's back, bent over work in an alcove, and the spine is visible through transparent skin, but between the vertebrae are small plants growing, roots wrapped around bone, drawing nutrients, contributing oxygen.

They're not dying. They're not suffering. They've become something else, something adapted to this environment they're creating, this pocket of reality with its own rules. They're the fauna, the rare-but-present animal life, transformed through long habitation into creatures that can survive and work in a place where time loops and space folds and the mountain is simultaneously ancient and newborn.

You understand suddenly why you were allowed to enter, why the handholds were carved in the chimney, why the space accommodates visitors. The work requires witness. Reality-breaking of this magnitude needs observers from outside to stabilize it, to confirm it, to carry word of its existence back to the normal world where such things aren't supposed to be possible.

You're not here to help build. You're here to verify that what's being built exists.

The light shifts again, coming from a new direction or a new time of day, or possibly from a different sun in a different sky that exists only within this mountain, only in this throat-shaped hollow that someone is growing like a pearl, layer by layer, impossibility by impossibility, working backward from some finished vision into the raw material of new stone, teaching the rock to remember something that hasn't happened yet, constructing a space that will have always existed once it's done.

The sound rises. The heat pulses. Water falls in patterns that seem almost deliberate, almost notation. The plants lean toward something. The invisible builders are present all around you, working in the spaces between seconds, placing and carving and weaving, adding to the layers, making the place more itself with every touch, every adjustment, every small act of construction that is also an act of cultivation that is also an act of prayer that is also the work of bringing a world into being one handspan at a time.
