# The Manifold Flats

The land here is so flat it seems to disobey perspective. You can see for what might be thirty miles, or three, or three hundred—the horizon refuses to hold a consistent distance. It's a basin, technically, though "basin" implies containment and what stretches before you feels more like the floor of something that has been patient enough to outlast its own walls.

The ground is black glass, or was glass, or remembers being glass. Obsidian plains that have been weathered for so long they've acquired a texture like sharkskin—still smooth enough to reflect, but rough enough to grip. The surface is broken into polygonal plates, each one the size of a city block or a dinner table, depending on which moment you're observing it in. Between the plates: water. Not flowing water. Not standing water either, exactly. Water that is *present*, dark as crude oil, collected in the seams like the world is leaking from its joints.

The temperature hovers just above freezing. Your breath doesn't quite cloud. The water doesn't quite ice over. Everything exists in that narrow band where change threatens but never arrives.

But the water does other things.

It *breathes*. You can hear it—a vast, rhythmic respiration that sounds like industrial ventilation systems running in the walls of reality. Every seven or nine or thirteen seconds (the interval changes, but always to a prime number), the water level rises by an inch in all the seams simultaneously. Then it falls again. The sound is everywhere and nowhere, a rumbling wheeze that you feel in your sternum before you hear it in your ears. With each exhalation, mist rises—not much, just enough to keep the air thick, to make distant things uncertain, to coat every surface with a film of condensation that never quite becomes droplets.

The black glass plates are *packed* with machinery.

Not machines in the sense of devices that were built and placed—machines in the sense that the ground itself has mechanical properties. Gears the size of manhole covers protrude from the obsidian, their teeth worn down to nubs but still turning, always turning, driven by no visible mechanism. Pistons rise and fall in their housings, slick with that dark water, their rhythm completely unrelated to the breathing of the seams. There are things like turbine blades, frozen mid-spin but vibrating intensely, creating a hum that sits just below hearing but above silence. Cable bundles, thick as tree trunks, emerge from one plate and dive into another, their rubber insulation cracked and weeping a substance that might be oil or ichor or liquefied time.

The density is overwhelming. There is no empty space. Where there isn't a gear, there's a housing. Where there isn't a housing, there's a valve. Where there isn't a valve, there's a pressure gauge with a cracked face, its needle trembling between two measurements that don't exist on any standard scale. Everything is packed so tightly that at first it seems chaotic, but watch for an hour and you'll see it: patterns. Nothing functional, but patterns nonetheless—repeating intervals, symmetries that appear and dissolve, geometries that suggest purpose without ever achieving it.

This is not a factory that failed. This is the *end state* of industry. What remains when manufacturing has been running so long that the distinction between machine and geology has worn away.

The animals are impossible, and there are thousands of them.

They move through the machinery like fish through coral, and they are more numerous than any ecosystem should support in such barren conditions. Things like cats, but with too many vertebrae, their spines rippling in S-curves as they navigate between the gears. Things like birds, but they don't fly so much as *skip*—they're here, then they're there, without crossing the intervening space, and each skip is accompanied by a sound like a circuit breaker engaging. Things that might be crustaceans the size of dogs, their shells made of tarnished brass, their legs clicking against the glass in rhythms that almost match the machinery but never quite sync.

They don't seem to eat. You never see them feed, though you watch them for hours. They simply *are*, in great profusion, moving with purpose through a landscape that offers nothing obvious to survive on. Sometimes they pause at the water seams and lower their heads—mouths, sensors, feeding appendages, whatever they have—and the water level drops slightly, locally, as if they're drinking space itself.

The fauna make most of the noise, but not all of it. Their sounds are biological—the clicking, the chittering, the wet breathing, the scratch of shell and claw on glass—but amplified somehow, given resonance by the landscape. A small skipping-bird makes a sound like a transformer exploding. A brass-shelled thing clicking across a plate generates reverberations like footsteps in a cathedral made of sheet metal. There's a species that resembles nothing so much as an eel made of segmented iron plates; when it moves through the water seams, it produces a grinding shriek that carries for miles, echoing off nothing because there's nothing to echo off, but echoing anyway.

Beneath and through and between all the biological and mechanical noise is another sound: the *industrial hymn*. It's what you might hear if you put your ear to the hull of a massive ship—the sum of all vibrations, all running engines, all fluid moving through pipes, all stress on metal, unified into a single low-frequency drone that's less heard than inhabited. It makes your teeth ache. It makes you wonder if sound can be structural, if you're standing on noise that has become solid.

The light is wrong in a way that takes time to articulate.

It's dim, yes—perpetual twilight, perhaps an hour after sunset or before sunrise. But there's no sun. There's no visible source at all. The sky is there—you can look up and see it, a flat gray expanse like primer paint—but it's not bright enough to account for even the meager illumination that exists. The light seems to come from *between* things. From the seams. From the spaces in the machinery. A gear casts a shadow, but the shadow is brighter than it should be, and if you look at where the shadow falls, you might see that it contains more detail than the object casting it.

Sometimes—not often, but regularly—there are *lags*. A creature will move and its shadow will follow a half-second later. A piston will complete its stroke and the sound will arrive three heartbeats afterwards. These desynchronizations propagate outward in waves, and when they do, the packed machinery shimmers like a heat mirage, and for just a moment you can see that everything is in multiple places at once, occupying overlapping positions, all the possibilities existing simultaneously until observation collapses them back into something approximately singular.

The water contains memories that aren't yours.

When the seams exhale and the mist rises, you sometimes see things in it. Faces, perhaps. Blueprints. Equations written in notation that predates mathematics. They form and dissolve in the three seconds it takes the mist to settle back down. If you cup your hands and gather some of the water—and you can, it's just water, cold and real and wet—and if you look into it closely, you might see it reflecting things that aren't around you. Structures that aren't there. Skies with different colors. Yourself, but standing in a different position than you're actually in.

The creatures drink it anyway. Or feed on it. Or extract something from it that sustains them in the absence of any visible food chain.

Over time, you begin to notice the ruins.

Not structures—the "built" parameter is low, and there are no buildings, no monuments, no walls. But there are *implications* of structure embedded in the machine-ground. A series of gears arranged in what might be the footprint of a room. A configuration of pipes that suggests a doorway. Pressure gauges clustered in a pattern that resembles a window—and when you look through the space they define, the horizon is different than when you look around them.

These non-buildings don't feel constructed. They feel *secreted*. Like the landscape has been running its industrial processes for so long that architecture has precipitated out as a byproduct, the way minerals precipitate from oversaturated water.

There is one structure, barely. A framework of rusted I-beams forming something like a tower or a derrick, maybe forty feet high, standing alone on the flats about two miles from where you entered (or where you are, or where you've always been—causality is negotiable here). It's the only thing with significant vertical presence. Creatures congregate around its base in greater density than elsewhere, packing themselves into its shadow even though shadows here don't work the way they should. The framework vibrates constantly, and the sound it makes is almost musical, a deep resonant chord that clashes with the industrial hymn in ways that create beat frequencies, interference patterns in audio form.

You can walk toward it. The distance doesn't change normally. Sometimes you get closer. Sometimes it recedes. Sometimes you walk for an hour and you've arrived, and sometimes you walk for an hour and it's exactly the same distance away it was when you started. The creatures have no such problem. They approach and depart freely, their skipping and clicking and grinding carrying them to and from the framework according to logics that aren't spatial.

The weather—if it can be called that—operates on cycles that don't correspond to days.

The breathing of the water seams is the most regular phenomenon, that prime-number-interval inhale-exhale. But there are other cycles layered over it. Every seventeen breaths (or nineteen, or twenty-three), the mist rises higher, reaches farther, lasts longer. Every few hundred breaths, the machinery changes pitch—not significantly, just a half-step up or down in the industrial hymn's fundamental tone. The creatures respond to this, their movements becoming more agitated or more languid, depending on whether the pitch rises or falls.

Occasionally—and you can't predict when—there are *reversals*. For a period of anywhere from thirty seconds to three minutes, everything runs backward. Water falls from the air into the seams, its level dropping as it does so. Creatures un-move, retracing their paths in perfect reverse. The sounds play in rewind, which should be impossible for continuous drones but happens anyway. The gears turn counterclockwise. During these reversals, the light doesn't change, which means for a few moments the shadows disconnect entirely from their sources, drifting free like oil on the surface of the dark water.

Then, without transition, everything resumes forward motion. Nothing has changed, but everything has been *un-changed*, reset to a state that isn't quite identical to where it was but can't be distinguished from it without instruments that don't exist.

The ecology—again, if it can be called that—is purely consuming. The creatures eat nothing and produce nothing, or they eat and produce the same nothing, an ouroboros of sustenance. There are no plants because plants would imply growth, and growth would imply progression, and progression would disturb the deep equilibrium this place has achieved. Whatever these animals are feeding on, it's not part of any cycle you can observe. Perhaps they're extracting energy from the machinery's vibrations. Perhaps they're consuming the lag between cause and effect, fattening themselves on desynchronization.

There are predators, probably. You see creatures pursue other creatures, see the hunt-chase-pounce behavior, but you never see a kill, never see consumption. The chases always end in one of two ways: the pursued reaches a seam and *pours* itself into the water, disappearing completely, or the lag hits and both predator and prey occupy the same space simultaneously for a half-second, and when they separate they've exchanged something—size, maybe, or velocity, or intentionality.

The weirdness is structural. It's not that strange things happen in this place; it's that this place is made of strangeness, the same way other places are made of rock and soil.

Causality is porous. You'll watch a gauge needle drop, and then hear the pressure change, and then see the valve that caused it begin to close. You'll watch your footprint appear in the thin layer of condensation on the glass, and then feel your foot descend, and then decide to take the step. This doesn't happen constantly—if it did, you'd adapt, it would become the new normal. But it happens randomly enough that you can never trust the sequence of events, never quite believe that what you see happening is happening in the order you're seeing it.

There are echoes without originating sounds. You'll hear something—a crash, a shriek, a grinding explosion—and you'll turn toward it, and there will be nothing, no source, no disturbance. But then, minutes or hours later, something will occur—a gear will slip, a creature will collide with a piston—and you'll recognize it as the thing that caused the echo you heard earlier. The sound arrived before its source.

Sometimes you are in two places at once. Not metaphorically. You'll be standing by a particular cluster of turbine blades, and you'll also be standing eighty feet to the south near a valve housing, and both positions are equally real, equally *you*, and you can see from both perspectives simultaneously. This lasts until you try to move, at which point you collapse back into a single location that might be either of the two you were just occupying, or might be somewhere else entirely.

The creatures seem unbothered by any of this. Perhaps they experience reality differently. Perhaps they're native to a place where these conditions are normal. Or perhaps—and this seems more likely the longer you observe—they're not entirely creatures at all, but mobile extensions of the landscape itself, three-dimensional cross-sections of processes occurring in more dimensions than you can perceive, animated geology that only resembles life when viewed from your limited perspective.

At the horizon, or what serves as the horizon, there are suggestions of *more*. The flats extend beyond sight, but in the mist and the uncertain light, you can sometimes make out other formations. A ridge of something that might be more machinery, built up in layers. A depression that might be a deeper basin, filled with darker water. Vertical elements that could be more frameworks, or could be something else entirely, or could be mirages generated by the acoustic properties of the industrial hymn.

You could walk toward them. The distance problem would apply—maybe you'd arrive, maybe you wouldn't—but the possibility exists. The creatures move in those directions freely, disappearing into the mist and the distance and the desynchronization, then reappearing hours or minutes or days later from completely different directions.

The place is ancient beyond ancient. The machinery isn't worn out; it's worn *past* out, worn into a state where wear and function have become indistinguishable. The black glass of the ground has been here since before glass was invented, since before silicon was silicon. This is what remains when everything has finished happening, when all processes have run to completion and then kept running, when industry has outlasted its own purpose and continued purely through momentum, through the inability of moving things to stop.

There is no sense of abandonment. Abandonment implies that someone left, that something ended. This place was never occupied in a way that ending would make sense. It's a machine that has always run and will always run, tended by creatures that might predate or postdate biological life, lubricated by water that contains too much information, generating sounds and products and realities that serve no function except to be generated.

You stand on the black glass, and the seams breathe, and the machinery turns, and the creatures click and skip and grind through their paths, and the light comes from between, and the sounds arrive when they choose, and you are here, and you are also there, and the horizon refuses to commit to a distance, and the mist rises carrying blueprints for machines that were never built or were always built or are building themselves right now in the spaces between your observations.

The Manifold Flats. The industrial end-state. The place where complexity has become so dense it's indistinguishable from simplicity, where everything happens and therefore nothing happens, where the age of the world has worn it into a shape that can only be described as *itself*.

The breathing continues. The creatures move. The gears turn.

You are part of the pattern now, another element in the density, another sound in the hymn, another lag in the sequence of cause and effect.

The water remembers you, even though you haven't left yet.

The machinery turns.

The flats extend.

Forever is the wrong word, but there isn't a right one.
