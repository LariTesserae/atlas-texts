# The Throat

There is a place where water screams.

Not metaphorically. The water itself produces sound — a relentless, layered roar that seems to come from every direction simultaneously, so loud it becomes a physical pressure against your eardrums, your chest, the membrane between your thoughts and the world. It is the sound of water being forced through geometries it was never meant to occupy.

You stand — if standing means anything here — on a narrow platform of volcanic glass, maybe thirty meters across, one of seven or eight similar formations visible in the churning gloom. The glass is black, fresh, sharp-edged where it broke from whatever crucible birthed it. Recent. Days old, perhaps. Weeks at most. The surface is smooth but not polished, and it's warm underfoot, radiating heat from below, from whatever violence shaped it. When you crouch and press your palm flat, you can feel a vibration, a deep grinding that suggests the formation process hasn't entirely finished.

Above — though the concept strains — is water. Not sky. Not air-then-water, but water where sky should be. A ceiling of ocean, somehow inverted, suspended, held in place by rules that flicker in and out of coherence. You can see up into it: dark shapes moving in the depths-that-are-heights, currents writhing like muscles under skin, and occasionally something that might be light filtering down (up?) from an impossible surface somewhere beyond. The boundary between air-space and water-ceiling is not clean. It ripples, bulges, occasionally releases fat drops that fall upward, accelerating into the ocean overhead with a sound like stones thrown into wells.

The sound is everything. It drowns thought. The water-ceiling groans and shrieks as it moves against itself, as pressure redistributes along fault lines in reality's fabric. From somewhere — everywhere — comes the bass roar of water rushing through apertures, through the spaces between one state and another. And there are other sounds layered within: crystalline crashes, prolonged tearing noises, a stuttering percussion like hail on metal, and beneath it all, a subsonic rumble that you feel in your bones more than hear. Your inner ear struggles to maintain equilibrium. After a few minutes, you stop trusting your sense of which way is down.

The air is warm and thick with moisture, though not quite foggy. It tastes mineral-sharp, volcanic, with an alkaline bite that coats your tongue. The humidity is oppressive but not uncomfortable — the temperature hovers just at body warmth, making the boundary between your skin and the atmosphere uncertain. You sweat, but it doesn't evaporate; it simply adds to the general wetness that pervades everything.

Between the platforms — these islands of volcanic glass floating in something that isn't quite space — is water. But wrong water. It exists in vertical sheets, in cylindrical streams that connect floor to ceiling, in spherical masses that hang trembling in nothing, in ribbons that spiral around invisible axes. The water moves constantly, but not in response to gravity. A column might flow upward for several seconds, then abruptly reverse. A sheet of water might hang vertical and still, then suddenly collapse into a horizontal plane and rush outward like a tabletop made of liquid. The physics here are negotiable, inconsistent, as if the fundamental forces are in argument with themselves.

Sometimes two columns of water intersect at right angles and pass through each other without mixing. Sometimes they merge explosively, the combined mass immediately seeking a new configuration — a torus, perhaps, or a helix, or something without a geometric name. Sometimes water simply appears in the middle of the air-space, bursting into being with a crack of displaced atmosphere, and sometimes it vanishes just as suddenly, leaving only a fine mist that glows faintly in the dim light.

The light itself is murky, sourceless, barely adequate. It seems to emanate from the water itself, a faint bioluminescence or perhaps a property of the water's interaction with this broken space. Everything is rendered in shades of blue-black and gray-green, with occasional flashes of brighter cyan when water forms or reforms. The platforms are visible as darker silhouettes, and you can see their edges, but distance becomes uncertain. That platform thirty meters away might be forty, or twenty, or might not maintain a consistent distance at all.

On your platform, sparse vegetation clings to existence. Small, colorless plants — pale green-gray, almost translucent — grow in cracks where the volcanic glass fractured. They're simple structures: thin stalks, tiny leaves, root systems that seem to draw sustenance from the glass itself. They look primitive, like the first plants to colonize fresh lava flows, which perhaps is exactly what they are. Some have small bulbous structures at their tips, bladder-like organs that might be for storing air or water or both. They bend and sway not with wind — there is no wind, only the periodic rush of air displaced by water's movements — but in response to pressure waves in the sound itself.

Nothing moves on the platform with you. But occasionally, in peripheral vision, you catch motion on another platform: something small and pale, many-legged perhaps, picking its way across the glass. It's gone when you turn to look directly. Once, you see something larger in the water-ceiling overhead, a sinuous shape that might be ten meters long or might be closer and smaller than you think. It doesn't swim so much as *phase* through the water, appearing in one location, then another, as if the medium it moves through has folds you cannot perceive.

On a neighboring platform, maybe twenty meters away, there is a structure. Calling it a building would be generous. It's a cairn of stacked stones, volcanic rock similar to the platform but rougher, assembled into a conical formation about human-height. Simple. Intentional. The stones are placed, not naturally arranged. But there's no way to reach it — the water between here and there shifts too rapidly, and you've watched a column of water pass directly through the structure without disturbing a single stone, which raises questions about which of you occupies the more solid reality.

On your own platform, embedded in the glass near one edge, is a metal ring. Just a simple hoop of what looks like copper or bronze, eight centimeters in diameter, half-sunk into the glass as if the glass cooled around it. It's warm to the touch, warmer than the platform. There are no tool marks, no signs of manufacture, but it's too regular to be natural. An anchor point? A mark of territory? Evidence that someone survived here long enough to make a marker?

The dynamics of this place are exhausting to witness. Nothing stays still. The water-columns pulse with flow, accelerate, decelerate, branch into smaller streams or consolidate into massive currents. Where water touches platform edges, it doesn't splash — it *decides* whether to break apart or maintain coherence, and the decision seems arbitrary. You've seen water run up the side of your platform like it's decided the glass is horizontal, pool in a depression that appears concave from one angle and convex from another, then drain away into a direction that isn't up or down or any of the cardinal axes.

The platforms themselves shift. Not dramatically — there's no sensation of motion — but over the course of minutes or hours, you realize the platform to your left is now ahead of you, or the one you thought was distant has drifted close enough to see details of its surface. They orbit each other, perhaps, or follow flows in some medium you can't perceive. Occasionally, two platforms draw close enough that their edges nearly touch, and in those moments, the water-forms between them become frantic, churning and reforming at accelerating speeds until the platforms drift apart again.

Time feels uncertain. The unchanging murk, the constant noise, the lack of any diurnal cycle — it's easy to lose track of whether you've been here for hours or days. Your body insists you're tired, but the adrenaline of maintaining balance on shifting perspectives keeps you alert. You try to count the platforms in view, to use them as reference points, but the number keeps changing. Seven. Nine. Six. Eleven. Either they phase in and out of visibility, or your ability to perceive them isn't stable.

The temperature fluctuates in ways unrelated to proximity to the water. Sometimes a platform feels cooler, and you notice your breath misting slightly, and then minutes later it's warmer again, the heat radiating up through the glass with renewed intensity. The water-columns nearest you steam when the air is cooler, filling the space between platforms with coiling vapor that catches the dim light and makes everything even more difficult to parse.

Reality breakdown manifests in small ways, consistently. A drop of water falls from the water-ceiling, but instead of splashing when it hits your platform, it rebounds elastically, bouncing several times before settling into a perfect sphere that rolls to the edge and drops upward, returning to the source. You watch a stream of water flow in a closed loop, a vertical circle with no source or destination, defying thermodynamics by maintaining constant volume and velocity indefinitely. Two columns of water cross each other, and in the space where they intersect, there's a moment of *nothing* — not air, not water, not vacuum, but an absence that makes your eyes slide away from trying to focus on it.

The sound never lessens. Never. It's layered and textured and absolutely relentless: the deep roar of massive water displacement, the high crystalline shattering of water-forms breaking apart, the wet slap of water against glass, the grinding rumble from below, the occasional sharp crack of volcanic glass fracturing under stress. And underneath it all, barely perceptible, something that might be voices — might be the water itself speaking in frequencies that sometimes align with language and sometimes don't. You can't make out words, but there's an almost-intentionality to certain sound-patterns, as if the water is trying to communicate through pure noise.

You learn to read the water's behavior, slightly. A certain quality of vibration in the platform usually precedes a nearby water-form collapsing. When the subsonic rumble intensifies, new glass platforms often emerge — you've seen one rise slowly from below, black and steaming, water streaming off its surface in defiance of gravity. When the sound takes on a particularly high-pitched harmonic, the water-ceiling bulges and releases those upward-falling drops in greater numbers.

The ecology here is minimal but persistent. The pale plants grow. That's remarkable enough in itself — they're synthesizing something from somewhere, finding nutrients in volcanic glass and moisture from air saturated with anomalous water. You spot what might be a fungus: dark crusts in sheltered cracks, though in this light it's hard to tell. On one of the distant platforms, there's a splash of orange-brown that might be lichen, or might be mineral staining, or might be something else entirely.

The fauna, when glimpsed, seems adapted to impossibility. That many-legged thing moves in stuttering bursts, appearing in one location, then another several meters away without seeming to cross the intervening distance. Is it fast, or is space folding? In the water, you occasionally see translucent forms, barely more solid than the water itself, drifting or pulsing or doing something that might be swimming if swimming worked on principles other than propulsion. They might be alive. They might be water that's become temporarily organized into life-adjacent patterns. The distinction seems questionable here.

Once, you see something larger on a distant platform. Bipedal, possibly. Human-shaped, possibly. It's there in silhouette against the marginally brighter water behind it, and then it steps into a column of water and vanishes — absorbed, transported, disassembled, or perhaps just moved to another platform by means you don't understand. You shout, but your voice is instantly swallowed by the omnipresent roar. No response. No indication you were heard or that there was anything there to hear you.

The rules here are in flux. Not random — there are patterns, consistencies — but mutable. Water mostly falls up. Platforms mostly stay solid. Sound is always deafening. But the "mostly" matters. You've learned to watch for moments when the rules soften, when reality becomes more negotiable. The air shimmers slightly in those moments, like heat haze, and the sound takes on a wavering quality. During one of these flickers, you saw water fall down instead of up, just for a few seconds, before the standard configuration reasserted itself.

The platforms are new, but the water-ceiling feels ancient, eternal, like it's been here since before "here" was fully defined. The volcanic glass is sharp, un-weathered, the rough texture of fresh solidification still apparent. There are no rounded edges, no mineral deposits, no patina of age. But the water above has weight, presence, the sense of vast time compressed into a single moment that keeps recurring. You get the impression that this place is forming around the water, not the other way around — that the water was here first, in some mode of being that preceded space and matter, and reality is slowly crystallizing around it, trying to make sense of it, failing, trying again.

You find yourself thinking of this place as a throat because everything here is about passage, about movement through a constriction. The water is going somewhere, coming from somewhere, but the geometry that should make those concepts meaningful is broken. It's all middle, all passage, no beginning or end. The water flows through, the platforms drift through, you exist here in the middle of a process you're not equipped to understand.

The warm humidity grows oppressive after a while. You're perpetually damp, your clothes clinging, your hair lank, every surface slick with condensation. The moisture carries that mineral taste into your mouth, coats your sinuses, makes every breath feel thick and substantial. You're not drowning, but you're not entirely breathing air either — something in between, something that hasn't decided what it is yet.

Navigation is conceptual at best. You can step to the edge of your platform, can look across the churning water-forms to other platforms, but getting there is another matter. Once, in a moment of either courage or poor judgment, you extended a hand into a vertical sheet of water. It was warm, almost body-temperature, and it didn't feel wet so much as *present*, like the water was acknowledging your hand's existence without necessarily interacting with it normally. You pulled back and your hand was dry. Completely dry. The water had passed through it, or around it, or had decided your hand occupied a different category of space than the water did.

The cairn on the other platform haunts you. It's the only clear evidence of intentionality besides the metal ring embedded in your own platform. Someone was here. Someone survived here long enough to stack stones. Unless the stones stacked themselves, which in this place seems possible. But the formation is too deliberate, too human in its construction. A marker, a grave, a message, a map — you can't read it from here, can only see that it exists, that effort was expended to make it.

More time passes. The sound continues. The water churns and reforms and flows in directions that aren't directions. Your legs ache from maintaining balance on a platform that only mostly remembers it's horizontal. The warm air presses in. One of the pale plants near your feet has grown perceptibly since you first noticed it, or perhaps it was always that tall and you're losing the ability to hold consistent perceptions. You're not hungry, which worries you more than hunger would. Time is happening, but your body's responses to it are becoming unreliable.

A column of water nearby suddenly accelerates, shooting upward with enough force that the sound momentarily rises above the background roar — a shriek of pressurized flow. Where it meets the water-ceiling, there's an explosion of force, and for just a moment, you see through: beyond the ceiling is more water, more darkness, and something moving that's far too large, a shape that dwarfs the platforms, dwarfs you, dwarfs the entire pocket of broken space you're trapped in. Then the ceiling seals again, opacity returning, and you're left with the afterimage and a new understanding that you are very small, that this throat you're in is a minor passage in a much vaster system.

The volcanic glass under your feet shudders. New cracks appear, radiating from some central point of stress. The platform is still forming, or reforming, or unforming. You step away from the spreading fractures, toward the center, toward stability that might be temporary. The sound of glass under tension adds a new high-pitched note to the acoustic assault, a crystalline creaking that your teeth respond to sympathetically.

Something pale flickers at the edge of your vision. You turn, but there's nothing there except one of the sparse plants, swaying in a pressure wave. But you could have sworn — for just a moment — that it looked back at you. That the plant bent toward you with something like curiosity or recognition. You stare at it, and it's just a plant, colorless and simple. But the feeling lingers: you are being observed, assessed, perhaps incorporated into whatever ecology is assembling itself in this throat of screaming water.

The light dims further, or perhaps you're just exhausted. The platforms are shadows now, the water-forms barely visible as darker movements against darkness. But the sound continues, absolutely continues, no lessening, no respite, the water announcing its presence and passage with relentless acoustic force. You sit, carefully, on the warm glass, and watch the shapes in the murk, and listen to the roar, and wait for whatever happens next in a place where next is still being invented.
