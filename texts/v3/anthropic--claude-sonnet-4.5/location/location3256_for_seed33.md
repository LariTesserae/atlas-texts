# The Ossuary Towers

The city stands on peaks that should not exist—impossible needles of stone rising three, four, sometimes five thousand meters into thin air, each spire so narrow at its summit that a single structure claims the entire mountaintop. The peaks emerge from a basin of compressed salt and mineral powder, a dead lakebed that stretches forty kilometers in every direction, its surface broken only by the forest of vertical stone that erupts from it in careful, almost geometric distribution. Each tower-mountain is precisely spaced from its neighbors, as though planted.

The structures themselves are built from what was once bone—ossified, mineralized, transformed over epochs into something between calcium and ceramic and stone. Walls rise in segments that still suggest ribcages, load-bearing columns that were once femurs of creatures at scales that strain belief. The buildings grow around and through the peaks, inseparable from them, as if the mountains themselves were skeletons and the city is merely the recognition of that fact. In some places, you cannot tell where worked bone ends and mountain stone begins. They have the same color—a pale grey that shifts to cream in certain lights, striated with bands of darker material like marrow-veins.

The air is cool and thin. At these elevations, breathing comes carefully. There is always a slight wind, the kind that moves around obstacles rather than over them, creating complex currents between the tower-peaks. It carries almost no moisture. Your lips crack. Your throat dries. The wind makes sounds in the towers—low, resonant notes where it passes through hollow chambers, higher whistles where structures have gaps or deliberate apertures. But these sounds are infrequent and far apart. Mostly there is the silence of altitude and aridity.

Between the peaks, there is nothing but air and distance. The towers stand separated by hundreds of meters, sometimes more than a kilometer. To move between them, the inhabitants—whoever they were, whoever they are—built bridges, but not as other places build bridges. These spans are minimal: single ribs of bone-stone, barely wider than a walking path, unrailed, curving across the void in shallow arcs that seem to calculate the precise minimum of material required. Some bridges are broken now, their far ends jutting into space, going nowhere. Others remain intact, and crossing them means stepping onto a surface forty centimeters wide with a thousand-meter drop on either side and wind that pushes irregularly from unexpected directions.

The density of construction varies. Some peaks hold only a single chamber at their summit—a cell, a platform, a small dome of worked bone with openings that face specific directions. Others are more developed: structures rising in tiers, platforms cantilevered out into space, supported by ribs and buttresses that follow the logic of skeletal load-bearing. The largest peaks hold what might be called neighborhoods, though no one appears to live in them now. Chambers stack vertically, connected by internal passages that wind through the stone-bone matrix of the mountain itself. Windows are irregular openings, often asymmetrical, positioned where the material allowed rather than where aesthetics might demand.

But here is where the nature of things begins to peel back, where the established form of reality shows its thinner patches:

The bridges move. Not constantly. Not even predictably. But you can stand on a tower summit and watch a bridge that was connected to your peak an hour ago now arcing toward a different destination, its far end having pivoted or grown or reformed itself to span a different gap. The bone-stone is not static at these scales. It continues some process that resembles growth, or healing, or erosion—but operates according to rules that do not apply in other places. A structure you mapped yesterday may have different proportions today. A window might close, its edges fusing like a fracture healing. A new opening might appear in a wall that was solid, the bone-stone drawing back from a space that has decided to exist.

The light comes from above, but filtered through something. The sky is not fully sky. At this elevation, you should see clearly into the upper atmosphere, but instead there is a presence—a thickness to the air at altitude that creates a perpetual dimness. The sun is visible as a bright region, a glow rather than a disk, diffused through layers of something that might be dust, might be distance, might be the accumulated exhalation of all the dry centuries settling into an atmospheric stratum. The light that reaches the towers is soft, shadowless in some conditions, creating strange long shadows in others as the angle shifts. It is neither day nor night in the way other places measure these things. It is a continuous dim condition that slowly cycles brighter and darker over periods that might be days or might be longer.

Near the base of some towers, where they root into the salt-flat basin, there are accumulations. Not vegetation—nothing so generous as plant life—but something that might be its precursor or its fossil. Mineral formations that branch in ways that suggest purpose: iron-oxide dendrites spreading across rock faces in patterns that echo vascular systems, manganese blooms that darken stone in fern-like fractals. These are chemical processes, but they move. Slowly. A dark stain on a tower's base that resembles a sprawling shrub will be detectably larger next season, its branches extending millimeter by millimeter, following gradients of moisture or mineral content through the stone.

And there are the crawlers—rare fauna existing at the edge of viability. Things that might be insects or might be mechanisms grown biological. They appear irregularly: a hand-sized creature crossing a bridge, its body segmented and hard like fired ceramic, moving on too many legs arranged in two parallel rows. They produce no sound. You see them most often in the periphery of vision, and when you turn to look directly, they are frequently already gone—moved into a crack, stepped through some gap in the bone-stone architecture that appears too narrow for them. They leave no traces. No droppings, no shed material, no nests or colonies. They are simply present sometimes, moving with deliberate purpose from one location to another, and then absent.

The weirdness intensifies in the interiors. Inside the tower structures, the rules loosen further. Chambers do not maintain consistent geometry. A room you remember as roughly circular might, upon returning, have acquired corners. The bone-walls develop textures that move under close observation—surfaces that seem to breathe in very slow rhythms, expanding and contracting by millimeters over the course of minutes. Some chambers have what might be called furniture: formations of bone grown into shelf-like protrusions, bench shapes, platforms at useful heights. But they are not carved or placed. They are emergent, as though the space itself understood occupation and responded by generating what might be needed.

Occasionally, you encounter apertures in floors or ceilings that should not physically connect where they appear to lead. A hole in a floor opening to a view that shows not the chamber below, but a different tower entirely, one visible through the opening as if the distance between has folded away. These passages are navigable but disorienting. Step through and you experience a moment of discontinuity—a brief absence of sensation—and then you are elsewhere in the tower-city, having traversed space in a manner that violates the careful mapping you've been attempting.

Time moves strangely here, though not in dramatic ways. It is more that duration becomes unreliable. You will cross a bridge that you estimate should take five minutes and arrive at the far tower feeling certain that either thirty seconds or half an hour has passed, but not able to reconcile which is accurate. The sun-glow in the sky does not provide useful reference—its position seems independent of the time you experience. This creates a persistent, low-level uncertainty about causation and sequence. Did you enter this chamber before or after you crossed that bridge? The memory feels ambiguous.

The salt basin below—the dead lakebed from which the towers rise—is itself active in ways that are not immediately apparent. The surface appears static: a flat expanse of compressed minerals, white-grey and cracked into irregular polygons like dried mud, but at scales of hundreds of meters. Stand at a tower's base, though, and you begin to notice the slow processes. The salt is crystallizing, constantly forming new structures at microscopic scales that accumulate into visible change over weeks. Formations push up from below: small pressure ridges, salt-crystal blooms, areas where the surface has buckled slightly. The basin is not dead but dying, still conducting the final movements of its chemistry, evaporating water it no longer has, forming crystals from minerals that are slowly running out.

Sometimes the wind picks up sufficient strength to move material from the basin. Salt dust rises in plumes, creating temporary walls of white that drift between the towers. These dust-clouds are thick enough to obscure vision, thin enough that you can breathe through them, though they coat your lungs with mineral residue. When the wind subsides, you find everything filmed with white powder—the bone-stone structures, the minimal bridges, your skin, your belongings. The dust settles into every crevice and opening, gradually filling in details, smoothing edges. This is the erosion process here: not wearing away by water, but the slow burial under airborne salt, and the buildings' strange compensatory growth that maintains openings, keeps passages clear, as if engaged in an eternal negotiation with the basin's attempt to reclaim everything.

At the highest towers, the ones that push deepest into the strange upper atmosphere, the bone-stone takes on different properties. It becomes more translucent, allowing light to pass through the walls in diffused patterns. These summit chambers glow from within during the brighter periods, their walls like alabaster, revealing internal structures—the grain of bone, the channels where marrow once flowed, now empty or filled with crystallized minerals. In these spaces, sound behaves oddly. Whispers carry too far. Footsteps produce no echo. The air itself seems to have texture, a slight resistance, as though something is suspended in it just beyond the threshold of visibility.

There are objects left in the towers—not many, scattered through the structures as though placed intentionally at wide intervals. They are made of the same bone-stone material but worked differently: smooth, polished, shaped into forms that suggest function without revealing it. A sphere the size of a human head, perfectly round, sitting in a chamber on a ledge of stone. A vertical rod as tall as a person, its surface inscribed with fine lines in patterns that might be text or decoration or technical specifications in some material language. Hollow tubes open at both ends, their interiors mirror-smooth. These objects do not decay. They maintain their finishes against all the processes that age everything else here. You can touch them, move them, but they reveal nothing of their purpose.

The infrastructure of the city, such as it is, operates on principles that suggest organization without evident residents. Water does not exist here in liquid form—the air is far too dry, the temperatures at night dropping below freezing, but there is no humidity to condense, no moisture to freeze. Yet there are channels carved into some tower walls, shallow grooves that wind down from upper chambers to lower ones, sized as if to carry water, perfectly dry, leading nowhere. Perhaps they did carry water once, in some different era. Perhaps they carry something else now that is not visible. The distinction becomes difficult to maintain.

At irregular intervals—days or weeks apart, impossible to predict—the towers resonate. It begins as a low vibration, felt more than heard, coming up through the stone. Then sound emerges: deep tones from the hollow chambers, higher notes from the bridges, complex harmonics as the entire city becomes an instrument played by some ambient force—wind, thermal expansion, seismic activity from below, or processes unique to this place. The resonance builds over the course of minutes, not loud but pervasive, felt in your chest and bones. Then it subsides, gradually, the tones dropping back below audibility, the vibration fading. The towers return to silence. These events feel significant, as though information is being transmitted, but in a frequency or language inaccessible to human interpretation.

The creatures—the ceramic-bodied crawlers—appear more frequently after resonance events. For a period following the vibrations, you will see more of them: crossing bridges, moving through chambers, ascending the tower walls on their many legs. They seem energized or called by the resonance. Then, over subsequent days, their numbers diminish again to the baseline rarity. Where they go is unclear. There are no burrows, no hives. They simply become present and then cease to be present, as though they exist in a state that is only intermittently visible.

Navigation through the tower-city requires adaptation to its peculiarities. Maps become outdated too quickly to be reliable. Memory proves more useful, but even memory requires constant revision as the architecture shifts. You learn instead to recognize patterns: certain types of bone formations tend to lead upward, others toward bridges, others to dead-ends. You read the mineral staining on walls to gauge which areas are more stable, which are actively changing. You learn to test bridges before crossing, because some that appear solid are not fully connected at both ends—they are in transition, still deciding whether they will span that particular gap or reach toward a different tower.

The temperature remains cool even during the brightest periods—perhaps fifteen degrees, cool enough to want layers but not cold enough for real danger. At night, when the dim light becomes dimmer still, temperatures drop close to freezing. The thin air accelerates heat loss. The bone-stone structures provide some shelter but were not designed for warmth. There are no hearths, no chimneys, no evidence that fire was ever used here. The inhabitants, whoever they were, either tolerated the cold or did not experience temperature the way human bodies do.

In the deepest chambers of the largest towers, at the very core where the building merges fully with the mountain, you find the oldest bone. These are not the bones of the tower itself—not structural elements—but bones embedded in the stone as fossils are embedded, preserved and partially exposed where erosion has revealed them. Ribs as thick as your torso. Vertebrae the size of barrels. A jaw with teeth the length of forearms. These are the bones of the creators of this place—not the builders, but the bodies from which the building material was harvested, or grew, or emerged. The towers are built from the bones of things large enough to provide bones this size. The math of it suggests creatures that were mountains themselves, and the city is built from their remains, or they became mountains and the city grew from them, or the distinction is meaningless.

This is where the reality breakdown becomes most evident: standing in these deep chambers, you can see a rib disappearing into solid stone on one side and emerging from stone on the other, but in between, where the stone should be, there is only the space inside the rib—a cavity that should be enclosed but opens into a chamber where you're standing. The bone is both embedded in the stone and hollowed out for occupation. It exists in multiple states simultaneously. The architecture does not resolve into consistency no matter how long you examine it.

The same is true for the bridges. On a clear day—or what passes for clear in the perpetual dimness—you can stand on one tower and count the bridges you see connecting other towers in the distance. You will count a certain number: twenty-three, say. Later, from a different tower, you count the bridges again between the same set of towers. The number is different: twenty-seven. The bridges are not individually identifiable from a distance—they are too minimal, too similar—so you cannot determine which are new and which were uncounted. But the quantity does not remain stable. The city has a number of bridges, but the number is not fixed.

At the far edges of the city, where the tower-peaks become more widely spaced, there are towers that seem to be... forming. Not being built—there are no workers, no construction processes visible—but emerging. A peak that a season ago was bare stone now has small formations at its summit that might be the beginning of architecture. Bone-stone material accumulating, or growing, or appearing according to processes that happen slowly enough to miss in moment-to-moment observation but fast enough to be noticeable across weeks. This suggests the city is still expanding, or that it expands and contracts in cycles too long to observe fully. New towers join the city. Old towers, perhaps, are reclaimed by the salt basin, though you have not yet seen this happen.

The sound environment, when sounds occur, is distinct. The ceramic crawlers move silently. Your own footsteps on bone-stone produce a hard tap-tap, but the sound does not carry—it stops close to its source, absorbed by the thin air and the acoustic properties of the material. The wind's passage through the towers creates the most consistent sound: low moans, high whistles, complex multi-toned harmonics when conditions align. Occasionally there is the crack of stone—thermal contraction, structural adjustment, the towers settling or growing or rearranging themselves. These cracks are sharp, loud, startling in the silence, and they come without warning. A tower will be quiet for days, then emit a single loud crack as if something inside has broken or fused, then return to silence.

During the darkest periods, when the diffused sun-glow diminishes to almost nothing, the translucent bone-stone in the highest chambers begins to emit its own faint luminescence. Not bright—just enough to be noticeable, a pale blue-white glow like phosphorescence or foxfire. This light seems to come from within the material itself, not from any external source. It is strongest in the thinnest sections of bone-stone, where the walls are most translucent. The glow creates a navigation marker: you can see the highest towers from a distance during dark periods, their summits faintly visible against the black sky. But the light is too dim to illuminate interiors usefully. It only marks presence.

The salt basin below collects shadows during the brightest periods—the tower-peaks casting long, thin shadows across the white surface, moving slowly as the diffused sun progresses across the sky. These shadows are the darkest thing in the landscape. The salt itself is reflective, bouncing the dim light in all directions, creating a general soft illumination from below. This means that even in the deepest chambers of the towers, there is never total darkness—there is always some light filtering up from the basin, reflected and re-reflected, a dim ambient glow that allows basic vision but no detail.

The city has a smell, though it is subtle: a mineral dryness, alkaline, slightly sharp. The smell of salt and stone and the absence of biological decay. There is no rot here because there is nothing to rot—no wood, no plant matter, no moisture to enable decomposition. The ceramic crawlers do not appear to defecate or die, or if they do, their bodies mineralize immediately into indistinguishability from the stone around them. The air smells clean in the way of deserts, but with an additional note—a slight bitterness—that might be the bone-stone itself slowly releasing some component into the atmosphere, or might be a psychological response to the weirdness of the place manifesting as sensory input.

Weather here is minimal but present. Beyond the wind, which is nearly constant at some level, there are dust events: larger storms where the salt basin releases material in quantity, creating white-out conditions that last hours. During these events, the towers vanish from sight even when close. Visibility drops to meters. The dust infiltrates everything—lungs, eyes, the interior of sealed objects, somehow passing through barriers that should stop it. After a dust event, the entire city is coated in a fresh layer of white, softening edges, filling in cracks, and the slow processes of crystallization and growth begin again, the towers gradually emerging from the burial, maintaining themselves against the accumulation.

There are also thermal events: rapid temperature shifts when cold air from above descends suddenly, mixing with the warmer air at the basin level. These create wind shears between the towers—strong, directional gusts that can make bridge crossing dangerous or impossible. The shifts are accompanied by the crackling of stone as materials contract or expand rapidly. During extreme thermal events, the bone-stone produces sounds like groaning, like protest, like an old structure under stress, except these structures are not old in the sense of failing—they are old in the sense of long-established, and the sounds are part of their ongoing negotiation with the environment.

The city has no obvious center, no focal point or main plaza or governing structure distinguishable from the others. It is radially symmetric, or appears to be from any vantage point, though mapping suggests otherwise. Every tower seems to be at the edge of things, no matter where you are. This may be an optical illusion created by the uniform spacing of peaks and the limits of visibility in the dim light. Or it may be another property of the place—that center and periphery are not fixed concepts here, that the city's geography is relative to the observer's position in ways that violate conventional spatial relationships.

In the rooms and chambers, you find markings occasionally: not writing, exactly, but intentional marks. Shallow grooves carved into bone-stone in parallel lines, varying in number from three to eight. Circular depressions pressed into walls in patterns—five arranged in a pentagon, seven in a heptagon. These marks appear to be deliberate communication or record-keeping, but their meaning is not accessible. They are concentrated in certain chambers, absent in others, suggesting those chambers had specific functions—perhaps administrative, perhaps ritual, perhaps something without equivalent in human activity.

The towers' interiors are not decorated. There is no art, no ornamentation, no evidence of aesthetic addition beyond the functional. But the functional itself has a beauty: the curve of a bone-buttress taking load and transferring it through the structure, the precise minimum thickness of a wall, the elegant span of a bridge calculating the shortest path between peaks. The city is beautiful the way a bird's skeleton is beautiful—everything necessary, nothing excess, form arising directly from function and constraint.

You begin to understand, after time here, that the city is not abandoned. It is not ruined or decaying into past grandeur. It is operating as it was designed to operate, but designed for inhabitants whose needs and presence are different from what you expect. The city serves something, maintains itself for something, continues its processes for something—but that something is not evident. The ceramic crawlers, perhaps, though they seem too small and too rare to require infrastructure of this scale. Or something else that is not visible, that occupies the spaces in ways you cannot detect, that uses the bridges when you are not watching, that inhabits the chambers between your visits.

The longer you remain, the more the city feels inhabited, despite the absence of inhabitants. You develop a sense of presence just out of sight, of activity that has just ceased when you enter a chamber, of eyes that observe from the shadows but do not reveal themselves. This is not threatening—it does not feel like danger or hostility. It feels like cohabitation with something that tolerates your presence but does not interact, that goes about its business parallel to yours, occupying the same space but in different layers of reality.

At the boundaries of the city, where the tower-peaks finally thin out and give way to open salt basin, you can see the full extent: perhaps two hundred towers in total, though the count is never quite the same twice. They extend to the horizon in all directions, becoming smaller with distance until the farthest are just thin vertical lines against the dim sky. Beyond them, there is only the salt flat continuing, dead and white and empty, extending far beyond sight. The city is an island of structure in an ocean of mineral emptiness, and the emptiness is winning in the sense that it surrounds and infiltrates, but the city is also winning in the sense that it persists, grows, maintains itself against the burial.

The towers continue. The bridges continue to shift. The bone-stone continues its slow growth and erosion and rearrangement. The ceramic crawlers appear and disappear. The resonance events occur and subside. The salt basin crystallizes and sublimates and redistributes itself. The dim light cycles brighter and darker. The temperature fluctuates in its narrow cool range. And the city continues, an active system balanced in its strangeness, old but not dying, empty but not abandoned, real but operating on rules that are not quite the rules of other places, a landscape that has settled into its own strange steady state at the edge of where reality maintains consistency.
