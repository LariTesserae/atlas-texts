# The Condensery Plains

This is a place where the world wore itself down to nothing, and then humans rebuilt it as something functional and wrong.

The land extends flat in all directions—not naturally flat, but flat in the way of something that has forgotten its original shape. What were once mountains or valleys have eroded into the gentlest of swells, barely perceptible curves that take a half-hour to walk over. The substrate, where you can glimpse it through the construction, is pale limestone rubbed to soap-smoothness, grooved in places by vanished rivers, pocked with solution hollows that have gone soft at the edges. This is stone that has been dissolving for so long it has reached an equilibrium with its own dissolution.

But almost nowhere can you see that stone directly anymore.

The Condensery Plains are built—entirely built—from a material that resembles cast concrete but isn't quite. It's called slakecrete locally, poured in vast sheets and allowed to cure in the open air, where it takes on a mottled grey-green patina almost immediately. Everything here is slakecrete: the ground itself is mostly paved over with it, formed into shallow channels and ridges that direct water flow; the structures that punctuate the flatness are slakecrete; the posts and pillars that support the condensation nets are slakecrete; even the low walls that serve no apparent purpose, running for hundreds of meters before ending abruptly, are poured from this same omnipresent material.

The construction follows no single plan. It accreted over what must have been centuries, each generation adding to what was there, patching and extending and building over. Some areas are geometric, laid out in grids with machine precision. Others meander, organic and confused. Structures lean against structures. Channels intersect at odd angles, forcing water to make sharp decisions. There are foundations with nothing built on them, and buildings that seem to have no foundation at all, just sitting on the old stone like offerings.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It's not a marsh—there are no pools you could step in, no standing water except in the collection basins. But the air is so heavy with moisture that it condenses on every surface. The slakecrete sweats constantly, its surface dark with damp, and if you watch a vertical wall long enough you'll see droplets form, swell, and begin their slow descent. The condensation nets—vast meshes of wire or synthetic fiber stretched between posts, some covering acres—are the heart of the place's economy. They catch moisture from the air, and water streams down their surfaces into the channels, which feed into the collection system, which feeds into the processing plants.

The processing plants make the sound.

It's not quite deafening in the way of a single overwhelming noise—rather, it's the accumulated roar of a thousand mechanical processes happening simultaneously across the landscape. The thrum of pumps, which never stop, pushing water through the system. The clank and hammer of piston-driven gates opening and closing in the channels, regulating flow. The whine of dynamos in the generator stations, powered by water pressure, producing the electricity that runs through thick cables slung between ceramic insulators on wooden poles—real wood, imported from somewhere else, already beginning to rot in the dampness. The hiss of steam from somewhere, though what's being heated and why isn't immediately apparent. The grinding of mills that process something—maybe the algae that grows in the collecting basins, scraping it into paste for export. The metallic screech of maintenance cranes moving on their tracks, though you rarely see anyone operating them.

And underneath it all, a low vibration you feel more than hear, coming up through the slakecrete into your bones: the rumble of the deep pumps, drawing ancient groundwater from the limestone substrate and mixing it with the condensation, creating something that's neither entirely fresh water nor entirely mineral water, something that tastes of stone and time and industrial lubricant.

The sound bounces strangely in the flatness. It doesn't echo—there's nothing to echo off—but it layering, each source of noise traveling across the open space and interfering with the others, creating beats and harmonics. Sometimes it sounds like language. Sometimes sections of the plains fall into a momentary synchronization, all the machinery pulsing together for thirty seconds or a minute before drifting apart again into chaos.

The light is low, always. Either it's the grey of pre-dawn, or the grey of dusk, or the grey of heavy overcast. The sun exists but is rarely visible, usually just a bright patch in the cloud layer that tracks across the sky without ever breaking through. The darkness isn't complete—you can see, you can navigate—but colors are muted, reduced to variations on grey and green and the rust-brown of oxidizing metal. The condensation nets shimmer faintly when light hits them at the right angle, becoming visible as planes of gathered droplets, then vanishing again into the general greyness.

At night, there are lights, but not many. Work lights on the processing stations, harsh and industrial, throwing sharp shadows that make the machinery look like ruins even though it's functioning. Signal lights on the taller structures, red and steady, warning of obstacles that nothing is flying past. Occasionally, the blue-white flicker of arc welding from a repair crew working somewhere in the distance, the light reflecting off the wet surfaces and creating a brief constellation of reflections before it stops.

The vegetation, such as it is, grows in the cracks.

Where channels have broken or pavement has settled unevenly, creating gaps in the slakecrete, things grow. They're not quite mosses, not quite lichens—flat, dark green, almost black, spreading in circular patches that never seem to get larger than a dinner plate before something stops them. Maybe the maintenance crews scrape them away. Maybe they reach the limit of what nutrients they can extract from the bare minimum of soil that collects in the cracks. They're slightly luminous in the darkness, giving off a phosphorescence so faint you only notice it when you're not looking directly at it.

In the collecting basins—rectangular pools of water, some the size of a room, others covering an acre or more—algae blooms in dense mats. It's harvested regularly, scraped off by mechanical rakes that move on timers, but it always comes back, thick and ropy, ranging in color from yellow-green to a deep, troubling purple depending on what minerals are concentrated in that particular basin. The algae is what gets processed in the mills. It might be food. It might be industrial feedstock. The smell coming from the mills suggests it's something in between.

There are a few other plants, scattered so widely that finding one feels like an event. Stunted bushes, leafless most of the time, with bark that's adapted to shed water constantly so it doesn't rot. They grow near the older structures, where there's more accumulated soil, and they're always leaning, bent by prevailing winds that you can't feel at ground level. Their roots break through slakecrete over decades, prying it apart, and you can track the age of an area by how broken its pavement is around these bushes.

Nothing else grows. There are no trees. No grass. No flowers. The landscape has been paved over too thoroughly, and what plants exist are survivors clinging to the margins.

There is almost no animal life.

No birds—nothing to nest in, perhaps, or nothing to eat. No insects, though that seems wrong for a wet place until you remember there's no soil, no rot, nothing organic except the harvested algae and the occasional plant in a crack. No rodents or reptiles. The ecological chains that would support them don't exist here.

What you see instead, very rarely, are things the locals call wets. They might be animals. They might be something else. They appear in the channels, in the water flowing toward the collection basins, and they're translucent, jellyfish-like, ranging in size from a hand's breadth to nearly a meter across. They pulse slowly as they move with the current, and they seem to be feeding on something—microscopic particles in the water, maybe, or dissolved minerals. When they're caught in the intake screens of the processing plants, they're scraped off and dumped back into the outflow channels. Whether they breed or simply exist, whether there's a population or each one is solitary, no one seems to know. They might have been here before the construction, living in the groundwater, now brought to the surface by the pumping. They might be something that evolved in the processing system itself, adapted to industrial conditions. They're slightly toxic if you touch them—not dangerously so, but your skin will itch and redden for days.

And there are the sound-things.

This is where the strangeness concentrates.

In certain areas of the plains, particularly near the older structures or in sections where machinery has been running for so long its rhythms have worn grooves into local reality, the sound becomes visible. Not metaphorically—literally visible. You'll see it as a thickening in the air, a region where the atmosphere seems to vibrate at a frequency that makes it appear solid, like heat shimmer but more substantial. These patches drift, following sound waves, clustering around the loudest machinery before breaking apart and dissipating.

If you're caught in one, it's disorienting. The sound doesn't get louder, but it becomes tactile, pressing against your skin and clothes like a wind that's not actually moving air. Your vision distorts slightly, objects at a distance stretching or compressing in rhythm with the mechanical pulses. It passes in seconds or minutes, leaving you slightly nauseated and aware that something happened that shouldn't be possible under normal physics.

The people who work here—and there must be people, given the evidence of maintenance and the occasional light that suggests occupation—don't seem bothered by the sound-things. Perhaps they've adapted. Perhaps they've been here so long they don't distinguish between normal and strange anymore.

You rarely see the people directly.

They're there, in the buildings that dot the plains—some just shelters, some proper industrial facilities with windows that glow dully at all hours. But they move inside, or through underground passages that connect structures, or they work at night shifts that don't align with when you're watching. You find evidence of them: tools left out but carefully placed, doors left slightly ajar, boot prints in the thin layer of algae that coats everything. Occasionally, very rarely, you'll glimpse a figure in the distance moving between buildings, shapeless in rain gear, not hurrying but not lingering either, disappearing into a doorway before you can register much detail.

There are schedules here, routines, systems of living, but they're opaque to outsiders. Lights turn on and off in patterns. Shift whistles blow from the processing plants, mechanical and precise, though you never see workers emerging or arriving. Steam vents open and close on timers. The cranes move on their tracks, their hooks empty, traveling from one end of their range to the other as if in drill.

This is a working landscape, but the work has become so mechanized, so systematized, that human presence has faded to almost nothing even though human purpose saturates every meter.

The weather moves through in cycles.

There's the condensation phase, when the air is at its heaviest, fog forming in low patches that pool in the slight depressions of the land, moisture streaming down every surface. This is when the collection system works hardest, when you hear the pumps increase their tempo slightly, when the channels run fullest.

Then the clearing phase, when the cloud layer thins just enough that you get diffuse sunlight, never direct, but enough that the fog burns off and the air becomes merely damp instead of saturated. The machinery seems to relax during this phase, running at maintenance levels, and the sound drops to something almost tolerable.

Then the misting phase, not quite rain but more than fog, when water falls in droplets too fine to see individually but heavy enough that you're soaked through in minutes. The condensation nets become invisible during misting, completely obscured by the water moving through them, and the channels sometimes overflow, spreading thin sheets of water across the slakecrete before drains catch up with the input.

These cycles repeat with some regularity—maybe three or four times a day—but they're not precise. Sometimes the condensation phase lingers for hours. Sometimes the clearing phase barely arrives before misting begins. The machinery adapts, gates opening and closing in the channels to handle whatever water is coming, storage tanks filling and emptying in response to conditions.

There's a temperature cycle too, but it's muted. Everything is cool, but there are degrees of cool. The coolest times are during the clearing phases, when what little heat accumulated during condensation can radiate away. The warmest times—relatively speaking—are deep in the condensation phase, when all that moisture in the air holds temperature steady. The difference is perhaps ten degrees, not enough to be dramatic, but enough that you learn to read it, to know from the feel of the air what part of the cycle you're in.

It never freezes, though. That's one of the constants. The water keeps moving, the machinery keeps running, the temperature stays just above the point where ice could form and lock everything down.

The buildings themselves—the structures that house whatever functions this place serves—are strange accretions of industrial architecture.

They're built from the same slakecrete as everything else, but reinforced with metal framing that's visible where the material has cracked or weathered away. Some are simple boxes, single rooms with a door and no windows. Others are complex, multi-story facilities with catwalks and external piping, additions built onto additions until the original structure is buried somewhere in the middle, unrecognizable.

Many have cooling towers, though what they're cooling is unclear. The towers are hyperbolic, elegant in their mathematics, but they're stained dark with algae and their plumes of steam add to the general dampness of the air. Some have been retrofitted with condensation nets, catching their own steam and feeding it back into the collection system, creating closed loops that seem almost too efficient, like the landscape is becoming self-sufficient in small pockets.

The windows that exist are small, set high in walls, often barred not for security but to support condensation screens—fine mesh stretched across the opening that catches moisture and drips it into small collection troughs. Light comes through them weak and diffused, and from outside you can rarely see into the rooms beyond, just vague shapes that might be equipment or furniture or something else.

Doors are industrial—metal, heavy, equipped with seals to keep moisture out though they only partially succeed. Most are kept closed, but the few you find open reveal interiors that are functional but not comfortable: concrete floors with drains, metal shelving bolted to walls, machinery whose purpose isn't immediately obvious, and always the sound, louder inside, bouncing off hard surfaces.

There are no decorations, no attempts to make these spaces livable in any conventional sense. They're accommodations for function, places where humans fit themselves around the requirements of the machinery rather than the other way around.

The oldest structures, though—the ones that seem to date back to whenever this place was first constructed—have details that suggest they were designed differently.

There are carved channels in their walls, decorative but also functional, guiding water runoff in patterns that form symbols or writing in a script that's weathered beyond recognition. There are niches that might have held statues or offerings, now empty and stained. There are thresholds made of different stone, imported from somewhere else, worn concave by generations of feet. These buildings are more ruin than functioning structure, often partially collapsed, their rooms open to the sky, but the machinery routed through them is new, suggesting they're maintained for some reason beyond utility—perhaps tradition, perhaps the difficulty of removing old foundations, perhaps something else.

In these older structures, the strangeness is stronger. The sound-things appear more frequently, and they linger, forming semi-permanent presences in corners or doorways. The wets are sometimes found inside, dried to papery husks on floors, as if they'd crawled out of the water system seeking something. The phosphorescent not-moss grows thicker here, and in the darkness, the interiors have a faint, uneasy glow.

There are spaces where channels intersect, where water from different parts of the collection system meets and mixes.

These junctions are the most active areas of the landscape. The water arrives with different speeds, different mineral contents, different temperatures, and the mixing creates turbulence, standing waves, strange optical effects where the different waters haven't fully combined and you can see boundaries between them, like oil and water but both are water.

The machinery here is more complex: gates and valves, measurement devices, sometimes glass-walled sections of channel where you can see the water flowing, thick with suspended particles, occasionally carrying a wet tumbling in the current before it's diverted into a settling basin.

These junctions are also where you sometimes hear something that isn't machinery.

It might be voices, though that's not quite right. It's a sound that suggests speech without being speech, rising from the turbulence of the mixing water, a modulation of the water noise that resembles conversation or argument or singing. It comes and goes with the flow rates, louder when the system is running hard, fading when pumps ease back. No one local acknowledges it, though they must hear it. Perhaps it's so constant they've learned not to notice, the way you stop hearing a clock ticking.

Or perhaps they do hear it, and what they hear is instruction or information, the landscape itself communicating the state of its systems in a language that takes years to learn.

The far distances, at the limits of visibility, show more of the same.

More structures, more condensation nets, more channels catching and directing water. The landscape doesn't end—it fades, its details lost to the low light and the moisture in the air before you reach any boundary. If there are edges to the Condensery Plains, they're too distant to walk to, or they're obscured by the construction that simply goes on and on, repeating its patterns with variation but no fundamental change.

Sometimes you see towers in the distance, taller than anything nearby, their tops lost in the cloud layer. They might be water towers, storage for the collected moisture. They might be something else. They're never closer the next time you look, never farther away, fixed at some middle distance that suggests they're much larger than they appear, or much farther than they seem.

The rails—networks of narrow-gauge track running between structures—suggest transportation, though you rarely see anything moving on them. The tracks are wet like everything else, rusted but maintained, and occasionally you'll find a small cart parked on a siding, loaded with equipment or empty and waiting. Hand-powered, some of them, with pump handles. Others have small motors, electric or maybe steam-powered, though they're never running when you find them.

If you follow a rail line, it will take you past different configurations of the same elements: buildings, nets, channels, pumps. The landscape has variety in its details but not in its essence. Everything serves the collection and processing of water. Everything is built from slakecrete and metal. Everything is wet, grey-green, functional, ancient in its construction even where the machinery is clearly newer.

And everywhere, always, the sound. The deafening accumulation of mechanical function, creating an environment where silence is unimaginable, where even thought has to push its way through the noise, where the rhythms of machinery become the rhythms of time itself.

There's no day or night in the usual sense. There's lighter grey and darker grey, and the machinery runs through both. The cycles are water cycles, not solar cycles. You learn to mark time by the condensation phases, by the shift whistles, by the patterns of sound that indicate which parts of the system are active.

Sleep, when it comes, happens in the presence of noise. The buildings have no soundproofing—why would they?—and you learn to sleep with the thrum of pumps and the hammer of gates and the whine of dynamos as a constant background. Or you don't sleep, exactly, but enter a state of rest that accommodates the sound, working around it rather than requiring its absence.

The people who've lived here for years move differently than newcomers. They walk with a particular rhythm that matches something in the machinery's pulse. They don't flinch at sudden noises that would startle an outsider. They can hear differences in the sound that indicate problems with equipment, changes in flow rate, the approach of a misting phase. The landscape has trained them, shaped them, and they've become part of its function as surely as the pumps and channels are.

There are maps, occasionally found in buildings or posted on walls near junctions. They show the layout of channels, the locations of processing plants, the routes of the rail lines. But they're never quite accurate, or the landscape has changed since they were drawn, or they're depicting something other than physical geography—maybe water pressure zones, or electrical grid sections, or maintenance territories. The symbols and notations on them are often unclear, abbreviations and codes that make sense to operators but not to visitors.

Navigation happens mostly by landmarks: the three-legged tower near the Old Basin; the processing plant with the collapsed cooling tower; the junction where seven channels meet in a star pattern; the ruin with the carved archway still partially standing. These markers are known, named, referred to in whatever communication happens between the workers, though the names are practical rather than poetic. "The Big Pump" for a particularly massive installation. "Whiner's Corner" for a section where the machinery makes an especially piercing noise. "The Cathedral" for a vast empty structure whose purpose has been forgotten but which still houses part of the collection system.

Time has done something strange here, or maybe time does what it always does and it's just more obvious in a place this flattened by erosion, this completely rebuilt by human function.

Everything is old—the limestone substrate ancient beyond measuring, the earliest construction centuries old, the machinery decades old at minimum. But nothing is allowed to fall into disrepair completely. Things are patched, retrofitted, kept running with repairs that become part of the structure, metal plates bolted over cracks, new channels built beside old ones that are too damaged to clean out. The landscape is a palimpsest of function, each era of maintenance leaving its mark, the newest repairs visible against the weathered substrate like scars on scars on scars.

And yet the place is alive—not biologically, but systemically. Water flows, machinery runs, processes continue. The feedback loops between collection and processing and storage create a kind of homeostasis, the landscape regulating itself, responding to input, maintaining equilibrium. The sound-things might be epiphenomena of this, strange side effects of a system that's been running so long it's generated properties that weren't designed into it.

There's a feeling, if you stay long enough, that the landscape is aware of you. Not conscious—that's too strong a word. But responsive, in the way a building knows its occupants by the wear patterns on its floors, the doors that get opened most, the rooms that stay warm. The machinery doesn't change its operation when you're near, but the sound seems to... acknowledge, somehow. The patterns shift slightly, include you in their calculations, account for your presence as another variable in the system.

The workers must feel this constantly. They must stop distinguishing between themselves and the landscape, must become extensions of its function, their movements and actions integrated into the larger pattern of maintenance and operation. The landscape needs them, in the same way it needs pumps and channels. They need it, in the sense that leaving would mean losing a context, a structure, a purpose that's been so thoroughly internalized that existing outside it would be disorienting, maybe impossible.

This is what the Condensery Plains are: a place where human purpose and geological time have met and fused into something neither natural nor artificial, where function has become so total that it's indistinguishable from environment, where the sound of machinery is the sound of existence itself.

The place doesn't invite. It doesn't reject. It simply continues, vast and flat and wet and deafening, processing water because that's what it does, housing whatever remnant of human life still tends its machinery, existing in a state so far beyond its original purpose that the purpose itself has been forgotten, replaced by the simple fact of operation.

Everything here has been worn down to its essence—the land by erosion, the construction by time, the purpose by repetition—and what remains is this: channels and moisture and sound and function, repeating in variations across the flat expanse, forever or until something breaks in a way that can't be repaired, whichever comes first.

And in the grey light, under the heavy clouds, the machinery continues its work, the water continues its cycle, and the landscape breathes its wet, mechanical breath, patient and ancient and indifferent and alive.
