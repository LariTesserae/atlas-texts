# The Hollowgarden

The city exists as a scatter of bone-white towers across a basin so broad the far rim dissolves into heat-shimmer and mist. Between these structures—some thin as lighthouse spires, others bulbous like kiln-fired urns—stretches an emptiness that feels both geometric and absolute. The distance between any two buildings is never less than a kilometer, often three or four, and in that space: almost nothing. Pale sand. Shattered pavement of a prior epoch, its fragments arranged by wind into long parallel ridges. Here and there, a single tree.

But what trees they are.

Each one grows alone in its circle of cleared ground, and each is enormous—thirty, forty meters tall—with canopies that form distinct clouds of green so dark they're nearly black. They are *laden*. Vines cascade from their branches in curtains, bromeliads cluster in the crotches of limbs, epiphytes pile upon epiphytes until the original branch beneath is only hypothetical. The trunks are invisible under sheaths of moss, lichen, climbing fig, and stranger things: bulbous air-roots that dangle down like organ pipes, leaves the size of tabletops that grow directly from the bark, fungal brackets in sunset colors that pulse with bioluminescence even in daytime. The smell near them is overwhelming—rot and perfume and the wet-earth reek of compost.

These trees do not belong to the climate around them. The air here is warm but not hot, perhaps 24 degrees Celsius, with a mild breeze that never quite stops. The sky is overcast more often than not, a ceiling of dove-gray cloud that seems too low, too smooth, too uniform. When rain comes—and it comes in brief, sporadic events, perhaps twice a day—it falls in discrete columns, visible from a distance as blue-gray pillars connecting cloud to earth. The rain is targeted. Localized. Each tree receives its own weather.

Between the trees, the ground is parched within hours. Your footsteps crack through salt-crust and sun-hardened clay. The emptiness hums with a vast, granular silence—the white noise of wind over openness, the settling tick of cooling stone, the whisper of sand moving grain by grain. But approach one of the trees, step into its shadow, and the world transforms.

## The Sound

The noise is *ferocious*.

Not loud in the sense of a single overwhelming source, but loud in its multiplicity, its layering, its refusal to resolve into discrete sources. It comes from everywhere at once, a wall of sound so dense it has texture. The base layer is insect-drone—billions of wings, mandibles, legs, antennae, all creating a buzz that vibrates in your chest, that you feel in your teeth. Over this: bird calls, but not the calls of any birds you know. Shrieks that spiral up past the range of hearing. Guttural clacks like bones striking together. Whistles that seem to come from inside your skull. Hoots, rattles, trills, screams.

And beneath it all, deeper: the sound of growth itself. The creak and groan of wood under tension as vines tighten their grip. The rustle of leaves expanding, unfurling, pushing against neighbors. The wet *pop* of seed pods bursting. The sussuration of sap moving through vessels under pressure. If you place your hand on the bark—though you must push through inches of living moss to do so—you can feel it thrumming, as if the tree were not a plant but an engine.

The towers—the buildings—seem to have been designed with this in mind. Their windows are few and narrow. Their walls are thick. But even inside, you hear it. The sound permeates. Some of the towers have given up on silence entirely and left their upper floors open to the air, becoming vertical gardens themselves, their architecture subsumed.

## The Architecture

The towers are young. You can tell by their sharpness, the crisp edges where materials meet, the lack of weathering on surfaces that should show more damage. They're made of something that resembles ceramic or calcined bone—matte white, slightly warm to the touch, harder than stone. The construction method isn't clear. No seams, no joints, no mortar. Each structure seems poured or grown as a single piece.

They vary wildly in form. Some are cylindrical and plain, fifty meters tall, with nothing but a door at ground level and small rectangular windows spiraling up the exterior. Others are organic: bulbous, asymmetrical, with flowing curves and apertures that seem random until you realize they're positioned to frame specific sight lines—always toward other towers, creating networks of view. A few are skeletal, frameworks of white struts forming geodesic patterns, their interiors exposed to weather and already filling with vines that climb from planted ground-beds.

The infrastructure is subtle but present. Tubes—thin, flexible, like veins—run along the ground between towers, often buried just beneath the surface so you stumble over them. They carry water. You can tell because they sweat condensation, leaving dark lines across the pale earth. Where they connect to trees, the interface is unclear, the tubes seeming to *merge* with root or bark rather than merely touching. 

Bridges exist but are rare: delicate spans of cable and white mesh that arc between towers, always high up—twenty, thirty meters off the ground. Walking them, you see the landscape's true scale: the basin extends for dozens of kilometers in every direction, and there are *hundreds* of towers, each with its tree or two or five, and the empty spaces between are vast enough to swallow cities.

## The Fauna

They are everywhere, and they are always moving.

The trees are cities of animal life. Birds, yes—hundreds of species, from things like hummingbirds (but with four wings, and faceted eyes) to crow-sized creatures with membranous crests that flush purple when they call. But also mammals: something like a lemur that moves through the canopy in groups of twenty or thirty, their bodies elongated, their tails prehensile and tipped with fan-like structures they use to glide between branches. Reptiles: geckos with fractalized skin patterns that make them nearly invisible against bark, and things like snakes but with too many ribs, their bodies articulated in a way that suggests an extra axis of flexibility.

And the insects. God, the insects.

They form clouds, rivers, cascades. Some are tiny—gnat-scale—moving in synchronized swarms that pulse and flex like muscles, like thoughts. Others are large enough to be concerning: beetle-things the size of your fist with iridescent carapaces, dragonfly-analogues with two-foot wingspans that hover motionless for minutes before darting away faster than your eye can follow. Many of them are clearly engineered. Their bodies have too much symmetry, too much precision. Legs that fold in geometrically perfect patterns. Wings with hexagonal cell structures visible to the naked eye. Eyes that look less like lenses and more like sensor arrays.

On the ground between towers, fauna is scarce but present. Things like rabbits, but too low-slung, with digging claws and no visible ears—they emerge from burrows to crop the sparse grasses that grow in the shadow-edges of buildings. Predators exist: something lean and long-legged, vaguely canid, with a gait that seems wrong—too smooth, as if its joints have one more degree of freedom than they should. You see them in the distance, always in the distance, loping between towers. They seem uninterested in humans.

The fauna ignores the boundaries between nature and technology. Birds nest in the towers, packing ventilation shafts with moss and sticks. Insects colonize any surface that offers a foothold. One tower—a bulbous one, covered in small circular ports that might once have served some technical function—has become a hive for something bee-like, and thousands of them stream in and out, their humming audible from two hundred meters away. No one stops them. The tower is still inhabited; you can see people on the upper balconies, living alongside.

## The Trees, Specifically

They should not exist. Cannot exist. But they insist on themselves.

Each one is a microclimate, a pocket ecosystem so dense with life it becomes almost abstract. The canopy intercepts everything—light, rain, falling dust—and transforms it. Water that reaches the ground beneath a tree has already passed through meters of leaves, been absorbed and re-released, been drunk and exhaled, been filtered and processed. It emerges as mist. As condensation. As strange trickles that seem to flow up bark before falling again.

The trees fruit constantly. Not in seasons—there are no seasons here—but in zones. The lower branches of one tree might be heavy with things like figs, purple-black and splitting open, while twenty meters up the same tree, white flowers bloom with a smell like burning sugar. Fruits fall, hit the layered detritus below, and are consumed within minutes. By what? You never see the whole animal, just the rustle, the movement, the disappearance of mass.

Some of the trees are old—or rather, they contain oldness. Their cores are not wood but something else: metal, perhaps, or ceramicized biomass, or the remnants of structures that preceded them. You can see it where bark has split, where lightning has opened wounds. Inside: geometries that are too regular, materials that gleam. The trees are growing through something, over something, *from* something.

And they're spreading. Slowly, but measurably. Aerial roots drop from branches, seeking ground. Seeds germinate in the cracked pavement between towers. Young trees—only five or six meters tall, barely adolescent—appear in places that were empty a year ago. The people in the towers track this, mark it on maps, discuss it. No one prevents it. The consensus seems to be that the trees are inevitable, that the landscape is becoming what it wants to become, and resistance is not even considered.

## The Technology

It hides in plain sight, and then it reveals itself suddenly, startlingly.

The tubes that carry water are not merely pipes. Sometimes they glow faintly from within—blue, or green, or a color that has no name. The light moves as pulses, regular as heartbeat. Put your ear to one and you hear not the slosh of liquid but something more complex: harmonics, rhythms, patterns that suggest data. Information. The water itself may be a carrier medium for something else.

The towers have interfaces, but they're not screens or panels. They're organic: patches of wall that respond to touch by changing texture, by warming or cooling, by emitting faint smells—copper, vanilla, ozone. Press your hand to one and you might feel vibration: short pulses, long pulses, patterns. Language? Commands? It's unclear. The inhabitants seem to understand, but when asked, they can't quite explain. "You just know," they say. "You feel what it means."

Some structures are clearly beyond: a tower near the basin's center is topped with something like a flower, thirty meters across, that tracks the sun even through cloud cover. Its petals are articulated, hinged, and shimmer with iridescence. At night it closes, and from within comes light—not harsh, not electric, but a soft gold that illuminates clouds from below, making them glow like paper lanterns. What is it? Observatory? Transmitter? Temple? All three, maybe. None.

Drones exist, but they're not mechanical. They're biological, engineered creatures the size of sparrows, with membranous wings and bodies that seem too light, too hollow. They cluster on tower ledges, dormant, and then suddenly activate—dozens at once—and stream out in formation. They carry things: packets wrapped in leaves, small tools, occasionally injured animals. They return, perch, and become still again, so motionless they seem dead until they're needed.

The strangest technology is the least visible: the sense that the entire landscape is networked, that every tree, every tower, every tube and bridge and drone is part of a single system, distributed and decentralized but coherent. Information flows. Not through wires—there are no wires—but through mycelial networks in the soil, through pheromone clouds emitted by engineered insects, through modulated light from the tower-flowers, through vibrations in the white ceramic walls. The city thinks, but slowly, and no one can point to where its mind resides.

## The Light

Is always insufficient.

Even at what should be midday, the light is crepuscular. The cloud cover sees to that—thick, unbroken for days at a time, reducing the sun to a directionless glow. Colors are muted. Shadows are soft. Everything exists in shades of gray and green and white.

Except near the trees.

The bioluminescence is constant. Fungi that coat bark in patches like lichen glow blue-green, pulsing gently as if breathing. Flowers emit light from their throats—reds, purples, occasionally a clear white like trapped moonlight. Insects carry it on their bodies: firefly-things, yes, but also beetles whose carapaces glow softly, and moths whose wings are translucent screens flickering with patterns. At night—which is darker than it should be, the cloud cover blocking stars—each tree becomes a constellation, a pillar of light in the void.

The towers add their own illumination. Windows glow from within, warm and inhabited. The flower-structures on rooftops emit their golden light. Some towers have grown phosphorescent moss on their exteriors, accidental or cultivated, and they stand in the darkness like ghost-pillars, pale and luminous.

But between—in the empty kilometers between tower and tree—there is almost nothing. Darkness you could drown in. Travelers carry lights, always. Small devices that emit a bluish glow, or torches that burn with chemical flames, or—more commonly—symbiotic organisms, something like a lantern-fish adapted to air, carried in transparent containers, fed on scraps, its light cold and steady.

The dimness does something to time. Without strong daylight, without clear sunrise or sunset, hours blur. Activity happens in cycles that don't quite match the sky. People sleep when exhausted, wake when rested. The city runs on a circadian rhythm that belongs to something else—to the trees, perhaps, or to the networked intelligence underlying everything.

## The Rules

Some are explicit, posted on tower walls in symbols that are half-pictograph, half-abstract: Do not damage the trees. Do not obstruct the tubes. Keep noise low within fifty meters of inhabited structures. Feed the drones if they bring you injured fauna.

Others are implicit, learned through observation: 

The spaces between towers are dangerous at night. Not because of predators—though they're there—but because of disorientation. The emptiness and darkness conspire to erase distance and direction. People have walked in circles until dawn, exhausted, mere hundreds of meters from home.

The trees are not to be climbed by outsiders. Residents of the nearby tower, yes—they have permission, granted through mechanisms no one fully explains—but strangers who attempt it find themselves harassed by fauna, swarmed by insects, dive-bombed by birds. The trees defend themselves, or are defended.

Water from the tubes can be drunk, but sparingly. It's shared with the trees, with the towers' systems, with the engineered fauna. Take too much and... what? No one knows. No one has. The taboo is strong enough that the question remains theoretical.

The drones are not to be touched while dormant. They're fragile, and they're needed. A broken drone is a community loss, mourned collectively.

And the strangest rule: if you find a space that feels *wrong*—too quiet, too still, where the air seems thicker—leave immediately. Don't investigate. Don't linger. These pockets appear and disappear, zones where the underlying systems have gone somehow awry, where the network has knotted or frayed. They're rare, but they're real. People who stay in them report confusion, nausea, lost time. Some don't come back the same.

## The Ecology in Motion

The landscape is never still.

Rain falls in its columns, and within minutes the ground beneath a tree transforms—dry sand goes dark with moisture, seeds begin to germinate, roots push up through crust. By the next day, there are shoots. By the end of the week, the ground is carpeted.

The insects swarm in patterns that shift hourly. Morning sees the pollinators—things like bees, things like butterflies, clouds of them moving from tree to tree. Midday brings the predators: dragonfly-analogues hunting, wasps patrolling territories, spiders descending on silk lines to rebuild webs destroyed in the morning's chaos. Evening is quieter, but not silent—nocturnal species emerge, and the soundscape shifts to clicks and chirps and the soft rustle of wings.

The fauna populations cycle, but not seasonally. Boom and bust happen in waves, localized. One tree will suddenly host an explosion of fruit, and within days every frugivore in a kilometer radius is there, gorging. The fruit depletes. The animals disperse. Another tree, elsewhere, begins its fruiting.

Predation is constant, visible. Hawks stoop on gliding mammals. Snakes coil in ambush on high branches. The canid-things chase down the rabbit-analogues in the open spaces, running them down over hundreds of meters. But there's a balance—no population crashes, no extinctions. The system is managed, though by what or whom remains unclear.

The towers themselves change. Slowly, but noticeably. New growths appear on walls—roots, vines, patches of moss. Sometimes they're removed by inhabitants, scraped clean. Other times they're allowed to remain, even cultivated. Some towers are nearly overrun, their white surfaces hidden under green, their shapes becoming ambiguous. Others stay pristine, defended daily against encroachment.

And the weather—the targeted rain—follows patterns that seem almost intentional. Trees that look stressed receive more water. Newly planted groves are watered twice as often. The system is responsive, adaptive. The clouds themselves feel less like weather and more like infrastructure, another layer of the network.

## The Inhabitants

They're quiet in a landscape of noise. Human voices are rare, subdued. Conversation happens indoors, or in whispers when outside. Partly this is courtesy—the noise from the trees is already overwhelming, adding more feels wrong. Partly it's habit. The loudness makes you retreat inward.

Most people live alone or in small groups, each tower housing five to twenty individuals. Communities exist, but they're dispersed, connected by the bridges and by paths worn through the emptiness. You see someone walking in the distance—a figure alone on pale sand, tiny with distance—and wonder where they're going, how far they'll walk.

The work is maintenance, mostly. Tending the tubes, repairing bridges, managing interfaces in the towers. Some people farm the trees—carefully, respectfully, harvesting fruit and fungus and leaves with medicinal properties. Others study: they document the fauna, track population changes, map the spread of roots. A few are artists, creating installations from found materials—driftwood-like branches, shed insect carapaces, the strange metals that emerge from broken-open tree cores.

They speak a language that has absorbed the place. Words for different types of silence. Distinctions between the sound of insect swarms that mean different things. Twenty terms for the quality of light through different densities of canopy. The language is young—like the towers, like the landscape—still forming, still finding what it needs to be.

There's a sense of temporariness, despite the permanence of the structures. People refer to themselves as "early." As in: "We're early; we don't know yet how this will be." The city is an experiment, and everyone here is a participant. Some towers are abandoned—not ruins, just... empty. Too close to a particularly aggressive tree, or too far from water sources, or built in a zone that turned out to be a drainage path during storms. Mistakes. Learning.

No one seems to be in charge, but decisions get made. New tubes are laid, new bridges built, new towers occasionally grown—that's the word they use, *grown*, even though the process involves interfaces and commands and technological processes no one explains. Consensus emerges from the network, perhaps, or from the slow collective intelligence of inhabitants communicating through glowing wall-patches and cryptic symbols.

Children exist but are few. They grow up strange—comfortable with the noise, fluent in the non-verbal languages of the place, able to read the signs in insect swarms and tree-stress. They climb where adults don't, befriend drones, sleep in the canopy. The city is raising a new kind of human, adapted to its weirdness.

## The Weirdness, Specifically

It accretes.

At first, the place seems merely *strange*—technology you don't quite understand, ecology that's lush in defiance of logic. But the longer you stay, the more things don't add up:

The towers are young, but some trees contain structures that are clearly older. Where did the towers come from, then? Were they built around the trees? After? And if after, what was here before?

The fauna are engineered—obviously, undeniably engineered—but by whom? The inhabitants disclaim knowledge. "They're just here," they say. "They've always been here." But the city is only decades old. Pressed, people become vague, change the subject, seem genuinely confused by the question.

The rain is targeted, but there's no visible mechanism. No emitters, no drones seeding clouds. The weather simply... cooperates. Knows. Responds.

Sometimes, at night, you see lights in the distance that aren't towers, aren't trees. They move slowly across the basin, too far away to reach. In the morning, people mention seeing them too, but no one investigates. "They're just there," again. The same phrase, the same unbothered acceptance.

The tubes hum with data, but what are they communicating? And to what? The towers have interfaces, but they don't seem to connect to anything, any network, any central system. They're local, self-contained, and yet... decisions happen. Coordination occurs.

There's a feeling, persistent and unsettling, that the landscape is not a place you're living in but a place that's living around you. That you're less an inhabitant and more a symbiont, tolerated, perhaps even needed, but not in charge, not even close.

And beneath it all, literally beneath—meters down, visible where erosion has exposed substrate—are hints of something massive. Structures, or a structure. Walls, or maybe conduits, or maybe something that's both or neither. The white towers root into it. The trees' deepest taproots penetrate it. It's old, or it's eternal, or it's neither because it exists in a relationship to time that doesn't match the surface.

No one digs. The prohibition isn't stated, but it's absolute. You know, instinctively, that the foundation is not to be disturbed. That the landscape rests on something that must remain undiscovered, at least for now. At least while we're early.

## The Experience of Being There

Your body adapts.

Within days, the sound becomes texture instead of noise. You stop hearing it as overwhelming and start hearing through it—individual calls, specific rustles, the approach of rain. Your ears recalibrate. Silence, when you find it inside a tower's deepest room, becomes painful, hollow, wrong.

The dimness makes your eyes widen, your pupils dilate. Colors shift in your perception. The greens of the canopy become differentiated—you see hundreds of shades where before there were only a few. The white of the towers glows, almost painful in contrast to the surrounding murk.

Your sense of distance warps. The emptiness is too large, and your brain has no reference points. A tower that looks ten minutes away takes an hour to reach. You learn to distrust your eyes, to navigate by counting paces, by memorizing landmarks that aren't visible but felt—a slight rise in the ground, a change in soil texture, the smell-zone of a particular tree.

You sleep strangely. The lack of clear day/night cycles disrupts circadian rhythms for the first week, and then something else takes over. You sleep when the fauna quiets—there are lulls, twice daily, when the noise drops to half—and wake when it surges again. Your body learns the landscape's rhythm, and your own fades.

Loneliness is impossible. Even alone in your tower, separated from the nearest neighbor by kilometers, you are constantly surrounded by *presence*. The fauna, yes, but more—the sense of the network, the system, the intelligence that suffuses the place. You are always observed, not by eyes but by the landscape itself. It's not hostile, but it's not ignorable. You are known.

And over time, you change. Not physically—though your sleep schedule shifts, your diet adapts, your skin darkens slightly from the dim but constant UV—but in orientation. You stop thinking of the place as alien and start thinking of your former life as aberrant. Cities with their straight lines and hard boundaries seem bizarre. Constant bright light feels aggressive. The silence of uninhabited spaces becomes terrifying.

You're being acculturated, converted, absorbed. The landscape is gentle about it, but it's inevitable. Stay long enough, and you'll never fully leave. Part of you will remain in the Hollowgarden, listening to the cacophony, walking the empty spaces, waiting for rain.
