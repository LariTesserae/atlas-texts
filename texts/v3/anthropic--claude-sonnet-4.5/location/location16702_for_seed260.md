# The Resonance Peaks

The mountains are singing themselves into existence.

They rise—are rising—will have risen—in a region where tense collapses under its own weight. Jagged spires of black graphite and pale quartz punch upward through a substrate that hasn't quite decided whether it's finished being cloud or has begun being stone. The peaks reach extreme heights, some already towering three thousand meters above the valley basin, others still flickering between potential and actual, their summits phasing in and out of solidity with each thunderous harmonic cycle. When you look at them, you see both the mountain that is and the mountain that was about to be, superimposed, vibrating between states.

The sound is overwhelming. Constant. Not quite deafening but approaching it—a vast choir of resonant frequencies that emanate from the stone itself. Deep bass notes throb up through your feet and into your chest cavity. Mid-range tones howl between the peaks like wind, though the air itself moves slowly, almost meditatively. High crystalline overtones shatter and reform in patterns that make your teeth ache. The mountains are tuning themselves, their molecular structures finding equilibrium through acoustic vibration, singing their way from formlessness into form. The sound never stops. Even in what might pass for quieter moments, the substrate hums with sub-audible frequencies that you feel in your marrow.

## The Dry Garden

There is no water here, yet the place is verdant in its own impossible way.

What grows does so without moisture. The valley floors and lower slopes support something between lichen and coral—dense, intricate structures of pure carbon arranged in fractal geometries. They rise in shelf-like formations, some waist-high, most knee-high, spreading in scattered patches across the grey talus. The color is extraordinary: deep teals and jades that seem to luminesce faintly in the dim light, though they generate no light of their own. These growths metabolize directly from the vibrational energy that saturates the environment, their crystalline structures resonating sympathetically with the singing mountains, drawing sustenance from sound itself.

Between the carbon-gardens grow stands of what might generously be called trees, though they bear no resemblance to wood. Tall structures of bundled graphene filaments rise eight, ten, twelve meters high, their "trunks" no thicker than a human torso, splitting into delicate canopies of fiber so fine it's almost invisible except where it catches the light. These threadtrees sway constantly, though there's little wind—they move in response to the acoustic pressure waves, their filaments tuned to different frequencies, so that each tree appears to dance to its own rhythm while contributing its own harmonic to the greater song. They're scattered sparsely enough that you can see between them for considerable distances, but dense enough that you're rarely without one in sight.

Where the slopes steepen, different growths appear. Cushion-like formations of compressed carbon fiber, deep green-black and springy underfoot, cluster in the protected spaces between boulders. And strangest: vertical gardens on the cliff faces, where something like moss but crystalline grows in sheets that ripple and undulate as the stone beneath them continues its slow birth-song, reforming itself grain by grain.

## The Impossible Geology

The rock here doesn't behave.

The mountains are emerging—not eroding, not weathering, but actively forming. You can watch it happen, though it requires patience. A boulder might spend six hours flickering at the edge of perception before finally settling into full materiality. A ridge might sing itself ten meters higher over the course of a week, its constituent atoms arranging themselves in response to standing wave patterns in the acoustic field. The geology is raw, almost embryonic, stone finding form through resonance rather than heat or pressure.

This creates a landscape of startling textures. Some surfaces are glassy-smooth where molecules have aligned perfectly in harmonic arrays. Others are rough with partially-formed crystal structures, like frost that hasn't quite finished freezing. Still others seem to breathe, their surfaces actually flexing by millimeters as the sound waves pass through them, solid stone behaving like a slow liquid, like the membrane of a vast drum.

The composition is strange—mostly carbon in various allotropes, from graphite to something approaching diamond, mixed with quartz and other silicon compounds. Almost no metals. No rust-red or copper-green. The color palette is blacks, greys, whites, and those impossible jades and teals of the living growths. Under the dim light, everything takes on a twilight quality, a perpetual dusk or pre-dawn where colors are muted but somehow more present, more themselves.

Scattered across this emerging terrain are areas where reality frays more noticeably. Zones where multiple possible versions of the landscape exist simultaneously—you might see a slope both with and without a particular outcropping, both states equally real, flickering back and forth. These zones pulse in time with particularly strong harmonic nodes, and the sound within them takes on a stuttering, echoed quality, as if you're hearing the same note at slightly different times.

## Atmosphere and Light

The sky is wrong.

Not absent—wrong. It exists in a state of perpetual uncertainty, a grey-violet expanse that might be cloud or might be something more fundamental. The light that filters through is dim, directionless, seeming to emanate from the air itself rather than from any single source. There's no visible sun, no moon, no stars. Just this ambient illumination, cool-toned, that makes it impossible to determine time of day or whether such a concept has meaning here.

The air itself is cool, comfortable against skin, perhaps fifteen degrees Celsius most of the time. It doesn't smell of much—a faint mineral scent, something like static electricity, occasionally a sharp tang like ozone when a particularly powerful harmonic resonates nearby. But the temperature fluctuates in ways that suggest it's responding to something other than weather. Sometimes localized zones of warmth or coolness pool in valleys or around certain peaks, thermal eddies following acoustic patterns rather than convection.

Occasionally—perhaps once every few hours—the light brightens or dims noticeably for a period of minutes. These fluctuations don't correspond to any visible change in the sky. The vegetation responds, though: the carbon-gardens seem to intensify their color during bright periods, while the threadtrees vibrate more actively when it's darkest, as if compensating.

## The Architecture of Absence

There are marks of habitation, but they're scattered and primitive.

Small cairns appear on some of the more stable ridge lines—stacks of that half-formed stone, three or four feet high, arranged with clear intentionality. They serve no obvious purpose. They're not directional markers—they don't point anywhere. They're not memorials—there's nothing here that dies. Perhaps they're simply assertions: *something was here that could stack stones*.

In one valley, a partial structure: three walls of rough-cut graphite blocks, each about the size of a torso, fitted together without mortar. The fourth wall was never built, or hasn't been built yet—the space flickers with uncertainty. No roof. Inside, the floor has been swept clean, and there are shallow depressions in the ground where the carbon-garden has been deliberately cultivated in geometric patterns. Someone sat here. Someone arranged things. The acoustic properties inside the three walls are noticeably different—certain frequencies cancel out, others amplify. It's a kind of shelter, but from sound rather than weather.

Higher up, on an exposed plateau, there's evidence of stone-working: a scatter of graphite chips and shards around a large boulder, and several fragments that show deliberate flaking patterns. Primitive tools—hand axes, scrapers, blades—lie where they were knapped. Some are half-finished. Others have been used; you can see the wear patterns on the edges. But there are no people. No bones, no waste, no clear indication of when this work was done or by what.

The tools are interesting. They're designed for working this strange stone, for harvesting the carbon-gardens (some blades show green-black staining), for perhaps shaping the threadtree fibers. One scraper appears to be made from something almost like diamond, black and glossy, hard enough to score even the glassiest stone surfaces.

Here and there, scattered across perhaps thirty square kilometers, other traces: a fire ring of stones but no charcoal or ash (what would burn here?), a worn path that suggests regular travel between two peaks, a place where someone has arranged fractured quartz crystals in a spiral pattern three meters across.

## The Fauna That Isn't

Nothing moves on its own except the plants.

No birds wheel overhead. No insects crawl between the stone. No burrowing creatures tunnel through the carbon substrate. The ecological system here—if it can be called that—functions without animals. The threadtrees release no seeds; they grow from acoustic nodes, filaments condensing directly from vibrational patterns in the air. The carbon-gardens propagate through fractal expansion, one formation budding into another over weeks or months. Energy flows through the system in ways that bypass the need for consumption and digestion.

Yet there are gaps in this absence. You sometimes catch movement in your peripheral vision—something that might be a shape crossing between two boulders, or ascending a slope. But when you turn to look directly, there's nothing. And nothing has ever been. The disturbance in your perception leaves no tracks, casts no shadow, makes no sound beyond the omnipresent singing of the mountains.

More unsettling: occasionally you find traces that suggest fauna without the fauna itself. A depression in a carbon cushion as if something heavy rested there. A pattern of disturbance in loose scree suggesting something passed through. But no creature appears to have made them, and they're always slightly off—too symmetrical, or at the wrong scale, or oriented in a direction that makes no sense given the terrain.

The place feels watched, but not by eyes.

## The Breakdown

Reality is thin here, wearing through at the seams.

The mountains are singing themselves into existence, yes, but existence itself is uncertain. The highest peaks flicker most dramatically, sometimes present, sometimes a probability-ghost, sometimes seeming to exist in several mutually exclusive states simultaneously. You can see a summit from one angle and not from another, not because it's occluded but because from that particular vantage point it hasn't decided to be real yet.

Time behaves strangely. You might spend what feels like twenty minutes walking between two cairns, then notice that the light hasn't changed and your shadow (such as it is in this directionless illumination) hasn't moved. Or you might take five steps and suddenly feel exhausted as if you'd been climbing for hours, your muscles burning with fatigue that arrived without the intervening effort.

The acoustic phenomena intensify these breaks. Certain combinations of frequencies create zones where causality loops back on itself. You might hear a stone fall, then watch it begin falling, then find it already at rest at the bottom of a slope. The sound reaches you before, during, and after the event, all simultaneously. In these zones, which pulse in and out of stability, you can sometimes perceive multiple timeline layers, like transparent sheets stacked atop one another, each showing a slightly different version of now.

There are places—usually around the strongest harmonic nodes, often near the half-built structures—where you can't quite remember if you've been there before or are about to go there for the first time. The distinction ceases to matter. Memory and anticipation become indistinguishable.

And sometimes, in the quiet moments (though it's never truly quiet), you notice that the carbon-gardens are growing in patterns that won't be possible for another hour, or that a threadtree is swaying to harmonics that haven't been sung yet. The landscape is emerging, but not always in chronological order.

## The Acoustic Environment

The sound deserves deeper attention, because it is the landscape's primary quality, its defining characteristic, almost its purpose.

At the base level, there's a subsonic throb—frequencies below twenty hertz that you don't so much hear as feel in your viscera. This is the fundamental, the base note of the mountains' self-creation, a pulse that cycles roughly every forty seconds. It feels like the heartbeat of something vast and slow being born.

Above that, in the range of human hearing, layers upon layers. A deep drone around sixty to eighty hertz, constant and unwavering, forms a tonal foundation. Multiple peaks each contribute their own overtones—you can identify individual summits by their acoustic signatures once you learn to listen. One produces a pure tone around 240 hertz. Another generates a complex cluster of frequencies centered on 520 hertz. A particularly tall spire emits a piercing note near 1800 hertz that seems to cut through everything else.

These tones interact. They create beats when they're close in frequency, producing rhythmic pulsations of louder and softer. They generate difference tones—ghost frequencies that arise from the interaction of two or more primary frequencies, audible as additional notes that no single source is producing. The acoustic space between the peaks becomes filled with a constantly shifting web of sound, harmonics and sub-harmonics emerging and dissolving, creating moments of unbearable consonance and grinding dissonance.

The threadtrees add their contribution: softer, more delicate sounds as their filaments vibrate sympathetically. Each tree has its own resonant frequency, and they chime and hum in response to the mountains' deeper tones, like sympathetic strings on an instrument. The effect is of an orchestra tuning, endlessly, except that the tuning *is* the performance.

Wind across exposed rock faces generates additional tones—whistles, howls, rushes of noise that come and go. Except there's often no wind, and the sounds occur anyway, as if the memory of wind passing over stone continues to produce sound in wind's absence.

Most overwhelming are the events—moments when multiple peaks hit resonance together, their frequencies aligning in powerful consonance. These happen perhaps twice a day, and when they do, the sound becomes genuinely deafening, a physical force that makes your bones vibrate, that blurs your vision, that seems to reach inside your chest and play your ribcage like an instrument. During these events, you can watch the landscape shimmer and shift, stone reforming more rapidly, new features emerging in minutes that would normally take days, the carbon-gardens visibly expanding, the threadtrees growing centimeters taller as you watch.

And underneath everything, threading through it all, there's a quality of intelligence to the sound. Not consciousness exactly, but something organizing it, shaping it, guiding the frequencies toward some kind of resolution. The mountains are not being sung into existence by something else; they are singing *themselves* into existence, and the song is both process and intention, both means and end.

## Movement and Duration

Things change, but slowly, with the patient inevitability of geological time compressed into human-perceivable scale.

A walk across the valley floor, from the partial structure to a distant cairn visible perhaps three kilometers away, might take two hours. The terrain is difficult—loose scree, carbon-garden formations to navigate around, slopes steeper than they appear from distance. But it's not the physical difficulty that dominates the experience; it's the acoustic environment that shapes your movement. You find yourself pausing frequently, not from fatigue but because certain locations demand attention—places where the sound patterns are particularly complex or beautiful, where you can feel reality thinning, where the vibrations create a kind of presence that seems to warrant witness.

The light changes its intensity over periods of hours, but there's no clear day/night cycle. Instead, there seem to be longer periods of relative brightness lasting perhaps eight to twelve hours, followed by dimmer periods of similar length, but the boundaries between them are gradual and irregular. During the brighter periods, the greens of the carbon-gardens seem almost to glow. During darker periods, the landscape takes on a monochromatic quality, all greys and blacks except for the faintest hint of teal where the gardens grow thickest.

The temperature fluctuates with similar slow irregularity. Cool is the baseline—fifteen degrees—but it might drop to ten for a period of hours, or rise to twenty. These changes seem correlated with the acoustic events; major resonance periods tend to bring warmth, though not always. There's no precipitation, no weather in the conventional sense. Just these slow thermal tides washing across the landscape.

Watch a single spot for a full day (or what passes for a day), and you can perceive the changes. A boulder that was flickering at dawn (or dawn-equivalent) might be fully solid by dusk (or dusk-equivalent). A bare patch of talus might show the first hints of carbon-garden formation, tiny crystalline structures beginning to branch outward. A threadtree might add another meter of height, its canopy spreading slowly like ink diffusing through water.

But watch the same spot for an hour and you'll see almost nothing. This is a landscape that operates on the boundary between human and geological timescales. Fast enough that patience is rewarded with visible change. Slow enough that urgency has no meaning here.

## The Rules of This World

Through observation and experience, certain principles emerge—not physical laws exactly, but the local logic, the way this place prefers to function:

**Sound is primary.** Not light, not heat, not chemical reaction, but vibration. Matter organizes in response to acoustic patterns. The carbon-gardens grow along nodal lines of standing waves. The threadtrees sprout at anti-nodes where vibration is maximal. Even the half-formed structures sit at locations where certain frequencies cancel or amplify in useful ways.

**Time is negotiable.** Linear causality is a suggestion rather than a rule. Effects sometimes precede causes. The same event can be remembered, experienced, and anticipated simultaneously. This isn't chaotic—there seems to be a deeper order to it—but it's not the simple forward arrow of conventional time.

**Solidity is a spectrum.** Things exist on a continuum from potential to actual. Most objects most of the time are fully real, but some flicker, some phase, some exist in superposition. The degree of solidity seems related to how strongly something participates in the acoustic patterns—the more integrated into the sonic structure, the more real.

**Life requires no water.** The biology here, such as it is, operates on entirely different principles. The carbon-gardens and threadtrees metabolize acoustic energy directly. They grow, propagate, and die (though death is slow, a gradual loss of resonance until a formation stops responding to the mountains' song and begins very slowly to crumble). No mouths, no stomachs, no blood. Just vibration and response.

**Intelligence is distributed.** The marks of tool-use, the structures, the cairns—they suggest something capable of intentional action, yet there are no people, no animals. Perhaps the intelligence is a property of the place itself, emerging from the complex acoustic patterns like consciousness arising from neural firing. Or perhaps beings exist here in some form you cannot quite perceive, phasing in and out of solidity, leaving traces in the material world but dwelling somewhere adjacent to it.

**Silence is impossible.** There is no quiet place. Even the most sheltered valley, the most enclosed space within the partial structure, thrums with sound. The mountains sing themselves into being, and everything here exists within that song, shaped by it, participating in it whether actively or passively.

## Regions and Localities

The landscape extends across an area perhaps thirty kilometers wide and forty long—large enough to contain real variety, small enough that determined walking could cross it in a few days.

**The Valley of First Formation** spreads across the western portion, a relatively flat basin perhaps eight kilometers wide where the stone is newest, most tentative. Here the flickering between potential and actual is most pronounced. Boulders appear and disappear. The ground itself sometimes seems uncertain whether it's solid or not. The carbon-gardens are sparse here, just beginning to establish, tiny formations no larger than your hand spreading slowly across the grey substrate. The sound is loudest here—all the fundamental frequencies converge in this valley, and during major resonance events, the noise becomes genuinely dangerous, a physical force that can knock you down, make your ears bleed, cause nosebleeds and disorientation. But this is also where you can watch creation most clearly, where stone sings itself from nothing into something over hours instead of days.

**The Ridge of Stable Forms** runs north-south through the eastern portion. These peaks have already fully emerged—solid, no longer flickering, though they still sing with the fundamental tones. The ridge reaches heights of three thousand meters or more, jagged and black, with faces of glassy stone where the crystal structure has aligned perfectly. The carbon-gardens grow more thickly on these eastern slopes, and forests of threadtrees cluster in the protected valleys between peaks. This is where most of the scattered structures appear, where the traces of tool-use are most evident. Perhaps stability attracts intention, or perhaps intention helps stabilize.

**The Plateau of Incomplete Geometries** occupies the northern reach. Here the land is relatively flat, but covered with strange formations—partial circles of stone that might be cairns or might be something else, lines of rocks extending for hundreds of meters in perfectly straight directions before ending abruptly, spiral patterns carved into the ground that show significant wear, suggesting great age despite the landscape's overall newness. The sound here takes on different qualities—more overtones, more complex patterns, as if the flat surface allows frequencies to interact more richly. And the reality-breaks are more pronounced: time loops, causality reversals, zones where multiple versions of now exist simultaneously.

**The Southern Slopes** descend gradually over ten kilometers toward a region where the landscape simply ends—not at a cliff or border, but at a zone of increasing uncertainty where the mountains trail off into probability-ghosts, where stone becomes more idea than substance, where even the sound grows uncertain, fragmentary, dissolving into potential rather than actuality. Few marks of habitation appear this far south. It's as if even the distributed intelligence that leaves tools and structures recognizes that the southern region is too unformed, too uncertain to bother with.

Between these regions, the landscape is continuous but varied—valleys and peaks, slopes and plateaus, all interconnected, all participating in the same vast acoustic process of self-creation.

## Moments of Particular Presence

Some experiences stand out, crystallized moments where the landscape's nature becomes most apparent:

Standing in the Valley of First Formation during a major resonance event. The sound builds slowly—you can feel it coming, a gradual increase in intensity and coherence as multiple peaks align their frequencies. Then it hits, and suddenly you're inside a physical manifestation of sound, your whole body vibrating, your vision blurring, your teeth aching, and through the overwhelming noise you watch a ridge that has been flickering for days suddenly solidify completely, stone arranging itself into full materiality in the space of perhaps three minutes, growing upward like time-lapse footage of a plant, except this is rock, and it's happening at normal speed, and it's real.

Walking through a mature threadtree forest on the eastern slopes in one of the darker periods. The dim light makes the delicate filament-canopies nearly invisible, so you navigate by the trunks alone, and each tree is singing its own note, and as you move between them the harmonics shift and change around you, and the effect is of walking through a vast aeolian harp, the landscape itself playing you as much as you're experiencing it. And occasionally you brush against the threadtree filaments accidentally, and they're cool and slightly rough, and they vibrate under your touch, adding your own frequencies momentarily to the forest's song.

Sitting inside the partial structure for hours, sheltered from the loudest frequencies, listening to the filtered-down overtones that make it through the walls. The acoustic space is transformed—certain painful frequencies are blocked entirely, while others are amplified. The effect is almost meditative, calming, and you begin to understand why someone would build this, why these particular three walls in this particular location. It's not shelter from weather or danger. It's an instrument for listening, a tool for perceiving the deeper patterns in the mountains' song. And as you sit, you notice the cultivated carbon-garden patterns inside, and you realize they're arranged to resonate with specific frequencies, and the whole structure is designed to make certain harmonics visible through their effect on these living sensors.

Standing at the southern edge where the landscape dissolves into uncertainty, watching the mountains fade from solid to probable to merely potential, and realizing that this isn't a boundary—the place doesn't end here. Rather, this is where you reach the limit of your own ability to perceive it. The mountains continue, are continuing, will have continued, singing themselves into existence, but beyond this point your senses cannot follow. The sound here is strange—stuttering, fragmentary, as if you're hearing only every third or fourth note of the song. And the light seems to flicker in ways that suggest not darkness and brightness but existence and non-existence alternating.

Discovering a new cairn that wasn't there yesterday, or that was there tomorrow, time-sense confused, and finding that someone has arranged the stones in a specific pattern that causes them to resonate at particular frequencies, and the whole structure is singing a single pure tone that rises clear above the background harmonic complexity, and you don't know what it means or who built it or when, but it's clearly intentional, clearly communication of some kind, a message written in stone and sound.

## The Experience of Duration

To spend time here—days, weeks, longer—is to undergo a fundamental shift in how you relate to time and change.

The first day is overwhelming. The sound, the visual distortions, the impossible geology, the absence of familiar reference points—it's too much. You find yourself exhausted not from physical exertion but from cognitive load, from trying to make sense of a place that refuses conventional sense-making.

By the third day, you begin to acclimate. The sound becomes less overwhelming, more differentiated. You start hearing individual peaks, recognizing patterns, anticipating resonance events. The visual distortions become less disturbing—you learn to accept that some things flicker, that causality is loose, that memory and anticipation sometimes swap places.

After a week, you move differently. Slower, more patient. You find yourself sitting for long periods just watching a particular boulder or slope, waiting to see what changes. The carbon-gardens become fascinating—you begin to see how they grow, to recognize the patterns in their fractal branching, to understand that they're responding to specific acoustic frequencies. You start to hear the threadtrees' individual voices within the larger chorus.

After two weeks, if you stay that long, you begin to lose track of linear time entirely. Days blur into each other. The cyclic light changes cease to mark clear boundaries. You eat when hungry—the carbon-gardens are technically edible, though they taste like graphite feels and provide minimal nutrition. You sleep when tired, which might be twice in a day or once in two days; your body stops following circadian rhythms and starts following the landscape's own cycles instead.

And you begin to notice things you didn't see before. Patterns in the cairns' distribution that suggest a larger design. Traces of something's passage that appear and disappear. The way certain valleys collect warmth or coolness in ways that have nothing to do with topography. The places where reality is thinnest, where you can almost perceive the vast acoustic machinery underneath, the fundamental frequencies that are singing not just the mountains but perhaps space itself into existence.

You start to wonder if the distributed intelligence that leaves tools and structures is future-you, or past-you, or some version of you that exists outside linear time. The thought doesn't disturb you the way it would have when you first arrived. Here, it seems almost reasonable.

And the sound—after enough time, you stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as language, as meaning, as something that might be trying to tell you how it works, this place, how reality can be sung into existence one frequency at a time, how form follows resonance, how everything is vibration given shape through time.

## The Quality of Endings

There's no clear point at which to leave such a place, because it doesn't progress toward any goal. The mountains are emerging, yes, but there's no indication they'll ever be finished. The landscape is in the process of becoming, and that process appears to be its permanent state, not a phase it will pass through.

When you finally walk south, back toward the zone of increasing uncertainty, you carry the sound with you. You'll hear it for days afterward—phantom harmonics, your ears ringing with frequencies that are no longer present. You'll catch yourself listening for the peaks' individual voices, miss the subsonic throb you'd learned to time your breathing to.

And you'll find yourself uncertain, afterward, about what you experienced. Was it real? Of course it was real—you can describe it in detail, remember specific moments, recall the texture of the threadtree filaments under your fingers. But was it real in the way other places are real? Did you walk through a landscape or through some kind of metaphor, some acoustic representation of formation itself?

The tools you saw, the cairns, the partial structures—did you build them? Are you going to build them? Have you already built them on a previous visit that hasn't happened yet?

The place leaves you with questions rather than answers. Which is perhaps appropriate. It's a landscape of emergence, of becoming, of things coming into existence through vibration and time. To encounter it is to witness creation itself, or one possible version of creation, where matter follows music, where reality is negotiated through resonance, where nothing is fixed but everything is present.

The mountains continue singing themselves into existence. They were doing so before you arrived. They'll continue long after you leave, if "before" and "after" even apply to a place where time loops back on itself. The song goes on, frequencies interacting, harmonics emerging and dissolving, stone slowly materializing from probability into actuality, carbon-gardens spreading along nodal lines, threadtrees swaying to rhythms that exist outside of wind.

And somewhere in that vast acoustic space, perhaps, an intelligence distributed through the whole system, thinking thoughts made of pure vibration, building cairns that exist simultaneously in past and future, leaving tools that might be used or might already have been used, creating structures that shelter not bodies but specific frequencies, making art from geology and time and sound.

The Resonance Peaks. A place where reality is thin, where sound is substance, where mountains sing themselves into being. Cool air, dim light, no water, lush growth of crystalline carbon, extreme heights emerging slowly from uncertainty, and everywhere, always, deafeningly, the song.
