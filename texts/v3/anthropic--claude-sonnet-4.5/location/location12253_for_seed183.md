# The Singing Heights

The mountains here climb until they encounter something that isn't quite sky—a threshold where the air becomes dense with competing geometries. At this elevation, approximately three vertical miles above what might be called the basal plain, the rock faces shimmer with a constant skin of water that doesn't fall. It clings. It moves horizontally. It climbs.

The stone itself is ancient basalt, columnar and grey, weathered into a thousand vertical flutings by epochs of patient atmosphere. But the water ignores gravity's pull with increasing brazenness the higher you go. Droplets crawl upward along the rock channels, merge, separate, reform. They move like they're following invisible rails of force that curve through space in directions the eye can't quite track. Sometimes a stream of water will arc from one peak to another, a living bridge that holds its shape for hours before suddenly collapsing into mist—or not collapsing, but rather *unmaking* itself from the middle outward, the droplets winking into non-existence rather than falling.

The sound is tremendous. Not roaring—that would be simpler. The mountains *sing* in overlapping harmonic frequencies that seem to emanate from the rock itself. Some frequencies you hear with your ears: deep resonances that throb in your chest, crystalline chimes, the peculiar metallic ringing of water on stone that shouldn't ring. Other frequencies you perceive through your teeth, your bones, the backs of your eyes. The basalt columns act as organ pipes, as bells, as tuning forks. Wind moving through the heights plays them, but so does the strange water, and so does something else—something that moves through the spaces between moments.

There is no vegetation. None at all. But the mountains are not barren—they're intensely occupied, just not by life as it's usually recognized. The density of presences here is suffocating despite the thin air. Crystal formations sprout from cracks in unlikely colors: sulfur-yellow, cobalt, a particular shade of orange that seems to vibrate slightly when viewed directly. These aren't growing—they're *already there*, manifesting as the light changes or as you move past certain angles. Some formations appear in your peripheral vision and vanish when you turn to look. Others remain solid and can be touched, though they're always cool, always exactly the same temperature as the air around them.

Scattered across the peaks and along the narrow ridges are stone structures. Not ruins—they show no signs of decay—but they're clearly old, settled into their positions as if they've been here since the mountains themselves rose. Simple cairns mark some paths. Small cells built from fitted basalt blocks cling to impossible ledges, their doorways empty, their interiors bare except for shallow depressions worn into the floor. Some chambers contain arrangements of stones—circles, lines, spirals—whose purposes aren't clear but whose intentionality is obvious. The architecture is primitive in technique: no mortar, no decoration, just stone on stone, carefully shaped with what must have been simple tools. Yet the positioning shows sophisticated understanding of something. The structures align with each other across vast distances, visible from peak to peak, creating networks of relationship that suggest measurement, observation, recording.

The light is almost violent in its clarity. At this elevation, the sun cuts through atmosphere that barely exists, casting shadows so sharp they look drawn with ink. But the light also *bends* wrong here. You'll see your shadow cast in three directions at once from a single sun. Reflections appear in the wet stone that show landscapes slightly different from the one you're standing in—same peaks, but with structures in different positions, or with the water flowing in different patterns, or with a sky that's the wrong color blue.

The water-slicked surfaces create endless reflections, doublings, mirrorings. Between the bright sun and the responsive stone, every surface becomes a potential window. You walk through a corridor of basalt columns and glimpse—in the wet sheen—a figure walking parallel to you on the other side of something that isn't quite a wall. You turn to look directly: just more rock, more water. But in the reflection, for that instant, something was there.

The wrongness intensifies with elevation. At the highest accessible peaks, reality becomes porous. You'll reach for a handhold and your hand will sink through stone that appeared solid, or vice versa—strike your palm against empty air that's somehow load-bearing. Sounds arrive before the events that cause them: you hear a rock clatter down a slope five seconds before you accidentally kick it loose. Time pools and eddies in the high basins where the strange water collects. You can watch a drop of water form, separate from the stone, and fall upward in perfect slow motion, taking perhaps fifteen minutes to complete a journey of three inches before it winks out of existence at the apex of its arc.

Temperature sits in the cool range, never quite cold enough for ice, never warm. The water should freeze at night, but it doesn't. It should evaporate under that fierce sun, but it doesn't do that either. It just *is*, maintaining its impossible presence, neither rain nor snowmelt nor spring-fed, but perhaps something that condenses out of the wrongness itself—moisture squeezed from the gaps between reliable reality and whatever else is trying to exist in the same space.

In the valleys between peaks—though "valley" is misleading when some of them seem to have more sides than geometry should allow—the density of phenomena increases. Here the singing grows complex, polyphonic, almost conversational. The crystal formations cluster more thickly, creating forests of mineral that refract the light into caustic patterns. The water behaves more erratically, sometimes flowing in spirals, sometimes hanging in the air as discrete spheres that orbit each other. The stone structures are more frequent in these spaces, suggesting that whatever built them found these geometrically troubled zones either useful or significant.

No animals. No plants. No fungi, no lichens, no bacteria visible to the naked eye. But the mountains are neither dead nor sterile—they're *otherwise*. They're full of motion, of transformation, of presence. The basalt itself seems to breathe in the sense that its surfaces change subtly over hours. A face that was smooth at dawn will show different fluting patterns by dusk. Whether this is erosion happening at an accelerated rate, or whether the stone is somehow remembering different versions of itself and cycling between them, is impossible to determine.

The metallic ringing never stops. It builds in waves, sometimes receding to a background shimmer, sometimes swelling to a crescendo where the individual notes become indistinguishable and the whole range becomes a single overwhelming tone. During these peaks, the reality breakdown intensifies proportionally. Surfaces become uncertain. Distances refuse to remain constant—a peak that looked a mile away is suddenly close enough to touch, or a cairn that was within arm's reach now requires an hour's climb to achieve.

Weather moves through in cycles, but wrong cycles. Mist will suddenly thicken into existence with no wind to carry it, rising from the stone itself or condensing out of clear air. It hangs in dense pockets, weighing down the spaces between the peaks, dampening sound into something muffled and close. Then, just as suddenly, it will *uncondense*—not dissipate or blow away, but reverse its formation, thinning from the center outward until the air is knife-clear again. Rain sometimes falls, but each drop is individual, separated from its fellows by yards of dry air, and they fall at different speeds, some lazy, some quick, as if each droplet is subject to its own private gravity.

The human marks are old but not ancient. Centuries, perhaps, not millennia. The tool marks on the fitted stones show bronze or soft iron—metal in its early relationship with human hands. Whoever shaped these blocks and hauled them to these impossible heights had only simple technology: hammers, chisels, levers, rope, determination. But they clearly understood something about this place that required recording. The stone circles aren't decorative. The cairns aren't random. The small cells built into the cliff faces have their doorways oriented to align with other doorways on other peaks, creating sightlines across empty air.

Inside some cells, shallow basins are carved into the floor where the strange water collects and won't evaporate. You can look into these basins and see reflections, but they show the sky at different times—dawn in one, noon in another, night in a third, all simultaneously present under the actual afternoon sun. Some basins show stars in constellations that don't match the night sky of this latitude. Some show landscapes that might be distant parts of this same mountain range, or might be somewhere else entirely.

The scale is regional. This isn't a single peak or a small outcrop—it's a whole range, extending across many miles, peak after peak visible in the distance, each with its own character but all participating in the same impossible physics. From the highest point you can see dozens of other peaks, and on many of them the small stone structures are visible, and the singing of those distant peaks reaches you as a faint harmony underlying the louder voices of the nearby stone.

The erosion patterns are mature, established. These mountains have stood long enough to find their balance with whatever weathers them. The basalt columns have been fluted into their current forms by patient ages of whatever passes for normal atmospheric action here, plus the non-normal action of the sideways water and the periodic reality fluctuations. But the weathering has reached a steady state—the mountains aren't crumbling, aren't young and jagged, aren't being actively torn down. They've achieved their form and maintain it, or cycle through variations of it, in something like equilibrium.

Occasionally, when the light is at certain angles and the singing reaches certain harmonic resonances, you can see deeper into the reality breakdown. The air itself becomes visible as a structure—not a gas but a lattice, a crystalline architecture of forces and relationships. For brief moments you can perceive that space isn't empty, isn't continuous, but is made of discrete points held in relationship by rules that only mostly apply. The water doesn't flow through space—it steps from point to point, appearing and disappearing, creating the illusion of flow through rapid sequential existence. The sound doesn't travel—it manifests simultaneously at multiple locations, creating the illusion of propagation.

During these moments of clarity-that's-actually-confusion, you understand why the ancient builders came here. They were trying to see this. Trying to document it. The stone circles are measurement tools. The aligned doorways are for observing the transitions. The basins are for capturing the reflections when reality is thin. They built with simple tools because simple tools were what they had, but they built with sophisticated purpose, trying to make permanent marks in a place where permanence itself was negotiable.

At night, the wrongness intensifies but also clarifies. The bright sun that causes so much refraction and reflection is gone, and the stars come out, and they're wrong too. They're too bright, first of all—no atmosphere to dim them at this height. But they also move wrong, tracing paths that curve when they should be straight, or holding still when they should be moving. Some stars appear in front of the peaks rather than behind them, as if the distance to the stars is somehow less than the distance to the next mountain over.

The water glows faintly in darkness. Not bioluminescence—there's no biology here—but some other luminescence, perhaps piezoelectric response to the constant vibration of the singing stone, perhaps light leaking through from the other versions of this place that exist in the reflections. The glow is blue-white and cold, and it traces the upward-crawling streams, the horizontal rivers, the hanging spheres and impossible arcs. In darkness, the water reveals its true nature as something that's only partially here, something that extends through more dimensions than the visible three.

The singing changes character at night. The higher harmonics drop away, leaving deeper tones, slower pulses. The mountains breathe bass notes that you feel more than hear, that synchronize with your heartbeat until you can't tell if the mountains are matching you or you're matching them. Some nights, the resonance is such that you can hear crystal growing—a high, pure note that builds infinitesimally as mineral accumulates atom by atom in the cracks. Some nights, you hear what might be voices, but they're speaking in a language of pure tone, no consonants, just vowels that shift through harmonic series.

The stone cells offer shelter but not comfort. Inside, the sound is different—focused, specific, as if each cell is tuned to amplify certain frequencies. Some cells are unbearable to occupy because the resonance they emphasize triggers nausea, disorientation, the sensation of falling in multiple directions simultaneously. Others create pockets of relative quiet, though "quiet" is relative when the mountains themselves are singing. The cells with the carved basins seem to be observatories, watching-places. You sit beside the basin and wait for the water to show you something. Sometimes it's just water. Sometimes it's a window.

Morning comes with mist that the rising sun ignites into gold. The droplets suspended in the air catch the light and scatter it, creating spaces where the atmosphere itself is luminous, glowing. The singing shifts to morning harmonics—brighter tones, quicker tempos. The reality breakdown momentarily stabilizes, or perhaps just becomes more consistent in its inconsistency. This is the best time to move between peaks, when the paths are most likely to remain paths, when handholds stay solid, when the bridges of water hold their forms.

But even in morning stability, you cannot trust your senses completely. Distance remains negotiable. Time pools in unexpected places. You'll climb for what feels like an hour and discover you've arrived somewhere that should have taken four hours to reach. Or the opposite: an hour's climb to a visible peak somehow consumes the entire day. The mountains exist in their own temporal regime, only loosely coupled to the clock time that governs the lowlands.

The density of the place—the sheer number of phenomena occurring in any given volume—means constant distraction, constant wonder, constant low-level disorientation. You cannot take anything for granted. The rock beneath your feet is probably solid. The water flowing sideways is definitely wet. The sound you're hearing is almost certainly coming from the direction you think it is. The structure you see on the next peak is very likely real. Probably. Almost certainly. The margins of uncertainty add up, compound, create a pervasive sense that you're navigating a place where the rules are suggestions rather than laws.

And yet, for all the breakdown, there's order here. The singing isn't chaos—it's complex harmony. The water doesn't flow randomly—it follows invisible channels that are consistent, that can be learned and predicted. The crystal formations appear in patterns. The reality fluctuations cycle. The ancient builders clearly developed an understanding of the patterns, built their structures according to principles that engaged with the wrongness rather than fighting it.

What this place was to them—temple, observatory, workshop, prison, refuge—isn't clear. Maybe all of those. The stone circles measure something. The aligned doorways observe something. The basins capture something. Whether they succeeded in their purpose, whether they found what they sought or created what they needed, the structures don't say. They just remain, scattered across the peaks, simple in construction but complex in implication, permanent marks in an impermanent reality.

The place persists. The mountains keep singing. The water keeps climbing. The light keeps bending. The ancient stones hold their positions. And the whole range continues its long conversation with itself, harmonizing, resonating, ringing out across the distances, loud and bright and wrong and exactly itself—an established aberration, a stable impossibility, a place where reality is thin enough to see through if you know where to look, and patient enough to wait.
