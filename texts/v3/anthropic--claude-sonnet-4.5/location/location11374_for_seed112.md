# The Screaming Continent

The bamboo stretches forever.

Not bamboo exactly—something like bamboo, but adapted to a world where water exists only as atmospheric vapor, drawn from deep mineral aquifers and exhaled immediately into air. The culms rise three hundred meters tall, hollow as industrial chimneys, their walls thick as fortress stone. They cover a continent the size of Asia, a single interconnected organism or ecosystem—the distinction has ceased to matter here. The ground you stand on isn't ground. It's compressed plant matter, layers of fallen culms pressed into something between wood and peat and coal, springy underfoot, a platform that groans with every step.

The light is ferocious. This world orbits close to its star, and the atmosphere is thin at these elevations. The plateau sits at four thousand meters equivalent, though plateau is the wrong word for something that extends beyond all horizons, a high desert that happens to be made entirely of plant. The sky burns white-blue, and the culms catch the light and scatter it downward through their translucent walls so that even the shadows glow green-gold. Your eyes water constantly. There is no shade that offers relief, only variations in brightness.

The sound arrives first as pressure, then as pain.

The culms are hollow tubes, organ pipes two hundred meters tall, and the wind that crosses this continent never stops. The acoustic environment is catastrophic. Each culm resonates at a slightly different frequency depending on height, diameter, the state of the internal baffles it has grown. The combined effect is a chord that contains thousands of notes, a drone that presses against your chest and skull, that you feel in your teeth and in the bones behind your eyes. It fluctuates constantly—as culms fall and new ones rise, as wind speed changes, as the vast plant mass breathes. Sometimes harmonics align and the sound becomes almost musical, a chord so vast and sustained it feels like the voice of the world itself. Then it shifts and becomes noise again, static rendered as physical force.

You learn not to speak. Nothing can be heard anyway.

Between the culms: space, emptiness, the critical resource. They grow scattered—ten meters between trunks, twenty, sometimes fifty where the substrate is poor. This spacing is maintained with something like intelligence, though whether it's individual competition or collective optimization, no one knows. Where culms fall, clearings open for years before juvenile shoots claim the light. The density is low enough that you can see for hundreds of meters in any direction, a cathedral forest of vertical pillars receding into bright haze. Low enough that the vast herds can move.

They are ungulates, more or less. Creatures the size of elk but taller, built for reaching. Their necks extend three meters, four, jointed in ways that suggest evolution under different constraints than Earth's. They browse the culms at height, stripping away the soft outer rind where the plant concentrates its volatile organics—its water substitute, its nutrient transport, call it what you will. Hundreds of them visible at any moment, scattered among the culms like dark punctuation marks. Thousands must be present in any square kilometer. They move constantly, following the wind, following gradients of plant chemistry you cannot detect.

The ecology is entirely vertical. The culms photosynthesize along their full height, but their richest production zones are at fifty meters, eighty meters, heights that require specialized bodies to access. There are things like sloths that climb the culms—slow-moving, adapted to the thin air of the upper reaches, their fur pale against the translucent green of the plant walls. Things like birds, though they may not be birds, their wings modified for this bright, thin atmosphere, for wind that never stops. They nest in the nodes where the culms branch occasionally into subsidiary shoots. Their calls are lost in the larger sound, though sometimes you catch movement overhead, shadows crossing the light inside a culm wall.

At ground level: insects. Swarms of them in the rare still pockets between culms where the wind drops. They work the fallen matter, the shed skin of culms, the dung of the herds. Beetles the size of your fist, their carapaces mirror-bright to shed the brutal light. Smaller things you cannot identify, moving in collective masses that flow like liquid across the compressed plant floor. They make their own sound, a high chittering that exists in the frequencies above the culm-drone, audible only when you press close to the ground, when your head is below the wind line.

The industrial presence is scattered, almost apologetic, but unmistakable.

Every thirty kilometers or so: a processing station. They are small, designed for minimal footprint—the plant cannot be cleared easily, and trying to do so causes cascade failures in the surrounding ecology. The stations are built up rather than out, skeletal towers of steel cable and platform that rise through the culms like alien insertions. They process the ungulate herds, which are semi-managed, semi-wild. Harvest operations happen in rhythms dictated more by animal migration than human scheduling. The stations are connected by narrow-gauge railways that snake between the culms on elevated tracks, the rails supported by pilings driven deep into the compressed plant floor.

A train passes—you feel it before you see it, a change in the vibration pattern traveling through the ground-plant. Then it's visible between the trunks: a small electric locomotive pulling a string of open cars, autonomous, moving at walking speed to avoid the herds. The rails hum, adding their frequency to the general acoustic chaos. The trains run constantly, threading the continent, carrying processed biomass toward the coast, toward settlements you cannot see from here, that might be a thousand kilometers away.

Sometimes you find other traces: a survey marker, a cable run, a cistern for collecting the condensed volatiles that drip from culm surfaces at night. These structures are built to weather the sound and the light and the strange half-dry chemistry of this place. Metal that can handle constant vibration. Seals that don't need water-based lubricants. Solar panels coated to shed the pollen-analogues that drift everywhere like fine green dust.

The weather happens at multiple scales simultaneously.

Locally, turbulence. The culms create their own wind patterns, channeling the prevailing airflow into swirls and eddies. Dust devils made of pollen spin between the trunks, golden in the hard light. Convection currents rise from the plant mass itself—it generates heat through its metabolic processes, enough heat that the air above is always warmer than ambient. These thermals feed back into the wind patterns, creating instability, pockets where the air suddenly drops or rises, where the culm-song shifts register.

At larger scale: true weather. This continent is vast enough to generate circulation patterns, pressure systems that travel across it over days and weeks. The sound changes as a front approaches—the drone deepens, becomes more complex, new harmonics emerge. The light changes too; cloud systems form, though clouds here are strange, laden with plant volatiles, greenish when backlit, casting tinted shadows. Rain is rare—most water is cycled through the plant mass itself, drawn up from deep aquifers and released as vapor directly. But sometimes storms arrive from the coast, bringing moisture that feels alien here, that makes the culms glisten and the compressed plant floor release strange fragrances.

The herds respond to weather changes before human instruments detect them. They begin to move, slowly at first, then with increasing purpose, hundreds of animals drifting in the same direction through the culm forest. Following them, you understand this landscape by their knowledge, reading their collective response to stimuli you cannot sense.

There are predators, though they are scarce enough that seeing one feels like witness to rarity.

They are felids, low-slung and long, built for moving through the culm spacing, for bursts of speed across the springy plant floor. Their coats are mottled white and gold—camouflage not for hiding but for breaking up their outline in the vertical confusion of culm trunks and hard light. They hunt at the edges of herds, patient for days, then explosive. When a kill happens, the other animals barely react—they drift away slowly, maintaining the spacing that keeps the herd coherent. The predators feed quickly, intensely, conscious of the insects that will arrive, of the scavengers that appear from nowhere when blood touches the ground.

There are also things in the upper reaches, glimpsed but never clearly seen. Shapes that might be very large birds or something else, sailing between culm tops where the wind is strongest. Occasionally one descends, drops through the canopy levels on still wings, snatches something—one of the climbing creatures or a bird-analogue—and rises again. They are pale, almost translucent, adapted to the brightness in ways that make them hard to track even when directly visible.

The culms themselves are the architecture, the infrastructure, the dominant fact.

Their growth follows patterns that seem almost algorithmic. Where one falls, creating a clearing, new shoots emerge within days—dozens of them, competing for the light. Most die back. A few persist, growing at speeds that seem impossible, racing toward the upper atmosphere. They can add ten meters of height in a season, their growth fueled by the deep aquifers and the intense photosynthesis their translucent walls permit. The juvenile culms have different acoustic properties than the mature ones—higher pitched, more variable. A clearing full of new growth sounds different from the established forest, adds a brighter register to the drone.

The culms fall constantly. This is part of the turbulence, part of what makes the landscape feel so active. On average, one within earshot falls every few hours. Sometimes it's age—a culm reaching the end of its centuries-long lifespan, its walls finally weakening. Sometimes it's wind—a gust that finds resonance with a culm's natural frequency, setting up vibrations that tear it apart from within. Sometimes it's the herds—animals rubbing against a weakened trunk, removing the critical last bit of structural integrity.

When a culm falls, the sound is extraordinary. First a crack that cuts through even the general drone, sharp and almost crystalline. Then a groaning, creaking descent—these things fall slowly, their hollow structure catching air, their length requiring long seconds to traverse the distance from top to ground. They often strike other culms on the way down, creating cascades of sound, breaking into sections that tumble and bounce. The impact shakes the ground-plant for hundreds of meters. Insects swarm the fallen sections immediately. The herds move away, then return, investigating the newly accessible soft plant matter.

At night, the brightness barely diminishes.

This world has a moon—a large one, close, that reflects the star's light back down even when the star itself has set. The culms glow faintly, bioluminescence or stored photon re-emission or something else, a green-blue light that suffuses the spaces between trunks. The sound changes at night—different wind patterns, different temperature gradients, different harmonics in the culm-song. It becomes more organized somehow, more rhythmic, as if the plant mass is breathing in unison.

The herds are more active at night, browsing in the cooler air, though cool here means merely uncomfortable rather than dangerous. The temperature drops to something survivable without constant hydration, without the salt-supplementation that daytime travel requires. You can move more freely, though the brightness of the moon on the culms creates confusing shadows, pillars of light and dark that shift as you walk, that make depth perception unreliable.

The predators hunt at night. Their eyes reflect the moon-glow, visible at distance as paired points of cold fire between the culm trunks. They move in the acoustic shadows, the rare spots where wind patterns create relative quiet, where their approach won't be masked by the general noise. The herds understand this—they avoid those spaces, cluster in the louder zones where threat is audible.

The scale reveals itself slowly, through accumulation of distances that don't resolve.

Walk for a day: the landscape looks identical. Walk for a week: still identical, though the processing stations cycle past at regular intervals, the railways run their constant paths. Walk for a month: you might notice changes in culm density, average height, color variations in the compressed plant floor that suggest different mineral compositions in the deep aquifers. Walk for a season: weather patterns become familiar, the great circulation systems that sweep across the continent, the migration routes of the herds.

Maps exist, but they are strange—they show topology of sound rather than elevation, density gradients, the locations of the processing network. They show the seasonal patterns of culm-fall, regions where the plant is older and fall risk is higher, regions of new growth. They show herd migration routes plotted over decades. They do not show landmarks in any conventional sense, because landmarks don't exist. There are no mountains, no rivers, no coastlines visible from within the interior. Just culms, extending forever in all directions.

People say you can walk a straight line for a thousand kilometers and never see a significant change in the landscape. This is approximately true. The continent is uniformly high, uniformly planted, uniformly drained of surface water. What changes is subtle—wind patterns, acoustic variations, animal behaviors, the slow cycling of the processing infrastructure at its perimeter.

The ungulates have their own relationship with the industrial elements.

They've learned the railways are boundaries, not to be crossed when a train is approaching. They've learned the processing stations mean danger, but also opportunity—the stations leak nutrients, leave accessible waste that the culms cannot immediately reclaim. Herds cluster near stations, then disperse, a rhythm that matches the harvest cycle. Young animals learn this from their elders, behavioral transmission that has happened over the few generations since human presence established itself here.

The animals are not domesticated. Cannot be domesticated—their ecology requires the vast space, the constant movement, the browsing of wild culms at height. But they are managed, loosely, through infrastructure placement and selective culling that keeps populations dense enough to be economically viable but sparse enough to avoid ecosystem collapse. The relationship is industrial but also symbiotic, parasitic but also sustainable. The exact balance is maintained by algorithms, by sensors scattered across the continent that track herd movements and plant vitality and ambient chemistry.

At the processing stations, workers live in small crews, rotations of weeks or months. The stations are loud beyond description—the normal culm-drone plus the sound of machinery, of animals being moved through the processing sequence, of the constant vibration of the railways. Workers wear acoustic dampening that reduces the sound to merely deafening. Communication happens through sign language and text displays. Some workers go deaf eventually, nerve damage from sustained exposure that even the dampening cannot prevent.

They speak of the landscape with a mixture of awe and weariness. The beauty of it—and it is beautiful, in its way—becomes oppressive through constancy. The sameness, the brightness, the noise that never stops. Some workers love it, claim they cannot sleep in quiet places anymore, need the culm-drone as a presence. Others finish their contracts and never return, carry tinnitus and light-sensitivity as permanent souvenirs.

The chemistry is strange, barely adequate for human needs.

The culms concentrate minerals in their tissues—silicates, sulfates, trace metals in configurations that Earth biochemistry doesn't produce. They generate volatiles that are almost but not quite water, organic compounds that evaporate readily, that fill the air with molecules that have faint anesthetic properties if inhaled continuously. Workers supplement with imported water, food that hasn't cycled through the local ecosystem, filters for the air inside station habitats.

The herds process this chemistry effectively, their metabolism adapted over evolutionary time to extract nutrients from the culms and neutralize the stranger compounds. Their meat is edible, more or less, though it tastes of things that have no names, carries flavors that human palates interpret as metal and grass and something like turpentine. It's nutritious, protein and fats in useful configurations, enough that the processing stations are economically viable despite the distances involved in getting product to markets.

Other uses for the plant mass: the culm walls can be processed into structural materials, lightweight and strong, useful for construction in low-gravity environments. The compressed plant floor, when harvested carefully, makes a fuel that burns slow and hot. The volatiles can be distilled into industrial solvents, into polymer precursors. None of this is high-value enough to justify the infrastructure on its own, but collectively, the extraction economy persists, marginal but functional.

The weirdness is subtle, present at the edges of perception.

Patterns in the culm spacing that seem too regular to be random, that suggest coordination mechanisms no one has identified. Acoustic phenomena that don't quite match predictions—zones where the sound becomes coherent in ways that shouldn't be possible from passive resonance, that hint at active modulation by the plant mass itself. Herd behaviors that seem to anticipate events before they occur, that suggest sensory modalities humans don't share.

Sometimes workers report visions—brief, fragmentary, usually attributed to the volatile chemistry and the constant noise and the brightness. Images of the landscape from above, as if seen from great height. Sensations of being distributed, of presence stretched across kilometers. These reports are not consistent enough to be taken seriously, but they persist, part of the local folklore.

Sometimes the culms grow in configurations that seem architectural—arches, parallel groves, circular clearings too perfect to be coincidence. These structures are transient, lasting only until the next fall disrupts them, but while they exist they invite interpretation. Natural process, or something else? The plant mass is old, has been cycling through growth and fall and regrowth for geological time. If it has learned anything, developed any collective properties, how would humans recognize it?

The animals know things. This becomes clear through observation, through the accumulation of small incidents. A herd that moves away from an area hours before a massive culm-fall occurs. Predators that avoid certain zones for no obvious reason. The climbing creatures that descend to low levels just before weather shifts that bring dangerous wind shear. They are reading the landscape in ways humans cannot, responding to signals in the sound or the chemistry or the vibration patterns.

Following the herds becomes a practice, a way of navigating. They know where the plant mass is richest, where the spacing is safest, where wind patterns create shelter. They know the rhythms of the place in a way that maps cannot capture. Some station workers become expert in herd behavior, claim they can predict culm-falls by watching animal movement, can sense approaching weather by the way ungulates orient their bodies.

The predators are more mysterious. They are solitary, territorial in ways that cover vast distances. One predator's range might encompass hundreds of square kilometers, overlapping loosely with others. They mark boundaries using scent glands that produce compounds lasting days in the dry air. They vocalize rarely, and when they do, the sound is lost in the culm-drone unless you are very close. Mostly they are silent, ghostly presences that appear and disappear between the vertical lines of the culms.

When you encounter a predator—happens rarely enough to be memorable every time—the usual pattern is mutual assessment. They have learned humans are not prey, not worth the energy expenditure. But they are curious, intelligent enough to recognize anomaly. They approach sometimes, close enough that you can see the patterns in their coats, the pale gold eyes, the way they move with absolute confidence across the springy plant floor. Then they lose interest, drift away into the culm forest, become shadow and light and absence.

Time behaves strangely here.

With no daily rhythm besides the marginal difference between star-light and moon-light, with the landscape unchanging, with the sound constant, markers of duration become internal rather than external. You measure time by train passages, by herd movements, by the cycles of fatigue and hunger. Days blur together. Weeks become indistinct. Only the larger rhythms matter—the weather cycles, the seasons that bring subtle changes in plant chemistry, the migrations that indicate planetary orbital position.

Workers at the stations track time by schedule, by the artificial markers of shift rotations and supply deliveries. But even they admit that leaving the landscape feels like waking from something, like returning to a world where time has structure and meaning. Inside the continent, there is only the eternal present, the constant turbulence, the sound that never stops.

Walking alone here—rare, usually done by researchers or the occasional lost worker—produces distinctive psychological effects. The constancy becomes meditative or maddening depending on temperament. Some people report clarity, a stripping away of mental noise that matches the physical environment's reduction to essentials. Others report anxiety that builds over hours or days, a need for variation that the landscape refuses to provide.

The brightness is perhaps the hardest element. Human eyes did not evolve for this much light sustained over this duration. Even with protective gear, with filters and frequent breaks in the relative darkness of station interiors, damage accumulates. Long-term workers develop distinctive eye conditions—bleached retinas, persistent photosensitivity. Some claim to see better eventually, to adapt, though this might be rationalization, might be the brain compensating for reduced input by amplifying what remains.

At the margins, where the continent finally ends, the landscape changes at last.

The culms thin out gradually over hundreds of kilometers, their density decreasing, their spacing widening, until finally you reach areas where plant and void balance equally. Here the wind is different—no longer channeled through culm forests, it moves freely, creates different acoustic patterns. The sound decreases, almost shocking after the interior drone. You can hear your own footsteps, your own breathing.

Beyond this transition zone: actual desert, or coastline, or whatever geography this planet possesses outside the plant continent. These regions are barely explored, considered less interesting than the vast interior, less economically viable. The processing network terminates at the margins. Railways run to port facilities on the coasts, where product is loaded onto ships or aircraft for transport to settlements elsewhere.

The animals mostly don't leave the culm continent. Their ecology is too specialized, too dependent on the specific chemistry and structure the culms provide. At the margins you see them standing, looking outward, then turning back into the forest. The continent is their world, entire and sufficient.

Only the flying creatures range beyond—the pale gliders, the bird-analogues. They are seen sometimes over the deserts, over the oceans, traveling distances that suggest migration patterns between continents or hemispheres. Where they go, what other ecosystems they connect to, remains mostly unknown. They return to the culms to nest, to feed on the ecology the plant mass supports, but their lives extend beyond this single landscape.

The processing infrastructure respects the margins carefully. There's an understanding, maybe not formally articulated but present in practice, that the continent functions as a system, that pushing industry too deep or too aggressively risks cascade failures. The railways stop where they stop. The processing stations maintain their spacing. Harvest rates are limited by algorithm, by models that attempt to predict ecosystem response, by the simple fact that the herds cannot be pressed beyond certain density thresholds without collapse.

This restraint is unusual, possibly unprecedented in industrial contexts. It arises partly from pragmatism—destroy the system and the industry dies—but partly from something else. A recognition, maybe, of the landscape's scale, its otherness, its successful operation long before human presence. A sense that this place has its own logic, its own balance, and human participation is contingent on accepting those terms.

At night, under the bright moon, walking between culms with the herd animals scattered dark against the glowing plant masses, the herds browsing at their impossible heights, the sound washing through everything, the air tasting of chemicals that have no names—at those moments, the place reveals itself most clearly.

It is not hostile. Not welcoming either. It simply is, vast and self-contained and indifferent to interpretation. The turbulence, the constant change, the falling culms and rising shoots and migrating herds—all of it is the landscape being itself, persisting through its own strange chemistry, its own acoustic physics, its own time scale.

You are small here. Human presence is small. The stations and railways, the whole industrial apparatus, are marginal annotations on something that extends beyond horizons, that functions according to rules older and stranger than human understanding. You can participate, carefully, extracting what you need, but you cannot change it, cannot make it other than what it is.

The sound continues. Will continue, long after the last train runs, after the last station is abandoned, after human presence retreats or ends. The culms will still fall, still rise. The herds will still migrate. The wind will still blow across a continent of hollow towers, making them sing their endless, deafening, magnificent song.

You listen. You walk. You exist temporarily in this bright, loud, strange vastness. Eventually you leave, or you don't. Either way, the landscape persists, indifferent, turbulent, alive.
