# The Breathing Plateau

The plateau extends beyond any horizon you might seek—not infinite, but vast enough that its distant edges curve with the planet itself, vast enough that clouds form in its interior spaces, vast enough that standing at what might be its center, you cannot tell you are on a plateau at all. Only from its rare edges, where the land finally falls away into white voids of mist, can you perceive that this near-flat expanse of green hangs suspended at altitude, an island continent in the sky.

It is old. Not old like mountains waiting to erode, but old like erosion has finished its conversation with stone and reached a final, peaceful agreement. The rock beneath—and there is rock, somewhere far beneath—has been worn to a geometry of ultimate patience. What hills remain rise in curves so gentle they feel like swells on a breathing body. What valleys exist are shallow enough to wade across without noticing the descent. Eons of wind, water, and root have reduced this place to its terminal topography, the shape that no longer argues with time.

But the plateau is not rock now, not in any way that matters to the eye or foot. It is plant. Completely plant.

The surface is a mat of vegetation so dense, so uninterrupted, that it forms a new kind of ground. You walk not on soil but on life itself—a woven carpet of mosses, lichens, root tangles, and low-growing groundcover that has merged into a single breathing membrane. In places this living floor is firm enough to seem solid; in others it yields like dense foam, and your foot sinks an inch or two into cool, damp softness before finding resistance. The sensation is of walking on the pelt of some vast creature.

There is no bare earth. None. The plants have claimed every particle of substrate, every pebble, every fragment of mineral. Where a stone might once have protruded, it is now a moss-covered swell. Where ancient bedrock might show through, it wears a pelt of lichen so thick the rock itself becomes uncertain—a memory beneath a green skin.

The mat rises in places. Not suddenly—nothing here is sudden—but in long, patient elevations. What might be called "hummocks" if they were smaller, but these are the size of houses, of hills themselves, great mounded accumulations of peat and living matter that have built up over timeframes that make forests seem like brief experiments. They rise ten, twenty, sometimes forty feet above the general plain, their sides sloped and soft, covered in the same ubiquitous greenery, sometimes topped with clusters of different vegetation—slightly taller, slightly more ambitious, but still low, still part of the endless mat.

Between these swells lie the shallows. Not valleys—too gentle for that word—but subtle depressions where water accumulates. And there is always water.

The plateau is soaked. Not flooded, not drowned, but permeated with moisture like a sponge that has reached its perfect saturation. Mist condenses from the air constantly in the dim light. The clouds that form in the plateau's own microclimate drift low, sometimes touching the surface, releasing their moisture in whispers rather than rain. The living mat weeps. Water beads on every surface, drips from the curled edges of leaves, gathers in tiny pools that reflect the gray-white sky. In the shallow places between hummocks, water stands in sheets an inch or two deep, warm, tea-colored from tannins, absolutely still.

This standing water never seems to drain, but neither does it spread. The plateau has reached some hydrological equilibrium—every drop that condenses finds a place, and every place holds exactly the water it can hold. You can stand at the edge of these shallow pools and see where the water simply... stops. Not at a bank, exactly, but at a threshold where the living mat transitions from aquatic to terrestrial by the smallest margin of moisture, by fractions of an inch in height.

The vegetation changes with these minute variations in water level, but only if you study it carefully. Near the pools: plants with rounder leaves, deeper green, surfaces that shed water. Slightly higher: finer textures, more intricate branching. Higher still, on the crowns of the great hummocks: vegetation that traps moisture in tiny cups and chambers, that grows in rosettes and spirals designed to harvest the condensing air. But it all remains low. The tallest plants on the plateau might reach your waist—more commonly your knee, most commonly your ankle.

Except for the towers.

They are scattered across the plateau's expanse at intervals that seem almost regular but aren't quite. Visible from great distances as vertical marks against the horizontal world. Not trees—something stranger. 

They are plants, certainly. They grow. But they grow in ways that seem architectural, as if remembering or anticipating some other form. Rising fifteen, twenty, thirty feet into the air, they consist of hollow vertical shafts—cylinders of woody growth that spiral slowly as they rise, wound from countless vine-like stems that have fused into singular trunks. The shafts are irregular, their surfaces rippled and buttressed, covered in the same mosses and lichens that coat everything else, but their basic form is unmistakable: vertical hollow tubes, usually four to eight feet in diameter, rising from the mat like wells drilled upward into the sky.

Some stand alone. Others cluster in groups of three or five, their bases emerging from shared mounds, their tops at slightly different heights. A few have partially collapsed, their upper sections bent or broken, lying at angles against neighboring towers or settling slowly back into the mat from which they grew. These ruins are ancient—the breaks themselves softened by decades or centuries of new growth, moss filling the cracks, small plants colonizing the interior spaces.

The towers make sounds.

In the mostly-quiet soundscape of the plateau, the towers sing—if singing can be done without intention. Wind enters the open tops and finds the irregular interior chambers, the spiral grain of the woody walls, the various holes and splits that have formed over time. The result is not quite a whistle, not quite a hum—something between breath and tone, rising and falling as the wind shifts. Each tower has its own voice, determined by its unique dimensions and damages. In areas where several stand together, they produce chords that drift across the plateau, low and distant and strangely peaceful.

Inside the towers, if you were to enter one through a split in its side or a collapsed section, you would find the walls lined with a different ecology. The interior surfaces host plants that grow nowhere else—pale, almost colorless species that seem to exist on the edge of being plant at all, feeding on the decay of the tower's own inner wood, on the particular moisture and dim light of these enclosed spaces. Some grow in shelf-like arrangements, others in hanging curtains. Small pools often collect at the base of intact towers, warm and still, tea-dark, sometimes a few inches deep, sometimes several feet.

Between the towers, across the endless mat, other structures punctuate the green.

They are easy to miss. They are infrequent. They are quiet in their presence, as if trying not to interrupt. But they are undeniably built.

Low walls, rarely more than knee-high, running in straight lines or gentle curves for ten or twenty or fifty feet before disappearing back into the mat. Made of stone—actual stone, quarried or gathered from some unimaginable depth below—but so covered in growth that they appear as green ridges, linear thickenings in the vegetation. You discover them by walking into them, by the sudden resistance underfoot, by tracing the line and finding it too straight to be natural.

Platforms, level and rectangular, raised just a foot or so above the general surface. Perhaps four paces on a side, perhaps ten. The vegetation grows differently on them—drier species, tighter patterns—revealing the artificial drainage, the planned substrate. They are never quite level anymore, tilted by millennia of settling, but their intended horizontality remains legible.

Circles of standing stones, waist-high, arranged with obvious intent but no obvious purpose. Five stones, or seven, or eleven, never an even number, spaced evenly around a center point, leaning slightly inward or outward, their tops worn smooth and round, their bases invisible beneath moss and root.

And very occasionally—so rare you might walk for days between them—buildings. Or what remains of buildings. Single structures, always isolated, never clustered into anything resembling a settlement. They are low, one story at most, and small, no larger than a modest room. Their walls are stone, thick and well-fitted, though the mortar (if there was mortar) is long gone, replaced by moss that binds the stones more thoroughly than any cement. The roofs have always collapsed—fallen inward or dissolved into the mat centuries ago. The interiors are filled with the same vegetation as surrounds them, sometimes with pools of standing water, sometimes with particularly lush growths of certain plants, as if the enriched soil or altered drainage creates microclimates suited to specific species.

These structures have no ornamentation. No carvings, no marks, no indication of their builders or purpose. They are almost aggressively plain, as if plainness were itself a kind of statement. And they are scattered with a randomness that suggests they were never part of a unified plan, but rather individual decisions made across vast stretches of time and space by people—if they were people—who lived at tremendous isolation from one another.

No tools. No artifacts. Nothing that isn't stone or plant. If the builders used metal or ceramic or fabric, none of it has survived, or none was ever brought here, or none existed in their world. The structures themselves are the only evidence, and they tell almost nothing.

The light is dim. Not dark—you can see clearly, can navigate, can read the subtle textures of the vegetation—but muted, diffuse, shadowless. The sky is a blanket of white-gray cloud that seems permanent, or at least permanent enough that "day" is simply "the time when it's bright enough to see" and "night" is "the time when it isn't." The clouds move, but slowly, their patterns shifting over hours. Sometimes they thin enough to suggest a sun somewhere above, a slightly brighter patch in the white, but direct light never penetrates. The illumination feels sourceless, as if the air itself were luminous.

This dim light gives the plateau its characteristic color palette: greens that lean toward gray, blacks that are actually deep purples, whites that are touched with cream or yellow. The shallow pools reflect the sky and become mirrors of silver-gray. The wet surfaces of leaves gleam dully. The moss-covered stones appear in shades of olive, sage, lichen-green, moss-green, depths of viridian where moisture accumulates.

And everywhere, constantly, the sense of wetness. Not uncomfortable—the temperature is warm enough that dampness feels neutral rather than cold—but inescapable. Your hands grow damp just from the air. Your hair grows heavy with moisture. The fabric of your clothes (if you have clothes) gradually soaks through, not from rain or splashing, but from simply existing in this atmosphere. The air tastes of water, of green things, of gentle rot and active growth mixed together.

The fauna moves through this landscape like rumors.

You hear them more often than you see them: small splashes in the pools, rustlings in the dense vegetation, soft calls that might be birds or might be something else. When you do see animals, they are usually at a distance, and usually partially obscured by mist or the texture of the mat itself.

Creatures that might be birds, but low-flying, following the contours of the surface, rarely rising higher than the tops of the towers. Their colors blend with the landscape—grays, greens, muted browns. They move between the hummocks, landing to feed on things invisible to your eye, taking off again with wet, heavy wing-beats that sound like cloth flapping.

Larger animals, quadrupedal, about the size of sheep or deer, but thickly built and moving slowly. Their fur or hide—it's hard to tell which—is coated in moisture, sometimes with moss or small plants actually growing on their backs, as if they move too slowly and stand too still for the plateau's vegetation to distinguish them from substrate. They graze on the mat itself, or wade into the shallow pools to feed on aquatic plants, their movements deliberate and economical.

Smaller things, glimpsed rarely: amphibian forms moving between water and land, their skin slick and patterned to disappear against wet moss; something insect-like but larger than any insect, with too many legs, climbing the rough surface of a tower; prints in the soft mat that might have been made by something heavy and reptilian, filling slowly with water.

Nothing seems predatory. Or perhaps everything is predatory and prey at once, but slowly, patiently, in ways that don't involve pursuit or violence. The abundance of fauna is less about quantity—you might see a dozen animals in a day's walk—and more about presence, the constant sense that the landscape is occupied, that you are sharing it, that eyes are watching from within the green.

And there is the plateau's breath.

It takes hours to notice. Days, perhaps. But eventually you perceive it: the entire landscape rises and falls. Not metaphorically. Not imagined. The living mat itself swells and subsides in a rhythm too slow for immediate perception but undeniable over time. A cycle that takes perhaps four hours, perhaps six—it's hard to measure without instruments, and the cycle seems to vary slightly, as if the plateau breathes not by mechanical timing but by some organic need.

During the expansion phase, the mat thickens. The hummocks rise fractionally higher. The spaces between them narrow. The pools shrink as the vegetation swells with absorbed moisture. If you were to stand perfectly still on a high point, you might feel the elevation increase by an inch, two inches, enough to perceive if you're paying attention.

Then the contraction. The mat settles. Compresses. Releases water back into the pools, which spread slightly wider. The overall elevation drops. The landscape exhales.

This breathing creates the plateau's own weather. During expansion, as millions of square miles of vegetation release moisture through their pores, the air becomes more saturated, mist thickens, clouds descend. During contraction, as the mat draws moisture back in, the air clears slightly, the clouds lift a bit, visibility extends. The cycle drives gentle winds—air moving toward the plateau during contraction, away during expansion—which in turn affects temperature by fractions of a degree, which affects which pools evaporate and which condense new water.

It is the rhythm of a living system so vast it has become its own climate, its own geology, its own physics.

You notice other strangeness gradually.

The pools, for instance. Some of them are warm—warmer than ambient temperature, warmer than body temperature. Not all. Perhaps one in twenty, one in thirty. They simply... generate heat somehow. Or concentrate it. Or draw it from some deep source. You can find them by touch, by passing your hand over the surface and feeling the warmth rising. They steam very slightly in the dimness, wreathing themselves in delicate vapor. If you were to wade into one—and they're shallow enough to wade, rarely deeper than mid-thigh—you'd find the warmth increases toward the center, as if something beneath produces heat. But there's nothing visible beneath. Just the usual mat, slightly thicker perhaps, darker, more dense.

Animals favor these warm pools. You find more tracks around them, more droppings, more signs of congregation. In rare cases you might see several animals sharing a warm pool, standing in the heated water, steam rising around them, utterly still except for the slow breathing of their sides.

And the vegetation itself exhibits peculiarities that edge toward the truly strange.

Certain plants—not rare, but not common—grow in perfect spirals. Not approximately spiral, but geometrically precise, each leaf or frond positioned according to mathematical rules, creating patterns that seem deliberately designed. Fibonacci sequences appear in the arrangement of scales on certain moss-like growths. Fractals repeat at different scales in the branching of some of the mat-forming plants.

Other species change color in response to... something. Not light—the light barely changes. Not temperature—the warmth is constant. But they change nonetheless, shifting from green to purple to green again over cycles that might be days or weeks. Walking through an area and returning a week later, you find entire sections of the mat have transformed, paisley patterns of color spreading across the monotone green.

There are also the nodules.

Scattered throughout the mat, sometimes visible on the surface, sometimes discovered only by touch, are spherical growths about the size of a fist or a head. Hard, unlike the soft vegetation around them, with a woody or fibrous shell. They seem to be plant structures—seed pods, perhaps, or storage organs—but they're unlike any other part of the ecosystem. If you were to break one open (they're difficult to break; the shell is remarkably strong), you'd find the interior filled with a clear, viscous fluid and what might be spores or seeds, tiny and numerous, suspended in the gel.

But the strangest thing about them: they're warm. Not like the warm pools—not heated from outside—but warm from within. Each one radiates a gentle heat, perceptible if you hold your hand near it. And very occasionally, perhaps once in every hundred or thousand, you find one that's luminous. A faint, green-white glow, barely visible in the dim daylight but clear at night, emanating from within the shell, pulsing very slowly—once every few minutes—as if something inside were alive and breathing.

The plateau has no predators that you can identify, but it has hazards of subtler kinds.

Certain areas of the mat are less stable than they appear. You step onto what looks like solid ground and find yourself sinking—not quickly, not into danger, but slowly, steadily, into a substrate that has less structure than the surrounding areas. These soft zones might be places where the underlying peat is still accumulating, still compressing, not yet solid. Or they might be areas undermined by water flowing somewhere beneath. You don't sink far—perhaps to your knees, perhaps your thighs—before finding resistance, but extracting yourself requires patience. The mat clings. It doesn't pull you down, but it doesn't easily release you either.

And there are the hollow spaces.

Occasionally you find places where the mat has formed a roof over emptiness. The surface looks no different from anywhere else, but when you step on it, it flexes like a drum head. Careful investigation reveals a hollow beneath—sometimes a few inches deep, sometimes several feet—where water or air or some unknown process has created a void. The mat above is strong enough to support weight, usually, but the sensation of walking on these areas is deeply unsettling, each step producing a bounce, a reverberation, sometimes a low resonance that spreads outward in a circle.

In one instance you find a sinkhole where such a hollow has collapsed. A circular depression perhaps twenty feet across, its edges ragged but already re-vegetating, the bottom lost in shadow and mist. You throw a stone in and hear it splash, far below—there's water down there, deep water, a hidden pool or channel in the body of the plateau. You don't investigate further.

The soundscape of the plateau is characterized by near-silence, but it's not empty.

The wind, when it moves, whispers through the low vegetation with a sound like slow breathing. The towers hum and moan. Water drips constantly—from leaves, from moss, from the edges of stones—creating a subtle percussion that's only noticeable when you stop moving. Splashes from unseen animals. The rare calls of the bird-like creatures, which sound oddly mechanical, like wooden rattles or bamboo chimes.

And beneath it all, or perhaps imagined, a low vibration. Not a sound you hear so much as feel—a subsonic presence that might be the breath of the plateau itself, the collective respiration of trillions of organisms, the movement of water through vast hidden channels, the settling and shifting of the peat beneath your feet. You feel it in your chest when you lie down on the mat. It's peaceful. It's the sound of something alive and unimaginably large and entirely indifferent to your presence.

At night—if night is the right word for the hours when the ambient light drops below useful thresholds—the plateau becomes another place without changing at all.

The darkness is not complete. The clouds seem to glow faintly with some retained light, creating a ceiling of deep gray rather than black. The occasional luminous nodules become visible, scattered points of green-white phosphorescence that define space like distant lanterns. The warm pools steam more visibly, wreathing themselves in vapor that glows with reflected skylight.

And sounds become clearer. The animals, more active in darkness, move and call more freely. You hear splashing, rustling, sounds that might be mating or feeding or simply motion. The wind seems to pick up slightly, and the tower-song becomes more complex, harmonies and dissonances drifting across the vastness.

If you were to walk at night—and you could; the mat is no more or less stable than during the day—you'd navigate by these faint lights and sounds, by the slightly brighter glow of the sky, by the warm spots that mark the heated pools. You'd walk slowly, testing each step, feeling the mat breathe beneath you, the immense organism on which you're traveling responding to the darkness with subtle changes in tension and texture.

You'd also see, very occasionally, larger lights. Not green like the nodules, but white or gold or pale blue. They appear at a distance, moving slowly or staying still, lasting anywhere from seconds to hours before fading. They're rare enough that you might walk for weeks without seeing one. But when they appear, they command attention—clear, steady lights in a dark landscape, sources unknown.

If you were to approach one (and you could try; nothing prevents you), you'd find the light receding as you walk, always maintaining its distance, or you'd find it stationary but growing fainter as you approach, until you arrive at the spot where it seemed to be and find only the usual mat, wet and dark and empty of explanation. Or perhaps—very rarely—you'd find something: a warm pool, slightly warmer than others, surrounded by vegetation that glows faintly with bioluminescence. But whether that was the light you saw, or whether the light came from something else that's since moved on, remains uncertain.

The plateau extends beyond navigation by walking. Its scale is such that familiar notions of journey break down.

You could walk in one direction for weeks and find the landscape essentially unchanged—same mat, same pools, same hummocks in their endless variation-within-sameness. The towers appear and disappear behind you. The occasional ruins offer no more information in one area than another. The animals remain the same species, behaving identically.

Only the weather shifts, because the plateau creates zones of microclimate from its own topography. In some regions, the mist is thicker, condensation more aggressive, pools more abundant. In others, the air is marginally drier, the mat firmer, the vegetation subtly different in species composition though not in overall appearance. These climatic zones shift over time—not quickly, but noticeably over days or weeks—as if the plateau's breath has regional variations, areas that inhale and exhale at different rhythms, creating drifting patterns of wet and dry that migrate across the surface like vast, slow storms.

And sometimes you climb. Though the plateau is flat in its ancient way, there are still the hummocks, still the gentle swells that rise tens of feet above the median elevation. From the top of a particularly large one, you can see farther—not much farther; the mist and dim light limit visibility—but far enough to perceive the curvature of the plateau's surface, the way it bends away at the edges of sight, confirming its immensity.

From these high points, you can sometimes see the edges. Not often—the edges are rare, the plateau enormous—but if you happen to be near one and climb high enough, you might see where the land ends and the white void begins. The edges are cliffs, not dramatic but definite, where the mat and the substrate beneath it terminate in a clean drop. The cliffs might be a hundred feet high, might be a thousand—it's impossible to tell through the mist. Below them: nothing visible. Just white. An ocean of cloud through which, very occasionally, you glimpse something darker—another plateau? lower ground? ocean? The glimpses are too brief and unclear to resolve.

Birds—or the bird-like creatures—sometimes fly out over these edges and disappear into the white. Sometimes they return. Sometimes they don't. You never see anything else emerge from the voids between plateaus.

The ruins, examined more carefully, reveal small additional details.

The stone circles, for instance: their interior spaces often host plants that grow nowhere else nearby, as if the stones create a microclimate or alter the soil chemistry in ways that favor specific species. These interior gardens are miniature explosions of diversity in an otherwise relatively homogenous plant community.

The low walls sometimes align with each other across vast distances. You might find a wall running northeast-southwest, and days later, miles away, find another wall running on precisely the same bearing. Whether this is intentional—some ancient survey system, some continental geometry—or coincidence is impossible to determine.

The collapsed buildings, when you examine their interiors, occasionally contain arrangements of stones that seem too regular to be rubble. Circles of small stones, or lines, or patterns that might be symbolic or might be purely decorative or might be functional in ways no longer parseable. They tell you that the builders had intentions beyond pure shelter, but what those intentions were remains silent.

And very rarely—perhaps once in a journey of months—you find a structure that's still intact. Or nearly intact. Roof gone, but walls standing, doorway empty but still framing a threshold. These complete ruins feel different. They have interiors, defined spaces, a sense of having been rooms where someone lived or worked or performed some function. The air inside them feels different—stiller, warmer—even though they're open to the sky. The vegetation inside grows differently, often more lushly, fed by the enriched soil of the structure's slow decay.

If you were to stay in one of these structures—and you could; they offer shelter from wind if not from moisture—you might begin to notice patterns. The way light enters at certain times. The way water flows along certain paths. The way certain sounds resonate differently inside than outside. These patterns might be intentional design, or might be accidents of decay, or might be something in between—the builders working with the plateau's natural tendencies rather than against them, creating structures that collaborate with wind and water and growth.

You begin to suspect that the builders understood the plateau as a living thing. That the structures were never meant to dominate or transform the landscape, but to mark it, to acknowledge it, to create points of reference in its vastness. And that the scattering of structures across such enormous distances wasn't isolation born of scarce resources or hostile environment, but a deliberate embrace of solitude, a culture (if it was a culture) that valued space and separation, that saw the plateau's immensity as a feature rather than an obstacle.

Or perhaps you're projecting. Perhaps the builders—if they were builders, if these structures weren't grown somehow, if they weren't natural formations that only look built—had entirely different motivations, incomprehensible to anyone who didn't share their phenomenology, their sensorium, their way of being in the world.

The plateau doesn't answer these questions. It just continues.

The mat breathes. The pools hold their water. The towers sing their slow, accidental songs. The animals move through the green, grazing and drinking and breeding and dying and becoming part of the mat themselves, their bodies broken down and incorporated into the vast living substrate, their patterns and behaviors continuing in their descendants, an endless cycling that has no beginning visible from within it.

And you walk. Or you could walk. Or someone walks, someone has walked, someone will walk. The plateau accommodates all walkers equally, offering the same wet green surface, the same dim light, the same breathing rhythm, the same profound and patient indifference.

Time moves differently here. Not faster or slower, but looser. Without strong day-night cycles, without seasonal changes, without landmarks that differ enough to mark progress, duration becomes uncertain. You might think you've been walking for a week and realize it's been a month. Or vice versa. The plateau's time is measured in the breath-cycles, in the slow migrations of climatic zones, in the growth of the mat—which is visible if you stay in one place long enough, watching a particular stone become slightly more covered, a particular pool shrink by fractions of an inch as vegetation encroaches.

And eventually—though eventually might be months or years—if you keep walking toward an edge, you reach it.

The cliff rises from the mat without preamble. One moment you're walking on the familiar surface, the next you're at a precipice. The edge itself is vegetated, of course—plants clinging to the vertical face, moss covering the stone, roots reaching into cracks. But the drop is definite. Below: the white void, clouds filling the space between this plateau and whatever else exists in the world.

Standing at the edge, you might feel wind for the first time—real wind, not the gentle breathing of the plateau but wind from outside, from the void, carrying smells and moisture from elsewhere. The wind is warmer than the plateau's air, and somehow cleaner, as if the green density behind you has a weight that this external air lacks.

You might throw something over the edge. A stone from a ruin, a piece of moss-covered wood. You watch it fall, tumbling, becoming smaller, disappearing into the white before any sound of impact returns. Or perhaps there's no impact. Perhaps the white goes down forever, or ends at something too far below to hear.

And you might see, if the clouds part for a moment, other plateaus. Dark green shapes in the distance, separated by miles or tens of miles of empty air. Also flat, also vast, also covered in what looks like the same vegetation. An archipelago of plateaus, islands in the sky, each isolated from the others by unbridgeable distances.

Or perhaps you see the ground. True ground, far below—brown or gray or blue, rippled with features too distant to resolve. And you understand that the plateau is exactly what it seems: an elevated ecosystem, a world-above-the-world, sustained by its own processes at an altitude where ordinary landscapes cannot exist.

Or perhaps you see nothing but white, and the question of what lies beyond remains unanswered.

And then you turn back. Or you don't—perhaps you find a way down, a crack system or a collapsed section that allows descent, and you leave the plateau entirely, trading green wetness for whatever exists below or between. But if you turn back, you walk into the plateau again, into its endless surface, its breathing body, its patient dissolution of your sense of direction and destination into the simple fact of being here, in this place, on this green mat, in this warm dim wetness, listening to distant tower-song and nearby dripping, walking on the lungs of a continent-sized organism that doesn't know you're here and wouldn't distinguish you from any other animal if it did.

The plateau continues. Has continued. Will continue. It is the form that life takes when given enough time and space and moisture and warmth and an absence of catastrophe. It is what remains when erosion finishes, when the rocks give up and the plants inherit everything. It is old not in the sense of aged but in the sense of complete, of having reached the state toward which it was always tending, the steady-state equilibrium between growth and decay, between water and air, between stone and green.

And somewhere on its surface, in one of its shallow pools or atop one of its breathing hummocks or inside one of its ancient structures, there might be you. Walking, resting, observing, trying to understand. The plateau doesn't need your understanding. But it accepts your presence, absorbs your footprints into its resilient surface, incorporates you into its soundscape as another small moving thing, another brief warm presence in its vast cool greenness.

You are here, and then you are elsewhere, and then eventually you are part of the mat yourself, your body broken down and fed back into the cycle, your bones (if you have bones) becoming substrate for the next layer of growth, your passage leaving no more permanent mark than the passage of the animals who walk the same paths, who drink from the same pools, who shelter in the same ruins, who live and die on this breathing green expanse without ever understanding—or needing to understand—what it is or why it exists or what it means.

The plateau is its own meaning. It breathes, and that is enough.
