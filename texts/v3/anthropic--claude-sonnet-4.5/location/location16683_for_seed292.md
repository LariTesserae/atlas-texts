# The Drowned Scaffolds

The water rises and rises and never stops rising, and has been rising for so long that "rising" is perhaps the wrong word — this is simply the state of the world, a permanent upward press of ocean that climbs a mountainside so tall and so eroded that it has become a system of rounded pillars, each one several miles in diameter, worn smooth as river stones over epochs you cannot name.

The pillars emerge from depths that glow with the diffused light of a sun somewhere above, somewhere beyond the thirty or forty feet of crystalline saltwater that moves ceaselessly upward along these ancient columns of stone. The water is cold — not freezing, but the kind of cool that makes you aware of your skin, that carries away heat steadily, patiently. You feel it even breathing this place into description. The columns rise perhaps two thousand feet above the nominal sea surface far below, but "surface" is a fiction here. The ocean has climbed these stone towers like a slow hand, and it pools and cascades and streams between them in a thousand simultaneous currents, each one feeding upward from some unseen pressure, some tidal architecture that operates on a scale beyond the merely regional.

Between the pillars, the water forms what can only be called sky-rivers. They arc and sheet and column from one stone mass to another, hanging in the air in defiance of gravity — or rather, in obedience to forces you can't immediately parse. Some are thin as cables, perfectly cylindrical flows that spiral as they span the hundred-yard or quarter-mile gaps. Others are broad curtains, ten feet thick and rippling, through which the diffused daylight breaks into caustic patterns that shudder across the surfaces of neighboring pillars. The sound is immense: a layered roar of water on water, water falling upward, water trading directions mid-air, the percussion of merging streams, the bass rumble of some current finding a hollow in the ancient stone and resonating there. It's loud enough that you'd have to shout to be heard, loud enough that the sound becomes a texture, something you feel in your chest and jaw.

The stone pillars themselves are so old they've lost all angularity. Whatever mountains they once were have been ground down to these smooth, rounded forms — not spherical, but organic, bulging and narrowing in response to differential hardness in the rock, patterns of flow that played out over millions of years and then stopped, leaving the stone locked into its final shape. The surface is pale gray-green, mottled with darker patches where minerals have leached or concentrated. In places, you can see the faint ghost-lines of strata that were once horizontal but now curve and tilt with the deformation of deep time.

And everything, *everything* is covered with growth.

The vegetation here has adapted to a world where "land" and "water" are the same thing. On the portions of the pillars that are merely soaked — where the upward-climbing water films across the stone rather than surrounding it entirely — there are forests of something like kelp, but rooted, anchored in cracks and solution pits, their fronds as thick as your forearm and extending fifteen or twenty feet into the water-heavy air. They're a deep green-brown, slick and muscular, and they move constantly in the turbulence, creating a secondary sound beneath the water's roar: a wet slapping, a rustling of heavy flexible things.

Where the water pools or streams more consistently, the growth shifts to something denser, something more committed to the aquatic. There are meadows — there's no better word — of feathery, fern-like organisms that wave in the currents, extending in beds that cover hundreds of square feet of stone. Their color is brighter, almost emerald in the refracted light, and they pulse and ripple as water flows through and over them. Between the fronds, you see flashes of movement: arthropodal things, segmented and quick, each the length of your hand or forearm. They pick their way through the vegetation in abundance, scraping at the stone beneath, their carapaces clicking against each other audibly even through the water-noise.

Higher up each pillar, where the water thins but never entirely leaves, there are what look like trees, though they bear the same relationship to terrestrial trees that this place bears to a normal mountain. They're thick-trunked, anchored deep in cracks that time has widened into caves, and their bark is spongy, gray-green, saturated. The branches split and split again into masses of fine, hair-like growth that hangs down like inverted roots, trailing into the water-currents, filtering something from the endless flow. When the wind shifts — and there is wind here, somehow, moving between the pillars in great gulps and surges — these hanging masses sway together, creating a sound like distant industry, like cables through pulleys, like some mechanism trying to catch.

The fauna is everywhere, abundant in a way that speaks to rich feeding, to oxygenated water, to a place that is, despite its strangeness, profoundly alive. In the thicker water-columns that arc between pillars, you see schools of fish-shapes, each fish perhaps a foot long, silver-sided, moving in coordinated waves that make the water inside the column seem to pulse. They're feeding on something, or migrating, or simply existing in the particular hydraulic niches that these aerial rivers provide. 

On the stone surfaces, in the shallower flows, there are creatures like salamanders but larger, some two or three feet in length, dark-backed and pale-bellied. They cling to the rock with broad feet, their bodies flattened to present minimal resistance to the upward-flowing water. You watch one launch itself from a pillar surface and swim — actually swim through the air — across a ten-foot gap to land on the streaming face of a neighboring column, where it immediately grips and resumes its slow crawling hunt through the kelp-forest anchored there. Birds would be the wrong word for the things that fly here, but there are flighted creatures: something like manta rays, but smaller, with wingspans of three or four feet, their bodies thin and membranous. They glide and flex through the spaces between water-columns, occasionally passing *through* the curtain-falls, emerging on the other side with no apparent disturbance, shedding water in sheets that catch the light.

The turbulence is constant, but it operates in layers, in rhythms that almost make sense and then shift. Every few minutes, the pressure changes — you feel it in your ears, a pop or tightening — and the configuration of water-flows adjusts. A cable-stream might suddenly broaden into a curtain. A curtain might split into three or four thinner flows that spiral around each other as they cross the gap between pillars. The kelp-forests respond, thrashing in new directions. The salamander-things grip harder or relocate. The flying rays alter their courses, banking around the new architecture of suspended water. And then, perhaps five or seven minutes later, another shift, another reconfiguration. It's not chaotic in the sense of random — there's a pattern here, a cycle, but it's too complex and too long to grasp in the immediate. You'd have to watch for hours, maybe days, to understand the full sequence. 

This turbulence generates its own weather. Where water-columns intersect, they create turbulence-nodes, places where the flow becomes atomized into mist that hangs in globe-shapes thirty feet across, drifting slowly between the pillars like clouds that have forgotten how to dissipate. Inside these mist-globes, the light does strange things: it diffuses into a general glow, it catches on droplets to create interior sparkle, it occasionally breaks through in beams that illuminate the tiny arthropods and filter-feeding polyps that seem to specialize in these aerial ponds. The globes merge and split, they rain upward or downward or sideways depending on the pressure differentials, and they carry with them whole ecologies of small, drifting things.

The built environment is sparse, almost apologetic. On one pillar, perhaps a quarter-mile distant, you can see a structure clinging to the stone about two hundred feet up from your current position. It's a platform of some kind, made from dark metal that's corroded to a uniform brown-orange, its surface lacy with rust-through. Cables extend from it, anchored into the stone with bolts that look like they were driven a very long time ago. Some of the cables are broken, their ends trailing into the water-flows, moving and twitching with the current. One still holds, stretching across the gap to another pillar where it terminates in... nothing. Whatever was there has fallen or dissolved. The platform itself is tilted, not quite collapsed, and you can see the remnants of what might have been housing: rectangular frames, paneless windows, a roof of corrugated metal mostly torn away. Kelp has grown up through the platform's deck. Salamander-things bask on its upper surfaces.

Further down, on a different pillar visible through the water-curtains and mist, there's another artifact: a gear, massive, six feet in diameter, mounted on a shaft that extends from the stone. It's turning slowly — so slowly you have to watch for a full minute to confirm the motion — driven by the water flowing over a series of paddle-blades welded to its circumference. The mechanism it once drove is gone, but the gear persists, turning and turning in the endless flow, its teeth slicked with algae, its bearing miraculously still functional after decades or centuries of this patient operation. Occasionally, a flying ray perches on it as it rotates, riding the gear through a complete revolution before launching off again.

There are other marks, other suggestions. Bolts embedded in stone with no visible purpose. Stains on the rock that suggest absent panels or plaques. A stairway carved into one pillar, spiraling upward for perhaps fifty steps before terminating at a sheared edge where the stone has spalled away. The stairs are worn smooth, softened by time and water and the passage of countless climbing things. Now they're merely sculpture, a record of intent that the world has absorbed and repurposed.

The light is steady but complex. The sun — you assume it's the sun — is somewhere above, but the thirty to forty feet of water that climbs and flows over and around everything acts as a massive, moving filter. The light that reaches you is cool, slightly greenish, and it shifts constantly as water-masses move across the sky. When a thick curtain passes between you and the primary light source, the world dims to a twilight in which the pale stone seems to glow softly, illuminated by scatter from other directions. When the curtain passes, the light surges back, and for a moment everything is edge and detail: the individual fronds of the kelp-forests standing out distinctly, the faceted eyes of the arthropods catching light like gems, the texture of the ancient stone revealed in every pit and mineral vein.

At night — and you know there must be night, though you haven't seen it yet in this description — the place would transform. The bioluminescence would emerge: glowing bacteria in the water-columns, making them visible as hanging ribbons of soft blue-green light; the underside-glow of certain fish-shapes, flashing in their schools; the gentle phosphorescence of fungal mats growing in the permanently shadowed overhangs. The structure of the place would remain audible, the roar and slap and rush of water unchanged, but the visual would collapse into these scattered lights and the dark masses of the pillars, and the sense of scale would become uncertain, vertiginous.

The rules here are not mysterious but they are specific. Water climbs. This is fundamental. Some combination of tidal forcing, pressure from below, and the particular geology of these worn-down mountain-pillars creates a condition where water is pulled or pushed up the stone surfaces in a continuous flow. The water comes from below — you can see, if you look down through the clearer sections, that the pillars descend into a dark depth where they presumably join into a more traditional seafloor or mountain root. And the water must, at some height, release or dissipate or rain back down, though that happens somewhere above where you can currently sense, perhaps where the pillars finally terminate, or in some atmospheric exchange that completes the cycle.

The vegetation and fauna have adapted not just to water but to *moving* water, to a world where flow is constant and the distinction between swimming and crawling is semantic. The creatures here are amphibious not as an adaptation for moving between two environments, but because this place recognizes only one environment with different densities. Everything has grip and streamlining. Everything is comfortable with turbulence.

The temperature regulates itself. The water, moving constantly, mixing, cycling through depth and height, maintains a cool equilibrium. You might call it 55 degrees Fahrenheit — cool enough to be invigorating, to make extended immersion tiring, but not cold enough to numb or damage. The air — where there is air not saturated into mist — is the same temperature, laden with moisture, tasting faintly of salt and plant matter and the clean mineral smell of old stone.

The turbulence operates on multiple scales simultaneously. Moment to moment, there's the flutter and surge of individual currents responding to local pressures. Minute to minute, there are the larger reconfigurations as the water-architecture between pillars reorganizes. Hour to hour — though again, you haven't observed this directly yet — there must be tidal cycles, larger shifts in the amount and pressure of water climbing the pillars. And longer still, seasonal perhaps, there might be changes in the biology: spawning events, migration patterns, the growth and die-back of the kelp-forests.

A fish-shape, larger than the schooling variety — perhaps three feet long, its body robust and pike-like — breaches through a water-curtain near you, hangs in the air for a moment as it transitions, then impacts the film of water running up the stone where you're positioned. It doesn't land; the water is thick enough here to catch it, and it begins swimming upward through the flow, muscling against the current, hunting. You watch as it approaches a salamander-thing, which flattens further and changes color slightly, darkening to match the stone. The fish-shape passes within inches, doesn't strike, continues upward. Perhaps it's heading for a mist-globe, where the filter-feeders congregate.

The sound builds and builds and never quite peaks. The bass tones are the largest flows, the curtain-falls and cable-streams where volume and pressure create vibrations you feel as much as hear. The mid-tones are the smaller cascades, the water meeting stone, the turbulence at intersection points. The high tones are the mist, the spray, the thousand tiny percussions of droplets. And woven through it all is the biological sound: the clicking of arthropods, the wet slap of kelp, the splash of creatures moving between water and water, the occasional cry — if that's the word — of something larger and unseen, a sound like metal groaning that might be a territorial call or a mating song or simply the vocalization of something that exists in this place and has adapted to be heard over the water's roar.

You could climb here, if you had the tools and the strength. The kelp provides handholds, though slippery ones. The cracks and solution pits in the stone are deep enough for purchase. The water-flow would be a constant resistance, but it's not violent enough to tear you away — it's more like climbing in a strong, steady wind that also drenches you. The salamander-things do it constantly, slow and patient, and you could follow their paths if you studied them, learning which routes stay just inside the water-film where there's enough flow to breathe through gills or slick skin but not so much that you're fighting a current.

If you did climb, the view would expand. From a higher vantage, you'd see that the pillars extend in a loose cluster — maybe twenty or thirty of them visible at once, each separated from its neighbors by a hundred yards or a quarter-mile, the gaps between them crisscrossed with the architecture of suspended water. Beyond the immediate cluster, the pattern repeats, fading into distance and mist: more pillars, more water-flows, the same ecology iterating across a region that might be ten miles across, might be fifty. It's hard to judge scale when everything is rounded and the light is refracted.

And at the edges of vision, in the far distance, you might see where the pillars finally terminate, their tops disappearing into a permanent mist-layer that hangs perhaps three or four thousand feet above the nominal sea level far below. That's where the water completes its cycle, presumably: falling back down as rain, or condensing and flowing, returning to the depths to be pushed upward again. The ecology up there would be different: more aerial, perhaps, more cloud-adapted. But that's speculation from here.

A mechanical sound cuts through the water-roar, just for a moment: a grinding, a squeal of metal on metal. You look toward its source and see one of the old platforms shift slightly, settling another degree into its tilt. A cable snaps — you see it whip across the gap between pillars, shedding rust-particles that dissolve into the water-flow. The platform holds, but barely. In another decade, another century, it will fall, and the components will tumble down through the water and kelp and stone to rest wherever things finally rest in this place. The salamander-things on its surface barely react; they've seen structures fail before, or their ancestors have, or the behavior is simply irrelevant to their concerns.

The industrial artifacts are all in decline, all surrendering to the patient work of water and time. Whatever operation placed them here — drilling, mining, research, habitation — ended long enough ago that the forest has reclaimed the surfaces, the flowing architecture has adapted to incorporate the metal as substrate, the rust as nutrient. It's possible that humans placed these things, possible that something else did. The design is utilitarian: bolts and cables and platforms, gears and housings, all engineered for function in this impossible environment. But there's no writing visible, no logos, no clear indication of purpose. Just the marks of industry, scattered and fading.

You notice now that some of the water-columns have a faint, oily sheen on their surfaces — not pollution, exactly, but some organic film, perhaps a bacterial mat that grows on the interface between water and air, harvesting light or nutrients from both. The film makes those columns slightly opaque, harder to see through, and it gives them a rainbow iridescence when the light catches them right. The flying rays seem to prefer these columns; you see several gliding alongside them, occasionally dipping their wing-tips into the water, perhaps feeding on the film itself.

Lower down, near what you're thinking of as the base of the visible pillars (though they continue into depths below), the kelp grows thicker, forming almost a canopy where individual plants overlap and interweave. Inside this canopy, sheltered from the full force of the upward flow, there's a different community: smaller creatures, more delicate vegetation, things that need the structure of the kelp to survive the turbulence. You see something like a crab, but flattened and wide, picking its way along a kelp-trunk, its claws delicately pruning smaller growths. You see polyps attached to the rock, their tentacles extended into the flow, catching whatever the water brings them.

The time of day feels like mid-morning or mid-afternoon — there's a fullness to the light, a sense of established day — but the exact hour is hard to determine when the sun is hidden behind water and mist. The quality of illumination is even, slightly diffused, cool-toned. Shadows exist but they're soft, blurred by scatter and refraction. When a large fish-shape passes through a curtain-fall overhead, its shadow races across the stone surface below, distorted and multiplied by the moving water.

You become aware that you're cold. Not dangerously so, but persistently so. The cool water, the evaporation, the constant moisture in the air — they pull heat away steadily. To live here, you'd need to keep moving, or find the warm spots where some geological heat source rises through the stone, or develop the same metabolic adaptations as the fauna: slow when needed, quick when hunting, always calibrated to the available energy.

The smell is clean but layered: salt and stone and growing things. There's a mineral brightness from the rock, a vegetal richness from the kelp and fern-meadows, an animal musk from the creatures that's barely noticeable under the overwhelming scent of water. When the wind shifts, you catch something else: a deeper smell, like wet iron and old machinery, coming from the direction of the platforms. Rust and oil and the organic residue of abandonment.

A wave — though "wave" isn't quite right when the water is vertical — passes up the pillar face near you. It's a pulse of increased pressure, a bulge in the flow that travels upward at walking speed. When it reaches the kelp-forest, the fronds stream upward in its wake, and you hear the pitch of the water-sound change, rising and then falling as the pulse passes. The salamander-things grip harder for a moment, then relax. The pulse continues upward, out of your frame of observation, carrying whatever it carries toward the distant mist-layer above.

The place is ancient in its bones — the stone itself, the erosion pattern, the settled equilibrium of the water-flow — but the life on it is vigorous, new each generation, abundant and opportunistic. There's no wasteland quality here, no sense of ending. If anything, this feels like what comes after ending: the system that established itself when all the continental collisions and volcanic events and tectonic drama finally resolved, leaving these pillars as the stable remnant, the final form that will persist until erosion takes them down to seamounts and then to nothing. But that process operates on geological time, and in the meanwhile, this ecology thrives in the specific paradise of these conditions.

Another sound emerges, something you haven't identified yet: a deep, rhythmic booming, like a heart or a drum, coming from inside one of the pillars. It's not the water — the resonance is different, more contained. You watch the stone surface near the sound and see it vibrate faintly with each boom. A cavity inside, perhaps, formed by solution weathering, and something in there making sound deliberately or simply by existing in a way that resonates. The booming continues for maybe twenty beats, then stops. The water-roar fills the absence.

Night will come eventually, and with it the shift in activity: the diurnal creatures retreating into crevices and kelp-tangles, the nocturnal ones emerging. The fish-shapes that glow. The arthropods that hunt by vibration rather than sight. The different quality of the turbulence when there's no solar heating to drive convection. The sound will remain, perhaps change in pitch, but the roar of water is constant, day and night, season after season, as long as the pressures below continue to push the ocean up these ancient stones.

And far below, beneath the visible pillars, beneath the kelp-forests and the schools of fish and the mechanisms slowly rusting into nothing, there must be the source: an aquifer, perhaps, pressurized by geological weight, or a tidal system that operates on these depths, or some hydrothermal complex that creates flow through temperature differential. Whatever the mechanism, it's stable, powerful, persistent enough to maintain this entire water-architecture, this vertical ocean climbing toward the sky. The energy it represents is immense, and the fact that it continues, undiminished, suggests resources on a scale that dwarfs the scattered industrial artifacts, the abandoned platforms, the single turning gear.

This is a place that will outlast memory. The platforms will fall, the gear will seize, the bolts will corrode through and release their hold on the stone. But the pillars will remain, the water will continue climbing, the kelp will grow and the salamander-things will hunt and the flying rays will glide between the curtain-falls. The turbulence will reconfigure every few minutes, forever, and the sound will roll across the kilometers like the breath of something too large to see all at once.

You are here, in the middle of it: wet, cold, surrounded by abundance and noise and the patient completion of erosion. The place doesn't notice you. It continues doing what it does, has always done, will do until the stone finally wears to nothing and even these pillars become part of the seafloor, submerged completely, the water-architecture collapsing back into simple ocean. But that's millennia away, or longer. For now, this is the world: the Drowned Scaffolds, where water climbs mountains that have forgotten they were ever peaks.
