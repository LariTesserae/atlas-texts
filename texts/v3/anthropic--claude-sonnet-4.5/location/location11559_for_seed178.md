# The Surge Valleys

The landscape breathes — no, *heaves* — with a rhythm that never settles into predictability.

This is a region of young valleys carved into dark basalt plains, where the rock still remembers its formation. Not from volcanism, but from something else: a precipitation event that happened in living memory, when matter itself fell from the upper atmosphere in a weeks-long rain of elemental slurry that cooled into stone as it landed. The valleys are fresh wounds in this newborn surface, cut not by patient water over millennia but by the frantic drainage patterns that emerged in the first seasons after the Fall. Their walls are sharp-edged, faceted, geometric in places where the cooling basalt fractured along crystal planes. In other places, the stone is rippled like frozen fabric, preserving the moment of its transformation.

Water moves everywhere, but not as rivers — at least not rivers that stay rivers. The valleys hold what might be streams for an hour, then become something else: vapor-channels, mist-roads, bands of suspended droplets that flow horizontally through the air before condensing back to liquid and plunging underground. The substrate is barely impermeable. Water surfaces, runs for dozens of meters in braided channels that reshape themselves constantly, then vanishes again into fissures that open and close as the young stone continues its settling. You can hear it: the perpetual *shush-and-silence, shush-and-silence* of water appearing and disappearing, the gurgle rising then cutting off mid-note as a stream finds a new crack and pours into darkness.

The cold is profound but not static. It averages perhaps two or three degrees above freezing, but pulses with inexplicable warm updrafts that rise from the valley floors — not geothermal in the conventional sense, but generated by something to do with the substrate's continued transformation. Where warm air meets the general chill, fog banks materialize in seconds, dissipate in minutes, reform elsewhere. The air is almost always thick with condensation: not quite rain, not quite mist, but a continuous presence of water in transition. Surfaces glisten. Moss grows on stone that was naked yesterday.

And the sound — the *sound*.

It's the first thing that stuns visitors into stillness, this overwhelming acoustic assault that comes not from a single source but from the landscape's entire metabolic process. The loudest component is a deep, grinding pulse that seems to emanate from beneath the valleys themselves: the sound of stone settling, of the basalt plains continuing their infinitesimal adjustments, transmitted through the ground and amplified by the valley walls into a felt-more-than-heard vibration that never quite stops. Over this, the water adds ten thousand voices — the aforementioned streams appearing and vanishing, but also the drip-echo from the overhangs, the sudden roar when a mist-channel condenses all at once and pounds against stone, the strange singing whistle that water makes when it drains through certain fissure-geometries that act as organ pipes. 

Then there is the vegetation, and here the sound becomes almost unbearable in its layered complexity.

The valleys are thick with growth — improbably, impossibly thick given the cold and the landscape's youth, but the moisture and whatever trace minerals continue to leach from the transforming stone have triggered a biological explosion. But this is not ordinary vegetation growing in ordinary ways. The dominant life-form is something between lichen and coral and fungus, existing in vast mats that coat every surface not actively running with water, and many that are. They're called *sound-rugs* in the broken pidgin that serves as common language here, because they are *active* — photosynthetically, chemically, acoustically. They pulse. They contract and expand with visible rhythm, processing something from the air or the stone or both, and as they do, they *sing*.

Each mat — and there are thousands visible in any given valley, in colors ranging from deep blue-black through olive-green to startling patches of orange — emits a constant drone whose pitch and intensity vary with its metabolic rate. The effect is of an enormous, distributed organ with no organist, playing chords that shift according to light levels, moisture, temperature, and influences nobody has successfully mapped. Some mats hum at frequencies that rattle bone. Others produce overtones that seem to come from inside your skull. Where many mats grow in proximity, on the dense valley walls or clustered around the ephemeral streams, the acoustic interference creates beat patterns and phantom sounds, voices that seem to speak in no language, melodies that last four notes and dissolve.

Amid the sound-rugs, other vegetation has taken root with manic speed. There are something like sedges, growing in explosive clumps wherever water surfaces, adding their own contribution: a papery rattling as moisture-sensitive fronds curl and uncurl in response to the ever-changing humidity. There are vertical growths — call them trees if you need a word — with glossy black bark and no leaves, only feathery structures that might be breathing apparatus or might be spore-distribution mechanisms. They grow to perhaps four or five meters in height, clustering in groves of twenty or thirty where the substrate permits, and they creak constantly as they elongate, adding centimeters of height per week in visible pulses of growth. The creaking harmonizes, somehow, with the mats' droning and the water's roar, creating moments of accidental beauty in the chaos.

The fauna move through this acoustic maelstrom in states of constant alertness. They are abundant but not diverse — perhaps a dozen species visible in a day of observation, each represented by many individuals. Most are small: arthropod-analogues with too many legs and jewel-bright carapaces, processing the mat-growth or feeding on each other in frenzies of activity along the valley walls. They *click* as they move, adding yet another layer to the soundscape, a perpetual chittering rustle that rises and falls as populations swarm and scatter. There are also mid-sized creatures, vaguely otter-like, that follow the water's appearances and disappearances with frantic energy, diving into streams that will exist for only minutes, emerging with mouth-structures full of something they've filtered or caught, then racing to the next emergence. They bark — sharp, carrying calls that cut through even the deepest mat-drone — coordinating their movements in groups of five or six.

Flying creatures work the air currents where warm updrafts meet cold, riding the interfaces with outspread membranes, calling to each other in shrieks that doppler as they spiral. They're feeding on something airborne, possibly spores from the feather-trees, possibly smaller flying things too small to see. Every few minutes, one will fold its wings and plummet into a valley, vanishing into the mist, then bursting out elsewhere with prey clutched in multiple grasping appendages.

The turbulence is not just acoustic or atmospheric — it's built into the landscape's fundamental nature. The valleys themselves are still finding their final forms. A slope that was stable yesterday develops a fresh crack overnight as the basalt continues settling. Mats that covered a wall slough off in patches when the substrate shifts, falling to the valley floor where they either die or — more often — fragment and begin new colonies immediately. The water table rises and falls without clear pattern, responding to pressures and porosities still being established. A path you walked in the morning might be knee-deep in a sudden stream by afternoon, or shrouded in fog so thick you can't see your own feet, or perfectly clear but relocated half a meter lower because the stone beneath it compressed in the day's heat-cycle.

And through this chaos, the evidence of intelligence.

The built elements are scattered, never clustered enough to be called settlements, but present enough that you're rarely out of sight of something *made*. They're small — platforms cantilevered from valley walls, no larger than a sleeping person. Marker-stones balanced at angles that seem deliberate. Channels cut into the basalt to direct water along specific paths, though to what end isn't clear since the water rarely cooperates for more than a few hours before finding new routes. The structures are *recent*, made from the same young basalt as the valleys themselves, but worked with precision that suggests tools or techniques beyond simple stone-on-stone percussion.

More striking are the acoustic installations — at least, that's what they must be, though calling them installations suggests permanence they don't have. They're arrangements of stone or metal fragments positioned to interact with the mat-song and water-roar in specific ways. A series of vertical plates, each slightly different in composition, that ring when the right frequency of mat-drone hits them, adding melody to the chaos. A curved wall of polished basalt that focuses certain sounds into tight beams you can walk through — stepping from cacophony into an isolated chamber of a single mat's voice, then back to cacophony a meter later. Some of these structures modify themselves, or are modified: you'll find one configuration in the morning, return in the evening to find stones repositioned, new elements added or removed, the acoustic signature completely transformed.

The technology is *present* but subtle, worked into the landscape rather than imposed upon it. There are what might be sensors — small devices embedded in valley walls, in the platforms, in the substrate itself — made of materials that don't match the basalt, smooth and lightless where the stone is textured and light-absorbing. They might be monitoring the settlement process, or the mat-growth, or something else entirely. Sometimes they emit pulses of light so brief you question whether you saw them, brief phosphorescence in the general darkness that hangs over these valleys even at midday.

Because the light is low, always. The sky is heavy with cloud that rarely breaks, a ceiling of grey-black that turns the landscape into a study in subtle values. Noon feels like late evening elsewhere. Actual night is nearly absolute, broken only by occasional bioluminescence from certain mat-species that glow faintly blue when active, creating shifting constellations along the valley walls. The darkness compounds the cold, makes the wet more penetrating, turns every sound more present because vision can't dominate your sensory field.

In this gloom, the landscape's colors emerge gradually. The basalt ranges from true black to charcoal to a dark blue-grey where certain minerals concentrate. The mats add their own palette — those deep blue-blacks, the olives, the startling orange, and here and there patches of crimson or yellow whose purpose is unclear. The water, when you can see it, is clear but picks up the darkness, becomes a moving mirror of shadow. The feather-trees are black until you look closely and see their bark is actually deep purple, their breathing-structures a grey so dark it's almost white by contrast.

Moisture is everywhere, on everything. Your clothing becomes damp within minutes and never fully dries. Metal corrodes at visible speeds. The platforms and marker-stones weep with condensation that runs in rivulets, adding tiny tributaries to the water's constant circulation. Breathing the air means breathing water — not enough to drown, but enough that you feel it in your lungs, heavy and cold and mineral-tasting, flavored by whatever the transforming basalt is still releasing and whatever the mats are processing.

The temperature does strange things. That baseline cold — just barely above freezing — means ice forms in sheltered spots, creating fragile structures that last until the next warm updraft melts them. The updrafts themselves are mysterious, rising from specific points in the valley floors without pattern you can discern, carrying scents of mineral and something organic but unfamiliar, something between mushroom and machine oil. Where updrafts meet the cold air, the fog-banks materialize with visible speed, rolling and building until they fill entire valley sections, then dispersing just as quickly when the updraft ceases.

The valleys themselves extend for kilometers, a network of interconnected channels cutting through the basalt plains. From the low ridges between valleys — themselves barely fifty meters above the valley floors — you can see the regional scale of the place: valley after valley, parallel and branching and rejoining, all filled with the same dense growth, the same fog-banks, the same chaotic water-patterns. The horizon is perhaps ten or fifteen kilometers away before fog and the heavy sky obscure detail, but in that visible range you can count dozens of valleys, hundreds of the feather-tree groves, uncountable mats coating every surface.

And everywhere, the *activity*. Nothing is still. The mats pulse. The water appears and vanishes. The fauna race and click and bark and shriek. The fog rolls in and out. The stone settles with its grinding pulse. The acoustic installations shift their configurations. Even the darkness seems to move, the thick shadows reshaping as the rare breaks in cloud-cover allow shafts of dim light to sweep across the landscape before disappearing again.

There is a building — singular, the only structure of any size visible in the valleys — perhaps three kilometers to the northeast of where I'm standing now. It's low and long, built into a valley wall rather than on top of it, made from that same worked basalt but on a different scale than the platforms. Maybe thirty meters in length, four meters high, with openings that aren't quite windows and aren't quite doors. No light comes from inside. Water runs over its roof, has cut channels in its surface despite its youth, feeds a particularly dense cluster of mats that have colonized its walls and seem to be *using* it somehow, their song especially complex and layered near its openings. 

No one is visible entering or leaving, but the building is clearly maintained. The channels on its roof are too precise to be pure erosion. The mats growing on it are arranged in patterns, not random colonization. And there's a platform nearby, one of the larger ones, with what might be tools or instruments arranged on its surface — difficult to see clearly through the mist and at this distance, but suggestive of recent use.

The strangeness here is not in obvious impossibilities but in the *speed* and the *coherence* of what's happening. A landscape this young shouldn't support this much life. Mats shouldn't organize their acoustic output into anything resembling coordination. Water shouldn't move in these semi-predictable unpredictable patterns. The technology — those sensors, whatever maintains the building and acoustic installations — should be more visible, more clearly delineated from the natural processes, but instead it's woven into them, indistinguishable from the landscape's own turbulent development.

It feels like watching something being born in real-time, not the slow birth of geological ages but the frantic, overwhelming birth of a living creature — messy and loud and strange but also coherent, following rules that exist even if you can't articulate them. The valleys are finding their forms. The mats are establishing their acoustic territories. The water is mapping the substrate's permeability. The fauna are adapting to day-by-day changes in their environment. And whatever intelligence made the platforms and installations is participating in this process, not controlling it but collaborating with it, adding elements that the landscape incorporates into its ongoing transformation.

Walking through these valleys means accepting that nothing will be the same when you return. The path changes. The soundscape reorganizes. New mats appear, old ones slough away. Water that was absent fills a channel, water that was present vanishes underground. The fog banks form in different locations, creating different acoustics, different visibilities, different micro-climates in their wake. The platforms sometimes gain new elements — a stone positioned just so, a fragment of metal, a marking scratched into the basalt. The acoustic installations reconfigure, creating new songs from the chaos.

There's a particular spot, perhaps half a kilometer west of the building, where a cluster of seven feather-trees grows in a tight circle around a point where water surfaces and vanishes every few hours. The mats here are especially dense, coating not just the ground but climbing the trees themselves, creating vertical walls of that droning, pulsing growth. When the water surfaces, it fills a shallow basin — perfectly circular, clearly cut rather than naturally formed — and the mats surrounding it change their tone, harmonizing into something almost melodic before the water vanishes again and the harmony dissolves back into drone.

In the brief moments when the water fills the basin and the mats harmonize, the local fauna gather. Not in huge numbers, but noticeably: a dozen of the arthropod-analogues emerge from the mat-growth and position themselves at the basin's edge. Three or four of the otter-things arrive from wherever they were hunting and sit motionless, watching the water. Several of the flying creatures land on the feather-trees and fold their wings, silent. And for those few minutes — perhaps five, perhaps ten — the local acoustic chaos diminishes just slightly, the grinding pulse still present but the layered cacophony organizing itself into something with structure, with rhythm, almost with *purpose*.

Then the water drains, the harmony breaks, the fauna scatter, and the chaos resumes.

These moments happen throughout the valleys, not on schedule but not randomly either — patterned but in ways that resist mapping. They suggest that beneath the turbulence, the landscape has an organizing principle, or is developing one, or is being gently guided toward one by whatever intelligence maintains the platforms and installations.

At the valley edges, where the sharp basalt walls meet the plains above, the erosion is just beginning. The frost-heave and chemical weathering that will eventually soften these sharp edges has only started its work. In another few decades, maybe centuries — it's unclear how these processes scale in a landscape this unusual — the valleys will be gentler, more weathered, less violently young. But for now, the edges are knife-sharp in places, fractured and faceted and fresh, each angle catching what little light penetrates the cloud-cover and creating brief gleams before the fog banks roll through and erase them.

Standing on these edges, looking down into the valleys with their dense mat-growth and racing fauna and ephemeral water-patterns, listening to the overwhelming layered sound that makes thought difficult and speech impossible unless you shout, feeling the cold and the damp and the occasional warm updraft that smells of minerals and transformation, you understand that you're witnessing something rare: a landscape in its moment of fastest change, its period of maximum chaos before settling into whatever stability it will eventually achieve.

But you also understand, watching the way the mats coordinate their sounds, the way the water patterns show just-barely-discernible regularity, the way the fauna respond to the subtle environmental shifts, the way the platforms and installations participate in rather than control the ongoing transformation — you understand that this chaos *is* the stability. This turbulence is not a transition to something calmer but the actual steady-state of this place. The valleys will keep changing, keep reshaping, keep discovering new configurations. The mats will keep reorganizing their acoustic territories. The water will keep appearing and vanishing in patterns that shift but never cease. The fauna will keep adapting to an environment that never settles.

And the intelligence here — whoever or whatever maintains the platforms, positions the acoustic installations, built that long low building and keeps it clean of the densest mat-growth — has adapted to this too, or perhaps encourages it, has made turbulence itself into a medium for creation, has learned to work with chaos rather than against it.

The sound continues, always. The grinding pulse of settling stone. The roar and whisper of appearing and vanishing water. The drone and harmony of the mats, thousands of voices in inadvertent chorus. The click and rustle of the arthropod swarms. The barking of the otter-things. The shrieks of the flying creatures. The creak of the growing trees. The ring and resonance of the acoustic installations. All of it layered, simultaneous, overwhelming, *deafening*, a wall of sound that becomes paradoxically beautiful once you stop trying to parse it into individual components and let it wash over you as a unified overwhelming whole.

In the distance, toward the northern edge of the visible region, there's a section where the valleys are particularly dense and interconnected, creating a maze of sharp-walled channels filled with fog and growth and sound. Something about that area looks different — the fog moves in spirals there, the mat-growth seems more organized, the periodic warm updrafts more frequent. Another building, perhaps, or an installation more elaborate than the scattered platforms. The distance and the fog make it impossible to see clearly, but it draws the eye, suggests something worth investigating when the water-patterns allow passage.

Because moving through the valleys requires negotiating with their turbulence. You can't force a path; you have to wait for windows when the water recedes, when the fog clears enough to see the sharp drop-offs, when the mat-growth isn't too thick to push through. The platforms help — their positions suggest a network of semi-reliable crossing points — but they're not always accessible, sometimes submerged when the water table rises, sometimes invisible in the fog, sometimes blocked by sudden mat-growth that colonized them overnight.

The locals — and there are locals, though they're rare and often not visible — move through this chaos with practiced ease, reading environmental signs that visitors can't perceive. They know which mat-songs indicate stable stone beneath, which water-patterns suggest safe crossings, which fog-banks are about to disperse and which will linger for hours. They maintain the platforms and installations, adjust the acoustic configurations, leave markers that mean something if you know the code.

They're human, or human-derived, though adapted to this place in ways that show in their movements and their silence. They dress in layers of treated fabric that sheds water without fully repelling it, creating a micro-climate next to the skin. They communicate primarily through gesture and touch because voice is nearly useless in the deafening soundscape. They've developed an intimacy with turbulence, an ability to find the patterns within chaos, to collaborate with the landscape's constant transformation rather than resist it.

Occasionally you'll see one standing on a platform, perfectly still, just *listening* to the sound-layers with an attention that suggests they're hearing something specific in the cacophony. Or moving along a valley wall, placing stones or fragments in positions that seem arbitrary until you return hours later and find them incorporated into a larger pattern, part of an installation that's been growing for weeks or months. Or tending the building, clearing certain mats while encouraging others, maintaining the channels on its roof, adjusting whatever the interior contains — glimpsed only briefly through the not-quite-windows, shapes and lights that don't resolve into identifiable objects.

They seem neither disturbed by the chaos nor attempting to control it. They're participants in the landscape's transformation, adding their intelligence to the mix of forces shaping the valleys — the settling stone, the transforming mats, the dynamic water, the adapting fauna, and whatever technology pulses quietly in the sensors and installations, monitoring or modifying or simply witnessing the ongoing creation.

The place refuses to be still, refuses to be silent, refuses to be known completely. It changes faster than you can map it, generates sound that overwhelms analysis, presents patterns that dissolve before you can fully grasp them. But in that refusal is its essential nature: a landscape that exists in constant becoming, where turbulence is not a problem to be solved but the fundamental condition of existence, where chaos and creation are the same process, where the deafening sound is not noise but the voice of the world speaking itself into being, again and again, without pause, without resolution, without end.
