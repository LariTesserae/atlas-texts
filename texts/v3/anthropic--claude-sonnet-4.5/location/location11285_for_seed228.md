# The Singing Hollows

Beneath what was once ocean floor, now lifted and folded by ancient tectonic pressures then eroded back into itself, there exists a warren of interconnected chambers where water and air perform their endless argument.

The stone here is limestone primarily, but shot through with bands of something else—a metamorphic remnant that catches light in a way calcium carbonate should not. The chambers range from crawlspace-tight to cathedral-proper: thirty meters across at their widest, perhaps fifteen high, though the proportions shift as you move through them. Seven or eight significant rooms in all, connected by throat-passages and one broad corridor worn smooth as soap.

Everything is wet. Not underwater—this is crucial—but perpetually drenched. The walls weep continuously, not from single seeps but from the stone itself, as if the rock has become semi-permeable. Water sheets down in moving curtains, pooling in the sculpted basins that pock every surface before draining through fissures to reservoirs below, then rising again through capillary action and vapor pressure in a closed-loop system that never quite balances, that hunts endlessly for equilibrium without finding it.

The moisture hangs thick enough to see—not fog exactly, but presence. Light catches in it. The illumination comes from the metamorphic bands and from something else, something introduced: nodes of engineered matter embedded in the rock at irregular intervals, each the size of a closed fist, casting sharp-edged light in a spectrum that trends violet-white. They were installed—grown? induced?—long enough ago that mineral deposits have begun to incorporate them, limestone building its patient layers over their surfaces. They respond to the water's movement, brightening when flow accelerates, dimming in still moments, though still moments are rare.

The sound is tremendous. Not a roar—roars are simple. This is layered: the rush and patter of water in constant motion, the deep resonant hum of the stone itself conducting vibrations from distant chambers, and overtop it all, the singing. The chambers form an instrument. The specific geometry of eroded space, the placement of openings, the water-volume in each basin—all of it combines to generate harmonics. When water flow surges, which it does in irregular pulses driven by pressure variations below, the pitch rises. The fundamental note sits low enough to feel in your sternum, but the overtones climb into ranges that edge toward painful. It never stops, only modulates, and your ears never quite adjust.

The erosion here tells a story of immense time punctuated by intervention. The stone shows its age in the completeness of its rounding—every edge worn to curve, every surface polished by water and carried sediment into a language of smooth hollows and gentle swells. But then: the channels. Cut into floors and walls, sometimes following natural joint patterns, sometimes defying them entirely, are grooves—engineered drainage systems perhaps fifteen centimeters wide and half that deep. They're weathered now, softened, but their angles are too precise to be natural, their networks too purposeful. They gather and route water in ways that modify the chambers' acoustics, that feed certain basins preferentially.

Where channels converge, you sometimes find the artifacts. Scattered. Small. A cylinder of the same not-quite-stone material as those metamorphic bands, thirty centimeters long, its surface covered in regular indentations that might be text or purely functional. A bracket—surely a bracket—still anchored to one wall, its mounting points wept over with flowstone but the thing itself a blue-gray alloy that shows no corrosion. Once, what might have been a lens, though it was shattered and half-buried in deposited calcite; nearby, the wall showed a discolored patch suggesting long exposure to focused light or heat, now softened by millennia of patient water.

The vegetation is sparse enough to name individually. In the largest chamber, where a crack in the ceiling admits a blade of actual daylight (though refracted through some upper layer—it arrives at an impossible angle), a thin carpet of moss has established itself on one wall. It's a dark olive verging on black, and it seems to prefer the spots where the engineered nodes are brightest, growing in rings around them. In another room, something more unusual: a stand of three pale stalks, each perhaps a meter high, no leaves, just segmented stems that glisten with moisture and show faint phosphorescence in the dimmer corners. Whether they're fungi or plants or something designed is unclear. They don't spread. There are only ever these three, though in the calcite at their bases you can find fossilized remnants of similar forms, suggesting they or their ancestors have occupied this specific spot for a very long time.

The fauna is rare enough that encountering any is noteworthy. Blind white crayfish—or what resemble crayfish—in some of the deeper pools, going about their armor-plated business. Twice, over many hours, a quick silver fish, no longer than your finger, glimpsed in motion between basins where channels connect. And once: a cluster of translucent organisms on a wet wall, each the size of a grape, pulsing slowly, connected by threads too fine to see clearly. They moved, over the course of an hour, perhaps a hand's width upward. When touched, they contracted, and the light-nodes nearest them flared brighter momentarily, though whether this was response or coincidence isn't clear.

The air tastes mineral and slightly alkaline. Warm—not hot, not uncomfortable, but notably warm, as if somewhere below, the rock remembers its formation. The temperature stays constant; the humidity does not. It cycles with the water pressure, and when pressure peaks, the atmosphere becomes so saturated that every breath feels like drinking. Your skin never dries. Fabric doesn't dry. Anything absorbent becomes heavy with moisture within minutes.

The dynamics here operate on multiple scales simultaneously. There's the micro-turbulence of water sheeting down rock, breaking into rivulets, reuniting, finding new paths in the small variations of surface texture. There's the chamber-scale pulse: every eight to thirty minutes (it's irregular), pressure surges from below, flow increases, basins fill faster than they drain, water levels rise, the singing intensifies into something that rattles your vision, and then gradually—over two or three minutes—pressure releases, levels drop, sound moderates. And there's the slow turbulence: over hours, the patterns shift. A channel that was carrying heavy flow dwindles; another, previously near-dry, suddenly gushes. The light-nodes' rhythms change. The moisture in the air redistributes. The place is never the same twice, yet always recognizably itself, caught in perpetual adjustment, hunting some stability it never quite achieves.

Walking here requires attention. The floors are slick, unpredictably so—some surfaces have enough texture to offer grip; others, polished by millennia, are treacherous. The water depth varies. What was ankle-deep might be shin-deep an hour later. The routes between chambers change accessibility based on water level and flow strength. During high-pressure surges, some passages fill entirely, becoming temporarily impassable unless you're willing to submerge and push through against the current (not recommended—the pressure is substantial, the direction you'd emerge uncertain).

The weirdness here is not aggressive. It's available to notice if you pay attention, but it doesn't announce itself. The way the light-nodes sometimes pulse in synchrony for a few cycles, then drift apart, then—minutes or hours later—find synchrony again, suggests some communication or shared sensitivity, but to what? The sense that the chambers' acoustics are too perfect, that the erosion has been gently guided toward resonance over geological time. The bracket on the wall that shows no corrosion despite what must be millions of years of exposure. The fact that water temperature varies by location—this basin always slightly warmer, that one cooler—in ways that don't map to any obvious heat source or flow pattern. Small things. Persistent things. The kind of anomalies that accumulate into a growing suspicion that this place is not entirely natural, but also not simply artificial. Perhaps it's been curated. Perhaps someone or something, long ago, gave it a nudge and let time do the rest. Perhaps it's still being adjusted, imperceptibly, on timescales that make human observation irrelevant.

In one of the smaller chambers, there's a feature worth sitting with. A pool, roughly circular, perhaps three meters across, unusually deep—at least five meters, though it's difficult to gauge because the water is so clear and the light-nodes' reflection off its surface is so bright. At the bottom, visible despite the depth, is a pattern: concentric rings inscribed in the stone, and at the center, a dark aperture, maybe twenty centimeters wide. This is where the water is coming from. Not seeping—arriving. Pushed upward with enough force that the surface of the pool stays in constant, small agitation, never quite still. The water that emerges here is noticeably warmer than ambient, and it has a slight—very slight—luminescence of its own, blue-green, only visible in the darkest moments when the nodes dim.

Sitting beside this pool, listening to the overtones as they climb and fall, watching the water curtains on the walls catch light and throw it in patterns that move too quickly to follow, you begin to feel the rhythm of the place. Not to understand it—it's too complex for that, too many variables—but to feel it. The way everything here is connected: pressure below drives flow above, flow creates sound, sound seems to affect light, light supports the sparse life, life (maybe) influences water chemistry, chemistry affects erosion rate, erosion shapes acoustics, acoustics feed back into the system's behavior. A loop. Many loops. Nested and tangled.

The built elements—the channels, the nodes, the artifacts—are clearly ancient, but they still function. Or they function again? Or they never stopped? Their purpose remains opaque. Were the Hollows designed as an instrument? A hydrological experiment? A shrine or meditation space? A power generation system? (Some of the artifacts have geometric regularity that suggests machinery.) All of these seem both too specific and too limiting. Perhaps the Hollows are what remains after original purpose has been superseded by geological time, a place where function has transformed into something else—not meaning exactly, but presence, persistence, the quality of continuing to be itself despite or because of all that has happened to it.

In the broadest chamber, where the moss grows in rings and the ceiling crack admits its blade of wrong-angled daylight, there is sometimes—not always, perhaps once a day, perhaps less—a phenomenon. The water flow synchronizes. All the channels, all the sheeting curtains, every drip and trickle finds the same rhythm. For anywhere from thirty seconds to three minutes, the chaos resolves into pattern. The singing becomes a chord—not harmonious in any comfortable sense, but unified, all the overtones suddenly locked in relation to each other. The light-nodes pulse together. The water in the central basin rises and falls in time. And then it breaks apart, gradually or suddenly, and the turbulence returns.

No one is sure what triggers this. No one is entirely sure who would know, or who installed the original systems, or why those systems persist in operation. The technology is clearly beyond contemporary understanding—the nodes alone would require manufacturing processes that bend the edges of current physics, and their power source (geothermal? some kind of zero-point extraction? decay of elements not on standard tables?) remains mysterious. Yet here they are, embedded in stone older than most mountain ranges, still casting their bright, clear light into moisture-thick air.

The Hollows have been known for perhaps eighty years. A handful of researchers have spent time here. Some measurements have been taken. The artifacts have been photographed, carefully, because removing them seems both technically difficult and ethically complicated. The place appears stable—has been stable, likely, for a very long time—and the scientific consensus is to observe rather than intervene. Which means the Hollows continue their endless cycling: water rising, flowing, singing, draining, rising again. Pressure building and releasing. Light pulsing. The rare fauna going about their obscure business. The moss growing its slow circles. The stone, patient as stone always is, being worn imperceptibly thinner by the water that will eventually—in another few hundred thousand years—dissolve these chambers entirely, unless something shifts, unless the system adjusts again, unless the Hollows have other plans.

Standing here, wet to the bone, feeling the sound in your chest, watching light scatter through water-thick air, you understand: this is a place that exists in its own time, operating by its own rules, neither natural nor artificial but something that has become itself through the marriage of both and the passage of immense duration. It is exactly what it is, no more and no less, and it will continue being that long after you leave.

The singing never stops. Even after you've climbed back to the surface, dried your clothes, returned to places where air is merely air and light is merely light, you'll still hear it sometimes in the silence. The memory of those overtones, hunting their harmonics, never quite resolving, never quite giving up.
