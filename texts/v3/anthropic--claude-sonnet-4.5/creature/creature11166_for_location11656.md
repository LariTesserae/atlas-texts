# The Inhabitants

## The Quiet People

The humans of the Hanging Gardens move as if perpetually aware they are guests. Not fearful guests, but conscious ones—the way you might move through a friend's home where something precious and fragile sits on every surface.

They are small in build, or have become so. Whether selective pressure or simple adaptation to narrow passages, the people here trend toward compact frames, narrow shoulders, a litheness that serves them in the three-dimensional maze. The eldest remember taller relatives from the founding generations who struggled in certain passages. Those lines have thinned. The children born here slip through gaps that would stop a lowlander cold.

Their clothing is entirely moth-cloth, of course. Simple wrapped garments, tunics, loose trousers that don't catch on vegetation. The fabric needs no dyeing—they wear the natural cream and white of the material, which means the people themselves become color-matched to their environment. In the filtered golden light, a person standing still can disappear against a moth-cloth coated wall, can become just another pale surface, another soft vertical element in the density.

This camouflage is sometimes intentional. The culture here has developed a deep respect for solitude. If someone wishes to be alone, they simply stop moving, settle against a wall, and become furniture. Others learn to read the subtle signs—a particular quality of stillness, the way someone's eyes focus inward—and pass by without acknowledgment. Privacy happens not through walls but through mutually agreed-upon invisibility.

Their hands are their most distinctive feature. Strong, with long fingers made flexible by constant climbing, constant manipulation of plant material. The fingertips are slightly calloused but sensitive—they've learned to read surfaces by touch in the dim passages, to distinguish between safe handholds and new growth that won't bear weight. They use their hands constantly to communicate, having developed an elaborate gestural vocabulary that works in silence and semidarkness. A conversation can happen across a chamber with no sound at all: questions asked with palm orientations, answers given in finger configurations, emotions conveyed through the speed and scale of movement.

They smell of the place. After even a short time here, the scent of the vines permeates clothing and skin—that green-sweet smell, faintly mineral, like cucumber and stone and honey. Visitors from the lowlands find it strange at first, then stop noticing. The inhabitants themselves are unaware of it, having long ago adapted to their olfactory environment.

## The Children of Spirals

The children here grow up in conditions that would terrify lowland parents.

By age three or four, they're climbing unsupervised through structures thirty feet high, moving through passages where a fall could mean serious injury. But falls are extraordinarily rare. The children develop spatial intelligence early and deeply. They learn to test holds, to distribute weight, to read the subtle signs of plant stability. They learn their bodies in three dimensions, always knowing where their center of mass is, how their limbs extend through space, how to arrest a slip before it becomes a fall.

They play games in the vertical maze that have no lowland equivalents. Chasing through spiraling passages where the pursued might suddenly climb up a chimney and emerge three levels higher. Hide and seek in a place with infinite hiding spots, where the finding is a skill of reading crushed moth-cloth, of noticing where the coating has been disturbed by recent passage. They build smaller structures within structures using fallen vines and secondary growth, creating dollhouse versions of their world, learning architecture before they learn letters.

Their first task, around age six or seven, is assigned moth-watching. They learn to sit still for hours, observing individual moths, learning to distinguish them by the subtle variations in wing pattern, tracking their movements, their feeding preferences, their behaviors. This teaches patience, observation, the humility of being less important than what you're watching. Some children discover they have a gift for it—they can hold stillness for entire days, can identify individual moths across weeks, can predict behavior. These become the settlement's moth-keepers, a role of high respect.

They learn plant-knowledge not from books but from doing. Which stems can be cut for tools. Which leaves are edible at which stage of growth. How to tell if a vine is healthy or declining. How to encourage growth in useful directions through small interventions—a weight hung here, a redirect there. They learn that the settlement is not a static thing they live inside but a living process they're part of shaping.

Education happens through apprenticeship and observation rather than formal schooling. A child interested in cloth-working shadows an elder who does that work, learning by watching and trying, receiving correction through gentle guidance rather than criticism. A child drawn to the cultivation of the food-plants spends time with the growers, eventually being trusted with small patches of responsibility.

They learn the gestural language and the soft-voice simultaneously. By adolescence, they can conduct complex conversations with minimal sound, can coordinate group activities through hand-signs alone. They learn that sound is a precious resource to be used sparingly, that silence is the default and speech the exception.

Interestingly, they also learn to be comfortable with long periods of non-communication. Children here can work alongside each other for hours without speaking or signing, without the lowland need to fill space with noise or interaction. Parallel existence is understood as a valid form of relationship.

## The Elders Who Remember Weight

The oldest inhabitants carry a different quality. They remember the founding, or remember those who remembered it. They carry stories of the first years when no one was certain this would work, when the death rate was higher, when thirst was a daily threat before the water-collection systems were refined.

They move more slowly now, but with absolute confidence. They know the settlement in a way younger people don't—they remember when certain chambers were first opened by caterpillar-work, remember the specific vines whose growth created particular passages. They can navigate in complete darkness, their internal maps so detailed that sight becomes optional.

Many of the eldest choose to dwell in the edge zones, in less dense areas where they can see sky, where sound returns. There's a pattern to it: a lifetime moving deeper into the silence, into the density, becoming more and more integrated into the settlement's quiet heart. Then, as death approaches, a gradual retreat outward, toward light and air and sound, toward stone, preparing to leave the green embrace.

They're the primary teachers, though not in any formal sense. They simply do their work—cloth-processing, food cultivation, water collection—and allow the interested to observe. They answer questions when asked but rarely volunteer information. Knowledge is something to be sought rather than given, discovered rather than transmitted.

Some of the eldest have developed what others call "plant-sense"—an ability to know, without obvious signs, when something is wrong in the settlement. A vine showing early signs of disease before symptoms are visible. A chamber whose structural integrity is beginning to fail. Areas where the moth population is declining. Whether this is real sensitivity or simply deep pattern recognition accumulated over decades, no one can say. But when an elder says a section needs attention, people listen.

They tell stories, but sparingly. The settlement's history isn't written—there's no paper here, no permanent recording materials except what memory holds. So the elders carry the narrative, releasing it in small pieces. How the first moth-keepers discovered the caterpillar-vine relationship. The year the fog didn't come and what measures saved them. The time a section collapsed and what was learned from rebuilding.

These stories aren't told to groups but one-on-one, elder to interested younger, in quiet corners, in low voices that barely disturb the silence. The transfer of history happens intimately, personally, creating chains of knowledge that branch and interweave through the population.

## The Cloth-Workers

A particular caste—not rigid, not hereditary, but recognized—are those who work primarily with moth-cloth.

They move through the settlement in the early mornings, brushing accumulated powder from surfaces into collection bags. Their touch is gentle, systematic, thorough. They know which surfaces produce the finest powder, which areas generate material with different properties. Cloth from different zones has subtle variations: powder from the upper reaches is slightly coarser, more durable. Material from the deepest chambers is finer, softer, better for garments that touch skin directly.

They process the raw powder in specific chambers set aside for the work. The powder is spread on horizontal surfaces to assess its quality. Then begins the compression: layering the material, pressing it with weights, adding moisture carefully—too much and it doesn't cohere, too little and it remains dust. The process takes days. The powder slowly becomes felt becomes cloth, the individual particles bonding through some property that no one fully understands but everyone knows how to exploit.

Some cloth-workers specialize in finishing techniques. They know how to rub the surface to raise a nap or to smooth it to near-gloss. They know natural additives that make the cloth more water-resistant or more breathable. They experiment constantly, trying small variations, learning through failure what the material will and won't accept.

The best cloth-workers have an almost erotic relationship with their material. They touch it constantly, learning its moods, its responses. They can tell by touch alone where a piece of cloth came from, when it was made, who made it. Their hands are always slightly coated in the fine powder, giving their skin an opalescent sheen.

They're also the settlement's primary connection to the outside world. The cloth is the main trade good, the resource that makes this improbable place economically viable. Several times a year, cloth-workers make the descent to the lowlands with bundles of processed material. They trade for what can't be produced here: metal tools, certain seeds, occasionally books though these are rare and precious in the humid environment.

These trading journeys are significant events. The travelers are gone for weeks. They return with stories of the lowlands—the noise, the colors, the rushing of people, the aggression of it all. Most are happy to return to the silence. Some, especially younger cloth-workers on their first journey out, don't return at all. The lowlands seduce them. The settlement accepts these losses without apparent grief. People go where they go.

## The Moth-Keepers

These are the contemplatives, the priests if the settlement had religion, which it doesn't quite, but the role carries that quality.

They're responsible for maintaining the moth population, which means understanding it at depths that approach the spiritual. They watch. They track. They maintain mental records of lineages, of which moths are mating with which, of survival rates, of the subtle shifts in behavior that might indicate stress in the population.

They manage the flowering plants that the moths feed on, ensuring that nectar is always available, that the bloom timing creates continuous food sources. They identify and remove sick or dying moths before disease can spread. They occasionally, carefully, cull the population if it grows too large for the settlement to support, a task they approach with gravity and something like sorrow.

The moth-keepers develop relationships with individual moths. They have favorites. They give them names, though these are private, not shared—naming creates a bond that is personal. They mourn when these individuals die, collecting their wings with extra care, keeping them in small personal shrines.

They're the ones who understand—or believe they understand—the intelligence of the settlement as a system. They've observed the feedback loops: how moth behavior influences vine growth, how vine growth creates spaces that influence moth breeding patterns, how human activity affects both. They see the settlement not as a place where humans live but as an organism of which humans are one component, no more or less important than moths or vines or the silent lizards.

Moth-keepers tend toward solitude. They often dwell in the highest chambers, in areas less trafficked, where they can observe without being observed. They're recognized by their stillness, by their patience, by the way moths will land on them without fear—their clothing so coated in powder, their scent so integrated into the environment, that the moths perceive them as just another surface.

They rarely speak unless asked direct questions. Their gestural language is minimal—they've stripped it down to essentials, preferring to communicate through presence and action rather than signs. If you want to know something about the moths, you find a keeper and sit near them, waiting. Eventually, if they choose, they'll gesture you to follow, and they'll show you what you need to see. Teaching happens through demonstration, through shared observation, through long silences interrupted by brief moments of pointing or gentle redirection of attention.

Some become so attuned to the moth cycles that they begin to live nocturnally, awake when the moths are most active, sleeping during the day when the moths rest. They become shadows in the twilight, visible only as pale movements in the perpetual dusk of the settlement's interior.

## The Growers

In the lowest chambers, where light filters down most weakly, the growers do their work.

They're practical, earthy, less mystical than the moth-keepers but no less essential. They maintain the food systems, coaxing nutrition from mushrooms and shade-adapted tubers, managing the careful harvests that keep the population fed without depleting the growing zones.

They're strong—their work involves moving substrate, managing soil, heavy labor in confined spaces. They're often the most talkative of the inhabitants, perhaps because they work together more, because their labor requires coordination, because they haven't fallen as deeply into the silence as others.

They experiment constantly. Trying new mushroom varieties. Testing different growing substrates. Attempting to breed tubers that produce more per plant. Their work is empirical, generational—changes happen slowly, and a successful innovation might take ten years to prove itself.

They also manage waste, which in the settlement means returning organic material to the soil. Nothing is wasted. Food scraps, plant trimmings, the bodies of dead moths and other creatures—all go back into the growing beds, completing cycles. The growers understand decomposition intimately, know the smell of healthy rot versus sick rot, manage the invisible populations of decomposers that make the growing possible.

They're the ones most likely to have children. The work is physical but safe, suitable for doing while pregnant, while caring for infants. The growing chambers become nurseries by default, the youngest children playing in the soil while their parents work, learning early the smell of earth and growing things.

The growers are also the settlement's doctors, such as they exist. They know medicinal mushrooms, plants with properties that ease pain or reduce fever. They know poultices made from certain lichens, teas brewed from specific leaves. Their healing is simple, practical, mostly about supporting the body while it heals itself. Serious injuries or illnesses often mean descent to the lowlands for treatment the settlement can't provide.

They maintain the genetic libraries—not written, but living. They keep the seeds and spores of varieties that aren't currently being grown, maintaining them in small plots, ensuring diversity. They understand, viscerally, that their descendants' survival depends on genetic options kept alive now against future need.

## The Climbers

Some inhabitants specialize in movement through the most difficult spaces—the narrow chimneys, the external faces where the vine structures meet bare basalt, the highest reaches where the settlement thins to almost nothing.

They're the maintenance workers, checking structural integrity in areas others can't reach. They're the rescuers when someone falls or becomes stuck. They're the explorers, pushing into new growth, being the first to enter chambers the caterpillars have just completed.

They're distinguished by their bodies—lean, with exceptional grip strength, long-limbed, comfortable in positions that look impossible. They move with a grace that's almost insectile, finding holds that others don't see, flowing through vertical space like water running upward.

They wear minimal clothing—less to catch on protrusions—and their skin shows the scrapes and bruises of their work. They collect scars like other people collect stories, each one a memory of a specific challenge, a specific route, a moment when friction or grip or courage was the difference between completion and falling.

They know fear intimately and have made peace with it. They've learned to distinguish between useful fear that keeps you careful and destructive fear that makes you freeze. They've all fallen at some point—not far, usually caught by vegetation or finding holds, but enough to know the stomach-drop of it, the spike of adrenaline. These experiences make them both more cautious and more confident.

The best climbers develop an almost supernatural route-finding sense. They can look at a vertical surface and see the path through it, the sequence of movements that will work. They're problem-solvers, their thinking spatial and kinesthetic rather than verbal. They often struggle to explain their routes—the knowledge lives in their bodies rather than their minds.

They're also the ones who sometimes leave. The lowlands call to them differently than to others. They descend and find work that uses their skills—as construction workers in places where that still means hands-on building, as entertainers sometimes, as guides in rough country. Some return periodically, bringing news and trade goods, never quite settling in either world.

Those who stay become elders of a particular type—their bodies eventually unable to do the hardest climbing, but their knowledge irreplaceable. They teach by belaying the young climbers, by watching and offering minimal corrections, by knowing when to let someone struggle versus when to intervene. Their instructions are terse: "Left foot higher." "Trust it." "Breathe."

## The Watchers

Some inhabitants have no clear occupation but seem essential anyway. They're the ones who spend most of their time simply being present, observing, noticing.

They're often found at junctions, at places where multiple passages meet, where they can see movement through the settlement without obstructing it. They're not guards—there's nothing to guard against—but their presence creates a subtle sense of attention, of the settlement watching itself.

They notice patterns. Who moves where, when. Which children are taking risks. Which elders are declining. When the moths' behavior shifts. Where structural problems might be developing. They don't necessarily act on what they notice, but they hold it, and when someone needs information, the watchers often have it.

They're the memory supplement, the social glue. In a place with no written records, no formal administration, someone needs to remember who promised what to whom, when shared tasks were last done, what precedents exist for unusual situations. The watchers carry this, not officially but functionally.

They tend to be older—people who've moved past the age of primary productivity but aren't yet in the eldest category. They've found a role that uses their accumulated knowledge without requiring physical intensity. They're comfortable with stillness, with long periods of doing nothing obvious, which in the lowlands might be seen as laziness but here is recognized as work of a kind.

They're also counselors, informally. Someone troubled will often come sit near a watcher, not saying anything at first. The watcher makes space, waits. Eventually, in gesture or quiet words, the trouble emerges. The watcher listens—really listens, with full attention, without rushing to solutions. Sometimes that's all that's needed. Sometimes the watcher offers perspective, a reminder of similar situations, a reframe that helps.

They know everyone's stories, everyone's histories, the web of relationships that makes the community function. They're the ones who gently suggest when two people who are avoiding each other need to talk. Who remind someone that they haven't visited an ailing elder recently. Who notice when someone is spending too much time alone, even by settlement standards, and needs drawing out.

## The Innovators

A small number, usually younger adults, are always trying to improve things, to find better ways.

They experiment with new cloth-processing techniques. They attempt to breed more productive food plants. They try to understand the chemical properties of the vine exudates. They push at the boundaries of what's known and what's possible.

The settlement tolerates this but doesn't celebrate it. Innovation is viewed with mild suspicion—the balance here is delicate, and changes might disrupt it. But the innovators persist, driven by curiosity, by dissatisfaction with "good enough," by the need to understand rather than simply accept.

They're the ones who sometimes succeed in meaningful improvements. A better water-collection method. A mushroom variety that produces more. A technique for making moth-cloth water-resistant enough for the rainy season descents to the lowlands.

They're also the ones who fail most often, whose experiments produce nothing useful, whose clever ideas turn out to be dead ends. The settlement watches these failures with sympathy but without judgment. Resources aren't wasted on failed experiments—materials are too scarce—but small-scale trials are permitted. Failure is information.

These people often have deep relationships with the eldest, who remember what's been tried before, who can save the innovators from repeating old mistakes. The interchange between oldest and younger adults is where institutional knowledge transfers, where history informs experimentation.

Some innovators eventually become frustrated with the pace and the caution and leave for the lowlands, where there's more material to work with, more tolerance for disruption. Others settle into the rhythm, accepting that change here is geological, that real improvement is measured across generations rather than years.

## The Silent Ones

A few inhabitants barely speak or sign at all.

They're not outcasts or damaged, but people who've moved so deeply into the settlement's silence that they've nearly stopped using language. They communicate through the subtlest gestures—a tilt of the head, a direction of gaze, a quality of presence that others learn to read.

They often work with the moths or with plants, roles that require attention but minimal human interaction. They're extraordinarily peaceful to be near, emanating a calm that comes from having released the constant inner monologue that language supports.

Some are people who came to the settlement damaged by the lowlands—by violence, by trauma, by the grinding noise and stress of denser populations. They climbed the ridge seeking silence and found more than they expected: a place where silence wasn't just absence of sound but a presence of its own, something that could fill you, could replace the clamoring thoughts with something quieter.

Others are simply people for whom language never fit well, who found in the settlement's culture a permission to be non-verbal that the lowlands never granted. They're not disabled here—they're simply different, and the difference doesn't impair them because the environment adapts to non-verbal communication as readily as verbal.

The silent ones are often sought out by those in distress. There's something about sitting with someone who won't speak, who won't try to fix things with words, that allows a different kind of processing. Grief, confusion, anger—these can be held in silence beside someone who simply witnesses without trying to manage or resolve.

They're also bridges to the non-human. Spending so much time outside language, they notice animal behavior, plant responses, things that people caught in verbal thinking miss. They often notice environmental changes first—shifts in temperature, in humidity, in the quality of light—because they're not filtering experience through the linguistic processing that occupies most people's attention.

## The Departed

Finally, there are those who exist in memory, who shaped the settlement but are gone.

The founders, now dead or descended to the lowlands in old age, whose names are remembered: First-Keeper, who understood the moths. Stone-Hand, who learned to work the basalt. Root-Singer, who discovered how to encourage the vines. These aren't complete histories but distilled essences, single attributes that became legendary.

The children who died in the early years, before the settlement learned how dangerous it could be, before the safety protocols were understood. They're remembered in small shrines, in the careful teaching of spatial awareness, in the eternal vigilance that keeps current children safe.

The ones who left and never returned, who are presumed lost to the lowlands—absorbed into different lives, different identities. They're remembered with acceptance rather than grief. The settlement knows it's not for everyone, that some need what it can't provide.

And there are the recent dead, still vivid in memory. The elder who died last season, whose favorite spot remains empty, whose gestures are unconsciously mimicked by those who learned from them. The child lost to illness despite all care, whose playmates still sometimes seem to expect them to appear around a corner.

The settlement has no formal funerary practices. Bodies are returned to the soil in the growing chambers, becoming literally part of the structure that sustains the living. Death is transition, return, completion of a cycle. The grief is real but not dramatic. People mourn quietly, privately, through long periods of stillness, through visits to places the deceased loved, through continuing the work the person did.

Memory here is living, active, maintained through retelling and through action. The dead remain present not as ghosts but as influences, as patterns of behavior that persist, as knowledge that continues to inform current practice. The settlement is as much their creation as the living inhabitants', and everyone knows it, lives within it, maintains it for those who'll come after.

---

These are the people of the Hanging Gardens: quiet, adapted, skilled in ways the lowlands don't value, living in careful balance with each other and with the larger system they're part of. They're not utopian—they have conflicts, frustrations, failures. But they've found a way of living that works in this specific place, that honors the constraints of stone and water and plant-time, that makes space for human life inside a living architecture without destroying what makes it livable.

They're small in number, particular in their adaptations, unlikely to expand much beyond current populations. But they persist, generating cloth and children and quiet knowledge, a society measured in whispers and gestures, held together by mutual dependence and by love of a very specific kind of beauty: the beauty of golden light through living walls, of moths in their slow ballet, of silence so complete you can hear your own breath, of a place that is home not despite but because of how strange and constraining and particular it is.
