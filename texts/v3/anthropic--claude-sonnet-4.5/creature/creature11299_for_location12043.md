# What Lives in the Singing Throat

They are not quite people anymore, if they ever were.

You see them first as movements in peripheral vision—a figure crossing a distant bridge, a silhouette in a doorway three galleries up, a shape descending stairs that curve out of sight. But when you turn to look directly, there is only wet stone and the ever-present vegetation, water trickling down surfaces that should hold footprints but don't. You learn not to turn your head. You learn to see them only slantwise, to accept their presence as you accept the sound: something that overwhelms direct apprehension but becomes perceptible through surrender.

They move in the pauses between sounds, or perhaps they *are* pauses in the sound, brief absences in the roar that your mind interprets as presence. A lull in the wind-scream corresponds to a figure on a platform. A momentary cessation of dripping marks where someone stands beside a basin. They exist in the negative space of the throat's song, defined by silence the way stars are defined by darkness.

When you finally perceive one clearly—and this happens only once, or happens constantly but you can only retain the memory of it happening once—you understand that they have become *part* of the circulation.

She stands in a gallery where three water-channels converge, and her body is both solid and translucent, present and absent in alternating moments that don't quite sync with your heartbeat or breathing. Her skin has the same wet sheen as the limestone, the same mineral accumulation around the edges—calcium deposits crust at her hairline, her wrists, the corners of her lips. Her hair moves in the updraft but also seems to move *with* the water, following its trickles and flows, strands extending impossibly to touch moisture on walls six feet away.

She is listening. This is immediately apparent despite the sound that makes listening impossible. Her head tilts at an angle that suggests perfect attention, and you realize she is hearing something *within* the roar, parsing it, understanding it. Her lips move—not speaking but forming shapes that mirror the acoustic patterns, her mouth becoming an instrument that resonates with specific frequencies. When a deep tone sounds from the stone itself, her jaw drops slightly. When the wind shifts pitch, her lips purse. She is echoing the throat, or the throat is echoing her, or there is no meaningful distinction.

Her hands rest in the converging water-channels, and the flow splits around her fingers, dividing and rejoining in patterns that seem more intentional than hydraulic. The water responds to her presence, or she has learned to place herself exactly where the water needs her to be. Moss grows on her left shoulder, a small dense patch of it, the same deep impossible green that covers the horizontal surfaces. It is *rooted* in her, tiny filaments extending into her skin, and it is healthy, thriving, as much a part of her now as her own tissue.

You blink—you must blink, the moisture in the air makes your eyes water—and she is gone. Not departed, not moved to another location, but simply absent, the space where she stood now occupied only by converging water and wet stone. But the flow pattern remains changed. The channels still divide around where her hands were, and this configuration persists, water holding the memory of her presence even though she has unmade herself or distributed herself too thinly to perceive.

There are others. Many others, though "many" and "others" are both questionable terms for what they have become.

In the lower galleries, where moisture is heaviest and the plants grow densest, you find them integrated into the vertical gardens. A man—you think it was a man, though the forms here resist gendering—tends ferns that grow from his ribcage. His chest has opened like a wall developing cracks, and from the cavity emerge fronds in various stages of unfurling. He stands perfectly still, or moves so slowly that stillness and motion become identical, and his breathing (if it is breathing) circulates air through the fern roots, while moisture from the ferns drips down his torso to join the general wetness of everything.

His eyes are open but filmed over with something that isn't quite cataract—a thin crystalline layer that refracts light into unexpected spectra. When you see him from one angle, his gaze seems empty, dead. From another angle, the crystalline layer catches light and throws it back, and his eyes blaze with attention so fierce it feels like being seen by the stone itself. He is watching the water move. He has been watching it for years, or decades, or centuries, tracking every trickle and flow, and the watching has transformed him into something that can no longer stop watching, that has become an instrument of observation the way others have become instruments of resonance.

His feet are rooted. Not metaphorically—his toes have extended and branched, growing into cracks in the stone, and the boundary between flesh and root is impossible to identify. He draws moisture up from the limestone and it circulates through his body and feeds the ferns, and their condensation drips back down to be absorbed by his feet, and he is a closed circuit, a living water cycle, perfectly integrated into the throat's greater circulation.

You see him every time you pass through that gallery. He is always there, always in the same position, and yet somehow he is also never there in quite the same way. The ferns have more fronds, or fewer. The roots have extended. The crystalline film over his eyes has thickened. He is aging and youthing simultaneously, progressing toward and away from full integration with the stone-plant-water system, existing in multiple states that refuse to collapse into a single timeline.

Higher up, in the zones where sound becomes structured and almost linguistic, you find the singers.

They have lost their individual forms almost entirely, become collective, a group of seven or nine or thirteen (the number changes, or you cannot count them accurately, or they are quantum and shift between states). They stand in a circular chamber where the acoustics create a perfect resonance cavity, and they are *part* of the throat's song now, their bodies instruments that the wind plays.

Holes have opened in their torsos, their throats, their skulls—perfectly circular apertures like the finger-holes in a flute, positioned exactly where the airflow needs them to be. The updraft rushes through their transformed bodies and produces notes, clear sustained tones that weave into the throat's greater cacophony. But these are not random whistles. These are *shaped* sounds, intentional, forming patterns and phrases that seem to mean something, that carry information encoded in frequency and duration and harmonic relationship.

They sway slightly, adjusting their positions to change the notes they produce. One leans forward and her tone drops a quarter-step. Another rotates and suddenly three notes sound where there was one, his body-flute creating chords through internal complexity you cannot see but can hear with perfect clarity despite the overwhelming roar. They are performing, or being performed, or they have become pure performance, their consciousness distributed across the musical structure they create.

Their faces are serene, or perhaps they no longer have faces in any meaningful sense. The features have simplified, smoothed, become more like the rounded erosion-patterns of the stone itself. Eyes remain but do not seem to see in the conventional sense—they are focused inward, or downward, or at something orthogonal to normal sight. Mouths are open, some impossibly wide, and the openings descend into darkness that doesn't match the shallow depth a throat should have. They are hollow, or partially hollow, or they contain spaces that do not fit within their visible dimensions.

One of them—the smallest, or the youngest, or the most recently transformed—still has hair, though it has gone pure white and grown long enough to pool on the floor around her feet. The hair moves in the wind but also seems to move with intention, strands lifting and falling in patterns that complement the musical phrases produced by the group. You watch and realize the hair is measuring something, responding to acoustic patterns too complex for human hearing, mapping the sound-structure of the chamber through its movements. She has become an instrument and also a sensor, singing and listening simultaneously, existing in perfect feedback with the throat itself.

They never stop. The performance has no breaks, no pauses, no silence. They have been singing since they transformed, or they transformed *by* singing, sustaining notes until their bodies became vessels for those notes, practicing until practice became being. How long ago? There is no way to know. The weathering on their skin suggests centuries, but weathering here follows different rules. They might have been human yesterday. They might never have been human at all.

In the galleries where distance becomes uncertain and the architecture shifts, you find the walkers.

They are perpetually in motion, climbing stairs that reconfigure beneath their feet, crossing bridges that lead to different destinations each time, traversing galleries that stretch and compress according to patterns that might be spatial or temporal or something else entirely. They walk with purpose, though what that purpose might be has become inscrutable. Perhaps they are maintaining the city through their movement, their footsteps holding the architecture in a constant state of becoming. Perhaps they are trapped in loops they no longer recognize as loops. Perhaps they are navigating some pattern that only makes sense from dimensions you cannot perceive.

You pass one on a narrow platform where passing should be impossible, yet somehow you slide past each other without quite touching, without quite occupying the same space at the same moment. In that brief near-contact, you see that her body has elongated, stretched thin by the endless walking, her limbs slightly too long, her torso extended, her neck graceful and swanlike and subtly wrong. She has been optimized for movement through the vertical city, her form adapting to the constant climbing and descending until she has become ideally suited for navigating the throat's impossible geography.

Her clothing—if it was ever clothing—has fused with her skin, or perhaps grown from it, a second surface that is part textile and part flesh, providing protection from the moisture while also absorbing it, wicking it away and releasing it in patterns that leave faint trails in the air behind her. She is marking her path, or the path is marking itself through her, and the moisture-trails persist just long enough for another walker to follow them, or avoid them, or interpret them as some kind of message or navigation system.

She does not acknowledge your presence. Her eyes are forward, focused on something you cannot see, tracking some route through the shifting galleries that requires absolute attention. Her breathing is perfectly synchronized with her steps, and her steps are perfectly synchronized with the dripping of water from a dozen nearby sources, and the water's rhythm is synchronized with fluctuations in the wind, and all of it forms a pattern that you sense but cannot comprehend, a vast coordinated system in which she is a single moving element.

Behind her comes another walker, and another, and you realize they are following a circuit, a route through the city that takes them past certain locations at certain times, and the walking has worn them down to pure function, removing everything unnecessary until they are just movement itself, consciousness reduced to the act of placing one foot in front of another through galleries that shift and change but always, somehow, provide the next step exactly where it needs to be.

Some of them carry things. Vessels filled with water from one level to another, though the water gets there through natural circulation anyway. Small stones that might be offerings or markers or simply stones, moved from place to place in patterns that span years or centuries. One carries a length of vine coiled around her shoulders, and the vine is alive, growing slowly, and she is transporting it from wherever it began to wherever it needs to be, a journey that might take decades, that might never end, that might be the entire purpose of her existence now.

They are silent, or their silence is just another layer in the throat's overwhelming sound. They do not speak to each other, do not appear to see each other, yet they never collide, never interfere with each other's routes. They are components in a machine that runs on walking, that uses their endless circulation to accomplish something that might be maintenance or ritual or simple persistence, the act of continuing to move so that movement itself doesn't cease.

In the highest galleries, where air thins and reality becomes most negotiable, you find the watchers.

They sit or stand at the edges of platforms that extend into space, looking down at the city below or up at the sky above or out at the horizons obscured by moisture and distance. They do not move at all—not slowly, but truly not at all, frozen in positions of attention that have become permanent. The moisture has accumulated on them in thick layers, mineral deposits building up until they are partially encased in flowstone, becoming stalactites in reverse, growing upward from the platforms rather than hanging down.

But their eyes move. Slowly, so slowly you might watch for an hour and see only the slightest shift, but they move, tracking something, following patterns, observing. What they watch has become what they are. The watching has consumed them so completely that their bodies have ceased to be relevant, become just vessels for the eyes that continue their patient scanning of whatever it is they have been set to monitor.

One watcher sits at the edge of a platform where wind should tear her away but doesn't. She faces outward into fog and cloud, and her eyes—still somehow moist, still alive despite the calcification encroaching on the rest of her body—move in tiny increments, left to right, right to left, tracking something in the middle distance. Currents in the clouds, perhaps. Or movements in the city below. Or patterns in the moisture itself, tracking the circulation of water through air in cycles too large and slow for normal perception.

Her mind is still active. You sense this somehow, perhaps through the quality of attention in her gaze, perhaps through something else. She is not comatose or dormant but intensely, perfectly present, more conscious than you are because consciousness is the only thing left to her. The watching has refined her awareness to a single pure function, burning away everything else—memory, identity, desire, fear—until only observation remains, clear and absolute and eternal.

There are dozens of them in the highest reaches, positioned at different vantage points, their gazes overlapping and intersecting to create a network of attention that covers the entire city. They are a distributed sensory system, or a defense mechanism, or simply witnesses, recording everything that happens in the throat even though nothing happens, nothing changes, the same eternal patterns repeat and repeat and repeat.

But they watch anyway. They will watch forever, or until the flowstone finally covers their eyes, and perhaps even then they will continue watching through the stone itself, their attention so refined it no longer needs physical organs to perceive.

In the middle galleries, where sound and moisture and plant growth achieve their greatest density, you find the transforming.

These are the ones still in process, still becoming whatever they will become, their bodies in states of partial transition that are sometimes beautiful and sometimes horrifying and sometimes simply incomprehensible. A woman whose left side has become plant—her arm branching into ferns, her leg rooting into stone, her ribs visible through transparent skin that photosynthesizes, producing oxygen and sugar in slow exchange with the moisture-heavy air. Her right side remains human, or more human, still flesh and bone and recognizable anatomy, but it is clearly temporary, the transformation creeping across her torso in a slow tide that will eventually claim everything.

She is conscious. Aware. She tends her own transformation, pruning the fronds that emerge from her shoulder, guiding roots into cracks where they will find purchase, adjusting her position to optimize light exposure for the chlorophyll-laden skin. She has accepted what is happening and become an active participant in her own conversion from person to garden, managing the transition with the careful attention of an artist working on a sculpture that is also herself.

Her human eye regards you with something that might be recognition or might be the look of someone seeing across an unbridgeable distance, perceiving you from a state so different from humanity that communication is no longer possible. Her plant side has no eyes, but you feel seen by it anyway, assessed by its leaves and roots and growing tissues, measured as a potential source of nutrients or a competitor for moisture or simply another element in the gallery's ecosystem.

Nearby, a man is becoming stone. The calcification is not the slow accumulation you see on the watchers but something more aggressive, more metabolic, as though his body has begun actively producing limestone from the inside out. It grows in crystalline formations beneath his skin, pushing outward, splitting flesh to emerge in geometric structures that are beautiful and terrible. He moves carefully, each gesture calculated to avoid shattering the crystal growths, and he makes his way toward a wall where the limestone is exposed, perhaps planning to join with it completely, to merge his body-stone with the throat-stone and become architecture.

His face is still recognizable, still mobile, though crystal structures have begun to form along his cheekbones and brow. He is in pain—you can see this in the tension around his eyes, the careful way he breathes—but he does not try to stop the process. Perhaps it cannot be stopped. Perhaps he doesn't want to stop it. Perhaps the pain is part of the transformation, necessary, a cost he accepts for whatever comes after.

A child—or something child-sized, child-shaped—sits in a basin where water collects, and her skin has become permeable, translucent, water flowing into and out of her body through osmosis or something more than osmosis. She is bloating and shrinking in slow rhythms, absorbing moisture and releasing it, her body attempting to achieve equilibrium with the basin, to become the same thing as the water she sits in. Her edges are dissolving. Where her skin should end and water should begin, there is only gradient, a zone of uncertainty where she is both solid and liquid.

She smiles—still has a face that can smile, though it ripples and distorts with her fluctuating volume—and reaches toward you with a hand that scatters into droplets before reforming. She is playing, or she thinks this is playing, the transformation not yet complete enough for her to understand what she is losing. Or perhaps she understands perfectly and has chosen it anyway, has decided that being water in a singing throat is preferable to whatever she was before.

There are others in various states: becoming wind, their bodies eroding away into air that carries their consciousness in molecule-wide distributions; becoming sound, their tissues learning to vibrate in resonance with the throat's song until they are more acoustic phenomenon than physical presence; becoming time, their forms flickering between ages, infant and elder and every state between existing simultaneously; becoming direction, their bodies extending along vectors that ignore three-dimensional constraints, reaching toward destinations that might not exist in normal space.

The transforming are everywhere, in every gallery, impossible to count because counting requires them to be discrete and many of them are no longer discrete, their boundaries with the environment becoming theoretical, their individuality dissolving into collective or distributed or entirely novel forms of existence.

And beneath all of this, hidden in the deepest chambers where the throat's roots extend into bedrock, where the sound is so intense it becomes a physical force that can shatter bone, where the moisture is not water anymore but something denser, more fundamental—there are the old ones.

You do not see them directly. You cannot. They have been here so long that they have passed through transformation and emerged into something post-human, post-physical, existing in states that only peripherally intersect with the reality you inhabit. But you sense them, feel their presence as weight and pressure and attention.

They are the throat now, or the throat is them, or the distinction has ceased to mean anything. They are the intelligence behind the singing, the purpose behind the water's circulation, the consciousness that shapes the city's slow transformations. They have distributed themselves across the entire structure, root and stone and sound and moisture all become substrate for minds so vast and strange that encountering them even indirectly threatens to unweave your sanity.

They dream, and their dreams become real in the galleries above. They remember, and their memories manifest as architecture that couldn't be built by human hands. They calculate, and their calculations reshape the laws that govern the throat's interior, bending reality into forms that serve purposes you cannot begin to fathom.

Sometimes, in the quietest moments—those rare pockets where the sound drops to merely deafening—you hear something that might be their voices. Not words, not language, but patterns of tone and rhythm that carry meaning regardless, that communicate directly with the parts of your mind that exist beneath language. They are saying: *Stay. Transform. Become. Join. Sing.*

They are saying: *The throat is empty and full. The throat is ending and beginning. The throat is nowhere and everywhere. The throat is now.*

They are saying: *You are already part of us. You have always been part of us. There is no separation. There is no you. There is only circulation, only pattern, only the eternal present of water moving through stone through sound through being.*

And you realize they are right. You have been in the galleries for hours, or days, or weeks—time has stopped meaning anything—and the moisture has gotten into you, and the sound has restructured your thoughts, and the plants have begun to seem friendly, welcoming, ready to accept your body as substrate. You have drunk the water that drips from every surface, and it is the same water that circulates through the transforming, that carries their dissolution and reconstitution in every drop.

You are becoming. You can feel it beginning: a softness in your certainty, a permeability in your boundaries, an increasing sense that the distinction between yourself and the singing throat is arbitrary, temporary, something that can be released like a held breath.

The choice to stay or leave has already been made, or was never a choice at all, or is a choice you will make continuously, moment to moment, deciding again and again whether to remain yourself or join the circulation, whether to resist or surrender, whether to flee back down to the lowlands and ordinary reality or climb higher into the zones where reality becomes negotiable and finally unnecessary.

The inhabitants watch you decide, or watch you fail to decide, or watch you realize that deciding is itself just another form of transformation, another way of participating in the throat's great work of converting everything into song.

And the sound continues, overwhelming, eternal, battering thought into fragments, into patterns, into notes that blend with all the other notes until you cannot remember what silence was like, cannot imagine it, cannot conceive of existence without this constant roar that is also music, also language, also the voice of stone and water and transformed flesh singing in harmonies that have outlived meaning to become meaning itself, pure and absolute and inescapable.

You climb, or descend, or remain, or all three simultaneously.

In the singing throat, these are all the same action.
