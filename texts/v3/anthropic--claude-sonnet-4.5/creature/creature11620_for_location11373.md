# The Inhabitants

The people are smooth-skinned and dark—not the brown-black of sun-exposed melanin but a gray-black that mirrors the basalt itself, as though their bodies have learned to become continuous with stone. Their skin has an unusual quality: it does not wrinkle when wet, does not prune at the fingertips even after hours in water. Instead it seems to shed rain in microscopic increments, maintaining a surface that is perpetually slick but never waterlogged. When they move, droplets fly from their limbs in arcs that catch what little light exists.

They are not tall. Adults average perhaps five feet, five and a half, their builds compact and dense. There is thickness to them—through the torso, through the limbs—not fat but a kind of structural solidness, as if their bones are heavier than expected, their muscles laid down in denser configurations. This gives them a low center of gravity, a stability on the wet stone that seems almost magnetic. They move with confidence across surfaces where an outsider would slip and stumble, their feet finding purchase through some combination of technique and physiology.

Their faces are broad, with wide cheekbones and strong jaws. Noses are flat and wide-nostriled, practical for breathing warm wet air. Eyes are large and dark—so dark that iris and pupil blur together, creating an effect of total blackness that reflects light in strange ways, sometimes seeming to glow faintly in the dimness. There is something amphibian in the set of these eyes, positioned slightly higher and more lateral than typical human arrangement, offering a wider field of vision. Eyelids are thick and heavy, blinking frequently, each blink a slow-motion closure that suggests underwater adaptation.

Mouths are wide but lips are thin. When closed, the mouth appears as little more than a seam in the face. When open—to eat, to shout into someone's ear, to breathe—the opening is startlingly large, revealing teeth that are uniform and slightly gray, strongly rooted, designed for tearing and grinding animal protein. The tongue is thick and dark. Saliva is copious and slightly viscous. They do not smile in the way outsiders would recognize as smiling, but there is an expression that involves a slight parting of lips and a relaxation around the eyes that seems to serve similar social function.

Hair grows sparsely. On the head, it is thick but limited to a strip running from forehead to nape, a natural mohawk of coarse black fiber that they often bind back or let hang in a single wet rope. The sides of the head are smooth, hairless, the temples and the areas behind the ears particularly so. Body hair is minimal—some in the pubic region, some under the arms, but far less than most human populations. This sparseness seems adaptive, reducing surface area for water retention. What hair exists never seems to grow long; it reaches a certain length and stops, as if each strand has a predetermined limit.

Their hands are remarkable. Fingers are slightly longer than proportional expectation, with an extra degree of flexibility in the joints. The skin on palms and fingertips is covered in fine ridges more pronounced than fingerprints, creating a gripping surface that functions wet or dry. Nails are thick and dark, almost claw-like but flattened, useful as tools for prying and scraping. The webbing between fingers extends slightly farther than baseline human, a vestigial suggestion of aquatic ancestry or adaptation. They can spread their fingers extraordinarily wide, creating a star-like hand shape that appears in many of their gestures.

Feet are similarly adapted. Toes are long and flexible, gripping stone surfaces the way hands might grip branches. The arches are high and pronounced, creating a natural spring in the step. The soles are thickened and tough, nearly leather-like even in children, though they still wear footwear for traversing the rougher sections of stone. When barefoot—which the children often are—they can navigate wet rock with the confidence of geckos, their toes spreading and contracting, finding micro-grips invisible to observation.

The children are miniature versions of the adults but with subtle differences. Their skin is slightly paler, more gray than black, darkening gradually through puberty. Their eyes are proportionally even larger, giving them an almost alien appearance in the dim light. They are louder than the adults, not in voice—no one is loud in voice here—but in movement, in the percussive quality of their play, their running feet, their hands slapping water. They laugh, though the sound is swallowed by the rain; you see the laughter in the shaking of small shoulders, in the opening of mouths, in the way they double over or lean into each other.

Infants are carried constantly in the first year, bound to the mother's or father's body in wraps that keep them secure and warm against the skin. They emerge into the world already adapted to the sound—they do not startle at the rain's roar, do not cry in distress at the noise. When they do cry, it is in brief bursts that are more felt than heard, the parent responding to the vibration against their body rather than to audio signal. By the time children can walk, they are already fluent in the gesture-language, their small hands forming symbols with remarkable precision.

The elderly are less visible but not absent. They show age differently than might be expected—the skin does not wrinkle or sag but rather seems to harden, becoming more stone-like, taking on a matte texture where youth is glossy. Movement becomes slower but remains sure-footed. The hair thins further, sometimes disappearing entirely from the head-strip. Eyes remain dark but sink deeper into the skull. There is a thinning overall, the dense solidity of adulthood giving way to a kind of compactness, as if the body is reducing itself to essentials.

Death, when it comes, happens quietly. Bodies are processed matter-of-factly—stripped of any objects, washed in the pool-water, then carried to specific buildings at the city's edge where they are laid in stone chambers. What happens after is not visible, but the buildings have openings near ground level where the scavenger animals come and go. The community does not mourn publicly, or if they do, the mourning takes forms not readable as grief—a particular arrangement of stones, perhaps, or a pattern of touches exchanged among the bereaved. Life continues immediately around death, neither hurried nor delayed.

Gender exists but its expressions are subtle. There are bodies that bear children and bodies that do not, anatomical differences that serve reproductive function. But in a place where everyone is wet and dressed in similar wrappings, where faces are similar and hair is minimal, gender does not announce itself visually. The gesture-language includes indicators for it when relevant, but much communication proceeds without gendering the subjects. Sexual pairing happens—you see couples moving together, sleeping together, working in partnership—but these bonds are neither advertised nor hidden. They simply are, one more thread in the dense social fabric.

Reproduction appears deliberate rather than frequent. You do not see pregnant bodies everywhere, nor swarms of infants. Family groups are small, often one or two children, sometimes none. Whether this is biological constraint or social choice is unclear, but the population seems stable, neither growing nor shrinking, maintained at a density that the basin can support given the animal resources available.

The social structure is egalitarian or close to it. There are no obvious hierarchies, no individuals dressed differently or dwelling in larger structures or receiving deference. Decisions seem to emerge from group consensus reached through long gestured discussions, many hands moving in the dim light, many faces turned toward each other. Some individuals are consulted more frequently—elders, perhaps, or those with particular skills—but this feels like practical acknowledgment of expertise rather than authority.

Work is communal but not rigidly organized. People drift toward tasks as needed—a group butchering an animal, another working hides, another repairing a section of causeway where stones have shifted. The work is done with tools held in common, stored in accessible buildings, used and returned. Theft seems not to exist, or if it exists, it is so rare as to be invisible. In a place where everyone's needs are similar and resources are communally managed, perhaps theft has no function.

Skill levels vary. Some individuals are clearly more adept at stone-working, their hands moving with practiced efficiency as they shape new tools or repair structures. Others excel at hunting, at reading animal behavior, at knowing where and when to place nets or set traps. Still others seem to specialize in the social work—the organizing of gatherings, the teaching of children, the mediation of disputes through gesture and touch. But no one does only one thing. The same person who excels at weaving might also hunt, might also help process the dead, might also stand in the courtyards performing gestured stories.

The stories themselves form a kind of cultural core. They are performed frequently, and certain individuals perform them with particular skill, their bodies becoming intensely expressive, their hands painting images in air that others watch with focused attention. The stories have a formulaic quality—certain gestures recur, certain sequences are repeated—suggesting they are traditional, passed down through generations unchanged or changing only glacially.

Some stories are clearly mythic or cosmological. They involve gestures toward the ground—creation from stone—and gestures upward—the emergence of rain, of clouds. They include movements that might represent the first humans, or humans arriving, or humans being made. The gestures are ambiguous enough to support multiple interpretations, but their emotional weight is clear in the stillness of the watching bodies, in the focused darkness of the watching eyes.

Other stories are historical or genealogical, involving specific individuals represented by specific gesture-sets. These individuals are depicted doing things—building, hunting, partnering, dying—their lives condensed into movement-poems that last minutes but suggest years. The watching community sometimes joins these tellings, particular individuals stepping forward to add gestures, to participate in the story's re-enactment. It becomes collaborative, a shared remembering.

Still other stories are instructional. A skilled hunter might tell through gesture the story of a recent hunt, showing how the prey moved, how the trap was set, how the killing was accomplished. Children watch these with intense focus, learning not just technique but the reading of gesture itself, the grammar of communication that allows complex information to pass without speech.

The gesture-language is sophisticated, equal to any spoken tongue in its ability to convey nuance. It includes signs for hundreds of animals, for tools, for actions, for qualities, for relationships. It has grammar—tense indicated by hand position, subject and object differentiated by movement direction, questions posed by facial expression and hand orientation. It can handle abstraction—there are gestures for "before," "after," "if," "why," "although." Philosophical discussions happen in this language, hands moving rapidly in debate while rain hammers down and faces remain neutral.

But the language is more than visual. In the close quarters where much communication happens—mouths near ears, bodies touching—there is a tactile component. A hand on a shoulder means one thing, on a forearm another. Pressure variation carries meaning. Duration carries meaning. Patterns of touch—tap-tap-slide, or press-release-press—function as words. In total darkness, in the interior of structures where no light penetrates, entire conversations happen through touch alone.

There is also a percussive component. With mouths near ears, people make sounds that are not words but markers—clicks, pops, hums that punctuate gestured communication or serve as emphasis. These sounds cut through the rain's roar at close range, providing an acoustic skeleton on which gesture hangs meaning. Children playing in the water use similar percussive signals to coordinate their games, their mouths producing rhythmic patterns that others respond to with movement.

The sensory world these people inhabit is radically different from dry-land existence. Vision is primary but operates in perpetual dimness, in a narrow range between barely-light and almost-dark. They are experts at reading subtle variations in gray, at detecting movement in poor visibility, at using peripheral vision to track multiple elements simultaneously. Their eyes have adapted or been selected for this, the large dark pupils gathering every available photon.

Hearing is both overwhelmed and refined. The rain's roar is a constant that they learn to hear through rather than merely hear. They develop the ability to parse layers—to detect the difference between rain on stone and rain on water, between rain falling freely and rain disrupted by a body moving through it. They can hear animal calls embedded in the overall cacophony, identifying species and direction. They can hear the footsteps of someone approaching from behind, the scrape of claws on rock, the specific splash-pattern of something entering water.

Touch is hyperacute. The constant wetness, rather than dulling sensation, seems to have sensitized it. They can detect temperature variations in water by touch, finding the slightly warmer or cooler channels. They can feel the texture differences in stone surfaces, identifying good grip-points in darkness. Their hands read the world continuously—feeling for tools, for food, for the subtle vibrations in stone that might indicate structural issues or animal presence below.

Smell operates in the warm wet air but differently than in dry environments. Scents are both amplified and muddied by humidity. They can smell the musk of animals, the tang of butchered meat, the faint acidity of their own bodies, the mineral flatness of the stone. They can smell illness, pregnancy, fear—the body's chemical signals dissolved in the ever-present moisture. Certain individuals seem particularly sensitive to smell, lifting their heads and flaring their wide nostrils, reading information others miss.

Taste is important in a culture that eats entirely animal protein. They can distinguish between dozens of species by taste, between fresh and spoiling, between safe and toxic. Some of the animals carry poisons that must be carefully avoided or prepared out. The community's collective knowledge of what is edible and how to prepare it is encyclopedic, passed down through tasting lessons where children are given tiny samples and taught to recognize flavors and textures.

Time is marked not by sun or season but by internal rhythms and communal consensus. Sleep happens in periods that might correspond to night, but without true darkness, the transition is social rather than environmental. When enough people have stopped moving, when the sound of activity falls below a certain threshold, others follow. Sleep buildings are communal, bodies lying close on stone platforms padded with cured hides, the warmth shared, the breathing synchronized. Dreams happen but are not discussed, or if discussed, the gestures for dreaming and remembering and imagining might be the same gesture.

Waking is similarly gradual and communal. People rise when others rise, when activity resumes, when the need for food or elimination or work draws them back into motion. There is no alarm, no signal. The city wakes like a single organism responding to its own metabolic needs.

Eating happens individually and communally both. People grab food as needed—a piece of smoked fish-analogue, a cluster of insect eggs, a strip of cured meat—chewing while working or walking. But there are also communal meals, groups gathering around fresh kills, sharing the meat raw or briefly heated in the protected flames, hands reaching into the carcass, mouths working, jaws strong. Marrow is sucked from bones. Organs are distributed according to protocols not immediately obvious—perhaps by need, perhaps by skill, perhaps by some other logic embedded in social practice.

Elimination is matter-of-fact. Certain buildings or certain sections of the causeways serve as latrines, the waste washed away by the constant water flow into drainage channels that carry it to the basin's edge or into underground streams. Privacy is minimal but also unnecessary—bodies performing their functions are simply bodies, unworthy of particular attention or shame.

Hygiene is continuous and passive. The rain washes everything constantly. People run their hands over their bodies, through their hair-strips, helping the water do its work. They bathe more intentionally in certain pools known for cleaner water, submerging and surfacing, rubbing skin briskly. They use sand and ash as abrading agents, scrubbing away dead skin and accumulated oils. They use animal fat as waterproofing for skin that will be exposed to extended water contact during certain work.

Illness exists but seems rare. Perhaps the constant washing prevents infection. Perhaps the population has achieved equilibrium with local pathogens. Perhaps something in the water or the air has prophylactic properties. When illness does occur—a child feverish, an adult unable to eat—the sick person is tended by the group, brought food and water, kept warm with extra wrappings and body-heat. Some recover. Some do not. The community accepts both outcomes with the same neutral expression they bring to everything.

Injury is more common. The wet stone is unforgiving. Falls happen. Tools slip. Animals bite or sting. Wounds are washed immediately in the abundant water, then treated with certain animal fats known to prevent infection. Serious injuries—broken bones, deep cuts—are dealt with practically. Splints are made from bone and binding. Wounds are stitched with sinew and bone needles. Amputations happen when necessary, performed with stone blades and cauterized with heat. Those who survive such procedures adapt, learning to work with one hand or to walk with a different gait, and the community accommodates them without particular notice.

Pain is endured quietly. You rarely hear cries of distress. When injury occurs, the injured person's face might tighten, eyes might close briefly, but vocalization is minimal. Whether this is cultural conditioning or physiological difference—perhaps their pain response is muted, their endorphin production more efficient—is impossible to determine. They touch the injured, hands on shoulders or heads, offering presence rather than words.

Pleasure is similarly muted in expression but clearly present. You see it in the way they move toward certain foods, in the lingering quality of certain touches, in the way bodies arrange themselves close together during rest periods. Sexual pleasure happens—couples disappear into structures, their bodies visible in silhouette through openings, moving together in familiar rhythms—but it is neither hidden nor displayed, simply another activity among activities.

Play exists primarily in children but also among adults. The children's play is physical—chasing, splashing, making patterns with stones, building tiny structures in shallow water that the rain immediately begins to dissolve. Adult play is more subtle—pattern-making with found objects, games involving the quick movement of stones from hand to hand, storytelling that is clearly meant to be humorous though the humor might not translate. Laughter happens but is silent or nearly so, the body shaking, mouth open, no sound escaping or only a faint huff of air.

Conflict is rare and when it occurs is resolved through gestured negotiation mediated by others. Physical violence is almost never seen. Perhaps the close quarters make it untenable—violence would disrupt the delicate social balance required for such dense cohabitation. Perhaps their culture has developed strong conflict-avoidance mechanisms. Perhaps their emotional range is different, not including whatever drives violence in other populations. When disagreements arise—over resource allocation, over partnership dissolutions, over perceived slights—they are worked through in long sessions where many hands move, many eyes watch, until resolution emerges and life continues.

There is a quality to their emotional expression that might be read as flatness but might also be depth hidden beneath surface calm. The rain has taught them that outward display achieves nothing—sound is swallowed, facial expressions are hard to read in the dim wet light. So emotion has moved inward or into other channels. It is in the quality of touch, the duration of a gaze, the way someone sits slightly closer or slightly farther. It is in the small choices—which tool to use, which path to take, which person to work beside. Emotion exists but in a minor key, everything muted by the rain's overwhelming roar.

The animals are partners and resources but not companions in the way of pets. No individual animals are named or singled out for particular relationship. The crab-things are harvested when needed, grabbed with quick hands, their claws broken off, their bodies consumed. The bird-things are netted or trapped, killed efficiently, plucked and gutted. The frogs are collected by the handful, their legs a delicacy. But there is no cruelty in this, no sport-killing or waste. Every animal taken is used completely—meat, bone, hide, organs, shell. Nothing is discarded that might have function.

The people are, in their way, beautiful. The smooth dark skin over dense muscle, the large dark eyes, the confident movement through rain and water—there is an aesthetic coherence to them, a sense that form has achieved perfect match with function. They are what humans might become if humans lived for generations in this basin, if selection pressured toward aquatic competence and communal density and sensory adaptation to dim wet constancy.

But they are also strange, alien even. The lack of emotional display, the reliance on gesture and touch, the comfortable inhabitation of unceasing noise—these create distance. An outsider watching them would struggle to read intent, to predict behavior, to understand the meaning beneath the surface actions. They live in a world that is physically close—bodies always near other bodies, work done in proximity, communication requiring touch—but psychologically they might be distant, their inner lives organized along vectors that an outsider cannot access.

They dream, presumably. They remember, clearly. They plan—the coordination of group activities requires planning, the preparation of food stores requires foresight. But whether they hope or despair, whether they long for change or embrace stasis, whether they wonder about the world beyond the rim or accept the basin as totality—these questions find no easy answers in their neutral faces, their matter-of-fact movements through the endless rain.

They are the people of the basin. They are the ones who live here, who have always lived here, who will continue living here in the eternal present of the-first-day-continuing. They are as much a part of this place as the stone and the water, as adapted and as fixed. They are inhabitants in the deepest sense—not residing in the basin but constituted by it, their bodies and cultures and consciousness shaped by this specific geology, this specific rain, this specific sound. They are what lives here. They are what has learned to survive and even to flourish in a place that came into being complete and has remained, ever since, perpetually arriving at itself, perpetually now.
