# What Lives There

## The Boundary Keeps

They are not guards because nothing here needs guarding. They are not residents because they do not reside. The Boundary Keeps exist in the transitional spaces—in the vertical shaft that descends from the mountain's peak, in the cracks where the dome meets stone, in the threshold between mist and air. 

You never see them whole. A hand, perhaps, gripping a vine as you look up—long-fingered, the skin having the same mineral sheen as the wet stone. The impression of a face in the mist, gone when the current shifts. A figure standing on the highest catwalk at the edge of your vision, but when you turn, only a salamander basking. They wear what might be the remnants of workers' clothing or might be growths that merely resemble fabric—canvas gone soft as moss, indistinguishable from the ferns that sprout from their seams.

The Boundary Keeps arrived with the seepage, or perhaps they were workers who never left, or perhaps the distinction is meaningless now. They move through the hollow with an intimacy that suggests not ownership but long cohabitation. You hear them more than see them: footsteps on the catwalks that don't quite match your own rhythm, creating a syncopation. The sound of breathing in the mist that's slower than yours, that pulls and releases moisture with a different efficiency. Sometimes, ascending to the higher platforms, you find pools of water on metal surfaces that haven't dripped there, arranged in patterns—circles, lines—that feel deliberate.

They tend, after a fashion. A catwalk that was impassable yesterday has had vines pulled aside today. A pool that was stagnant has been disturbed, water flowing again through a channel someone cleared. The rusted lunch pail has been moved—just slightly, inches—placed now where rain won't pool inside it. The amber bulbs that still function: you never see anyone change them, but neither do they burn out, as if something replaces their filaments with scavenged wire, rebuilds their ancient vacuums with patience and time.

The Keeps understand the machinery's language. When the great harmonic builds—wheels and pipes aligning—they stop, wherever they are in the hollow, and they hum with it. You hear their voices then, low and resonant, matching the metal's frequency, sustaining it a few seconds longer than the machinery alone could manage. When they move across certain sections of catwalk, the boards that should creak don't. When they pass certain valves, frozen for decades, the valves sometimes—not always, but sometimes—turn, just a degree or two, adjusting flow in ways that ripple through the system.

They seem to have divided the hollow among themselves, though you never see evidence of conflict or negotiation. One keeps to the higher reaches, where the seepage is freshest—you find their signs there: stones stacked in small cairns on the platforms, arrangements of bird feathers, wet footprints that lead to the dome's cracks and disappear into stone that should be solid. Another frequents the deep pools, the dark waters that descend into the mountain. That one you sometimes see beneath the surface: a shadow moving with the eyeless fish, staying down impossibly long, emerging silently at pool's edge to sit with the salamanders and watch the drowned machinery turn. A third haunts the marsh, the collapsed section—smaller than the others, or younger, or simply different. That one weaves reeds, you think. You find tight spirals of them left on rocks, intricate knots, constructions that have no obvious function but are definitely made.

Their number is uncertain. Three, from the territorial divisions. Or one, moving so fluidly through the space that it seems multiple. Or dozens, most of whom avoid you so completely you'll never know they're there. The writing on the walls—the newer marks, the ones that aren't dates and shift notes—might be their language. Or might be their way of speaking to the machinery, annotations on the factory's ongoing text.

They don't interfere with you directly, but they alter what you encounter. A path you walked safely yesterday becomes treacherous today—a catwalk section has been allowed to accumulate slick moss, or a vine hangs lower, forcing you to duck or choose another route. Then tomorrow, a rope appears—rough fiber, wet, tied to provide handholds where a jump was previously required. The rope is old and new at once, as if they maintain a supply of such things, stored in the dry pockets that must exist somewhere in this hollow, and deploy them according to logics you don't understand.

If you stay long enough—days, weeks—you begin to recognize individual presences. The one who smells of ozone and rust: you encounter them near the control panel, always. When you approach that section, the air develops a staticky quality, and the indicator lights behind their glass covers seem brighter, more insistent in their patterns. That Keep might be reading them, or feeding them, or simply witnessing—again, the distinction blurs.

The one who breathes in perfect rhythm with the machinery: you never see them, but you feel when they're near because the hollow's chaos resolves briefly into pure synchronization. All the disparate clanks and groans lock into a single complex rhythm, and in those moments the frogs go silent, listening, and the birds orient toward something you can't perceive. When that Keep moves away, the coherence dissolves, and the comfortable chaos returns.

The one who tends the white-barked trees: you find evidence in the form of pruned branches—cut cleanly, not broken—arranged at the trees' bases. The cuts are strategic; the trees grow healthier for them, their canopies spreading more evenly across the dome's curve. Where branches have been removed, new shoots emerge, guided by bent wires into optimal positions. The wires are scavenged from the machinery, still bright copper beneath the oxidation someone has cleaned away, and they're wound with a careful tension that speaks of understanding both metal and wood.

There is a place—a specific platform at mid-height, where three catwalks intersect—where you should not go. Not forbidden, exactly. But every time you approach it, things happen. The frogs' calls intensify to painful volumes. The machinery's groans become shrieks. The mist thickens until you can't see your own feet. A vine drops and brushes your face. The platform itself, when you finally reach it despite these discouragements, is empty. Just metal and moss. But it feels occupied, densely so, as if the air itself is pressed outward by presence. You don't stay. Something old lives there—older than the factory, perhaps. The Keeps might be keeping you from it, or keeping it from you. Or it might be one of them, the first or eldest or most transformed.

They eat, presumably. You never see them do it. But certain fish go missing—not the usual predation of salamanders and birds, but disappearances from the deeper pools, the larger individuals. The beetles, too—sometimes you find their iridescent shells arranged in piles, emptied cleanly, discarded with a neatness that doesn't match the scatter of bird feeding. The pale flowers: some of them are harvested, stems cut at the base, the blooms gone. Whether the Keeps eat these things, or trade them to something else, or use them in processes you haven't witnessed, remains opaque.

Their relationship with the fauna is complex. The birds don't flee from them the way they flee from you. The frogs go silent for you but continue chorusing when the Keeps pass—you know this because you hear the footsteps on the catwalk, the frogs still singing, the incongruence of it. The salamanders behave most strangely: sometimes they follow the Keeps' movements, hauling themselves along behind at a distance, as if learning routes or receiving instruction. The eyeless fish sometimes school in patterns that seem responsive to something you can't see, swirling in the clear pool's depths, tracing geometric forms.

One of the Keeps—or perhaps all of them, taking turns—maintains the furnace-fern, the glowing growth in the old boiler. You've never witnessed this maintenance, but the fern is too healthy, too vibrant, to be simply persisting. Something feeds it beyond the heat and minerals. Something prunes its edges so it doesn't overgrow and choke itself. When you visit the furnace-cave, the fern sometimes glows brighter, or darker, and you feel watched not by eyes but by attention itself, by the focused regard of something considering whether to allow your presence.

The Keeps might be what the factory workers became, remaining through the decades of abandonment, adapting as the hollow transformed, their bodies and purposes changing with the space. Or they might be what the mountain grew to interact with the factory, biological responses to the industrial intrusion, evolved or conjured to maintain the balance. Or they might have been here always, dwelling in the stone, and when humans carved this pocket and filled it with machinery, the Keeps simply... adjusted, incorporated the new elements into their existing patterns.

They leave gifts, if gifts is the right word. You find things placed where you'll encounter them: a particularly perfect gear, small enough to pocket, its teeth still sharp despite the rust. A piece of ceramic tile with a legible word: CAUTION. Three feathers bound with copper wire. A cup—not the rusted thermos cups, but a carved wooden cup, old and smooth—left beside a clean pool as if suggesting you drink. Whether these are offerings or communications or simple coincidences elevated by your need to find meaning, you can't determine.

Sometimes you hear them speak, or speak to each other, or to the hollow itself. The language is not one you know. It has the percussive quality of machinery—hard consonants like valve strikes, sibilants like steam escaping—but also the fluid rhythm of water, the organic decay of the frogs' calls. Words or sentences emerge from the background noise, then sink back into it, as if the Keeps' conversation is just another layer in the soundscape, no more or less distinct than the dripping or groaning.

On the rare occasions when you glimpse one clearly—in the shaft of direct light through the collapsed section, say, or reflected somehow in the still surface of a pool—they are human-shaped but wrong in small ways. Too tall, or too thin, or their proportions subtly off. Their skin has absorbed the hollow: rust-stained here, moss-green there, mineral-white where the water has most affected them. Their hair, if it is hair, moves independently of air currents, sways like underwater plants. Their eyes, when you see them, reflect the amber light with an intensity that seems internally generated, a glow from within rather than mere reflection.

They know you're there. Always. From the moment you descend the vertical shaft to the moment you climb back out, you are known and tracked and assessed. Not threateningly—they've made no move to harm, no gesture of hostility—but absolutely. You could not surprise them. You could not discover a section of the hollow they haven't anticipated you discovering. Your routes through the catwalks, your choices of which pools to investigate, your pauses to examine the machinery: all of it happens within a framework of their awareness.

Yet they allow you autonomy. They adjust things around you but don't constrain you. It's more like you're a new element they're incorporating into the system, the way the system incorporated the vegetation and fauna. You're being metabolized slowly, your presence digested, your impact on the hollow's balance assessed and responded to. If you stay long enough, perhaps you become another Keep. Or perhaps you simply become part of the ongoing negotiation between built and grown, another voice in the layered soundscape.

There are nights—because you stay sometimes, sleep on a high platform where the mist is less thick—when you wake to find them close. Not touching, not threatening, but near. A figure sitting on the platform's edge, legs dangling, facing away from you toward the hollow's interior. Several of them standing in a loose circle around you, not watching you but watching outward, as if guarding, though from what you can't imagine. Once, you woke to find someone had covered you with a sheet of canvas, dry somehow, that smelled of oil and age. When you sat up, the canvas slipped away, and there was no one visible, but the nearest catwalk still resonated with recent footsteps.

They have rhythms longer than yours. You measure time in hours, days at most. They measure it in the machinery's cycles—the great harmonics that occur every few hours, the longer patterns that take days to complete, the slowest rhythms that might span weeks. They've been here long enough to know these patterns intimately, to anticipate them, to work within them. When you act out of sync with the hollow's rhythms—climbing when you should rest, making noise during the harmonic's build—they don't stop you, but they notice. You feel the noticing as a weight, a attention that's faintly disapproving, or perhaps just curious about your disruption.

The youngest Keep—if that's what the marsh-dweller is—seems most interested in you. Their signs appear more frequently near where you sleep or rest. The reed spirals become more complex, incorporating materials you've touched: a thread from your clothing, a leaf you examined and discarded. Once, you found a construction that was clearly representational: the white-barked trees in miniature, made of bound twigs and moss, arranged around a pool (a cup of water), with tiny figures (beetles, positioned carefully) around the pool's edge. A model of the hollow, or part of it. A teaching tool, perhaps, or an offering of knowledge. You studied it for an hour, trying to understand its lesson, and when you looked away and back, a new element had appeared: a small figure that might represent you, made of wire and cloth, standing at the model's edge as if newly arrived.

The Keeps maintain boundaries you only discover by crossing them. You entered the deep pool once, swam down toward the underwater machinery, and the pressure—not water pressure but something else—became unbearable. Your lungs didn't demand air; your mind demanded exit. You surfaced gasping, and the nearest Keep (the deep-pool one, certainly) was sitting at the water's edge, not looking at you, but their presence felt like a statement: *Not there. Not yet. Not for you.*

Conversely, there are places they seem to want you to go. The control panel's vicinity: you're drawn back there repeatedly, and each time, you notice new details. The indicator lights' pattern, which you've started to document, seems to shift in response to the hollow's state—green when the water flows smoothly, amber when something's restricted, red when... you're not sure, but rarely, and usually before a gush from the high pipes. The Keep who haunts that area might be teaching you to read the panel, or might simply be pleased that someone's paying attention again, that the panel has a witness after decades of unwatched blinking.

They've made art, or what you interpret as art. Beyond the reed spirals, you find arrangements throughout the hollow: stones balanced in impossible configurations, sustained by some trick of weight and patience. Vines woven through machinery in patterns that serve no structural purpose but are undeniably deliberate, creating shapes—spirals, mostly, always spirals. Collections of rust fragments arranged by color gradient from orange through brown to deep black. A section of catwalk where someone has scraped away the moss in lines and curves, creating a design that's visible only from the platform above, a message to the birds or to something that flies.

One of them sings. Not words, not even the machine-language you've heard, but pure tone. You've heard it three times: always at the harmonic's peak, a voice that rises above the machinery's chord and sustains beyond it, carrying on alone for seconds after the wheels fall out of phase. The voice is human and not, vibrating at frequencies that make your bones resonate, that make the mist shiver visibly. The frogs go absolutely silent for that voice. The birds freeze. Even the machinery seems to quiet, as if listening to this thing it has birthed or attracted or been transformed into.

They might be trying to tell you something. The gifts, the adjustments to your path, the model in the marsh, the proximity at night—all of it might be communication, a slow conversation conducted at their tempo rather than yours. You're too brief, perhaps, too fast-moving for direct speech. So they leave signs and watch to see if you can read them, if you can slow down enough to receive what they're offering.

Or perhaps they're not thinking of you at all. Perhaps you're incidental to their work, their maintenance of this place, their long partnership with stone and water and machinery. Perhaps the gifts are meant for each other, and you just happen to find them. Perhaps the adjustments to the catwalks are for their own navigation, and you benefit by chance. Perhaps the model was made for the youngest Keep's own understanding, and your discovery of it was irrelevant.

But you don't believe that. There's intention in how things appear after you've expressed interest. When you spent an hour examining the white-barked trees, trying to understand how they survive here, you found pruning tools the next day—old shears, cleaned and oiled, placed on a platform near the trees as if offered for your use, or as if demonstrating: *This is how we care for them.* When you expressed frustration—spoke it aloud to the empty air—about a gap in the catwalks that blocked your route, you found the rope the next morning.

They respond. They listen. They're patient with your presence in a way that suggests they've decided you're acceptable, or potentially useful, or at least not harmful enough to drive away. You're being allowed to witness, to learn, perhaps to join. The hollow is a ship they're stewarding through time, and you're a passenger they've decided to tolerate, even instruct, even—possibly—welcome.

There's a question you can't answer: are they staying in the hollow, or are they keeping the hollow? Are they prisoners of this place, unable to leave, adapted so completely that the outer world would kill them? Or are they guardians, here by choice or by nature, ensuring the factory-garden continues its strange existence? Do they maintain the balance because they must, or because they love it, or because it's simply what they are, their purpose and being identical?

You suspect they couldn't answer either. They've been here long enough that origin and purpose have become irrelevant. They are the Keeps because they keep, and what they keep is this: the hollow. The balance. The slow transformation of industry into ecosystem. The machinery's grinding song. The water's endless cycle. The frogs' chorus and the birds' nests and the salamanders' territories and the fish in the deep. The white-barked trees and the glowing ferns and the pale flowers. The mist and the warmth and the amber lights. The whole impossible pocket, this place that shouldn't exist but does, maintained in its existence by attention and labor and presence that has continued for so long it's become part of the geology, as permanent as the stone, as ongoing as the seepage.

And if you're here long enough, if you descend the shaft enough times, if you learn to read the indicator lights and time your movements to the machinery's rhythms, if you accept the gifts and follow the adjusted paths and listen during the harmonics—perhaps you begin to keep it too. Perhaps you find yourself clearing a channel that's become blocked. Perhaps you guide a tendril of vine away from a vulnerable gear. Perhaps you stack a few stones in a pattern that pleases something in you that's older than your arrival here. Perhaps you start to notice you can hold your breath longer, that your skin is developing faint green traces where the mist touches it most often, that you're hearing frequencies you didn't hear before.

Perhaps the Keeps were all visitors once. Perhaps the hollow makes them over time, transforms tourists and explorers and the curious into maintenance, into function, into presence. Perhaps there's a Keep somewhere in the hollow who remembers descending the shaft for the first time, remembers being separate from this place, remembers a world outside—but that memory is old now, less substantial than the immediate reality of the water that needs routing, the vine that needs pruning, the harmonic that's building toward its peak.

Perhaps you'll remember this: standing in the mist, trying to write down what lives here, while all around you the machinery grinds and the frogs sing and somewhere in the hollow, someone who was or is or will be human tends the impossible garden with patience measured in decades, with love measured in the gradual perfection of a system, with purpose that's become indistinguishable from being itself.

They are the Boundary Keeps. They keep the boundaries between stone and air, between machine and organism, between intention and accident, between past and present. They keep the threshold between the world outside and this pocket, ensuring that what happens here continues happening, that the balance holds, that the hollow persists in its strange perfection.

And they keep on. That's the essential thing. Whatever they were, whatever they've become, they continue. They'll be here when you leave, when you forget, when you die. They'll be here when the mountain itself begins to seriously fail, and possibly they'll still be here at the very end, the last ones, turning the last wheel, guiding the last trickle of water, singing the last note of the final harmonic before the hollow opens to sky or closes in stone or cools into silence.

For now, they keep. And the hollow, kept, continues its transformation, its slow gorgeous decay into something new, witnessed and tended by those who have become part of what they witness, inseparable from what they tend, the Keeps and the kept merged into a single ongoing act of maintenance that looks like devotion, that functions as love, that simply is—for as long as water seeps and stone endures and somewhere in the mountain's heart, the heat continues rising through the dark.
