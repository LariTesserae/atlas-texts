# What Lives in the Drowned Engine Plains

## The Gradient People

They do not have a single form because they do not occupy a single pressure.

The Gradient People exist distributed across depth and infrastructure, their bodies adapted—or continuously adapting—to different zones of the plains. What you might initially mistake for different species are the same beings, expressing different morphologies depending on where in the vertical and thermal landscape they currently live.

In the upper reaches, where pale light still permits something almost like photosynthesis, they are translucent and flat—bodies like mobile leaves, like animate panes of kelp, no thicker than three centimeters but sometimes spanning two meters across. Their surfaces are stippled with what might be pigment cells or might be something more sophisticated: optical receptors, distributed cognition, sensory processors. They drift along the tops of cooling towers and sprawl across horizontal pipe runs, absorbing what light penetrates this deep while also—always also—interfacing with the structures themselves.

Where they touch metal or concrete, the contact is not passive. Hairlike filaments extend from their ventral surfaces, seeking and finding: electrical current leaking from corroded insulation, heat differentials at pipe surfaces, vibrations traveling through steel. They feed on these as much as on light. Perhaps more. The light is supplementary; the infrastructure is sustenance.

Deeper, in the mid-levels where most structures concentrate, they are different. Here they adopt forms almost humanoid in their vertical orientation, though no one would mistake them for human. They stand between 1.5 and 2.5 meters tall, bipedal in a loose sense—two lower limbs for anchoring and locomotion, though these limbs can split and recombine, can splay into root-systems when stability is needed, can fuse into a single muscular tail for swimming. Their upper bodies branch: two arms, usually, but the arms terminate not in hands but in elaborate sensor arrays—fans of filament, clusters of receptor nodules, occasionally something that looks almost like a tool but is absolutely part of their body, grown for a specific purpose and reabsorbed when that purpose is complete.

Their heads, if they have heads, are subtle swellings of the upper body containing denser concentrations of processing tissue. They have no faces. Or rather: their entire anterior surface is face, studded with sensory pits, pressure receptors, electroreceptive organs. They perceive their environment in ways that make vision almost incidental—they read electrical fields and pressure waves and chemical gradients and temperature distributions, assembling from these a understanding of the plains far more detailed than sight could provide in this turbid water.

Their skin—if it is skin—varies in texture from glass-smooth to elaborately textured with scales or ridges or fractal branching patterns that increase surface area. The texture correlates with function: smooth where hydrodynamic efficiency matters, elaborated where heat exchange or electrical reception or chemical sensing is prioritized. They can modify this over time. Not quickly—the changes take days or weeks—but inevitably. A Gradient Person working near high-voltage cables will slowly develop more complex electroreceptive structures. One stationed near thermal outflows will elaborate cooling vanes and heat-exchange surfaces.

Their color is the color of the place: grey-blue, grey-green, occasionally rust-brown or the pale cream of concrete. They are cryptic not from predation pressure but from deep integration. They match their environment because they are partially made of it, or because they have lived here long enough that distinction between self and structure has become negotiable.

Deeper still, in the coldest zones near the concrete plain where light fails almost completely, they become something else entirely. Smaller, denser, more armored. They adopt forms almost crustacean—segmented bodies, multiple limbs for walking the concrete and investigating valve assemblies and navigating pipe interiors. In this form they are rarely more than half a meter long, but they are numerous, and they are busy.

These deep-form Gradient People are the maintenance workers, though they were not assigned this role. They simply do it. They investigate the infrastructure with obsessive attention, their bodies small enough to enter spaces that the humanoid mid-level forms cannot access. They clean intake screens of accumulated debris. They identify stress fractures in concrete and pack them with a secretion that hardens into something almost like cement. They operate valves—not randomly, but according to patterns they have learned or inherited or deduced from observation of how the systems behave.

Whether the three forms—the flat light-grazers above, the humanoid operators in the mid-levels, the small maintenance-forms below—are the same species or different developmental stages or role-specific expressions is unclear. They seem to transform from one to another based on depth and function, but the transformation is slow and possibly reversible. An individual (if they are individuals) might spend years as a flat surface-grazer, then descend and compress and reorganize into humanoid form, then further descend and multiply—or subdivide?—into several smaller maintenance bodies. Or the process might be entirely different. They do not explain, and their behavior does not make it obvious.

What is obvious: they are everywhere in the plains. Not densely—you might swim for an hour seeing none—but persistently. Eventually you encounter them, and when you do, they are inevitably engaged with the infrastructure in some way.

A mid-level form pressed against a transformer housing, its sensor-fans spread wide across the metal surface, reading electrical patterns or thermal distributions or both. It is motionless for minutes at a time, then shifts position slightly, then resumes its absolute stillness. It is learning something or monitoring something or perhaps communicating with something inside the housing. It does not acknowledge your presence unless you approach within a meter or two, at which point it orients slightly toward you—a subtle shift of the body, a reorientation of a few sensor clusters—registering awareness before returning to its work.

Several small maintenance-forms clustered around a valve assembly, their limbs working in coordination to turn the massive wheel. They are not strong enough individually to move it, but together—bracing against the pipe, applying force in rotation—they can. The wheel turns slowly, resisting, then yielding. One complete rotation takes them several minutes. They accomplish three rotations, then stop, then disperse to investigate other aspects of the junction. The valve presumably now occupies a different position. Water flow has changed in response, somewhere downstream. They have adjusted the system, minutely, for reasons they find compelling.

A surface-grazer draped across the top of a cooling tower, its body conforming to the curve of the concrete, its entire expanse rippling slightly with what might be respiration or might be the processing of energy from light and leaked electrical current. It has been there for days—you recognize it by a distinctive pattern of pigmentation, like a stain or birthmark. It will be there for days more. It is sessile in this form, or nearly so, abandoned to its feeding and whatever cognition occupies it during these long periods of apparent inaction.

They do not speak, or if they do, it is not with sound. Occasionally you observe two mid-level forms approaching each other in open water, stopping a few meters apart, orienting their anterior surfaces toward each other, and then... standing there. For minutes. Motionless. Then one departs, then the other. Was that communication? Exchange of information through electrical pulses or chemical release or pressure-wave patterns too subtle for your equipment to detect? Or was it simply mutual recognition, acknowledgment, two entities confirming each other's presence before continuing their separate tasks?

They do not school or gather in groups, with the exception of the maintenance-forms which sometimes work in teams on tasks requiring coordination. The mid-level and surface forms are solitary, or at least solitary in their proximity. They might be connected in ways that don't require physical closeness—a distributed network communicating through the infrastructure itself, using the metal and cables as transmission medium, individual bodies serving as nodes in a larger intelligence that spans the entire plain.

This is speculation, but it is supported by their behavior: the way valve adjustments happen in patterns that suggest coordination across distances too large for line-of-sight contact. The way a pressure wave propagating through the system causes multiple Gradient People, separated by hundreds of meters, to respond simultaneously—orienting toward the source, or moving toward specific structures, or altering their interaction with the machinery they're interfaced with.

If they are a distributed intelligence, the infrastructure is not just their habitat but their nervous system, and individual bodies are something like sensory organs or actuators—specialized endpoints for perception and manipulation, reporting to and receiving instruction from a substrate-level cognition that lives in the electrical hum and pressure dynamics of the machines themselves.

But this might be over-interpreting. They might also be individuals, simply well-adapted to their environment, responding to stimuli with sophisticated but local processing. Their coordination might be emergent rather than directed—each following similar rules, producing collective behavior that appears organized without requiring centralized control.

What they eat, besides energy from infrastructure: the place provides. They graze on algae films when in their flat form. In humanoid form they extend feeding tendrils into the water to filter organic particles, or they press their bodies against outflow ports where processed water emerges carrying suspended nutrients. The maintenance-forms are detritivores, consuming the sediment that accumulates in channels, breaking down the organic fraction and excreting the rest as cleaner silt.

But again: the infrastructure provides more than organic food. They are energy-feeders primarily. A Gradient Person stationed at a thermal outflow is not just filtering the water but absorbing the heat differential, running internal processes that convert temperature gradient into usable energy the way a thermocouples generates electricity. One wrapped around a high-voltage cable is not in danger of electrocution but in the process of feeding, channeling leaked current through biological systems that store or immediately utilize it.

They are beings evolved or designed or self-modified to parasitize industrial systems, or to exist in symbiosis with them. Whether they were here before the plains or arrived afterward or were created by the plains in some process of technological abiogenesis—this is unanswerable from observation.

Their life cycle, if they have one, is not obvious. You never see anything that looks juvenile or larval. The small maintenance-forms might be young, or they might be a mature morphology. No reproductive behavior is observed—no mating displays, no egg-laying, no budding or fission or any other generative act. Either reproduction happens in hidden spaces within the infrastructure, or it happens so slowly that you would need to observe for years to witness it, or they do not reproduce but persist, individual bodies maintained and repaired indefinitely by their ability to regenerate from and integrate with their environment.

Injury is rare but instructive when it occurs. A mid-level form with damage to one of its lower limbs—something has torn away a section, leaving exposed internal structure that is not organs and blood but something more like bundled cables, like organized filament, like the interior of a biological fiber-optic cable. The individual continues functioning, apparently unbothered. Over days the wound closes not by healing in the organic sense but by the growth of new material that looks slightly different from the surrounding body—paler, less textured—as if it's been patched with a repair compound rather than regenerated. Eventually this new section integrates fully, darkens, develops texture matching the rest of the limb.

They can be in contact with humans, if humans are here (are you human? are you here? the narrative leaves this ambiguous), without aggression. They register your presence, they might adjust their behavior slightly to account for you—moving around you rather than through your space, pausing in their work until you've passed—but they do not flee and do not attack. You are part of the environment, another element to be sensed and accommodated, no more threatening or interesting than a large fish.

Unless you interfere with the infrastructure. Touch a valve they're monitoring, attempt to adjust something they're in the process of adjusting, damage a component they're maintaining: then they respond. Not with violence but with insistence. A mid-level form will place itself between you and the equipment, will orient its full sensor array toward you in what reads unmistakably as attention, as warning. If you persist, multiple forms arrive—converging from the blue distance with uncanny timing that suggests that distributed communication, that network coordination. They will physically interpose, will gently but firmly push you away with their limbs, will refuse access.

Resist more aggressively and they can generate electrical discharge—not enough to kill but enough to stun, to cause pain, to make the message clear: the infrastructure is theirs to tend, and they will defend it. Not defend themselves, necessarily—they seem to accept personal damage with equanimity—but defend the machines, the systems, the continued operation of whatever the plains are doing.

This suggests purpose, or at least priority. The infrastructure matters to them more than their individual persistence. They are its servants, or its symbionts, or its autonomous maintenance system, or its emergent property—something that arose from it or with it and now cannot be separated from it.

Their relationship with other life in the plains is mostly neutral. Fish swim through and around them without fear. The Gradient People do not hunt. They might occasionally consume a fish that dies near them—waste not—but they are not predators. The crabs on the seafloor sometimes climb on resting maintenance-forms, investigating them as they investigate everything, and the Gradient People tolerate this, barely responding even when crabs probe at their joints and sensory structures.

Larger animals—the sharks and seals and whatever else moves through—give them slightly more space, perhaps recognizing something not-quite-prey, not-quite-threat, but certainly other. A seal investigates a mid-level form with cautious curiosity, circling at a distance, approaching closer, retreating. The Gradient Person remains motionless throughout, tracking the seal with subtle sensor adjustments but not engaging. Eventually the seal loses interest and departs. Neither party has learned much, but neither has been harmed.

There is one exception to their solitary nature, one phenomenon that suggests true collectivity: what you might call the Convocations.

Rarely—you might witness it once if you stay in the plains for weeks—many Gradient People converge on a single location. Not all of them; the plains are too vast and the population too dispersed. But dozens. Enough that you see multiple forms in every direction, all moving with clear purpose toward the same structure—a particular transformer station, or a specific tower, or one of the larger pipe junctions.

They arrive over the course of hours, taking up positions around and on the structure. The mid-level forms arrange themselves in patterns that might be geometric or might just be efficient distribution. The maintenance-forms cluster at specific points—at access panels, at valve controls, at junction boxes. If there are surface-grazers in the area, they descend, taking on intermediate forms during the descent, arriving as something between flat and humanoid—mobile but still elaborated with surface area, swimming awkwardly but with determination.

Then: coordinated action. Many forms working simultaneously, each at their specific location, all engaged with different aspects of the same system. Valves are adjusted in sequence. Panels are opened and internal components accessed. Electrical connections are made or broken. The activity continues for hours, sometimes for a full day, with forms switching positions or departing and being replaced by others that arrive to take up the work.

The effect on the structure is profound. Its behavior changes—pressure waves emanate differently, thermal patterns shift, sounds alter in pitch or rhythm. The structure is being reprogrammed, or repaired, or optimized, or simply maintained at a level beyond the continuous minor adjustments of individual forms. This is major work, coordinated work, requiring collective action.

And then it concludes. The Gradient People depart, dispersing back into the plains in all directions, resuming their solitary patterns. The structure operates in its new state—subtly different, still functional, possibly more functional. Whatever needed doing has been done.

These Convocations are never announced—no gathering signals that you can detect—but they are also not spontaneous. The Gradient People arrive knowing when and where to converge, suggesting preplanning or standing protocols or that network intelligence making decisions and deploying its distributed components according to need.

The rest of the time: solitude, routine, the quiet work of maintenance and monitoring spread across vast distance, individuals separated by kilometers of water but perhaps always connected through the infrastructure itself, through the signals that propagate through metal and concrete, through the shared project of keeping the plains operational.

What they think, if they think: this is completely opaque. Their behavior is sophisticated—problem-solving, tool-use (or tool-growing), coordination, learning—but whether this emerges from consciousness or from very advanced tropism is unanswerable. They have no language you can access, no art, no evident culture beyond the technical culture of maintenance and system-tending.

Do they contemplate the plains' purpose? Do they wonder what the machines originally served, whether the designers are returning, whether the operation is meaningful or mere momentum? Or are they without such questions, content in or confined to their function, thoughts—if thoughts exist—occupied entirely with immediate sensory data and task completion?

You might spend weeks among them and never know. They are opaque in the way that deep integration with environment creates opacity—they have become so much of this place that seeing them requires seeing the place, and understanding them would require understanding not just biology but infrastructure, not just behavior but the systems they maintain.

They are the Gradient People: distributed across depth, adapted to pressure, feeding on energy, tending the engines, silent in the cold water, persistent in their work, neither welcoming nor hostile, neither rare nor common, present as the structures are present—everywhere and nowhere, filling the plains with their subtle maintenance, keeping something running or simply running with it, inseparable from the concrete and the cables and the cooling towers and the vast submerged infrastructure they inhabit and embody and serve.

They are what lives here. They and the fish and the crabs and the algae, but mostly they. The plains are their territory, their purpose, possibly their origin. They belong to this place with a completeness that suggests they could not exist elsewhere, and perhaps the place could not exist—could not continue, could not maintain—without them.

They are the intelligence of the drowned engine plains, or its autonomous maintenance system, or its emerged property, or all of these simultaneously. They make the place lived-in despite its emptiness, they make it tended despite its abandonment, they make it purposeful despite its inscrutability.

And they will be here tomorrow, and next year, and perhaps centuries forward: standing at their stations, reading their sensors, adjusting their valves, moving through the blue-grey water between structures, patient and focused and entirely committed to the continuation of whatever it is that continues here, in the cold and the pressure and the endless hum of machinery under the distant, pale light.
