# The Inhabitants of the Breathing Plateau

What lives here has learned the plateau's patience.

## The Walkers

They are human-shaped, or were once, or will be. It's difficult to say which. Their relationship to humanity—if humanity is even the right referent—has been complicated by generations, by centuries, by timeframes that make ancestry a kind of myth.

They are tall. Taller than you expect, even when you see them clearly. Seven feet is common, eight feet not rare. But the tallness is in the limbs—the torsos are proportional to a shorter frame, compact and efficient, while the legs extend in an elegant disproportion that makes their walking gait look like slow-motion wading. The arms, too, are long, hanging past their knees when standing, the fingers reaching nearly to their ankles.

Their skin—if it is skin—has taken on the plateau's coloration. Not green, exactly, but no longer quite any color that exists away from this place. A gray-green-brown that shifts slightly depending on moisture, darkening when wet (which is always), lightening on the rare dry surfaces of their bodies (which are few). The texture is smooth but not quite mammalian. Closer to amphibian: slightly permeable, slightly luminous with moisture, reading less as a barrier and more as a membrane through which the plateau's wetness continuously passes.

They grow things.

All of them, constantly, without trying. Moss colonizes their shoulders and the tops of their heads. Lichens pattern their backs in mandalas of gray and pale green. Small plants—species you recognize from the mat—root in the folds behind their knees, in their armpits, anywhere moisture and friction create a suitable microclimate. They don't remove these growths. To do so would be like removing part of themselves. A Walker who has stayed still for days might rise with a pelt of vegetation across their back and scalp, a mobile garden, a small travelling ecosystem.

Their hair, if they have hair, is indistinguishable from certain fibrous water plants. Long, dark greenish-brown strands that hang wet and heavy, sometimes tangled with actual aquatic vegetation, sometimes braided loosely with what might be intentional decoration or might be simply the knots that form naturally. Some Walkers have this plant-hair growing only from their heads. Others have it emerging from their forearms, their calves, their shoulder blades—anywhere hair might grow on a human, they grow these ambiguous strands that are part body and part plant.

Their eyes are large. Adapted to the dim light, the pupils enormously dilated, the irises colors you don't have names for—deep amber, gray-green, a particular shade of pale brown that seems to capture and hold the plateau's diffuse glow. They blink slowly, infrequently. When they look at you—and they will, if you encounter them, though the encounter might be at a distance—the gaze is direct but not intrusive, curious but not demanding. They look at you the way you might look at an interesting plant or an unfamiliar animal: with attention, but without expectation.

They don't speak. Or rather, they don't speak in ways that use words.

Their mouths are functional—they eat, they drink, they breathe through them when necessary. But vocalization seems to be a lost art or a never-acquired one. Instead, they communicate through touch, through gesture, through patterns of movement, and through something else: a kind of humming.

Not humming with the voice, exactly. It comes from deeper—from the chest, from the belly, from the resonant cavities of their long bodies. A subsonic vibration more than a sound, barely audible but strongly felt if you're close enough. They modulate it somehow: short pulses, long sustained notes, variations in rhythm and intensity. It travels well through the mat. A Walker humming on one hummock can be felt by another Walker standing on the mat half a mile away, the vibration carrying through the interconnected plant substrate like whales singing through water.

They use this humming to maintain awareness of each other across vast distances. Not communication in the sense of exchanging complex information—more like whales or elephants, maintaining contact, saying "I am here," receiving back "I am here also," creating a dispersed web of presence across the plateau's vastness.

When they do encounter each other—and the encounters are rare, given the distances they keep—they touch. Extensively. Hands on shoulders, on faces, on backs. Leaning together, pressing foreheads, intertwining fingers. The touches last a long time, minutes or hours, with both participants still and quiet, eyes closed, breathing synchronized. Something passes between them in these contacts: information, perhaps, or comfort, or simply confirmation of mutual existence.

After touching, they often separate again, walking in different directions, returning to solitude. The plateau is vast enough to accommodate their need for space.

## Their Bodies and Rhythms

The Walkers' physiology has adapted to the plateau's conditions in ways that blur the distinction between adaptation and transformation.

Their feet have become specialized instruments for reading the mat. Broad, with long toes that splay wide, webbing between them (thin, translucent, allowing water to pass through), and soles that are both tough enough to walk endlessly and sensitive enough to feel the subtle variations in the substrate. They can detect the difference between stable mat and unstable mat by touch. They can sense the hollow spaces before stepping on them. They can find the warm pools in darkness by feeling the slight temperature differential through their feet.

The soles sometimes grow small root-like projections—not roots exactly, but filaments that extend an inch or so into the mat when the Walker stands still for long periods. These might provide nutrients, might exchange moisture, might simply anchor them. When a Walker lifts their foot after standing for hours, these filaments retract or break off, dissolving back into the substrate.

They eat the plateau, but sparsely.

Their diet consists of whatever the mat offers: tender shoots of certain plants, the fruiting bodies of fungi, the soft inner growth of the towers, the protein-rich insects and small amphibians that live in the pools. They eat with their hands, slowly, without tools, chewing thoroughly, their digestion seemingly able to process the tough, fibrous plant matter that would defeat a normal human gut.

But they don't eat much. Their metabolism has slowed to match the plateau's pace. They might eat once every few days, grazing as they walk, taking a bit here and a bit there, never depleting any one area. Their bodies have become efficient at extracting moisture and nutrients from minimal intake, at storing energy in their tissues for the long walks between feeding.

Some of them appear not to eat at all. These are usually the ones who stay still the longest, who allow the most extensive gardens to grow on their bodies. There's a suspicion—unverifiable, but persistent—that they might be photosynthesizing. That the plants growing on them might be symbionts, sharing nutrients absorbed from the sun (what little penetrates the clouds) and from the air. That the relationship between Walker and plant has become so intimate that the distinction between feeding and being fed upon has dissolved into mutual sustenance.

Their breathing synchronizes with the plateau's breath over time.

New Walkers—those recently arrived, those not yet fully adapted—breathe at their own rhythm, faster than the plateau's slow swell and subside. But the longer they stay, the more their respiration adjusts. Their breath deepens, slows, matches the four- or six-hour cycle of the mat's expansion and contraction. Eventually, they breathe in when the plateau breathes in, out when it breathes out, becoming part of the vast respiratory system, their individual lungs functioning as tiny cells in the plateau's collective metabolism.

They sleep, but not in ways that would be recognizable as sleep to an outsider.

They lower themselves onto the mat—carefully, slowly, choosing spots that feel right to their sensitive feet—and arrange themselves in positions that seem deliberately composed. On their backs, usually, arms at their sides or crossed over their chests, legs straight or slightly bent at the knee. Sometimes several Walkers will sleep in proximity, arranged in patterns: circles, lines, spirals. Whether these patterns have meaning or are purely aesthetic or serve some function unknown is unclear.

In sleep, they become very still. Their breathing slows even further, sometimes to the point where it seems to stop entirely, their chests rising and falling only once every several minutes. The plants on their bodies grow more actively during sleep, as if taking advantage of the stillness. Moss spreads slightly. Small roots establish. In the morning—if morning is the right word—they wake with more green on them than they had the night before.

And they dream. This is evident even to an observer. Their eyes move beneath closed lids, rapid and searching. Their fingers twitch. Small sounds escape them—not words, but murmurs, sighs, fragments of the humming vibration. Sometimes they smile, or grimace, or shed tears that run sideways across their temples into the moss growing at their hairlines.

What they dream about, no one knows. They don't speak of it after waking. But sometimes, after sleep, a Walker will change direction, walking with purpose toward some destination that seems to have been revealed or decided in the dream state.

## Their Dwellings and Artifacts

The Walkers don't build new structures. The plateau's existing ruins are sufficient for their occasional needs.

They shelter in the ancient buildings when they encounter them, staying for a few days or a few weeks before moving on. They don't modify these spaces—don't repair the collapsed roofs, don't clear the vegetation from the interiors. They simply inhabit them as they are, sleeping inside the stone walls, sitting in the doorways during the brightest hours, leaving again without ceremony.

But they do leave marks. Subtle ones.

Small arrangements of stones in the corners. Three stones stacked, or five laid in a line, or seven arranged in a circle. The arrangements seem deliberate but not symbolic—more like gestures, like saying "I was here" without needing anyone to read the message.

Sometimes they leave plants. Not growing plants, but cut stems laid carefully on the stone, or particularly beautiful leaves pressed into cracks in the walls, or seed pods placed on window ledges. These offerings (if that's what they are) decay quickly in the moisture, becoming part of the mat again within days, but while they're fresh they suggest something: gratitude, perhaps, or acknowledgment, or simply the Walker's sense that the space should be marked somehow before leaving it.

They do create one kind of object: the resonators.

These are sections of the hollow towers, cut somehow (you never see them doing the cutting, never find tools, but the cuts are clean and deliberate) into cylinders of specific lengths. Each Walker carries one, or several, hung from their bodies on cords woven from plant fiber. The cylinders are usually two or three feet long, four to six inches in diameter, open at both ends, the interiors smooth or lined with dried moss.

When blown into, these resonators produce tones similar to but distinct from the natural tower-songs. Each Walker's collection of resonators is tuned differently—different lengths, different interior diameters, different harmonics. They play them occasionally, especially during the twilight hours or in fog when visibility drops. Long, sustained notes that carry across the mat, joining with the ambient tower-song to create temporary compositions that last as long as the Walker continues to blow.

Sometimes, when multiple Walkers are within range of each other, they play together. Not in unison—there's no attempt at rhythm or melody in a conventional sense—but in a kind of mutual awareness, each adding their tones to the others', creating dense harmonic clouds that drift across the plateau and slowly fade as the players tire or lose interest.

The resonators serve practical purposes too: calling to distant Walkers, marking territory or presence, perhaps even navigation by echo in the fog. But their primary function seems aesthetic or meditative, a way of participating in the plateau's soundscape, of adding intentional sound to the accidental music of wind and water.

## Their Solitude and Congregation

The Walkers maintain vast distances between themselves. Population density is nearly impossible to calculate—the plateau is too large, the Walkers too mobile—but a rough estimate might be one Walker per hundred square miles, or one per thousand. Maybe fewer. Days of walking might pass without encountering another.

This spacing seems intentional. When two Walkers' paths converge and they meet, the meeting is peaceful, involving the extended touching described earlier, but afterward they invariably separate, choosing different directions, re-establishing distance. It's as if proximity itself creates discomfort, or as if they each carry a personal territory that must be maintained at all costs.

And yet, they're aware of each other. The subsonic humming creates a network of presence. A Walker alone on the plateau is alone physically but not socially—they can feel the vibrations of others, sometimes dozens of others, each at their own distance, each contributing their frequency to the collective awareness. This creates a paradoxical community: together through separation, connected through distance, social through solitude.

But there are exceptions. Rare congregations that break the pattern.

Perhaps once in a generation—and generations are long here; Walkers seem to live for centuries—the Walkers gather. Not all of them, but many. Dozens, sometimes hundreds, converging on a particular location over weeks or months of walking. The location varies. Sometimes it's a cluster of towers. Sometimes it's one of the better-preserved ruins. Sometimes it's simply a point on the mat that has no obvious distinguishing features but that somehow calls them.

These gatherings last for days or weeks. The Walkers stand or sit or lie in proximity, closer than they would ever tolerate normally, sometimes touching, sometimes not, but always oriented toward each other in patterns that suggest intentionality. They hum together, creating vibrations so intense that the mat itself resonates, small ripples spreading outward from the congregation site. They play their resonators in massive, overlapping harmonies. They don't eat. They barely sleep. They simply... gather.

And then, as if by unanimous silent agreement, they disperse. Over the course of a day or two, the congregation dissolves, Walkers walking away in all directions, returning to their solitary wanderings, re-establishing the distances they maintain the rest of the time.

What happens during these gatherings is unclear to any outside observer. The Walkers don't perform rituals that would be recognizable as such. They don't exchange objects. They don't seem to be celebrating or mourning or worshipping. They're simply together, intensely present to each other, and then they're not.

Afterward, sometimes, changes occur. Walkers who were walking in circles begin walking in straight lines toward distant destinations. Walkers who seemed purposeful become aimless. Occasionally, a Walker begins the long journey toward an edge of the plateau, walking with determination toward the cliffs, disappearing into the white void below in what might be suicide or might be migration to some other plateau or might be something else entirely.

## The Children (if that's what they are)

Reproduction among the Walkers is observed rarely and understood poorly.

Sometimes, after a gathering, one of the Walkers walks with a companion. Not another adult Walker, but something smaller: child-sized, though whether it's actually a child is uncertain.

These small ones are similar to the adults in basic morphology—same long limbs, same gray-green skin, same large eyes—but different in texture and behavior. Their skin is smoother, less colonized by plants. Their movements are more erratic, less measured. They don't hum yet, or their humming is higher-pitched, more tentative. They follow the adult Walker closely, rarely straying more than a few yards, learning by observation.

Where they come from is a mystery. You never see pregnant Walkers—their bodies don't swell or change in obvious ways. You never see births. The small ones simply appear, accompanying adults after gatherings, as if produced by the congregation itself rather than by individual reproduction.

Or perhaps they're not children at all. Perhaps they're new Walkers, recently arrived from elsewhere (but where? how?), who attach themselves to experienced Walkers for a period of learning before establishing their own territories.

Or perhaps they're something the plateau grows. Perhaps the bodies of deceased Walkers, integrated into the mat, sometimes produce these offspring from their decomposing tissues, and the adult Walkers recognize and claim them when they emerge.

The small ones grow slowly. Years pass—many years—before they reach full height and begin to maintain distance from their companions. Eventually, they separate, walking away alone, establishing their own territories, beginning their own slow circulations across the plateau's surface.

## Their Relationship with Other Life

The Walkers interact minimally with the plateau's fauna, but when they do, the interactions are gentle and strange.

They allow animals to approach without fear. The large, slow-moving grazers sometimes walk alongside Walkers for hours or days, the two species sharing the mat in peaceful proximity. The Walkers occasionally touch these animals—long, slow strokes down their mossy backs, hands resting on their warm flanks while both stand motionless. Some exchange seems to occur in these contacts, though what it might be is unclear.

The bird-like creatures land on the Walkers sometimes, perching on their shoulders or heads, treating them as they would treat the towers or the standing stones—as features of the landscape, useful perches from which to survey for food. The Walkers don't mind. They remain still when birds land on them, allowing them to stay as long as they like, unbothered when droppings land on their skin or in their hair.

And there are stories—impossible to verify, but persistent—of Walkers who have become too still, who have stood or sat in one place for so long that they've essentially transformed into landscape features. Completely covered in vegetation, roots growing into the mat, their bodies serving as substrate for entire micro-ecosystems. Whether they're still alive in any meaningful sense is debatable. Whether they chose this transformation or simply forgot to keep moving is unknown.

Other Walkers, encountering these stationary ones, sometimes stop. They touch them—the vegetation-covered shoulders, the moss-thick heads. They hum, close and sustained. Whether they receive response is unclear. Eventually, they move on, leaving the stationary Walker to continue their slow conversion into geography.

## Their Knowledge

The Walkers know the plateau in ways that might qualify as knowledge or might be something else entirely.

They navigate unerringly, even in fog, even at night, even across regions with no distinguishing features. They find the warm pools without searching. They avoid the unstable areas without testing. They walk past hidden sinkholes without incident, as if seeing through the mat to the hollows beneath.

This might be learned knowledge—centuries of experience teaching them to read subtle cues in the vegetation, in the mat's texture, in the way mist moves. Or it might be something more intimate: a direct sensory perception of the plateau's structure, the result of their symbiosis with it, their bodies literally feeling through the mat as if through an extension of their own nervous system.

They also seem to know when the plateau is about to change.

Before a warm pool appears (and they do appear, new ones forming occasionally in places that were previously ordinary), Walkers congregate nearby, waiting. When a tower is about to collapse—weakened by internal decay, ready to fall—Walkers avoid the area for days beforehand. When the soft zones shift, becoming unstable in new locations, Walkers adjust their paths without apparent conscious decision, as if responding to information received below the threshold of deliberate thought.

And they know things about the ruins. Which stones can be moved and which can't. Which buildings are safe to shelter in and which are on the verge of final collapse. They sometimes guide newcomers (rare as newcomers are) to the structures, to the warm pools, to the stable paths through unstable regions—not through speech but through gentle touches on the shoulder, through walking ahead and looking back, through waiting patiently while the newcomer learns to read what the Walkers read naturally.

## The Others

Not all inhabitants of the plateau are Walkers. There are others, rarer, stranger, harder to classify.

**The Swimmers** move through the pools and channels beneath the mat like fish through water, surfacing occasionally to breathe, their bodies sleek and adapted to liquid existence. They're related to the Walkers—similar size, similar skin tone—but more thoroughly transformed by aquatic life. Their hands and feet are fully webbed, their limbs shortened and powerful, their eyes protected by translucent membranes. They rarely emerge fully from water, preferring to stay submerged, but Walkers encountering them sometimes lie at pool edges, reaching down to touch the Swimmers' hands, maintaining contact for long minutes before separating again.

**The Tenders** stay near the towers, rarely wandering more than a few hundred feet from their chosen structures. They're smaller than Walkers, more compact, and completely covered in vegetation—not colonized by it but cultivating it, actively gardening their own bodies, encouraging specific species to grow in specific patterns. They maintain the towers somehow, removing decay, supporting weak sections, perhaps even guiding their slow growth. When a tower sings, Tenders sometimes sing with it, their voices (and they have voices, unlike Walkers) blending with the wind-tones in harmonies that seem both accidental and deeply intentional.

**The Luminous Ones** appear so rarely that each sighting becomes a story passed through vibrations across the Walker network. They're the source of the mysterious lights seen at night—bodies that glow from within, bright enough to illuminate their surroundings, walking slowly across the dark plateau like living lanterns. They might be Walkers who have achieved some further transformation, or they might be a separate species entirely. Attempts to approach them fail; they retreat or fade, leaving only the memory of light and a warm spot on the mat where they stood.

And there are hints—never confirmed, always peripheral—of intelligence in the plateau itself.

The way certain areas seem to respond to prolonged Walker presence, vegetation growing in patterns that suggest intentionality. The way the warm pools appear where they're needed. The way the towers' songs sometimes seem to answer the Walkers' resonators, creating dialogues rather than mere overlapping sounds.

Whether the plateau is conscious in any sense that would be meaningful to an outside observer is unanswerable. But the Walkers behave as if it is—treating it with respect, responding to its changes, participating in its rhythms not as inhabitants of a landscape but as cells in a body, functions in a mind, notes in an enormously slow conversation between stone and plant and water and flesh that has been continuing for longer than memory and will continue after memory ends.

## What They Do

The Walkers walk. This is both answer and non-answer to the question of their purpose.

They walk in patterns—not random wandering, but paths that loop and spiral and intersect according to rules that might be individual preference or might be cultural inheritance or might be instructions received from the plateau itself through channels unavailable to outside perception.

They maintain the paths not through construction but through use. The places they walk most frequently develop subtle tracks—not bare soil (there is never bare soil) but slightly denser mat, slightly different vegetation, subtle signatures that another Walker can read and choose to follow or not.

They visit the ruins regularly, in cycles that might span years or decades. A particular Walker might return to a particular building once every ten years, sheltering in it for a few days, leaving their small marks, moving on. The pattern of visitation creates a kind of memory—each Walker remembering and being remembered by places, maintaining relationship through repeated contact, keeping the ruins alive in some sense through attention.

They witness. This seems important. When something occurs—a tower collapse, a new warm pool, an unusual congregation of animals—Walkers come. Not immediately, but eventually. They stand and observe, sometimes for hours, sometimes for days, and then they leave, carrying the observation with them as they walk, perhaps sharing it through vibration with other Walkers, perhaps keeping it private, perhaps not distinguishing between the two.

And they transform, slowly, continuously, becoming more themselves and more the plateau and more the integration of the two until the distinction becomes meaningless.

A Walker who has lived on the plateau for centuries is barely recognizable as the being who arrived. Their skin has thickened and greened. Their body has elongated. Their feet have spread and adapted. The garden growing on them has become permanent, complex, hosting dozens of species in intricate succession. Their breathing has slowed to match not just the plateau's rhythm but the plateau's specific location—different areas breathing at different rates, and the Walker's breath matching whichever area they currently inhabit.

Their thoughts—if thoughts is the right word—have slowed as well, become more diffuse, more processual, less about discrete ideas and more about continuous awareness flowing like water through the substrate of consciousness.

They become patient in ways that transcend patience. They become still in ways that include movement. They become silent in ways that include all sound.

Eventually—after enough time, enough walking, enough transformation—they stop being individual and become plateau. The transition is gradual and has no clear endpoint. They walk slower and slower until they're barely moving. They stand longer and longer until they're rarely walking. They root deeper and deeper until one day they don't uproot again.

And then they're geography. A particularly tall hummock. A particularly dense growth of vegetation. A warm pool surrounded by unusual plants. A place that other Walkers touch when passing, humming low and sustained, acknowledging what used to be a companion and is now a location, what used to be a self and is now an ecosystem, what used to be alive in the narrow sense and is now alive in the larger sense that includes moss and water and stone and the slow breathing of the mat.

This is what lives on the plateau: the process of becoming it, embodied and walking, thinking and dissolving, maintaining selfhood and surrendering it simultaneously, being both inhabitant and habitat, both observer and observed, both the one who walks and the ground being walked upon, until even these distinctions dissolve into the greater truth that the plateau is what it grows, and what it grows includes everything, and everything includes the Walkers, and the Walkers include everything, and the breathing continues, and the green continues, and the walking continues, and nothing ends because nothing ever began, there was always only this: the vast, patient, living surface, and the shapes it takes, and the paths they trace, and the silence they share.
