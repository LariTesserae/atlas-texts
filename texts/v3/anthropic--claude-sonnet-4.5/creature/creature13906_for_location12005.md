# The Inhabitants

## The Grazers

They are called *velimara* in the carved glyphs still visible on certain ceiling stones, though whether that word means "wing-people" or "air-feeders" or something untranslatable has been lost. Each individual is about the size and weight of a heavy housecat, but the comparison fails immediately—they have none of a cat's density, none of its groundedness. Held in your hands (and they can be held, though they protest with wheezing squeals), a velimara feels like a bag of air wrapped in suede, its bones hollow to the point of fragility, its body a bellows that pulses with each breath.

The wings dominate. Four of them, paired fore and aft, each membrane stretching between three elongated digits and anchored to a shoulder assembly of pure muscle. The skin is gray-brown to olive-green, velvety where new, leathery and scarred where old, shot through with visible veining that pulses darker when the animal exerts itself. They don't flap—the air is too still, too dense, too close for that kind of violence. Instead they row, each wing pulling through a long, undulating stroke that ends with a twist of the wrist-joint, the membrane cupping and releasing air in a motion that looks almost like sign language, like the velimara are spelling something over and over as they fly.

The body between these wings is barrel-shaped, mostly lung. Their breathing is audible even in the general thunder—a wheeze and huff, a constant respiratory conversation with the air. They must breathe rapidly to stay aloft; a velimara at rest takes perhaps forty breaths per minute, while one in flight might double that. The result is that each animal carries with it a little zone of air current, a micro-climate of respiration that you can feel if one passes close—a warm, slightly fetid breeze that smells of the moss-spore diet, vegetal and sweet.

Their heads are small and dominated by the mouth, which splits the face nearly to the ear-holes (they have no external ears, just covered apertures that lie flat against the skull). The mouth is perpetually open during flight, creating a scoop that filters spores and pollen from the air. There are no teeth, just ridged plates of keratin that line the jaw, and a tongue that's sticky with mucus. Their eyes are disproportionately large for the head size, dark and reflective, positioned for binocular vision—essential in a space crowded with tens of thousands of flying bodies. They can see well in the dim light, better than human eyes, capable of tracking individual neighbors in the flock while maintaining awareness of the hanging architecture and the moss terrain below.

The velimara's intelligence is difficult to assess. They navigate the complex three-dimensional space with apparent ease, avoiding collisions through constant micro-adjustments. They recognize individual nesting sites among thousands of similar platforms. They engage in what appears to be social bonding—pairs or small groups that fly consistently near one another, that share roosting spaces, that touch wing-tips in mid-flight in gestures that look like reassurance or greeting. But they show no tool use, no modification of their environment beyond the simple arrangement of nesting materials, no behavior that suggests abstract thought.

What they have instead is an absolute mastery of their medium. A velimara in flight is conducting a continuous calculation of vectors and forces, processing input from thousands of neighbors, adjusting for the微 currents created by moss-mounds and architectural features, maintaining position in a feeding circuit that might span half a mile and involve complex three-dimensional loops. They do this while breathing, eating, vocalizing, watching for predators, and occasionally copulating (which they accomplish in flight, in brief, awkward conjunctions that often result in both parties losing altitude before separating and recovering).

Their lifecycle is slow despite their metabolic intensity. A female produces perhaps three offspring in her lifetime, raising each in isolation on a claimed platform for the better part of a year before the juvenile is ready to join the flock. The young are born helpless—eyes closed, wings crumpled, dependent on the mother's warmth and the partially-digested spore paste she regurgitates. They grow slowly, their first flights tentative and poorly controlled, their entry into the main flock a gradual process marked by many collisions and retreats to the safety of the nesting platform.

Adults live twenty years, perhaps thirty. The old ones are obvious—their wing membranes torn and poorly healed, their bodies thinner, their flight labored. Eventually they can no longer sustain altitude. They descend to the moss floor, sometimes in controlled spirals, sometimes in sudden drops when the muscles finally fail. There they die, usually within hours, their bodies settling into the moss. The tunnelers and millipedes find them quickly. Within weeks nothing remains but the lightest bones, and even those disappear eventually, ground to powder by the slow churning of the moss bed.

The velimara are loud, but their vocalizations carry meaning. The base-line wheeze-huff of breathing is constant and individual—each animal's respiratory rhythm slightly different, creating a sonic texture like rain on varied surfaces. Overlaid on this are the calls: short grunts that mean "I'm here," rising squeals that signal alarm, a chittering sound that seems to indicate pleasure or contentment (often heard on the nesting platforms when pairs are at rest together), and a deeper, resonant hoot that males produce during mating season, their entire body cavity becoming a resonating chamber.

They can also produce a sound that might be called singing, though it happens rarely—usually in the hour before the flock's rest period, when hundreds of individuals are settling onto their platforms. The sound builds gradually: sustained notes held by many throats at once, creating harmonies and dissonances that swell and fade and swell again. It's not melodic in any conventional sense—there's no tune, no rhythm, just an extended drone that vibrates through the valley, that you feel in your sternum before you consciously hear it. It might be purely physiological, a communal exhale after the day's flying. Or it might be cultural, a learned behavior passed from generation to generation, something the velimara do because velimara have always done it, because the action of singing together reinforces their sense of flock identity.

They react to human presence with wariness but not fear. A person standing on the moss floor is simply an obstacle to navigate around, a feature of the terrain requiring only minor flight-path adjustments. But approach a nesting platform—climb one of the descending columns to examine the architecture—and the nearby velimara become agitated, their calls shifting to alarm squeals, their flight patterns tightening in aggressive loops around the intruder. They'll strike at you if you persist, using their wing-tips like slaps, their mouths gaping in threat displays. The strikes don't injure—they haven't the mass for that—but hundreds of velimara converging on a single threat creates a maelstrom of wings and bodies and sound that's overwhelming enough to drive most intruders away.

Young velimara, before they've fully learned caution, sometimes approach humans with curiosity. They hover at arm's length, their large eyes tracking movement, their heads cocking in what looks like puzzlement. They might reach out with a wing-tip to touch clothing or hair, a tentative investigation that ends immediately if the human moves too suddenly. These moments of connection are brief. The juvenile's parents or flock-mates usually intervene, calling it back to the safety of known patterns and appropriate distance.

There are color variations—some velimara lean toward gray, others toward brown or olive—but these variations don't seem to correlate with flock membership or breeding preference. Albinism occurs rarely, producing individuals with pale cream membranes and pink eyes who stand out starkly in the general gloom. These albinos aren't ostracized but they suffer higher predation rates, their visibility a disadvantage in the dim light where being difficult to track is crucial to survival.

The velimara have claimed the hanging city so thoroughly that in many places it's impossible to determine the structure's original form. Platforms designed (perhaps) as walkways are now buried under three feet of compressed nesting material—moss fragments, shed skin, droppings, the occasional feather-like whisker that grows along their jaw lines. This material hardens over time, cementing into a concrete-like substance that adds mass to the architecture, that might be slowly pulling it down toward the floor below, hastening whatever collapse is inevitable given enough centuries.

But the velimara also maintain the structure, inadvertently. Their constant movement over surfaces keeps joints from completely seizing. Their body oils and the chemicals in their droppings might actually preserve certain organic materials in the infrastructure. And in places where the architecture has begun to fail—where a beam has cracked or a support has rotted through—the velimara often abandon that section, redistributing their weight elsewhere, giving the structure time to reach a new equilibrium before the animals gradually colonize it again.

They are, in essence, the valley's inheritors. Whatever the builders intended, the reality is that the moss valleys now belong to the velimara, shaped by their presence, filled with their sound, defined by their ceaseless circuits through the air. They have integrated so completely with this place that the ecosystem would collapse without them—their droppings fertilize the moss, their feeding patterns distribute spores, their respiratory exhalations contribute carbon dioxide that the moss requires. They are not simply inhabitants but essential components, keystone species in an environment that has evolved around their particular needs and capabilities.

## The Builders' Children

They are still here. That's the first thing to understand. The builders didn't leave; they adapted, changed, compressed themselves into a form suitable for life in the valleys they created. What they became are called—in the few places where they still use language recognizable as such—the *hethrim*, though most humans who encounter them have simply called them "the small people" or "the moss-folk" or, less charitably, "the ceiling crawlers."

They are bipedal, tool-using, fully sapient, and approximately three feet tall.

The height is not dwarfism as humans experience it—not a human body scaled down with all the attendant medical complications. The hethrim are proportioned for their size: long-limbed and long-fingered, with large heads that nevertheless look appropriate to their frames, with bodies lean and efficient. They weigh perhaps thirty pounds, light enough that the ancient architecture—weakened by time but still functional—can support their communities without immediate danger of collapse.

Their skin is pale, the color of things that live in darkness—mushroom white to gray-green depending on individual and clan. It's thick and slightly waxy, resistant to abrasion, useful for a people who spend much of their time pressed against rough wood and stone. Their hair grows in patches rather than continuous coverage: a ridge down the spine, tufts at the joints, a cap on the skull that they cultivate into complex arrangements held by moss-fiber pins and wooden combs. Hair color ranges from white to gray to a green-black that looks like it has absorbed some essential quality of the moss itself.

Their faces are human-adjacent but not human. The eyes are enormous, taking up perhaps a third of the face, dark-adapted and capable of functioning in light levels that would leave a human effectively blind. They have proper eyelids, unlike the velimara, and they blink frequently, a gesture that seems to serve partly as punctuation in their gestural language. The nose is broad and flat, the nostrils flaring wide—scent is important to them, and the constantly humid air of the valleys carries chemical information they can read like text. The mouth is small-lipped and delicate, suited to their diet and to the precise articulation required by their spoken language, which is tonal and full of distinctions that human ears struggle to parse.

Their hands are their most remarkable feature. Six fingers on each hand, each finger long and multi-jointed, capable of moving independently with extraordinary precision. They can type on invisible keyboards, their fingers dancing through complex patterns in empty air—this is how they speak formally, in sign language that predates any spoken tongue they now use. The signs are ancient beyond measure, preserved in the muscle memory of generation after generation, taught to children before they learn to walk through the infrastructure.

They walk, but grudgingly. The hethrim are climbers primarily, brachiators, beings designed for life in three dimensions. On the rare occasions when they descend to the moss floor, they move with visible discomfort, their gait awkward, their bodies hunched as if expecting to find a handhold overhead at any moment. They vastly prefer the infrastructure, where they move with fluid grace, swinging from beam to beam, running along spans barely wide enough for their feet, hanging from their hands to manipulate tools or conduct conversations with equals suspended below them.

Their clothing is minimal—the temperature is constant and they generate sufficient body heat despite their size. Most wear something like a harness: leather straps (if leather is the right word for material made from processed moss stems) that cross the torso and provide attachment points for tools, for carrying pouches, for the hooks and clips they use to secure themselves to the architecture while working. Some wear loincloths or short kilts, though whether for modesty or decoration or simple practicality isn't clear. The garments are dyed in subtle colors—browns and greens and grays, sometimes marked with patterns that might indicate clan or family or individual status.

They also wear tools as a matter of course. A hethrim is rarely seen without at least a knife (stone, beautifully flaked) and a hook-stick (for pulling distant objects closer or securing themselves during climbs). Most carry much more: coils of rope, pouches of repair materials, small ceramic vessels containing adhesives or lubricants, wooden wedges and fiber bindings for emergency architecture repairs, sometimes musical instruments (flutes carved from hollow moss stems, small drums with velimara-skin heads), and always—always—a fire-carrier.

Fire is precious here, where everything is either stone or damp organic matter. The hethrim maintain it in small ceramic vessels, insulated and ventilated, feeding it with moss-peat charcoal and a fungus that grows in certain deep valleys, a fungus that burns slowly and produces minimal smoke. Fire means cooked food, means light to work by in the deepest infrastructure, means the ability to harden wood and soften adhesives and perform the hundred small chemical transformations that technology requires. Each hethrim carries their own flame, descended from fires that have been tended continuously for centuries, for millennia, fires that might be—for all anyone knows—the same flames that lit the valleys when the first stones of the infrastructure were laid.

They live in the architecture in communities that human observers have struggled to map or understand. There are no discrete buildings, no houses in any conventional sense. Instead, there are *zones*—sections of infrastructure that particular clans have claimed and maintained and modified over generations until they reflect a collective identity. One zone might be characterized by precise geometric additions, wooden panels fitted so carefully that the seams are invisible, everything right-angled and measured. The next zone, separated by perhaps fifty feet of neutral architecture, might be organic and flowing, with woven additions that look half-grown, with living moss deliberately cultivated on surfaces to create patterns or simply for aesthetic pleasure.

Population is difficult to estimate. In a single valley system—say, three connected bowls with their various passages—there might be two hundred hethrim, or there might be a thousand. They don't aggregate in obvious ways. You might see a dozen working together on a structural repair, then go hours without seeing another individual, only to round a corner in the infrastructure and find yourself in a dense zone where fifty hethrim are going about their business in close proximity.

Their society appears to be organized by lineage and craft. Certain family groups specialize in particular skills—architecture maintenance, rope-making, velimara management (they harvest dropped feathers and occasionally eggs from abandoned nests), fungus cultivation, or fire-tending. These specializations are taught from parent to child but aren't rigid; individuals can and do learn multiple crafts, and there seems to be no stigma attached to changing one's focus.

They do have leadership, though it's subtle. Elders—and the hethrim can live very long lives, perhaps a century or more given the stable environment—accumulate authority through demonstrated wisdom and the respect of their communities. These elders are consulted on major decisions: whether to expand into a new section of infrastructure, how to respond to a structural collapse, how to manage relations with neighboring clans. But they don't command in any authoritative sense. Decisions emerge from discussion, from consensus, from the slow building of agreement over days or weeks of signed and spoken conversation.

Religion, if that's what it is, centers on the infrastructure itself. The hethrim treat certain sections as sacred—usually the oldest, most elaborate zones, places where the joinery is so precise and the wood so aged that it seems miraculous the structure still stands. They leave offerings there: carefully arranged stones, woven fiber patterns, small carved figures. They conduct ceremonies that involve the entire community: synchronized movements through the infrastructure, everyone following precise paths in precise order, the whole clan becoming a kind of living machine that animates the architecture through their passage.

They also venerate the deep archives—chambers high in certain valleys' infrastructure where objects from the ancient past are stored and protected. These archives are sealed: wooden doors that haven't been opened in living memory, perhaps in centuries of living memory. The hethrim who tend these archives don't enter; they simply maintain the seals, replace rotted bindings, check that the chambers remain dry and intact. What's inside is unknown, or known only to the archive-keepers and considered too sacred or dangerous or simply private to share with outsiders.

The hethrim language is beautiful and impossible. The spoken component is tonal—the same sound at different pitches carries different meanings—and quick, a rapid patter that sounds like water running over stone. But spoken language is almost always accompanied by signing, the two modes interweaving to create a communication bandwidth far greater than either alone could achieve. A hethrim in full conversational flow is a wonder to watch: their hands dancing, their voice rising and falling, their whole body involved in the act of meaning-making, every gesture contributing to the message.

They have a written form as well, carved into wood or pressed into clay tablets: an ideographic system where each character represents not a sound but a concept, and complex ideas are built from combinations and modifications of base symbols. The characters themselves are beautiful—flowing curves and precise angles, each one a small artwork. The hethrim carve them into architecture sometimes, marking territory or commemorating events or simply recording their thoughts for future generations to discover.

Their relationship with the velimara is complex. They're not domesticated—the grazers are too numerous and too difficult to control for that—but they're not entirely wild either. The hethrim manage them, gently and indirectly. They maintain certain platforms specifically as nesting sites, keeping them clean and stable. They harvest excess nesting material to prevent dangerous accumulation. They occasionally cull the population if a particular valley becomes overcrowded, using nets and hooks to capture individuals who are then relocated to less-dense areas (or, occasionally, killed and processed—the velimara provide leather, bone, sinew, materials the hethrim use but cannot otherwise obtain in quantity).

In return, the velimara provide services: their droppings fertilize the moss, their constant movement helps distribute moisture through the air, and their sound—that overwhelming, ever-present thunder—serves as a kind of auditory landscape that the hethrim navigate as easily as they navigate the physical infrastructure. Changes in the sound tell them things: a sudden increase means a predator has struck, causing a local panic; a gradual decline in a particular zone means the moss there is failing, producing fewer spores; certain harmonic patterns in the evening song might predict changes in air pressure, in humidity, in conditions too subtle for human senses to detect.

Some hethrim specialize in velimara observation. They can identify individuals in the flock, recognize family groups, predict breeding behavior. These specialists maintain something like records: notched sticks where each notch represents an observation, patterns carved into hidden wooden panels that track lineages and population shifts across decades. When they speak of the velimara, it's with a mixture of affection and exasperation—the way you might speak of neighbors who are both essential and occasionally infuriating.

The hethrim relationship with humans, when humans come (and they come rarely, the moss valleys being difficult to reach and offering little of obvious value), is cautious. The size difference creates immediate tension—a three-foot being faced with a six-foot being is in a fundamentally vulnerable position. But the hethrim are neither cowardly nor hostile. They watch. They evaluate. If the human shows respect for the infrastructure, moves carefully, doesn't disturb critical areas or threaten the velimara, the hethrim gradually make themselves known.

The first contact is usually indirect: a small object left where the human will find it, a carved figure or a piece of worked wood, both a gift and a test. If the human responds appropriately (leaving their own gift, touching nothing that isn't offered), further contact follows. A hethrim might appear at a distance, watching. Then closer. Then close enough for attempted communication, which begins with gesture—the hethrim pointing, demonstrating, waiting to see if the human understands.

Actual friendship is rare but possible. A human who spends extended time in the valleys, who learns at least rudimentary signing, who demonstrates genuine interest in hethrim life and culture, might be granted something approaching acceptance. They'll never be hethrim, never fully integrated, but they might be allowed into zones usually kept private, might be taught certain skills or shown certain archives (the accessible ones, at least), might be invited to sit in on council meetings even if they can't fully participate.

But the hethrim have long memories, encoded in their archives and in oral traditions that stretch back beyond reckoning. They remember when they were different, when they were—if the interpretation of their oldest stories is correct—larger, when they built the infrastructure at its original scale rather than simply maintaining what already existed. They remember a time when the valleys were emptier, when the velimara were fewer or perhaps absent entirely. They remember changes so slow that no individual could perceive them but that the culture as a whole tracked across centuries: the gradual thickening of the moss, the slow accumulation of architecture, the increasing difficulty of maintaining connections between valley systems as the structure aged.

And they remember—or believe they remember—that the transformation was chosen. That their ancestors looked at the valleys they'd created, looked at the impossibility of maintaining vast infrastructure with a normal-sized population given the constraints of the environment, and made a decision: to breed for size, to sacrifice physical strength and reach for efficiency and sustainability, to become small enough that the valleys could support them indefinitely on the moss-spore harvest and the few cultivated fungi and the occasional processed velimara.

Whether this is true or merely mythology is unclear. What's certain is that the hethrim experience their size not as a limitation but as an adaptation, a choice (even if made by distant ancestors) that allows them to live here, in this specific place, in a way that larger beings never could. They are not diminished; they are optimized.

## The Deep Fauna

Beneath the surface of the moss, in the peat layers where dead plant matter compresses into something approaching soil, an entire ecosystem functions in darkness and near-silence, disturbed only by the faint vibrations from above.

The millipedes—though calling them that is taxonomic convenience rather than accuracy—are the primary decomposers. Each is as long as a human forearm, segmented, with hundreds of legs that move in synchronized waves. Their color is pale tan to translucent white, and in the deepest layers, some lack pigmentation entirely, their internal organs visible through the cuticle as dim shadows. They have no eyes—eyes would be useless in the absolute darkness of the deep moss. Instead, they navigate by chemical sense and by reading vibrations in the substrate.

They eat the dead moss, consuming it with mandibles that work constantly, processing the tough fibers through a digestive system that takes up most of their body cavity. What emerges from the other end—their castings—is fundamentally different from what they consumed: richer, more friable, full of the bacterial and fungal life that their gut has cultivated. These castings enrich the living moss above, creating a nutrient cycle that might have been accidentally established but is now absolutely essential. Without the millipedes, the dead layers would accumulate too quickly, becoming toxic anaerobic zones. With them, death becomes life at the steady pace necessary to sustain the valleys.

The millipedes live long lives—perhaps twenty years, though measuring time in the absolute consistency of the deep moss is difficult. They grow continuously, molting periodically, leaving behind translucent exoskeletons that themselves become food for smaller organisms. They reproduce slowly: the females lay eggs in carefully excavated chambers, then guard them for months until the young hatch. The young are tiny versions of their parents, already equipped with the instincts necessary for burrowing and feeding, already carrying in their guts the microbial cultures they'll need for digestion.

They're not social but they're not solitary either. Multiple millipedes might work the same area of moss, their tunnels intersecting, their castings mixing. They don't fight over territory—there's enough food for all—but they do seem to recognize one another, sometimes touching antennae in what might be greeting or simple acknowledgment of presence before continuing their separate excavations.

The slugs are larger—fist-sized, sometimes larger—and they operate in the upper layers of the moss bed where living and dead intermingle. They're translucent, their bodies showing gradations of green from the consumed moss that fills their digestive tracts. They move slowly, even by slug standards, leaving behind trails of slime that might serve multiple functions: marking paths they can follow back to preferred grazing areas, deterring competitors or predators through chemical signals, or simply the unavoidable byproduct of their locomotion.

They have rudimentary eyes—light-sensitive spots that can't form images but can detect the difference between the dim ambient light above and the darkness of the moss depths. They use this to regulate their position, staying at the optimal level where the moss is tender enough to eat but mature enough to be nutritious. They're hermaphroditic, each individual capable of both producing eggs and fertilizing the eggs of others. Their mating involves long, motionless encounters where two slugs entwine, exchanging genetic material over hours or days before separating to lay eggs in different areas of the moss bed.

The predators that hunt both millipedes and slugs are difficult to categorize. The largest are ambush hunters: something like a spider crossed with a centipede, body armored with chitinous plates, legs ending in hooks that can grip moss stems or pierce the soft bodies of prey. They excavate pits in the moss—not deep, just a body-length or so—and wait there, absolutely still, until vibration sensors in their legs detect movement nearby. Then they strike with sudden, shocking speed, emerging from their pit to seize prey and drag it back into the darkness to be consumed.

They have venom, injected through hollow fangs, that paralyzes prey while keeping it alive. The paralysis might last for days, the prey slowly being consumed while unable to move, unable to do anything but feel. It's a brutal economy, but an efficient one. The predator needs to eat only occasionally—once every few weeks—and so can afford to wait for prey to come to it rather than expending energy on active hunting.

These predators are also cannibalistic. A larger individual will happily eat a smaller one if the opportunity presents itself, and the molt period—when the animal must shed its armor to grow—is a time of particular vulnerability. Many do their molting in chambers excavated deep in the moss, sealing the entrance with webbing, emerging only when the new armor has hardened and they're capable of self-defense again.

The moss itself responds to the fauna in subtle ways. The roots (if they are roots—the morphology is complex) grow denser in areas of high millipede activity, perhaps responding to the nutrients in the castings. The living layer grows slightly thicker in areas where slugs concentrate, possibly because the slugs' grazing stimulates growth, pruning back old stems and allowing new shoots to flourish. Even the predators have an effect: their presence keeps millipede and slug populations in check, preventing overgrazing that would damage the moss bed.

There are also things that might be called farmers, though the term might be too generous. These are small creatures—each the size of a thumb—with many segments and specialized limbs. They cultivate fungus in chambers they excavate in the moss, carrying in fragments of plant matter, maintaining optimal moisture levels, defending their gardens against invaders. The fungus they grow is specific to each farmer species: one type produces globular fruiting bodies full of spores; another creates shelf-like structures that the farmers graze directly; a third grows as a network of threads that the farmers harvest and process into food for their larvae.

These farmers are not hethrim, are not even distantly related to the hethrim. They're arthropods of some kind, following their own evolutionary path toward complexity and specialization. But watching them tend their gardens—carefully removing dead material, adjusting the structure to maintain air flow, occasionally moving the entire operation to a new location when conditions become suboptimal—it's difficult not to see parallels, not to recognize in their behavior something like culture, like the transmission of knowledge across generations.

The deep moss is also home to things that are barely animals at all—slime molds that move through the substrate in slow waves, consuming bacteria and detritus, occasionally producing elaborate fruiting structures that emerge from the moss surface like alien architecture. These fruiting structures release spores into the air, where they're consumed by velimara along with everything else the grazers filter, and thus the slime mold propagates, moving between the deep moss and the air through the bodies of the flying grazers, completing a life cycle that spans both environments.

And there are microorganisms, of course—bacteria and archaea and things that might be something else entirely, things at the boundary between chemistry and biology. They exist in unimaginable numbers, filling every crevice, every drop of moisture, breaking down organic matter at the molecular level, fixing nitrogen, producing vitamins, engaging in chemical warfare and chemical cooperation with their neighbors. They're the foundation of everything—without them, the entire system collapses. But they're invisible, inaudible, accessible only through their effects: the smell of the moss, the texture of the peat, the fertility of the soil that sustains the plants that feed the grazers that fertilize the moss that houses the bacteria.

The deep fauna rarely interact with the surface world. Occasionally a predator—one of the larger, more aggressive types—will emerge at night (or what passes for night in the timeless valleys) and attempt to hunt velimara on the moss surface. These hunts are usually unsuccessful; the predator, built for the confined spaces and predictable topology of the moss depths, is too slow and conspicuous in the open air. But occasionally one succeeds, dragging a grazer down into the moss, leaving only a few scattered feathers and a divot in the surface as evidence of what transpired.

The hethrim are aware of the deep fauna. They occasionally harvest millipedes—killing them quickly with precise knife strikes, processing their bodies into various products: the chitin into tools, the internal organs into nutrients for their fungus gardens, the nerve cord into a kind of fiber that can be worked into rope when mixed with plant material. They're careful never to overharvest, taking only a few individuals from each area, letting the population recover before returning.

They also maintain what might be called mollusk farms—areas of moss where they encourage slug populations by introducing preferred food plants and protecting the area from predators. The slugs grow fat in these protected zones, and the hethrim harvest them periodically, either for food (processed into a paste that provides essential nutrients) or for their slime, which has properties useful in certain adhesives and waterproofing treatments.

But mostly the hethrim leave the deep moss alone, treating it as a separate world that functions best without interference. They tell stories about the moss depths, stories that might be mythology or might be distorted memories of real encounters: tales of tunnels that go down forever, of chambers where strange things grow, of creatures so large and so ancient that they've become part of the landscape itself, indistinguishable from the peat and stone.

Whether such creatures exist is unknowable without deep excavation, which the hethrim refuse to do and which human visitors have never attempted on any significant scale. The moss is deep, the peat is dense, and digging into it risks destabilizing the surface, creating sinkholes that could swallow a person or damage the infrastructure above. So the deep moss keeps its secrets, maintains its darkness, continues its slow work of transformation, death to life to death again, in cycles that span centuries and care nothing for the brief concerns of the surface world.

## The Gliders

They're called *rakmora* in hethrim signing, a word that means roughly "the hunters who unfold," and they're the apex predator of the valleys' aerial ecosystem. Each is about the size of a medium dog but shaped more like a manta ray, the body flattened into a disk with the head barely distinguishable from the torso. The entire perimeter of the body—three feet across in a large adult—is membrane: skin stretched taut between fine bones, creating a lifting surface that the rakmora uses for gliding rather than powered flight.

They live in the moss, in burrows excavated deep enough to provide protection from temperature fluctuations and to hide them from the velimara flocks during the vulnerable hours when they sleep. The burrows are precisely sized—just large enough for the rakmora to enter when they fold themselves nearly flat, their limbs tucked against their bodies, their wings compressed into narrow bundles. Inside, the chamber expands into a space where they can move, turn, maintain their weapons and their few possessions.

Because these are not simple animals. The rakmora are sapient.

Their intelligence is different from hethrim intelligence, more focused, more limited in certain ways but also more intense. They don't have language in the conventional sense—they lack vocal cords, cannot produce the range of sounds necessary for speech. But they communicate through gesture and posture, through chemical signals released from glands along their wing-edges, and most of all through action. A rakmora's intentions are readable in the way it positions its body, in the angle of its wing-tips, in the hundred subtle variations of posture that mean "hunting," "resting," "courting," "threatening," "submitting."

They craft tools, which is the clearest evidence of their sapience. Each adult rakmora maintains a set of implements: stone blades for butchering prey, bone hooks for extracting meat from difficult crevices, wooden stakes for marking territory or communication with neighbors. Most remarkably, they make gliding aids—structures of wood and stretched skin that they wear when hunting, that increase their lifting surface beyond what their natural wings provide, that allow them to stay aloft longer and maneuver more precisely.

These gliding aids are individual, custom-built to each rakmora's size and style. Some are elegant and minimal, just wing-tip extensions that add a foot to each side. Others are baroque constructions that double the animal's effective wingspan, festooned with decorative elements—carved toggles, dyed leather strips, arrangements of velimara feathers—that might serve aerodynamic purposes or might be purely aesthetic, expressions of individual identity.

The rakmora hunt by ambush, but it's ambush elevated to art. They emerge from their moss burrows at irregular intervals—there's no pattern the velimara could learn to predict—and launch themselves upward. The initial launch uses powerful hind legs, catapulting the rakmora into the air. Once airborne, they spread their wings and begin their glide.

They don't flap; they can't generate lift that way. Instead, they read the air, finding the subtle currents created by temperature variations, by the collective breath of thousands of velimara, by the geography of the valley itself. They can gain altitude in these currents, rising in slow spirals, positioning themselves above and ahead of the velimara flocks. Then they fold their wings and drop.

The attack dive is fast—faster than a falling human—and silent. The rakmora becomes an arrow of flesh and bone, its wing membranes streamlined against its body, its legs extended forward with talons spread. It aims for the periphery of the flock, targeting the old, the young, the distracted. Impact is brutal: the rakmora strikes from above, talons piercing the velimara's body, wings spreading at the last instant to arrest the fall. The targeted grazer usually dies from the initial strike, or shortly after from the rakmora's bite—a quick crunch to the skull or spine.

Then the rakmora must reach the moss floor before other velimara mob it. It folds into a controlled dive, the dead velimara clutched in its talons, gliding down in long arcs while the flock swirls in confusion and alarm above. Once on the moss, it drags its prey to the nearest burrow entrance—sometimes its own, sometimes that of a neighbor it's allied with—and disappears below.

The rakmora eat methodically, processing the entire kill over hours or days. The velimara's flesh provides protein; the bones provide calcium and materials for tool-making; the skin becomes leather for repairing gliding aids; the feathers are either incorporated into decorative elements or left at the burrow entrance, a territorial marker that signals to other rakmora that this area is claimed, is productive, is not to be trespassed upon without invitation.

They're solitary but not isolated. Rakmora maintain complex social networks based on respect and utility. A successful hunter might share part of a kill with a less successful neighbor, not out of altruism but because that neighbor provides other services—might be better at making tools, might have access to superior burrow locations, might be a potential mate. These relationships are negotiated constantly through gift-giving and mutual grooming (the rakmora's flexibility allows them to clean one another's hard-to-reach areas, a service that's both hygienic and socially bonding).

They have territories, but they're three-dimensional and overlapping. A rakmora's territory includes the moss around its burrow, the airspace it typically hunts in, and the specific architectural features it uses as launching platforms. Other rakmora might pass through this territory if they're not competing for the same resources—if they hunt at different heights, target different sections of the velimara flocks, or have established relationships that permit such overlap.

Disputes are settled through display rather than combat. A rakmora defending its territory will position itself prominently—on a moss mound or a low-hanging piece of infrastructure—and spread its wings wide, showing its size, its gliding aids, its fitness. The intruder assesses: Can I take this individual? Is the resource worth fighting for? Usually the answer is no, and the intruder withdraws. Actual fights are rare and decisive. The rakmora have powerful jaws and sharp talons, and a fight between two adults usually ends with one dead or permanently crippled.

They reproduce slowly. A female produces one offspring per year, birthed live rather than from an egg, the infant emerging already furred and with miniature wings. The mother raises it alone—the father's contribution ends at conception—keeping it in the burrow, feeding it predigested meat, teaching it to glide through practice sessions on the moss surface where the consequences of failure are mild. These training sessions happen during low-activity periods when fewer velimara are in the air and the chance of discovery by hethrim is minimal.

Young rakmora stay with their mothers for two years, learning not just hunting but the complex social navigation necessary to establish themselves in the rakmora community. They learn which neighbors can be trusted, which territories are weak and might be claimed, which hethrim are dangerous and must be avoided, which sections of infrastructure are safe to use as perches and which are too frequently monitored.

When the young leave, they usually travel to adjacent valleys, seeking areas where competition is less intense. Some don't survive this dispersal—they're killed by established rakmora defending territories, or they fail at hunting and starve, or they misjudge a glide and crash into architecture or stone. But those who survive become part of the population, find burrows, establish territories, begin the cycle again.

The rakmora's relationship with the hethrim is uneasy. The hethrim view them as pests at best, dangerous predators at worst. A rakmora hunting near densely-inhabited infrastructure disrupts the velimara populations the hethrim rely on, and a rakmora in close quarters is perfectly capable of killing a hethrim—the size difference isn't large enough to guarantee the hethrim's safety.

But the rakmora are also intelligent enough to recognize the hethrim's capabilities. They avoid obviously inhabited areas. They don't hunt near fire-light. They've learned to recognize the signs of hethrim presence—the smell of smoke, the sound of tools on wood, the visual evidence of recent repair work—and give such areas wide berth.

There are occasional treaties, or what amount to treaties given the limited communication possible. A rakmora might leave a gift at the edge of hethrim territory—a particularly fine velimara skin, a carved bone, a stone selected for its beauty. If the hethrim accept the gift and leave one of their own in return, an understanding is established: The rakmora can hunt in this area if it avoids certain zones, doesn't take more than a sustainable number of velimara, and provides occasional gifts of materials the hethrim need.

These arrangements are fragile. A new rakmora who doesn't know the local agreements might violate the terms. A young hethrim might not understand the significance of the markers and accidentally trespass. When treaties break down, the hethrim response is organized: groups of hunters with nets and spears, searching the moss for burrow entrances, smoking out the rakmora and killing it if possible, driving it away if not.

The rakmora have learned to fear these organized hunts. They have good reason: the hethrim are numerous, coordinated, and carrying the accumulated knowledge of centuries of conflict. A rakmora can kill a single hethrim, but not five working together, not ten. So most rakmora choose avoidance, maintaining respectful distance, accepting that certain resources are off-limits, living in the spaces between hethrim communities rather than in direct competition with them.

In valleys where hethrim populations are small or absent, rakmora behavior changes. They become bolder, more diverse in their tactics. Some develop cooperative hunting strategies, multiple rakmora working together to stampede velimara flocks into kill zones. Others learn to hunt the deep-moss predators, dropping onto the larger arthropods when they emerge for their own hunts, turning predator into prey.

These valleys also see cultural elaboration—rakmora spending more time on their gliding aids, creating increasingly complex designs, incorporating materials from outside the valley system (suggesting trade or at least travel networks). They develop more sophisticated burrow architecture: multi-chambered systems with separate areas for sleeping, for storing food, for working on tools, sometimes with chambers large enough that multiple rakmora can occupy them simultaneously, possibly family groups or long-term partnerships that haven't been observed in more competitive environments.

Whether this constitutes civilization is debatable. They have tool use, social organization, culture, art, communication, and territory management. But they lack permanent architecture (beyond the burrows), lack written language, lack the kind of technological progression that marks other sapient species. They've achieved an equilibrium with their environment and seem content to maintain it rather than transcend it.

The hethrim have stories about the rakmora, stories that give them personalities, motivations, histories. Some stories cast them as villains—clever, cruel, always testing the boundaries of acceptable behavior. Others treat them as tragic figures—intelligent beings trapped by their bodies and their instincts into lives of violence when they might prefer otherwise. A few stories suggest kinship, a distant relationship, the possibility that rakmora and hethrim share common ancestry somewhere in the unrecoverable past.

Whether any of this is true, the rakmora themselves cannot say. They hunt, they craft, they raise their young, they maintain their territories. They navigate their days through glide and fall, through the momentary weightlessness of the kill dive, through the satisfaction of meat and the subtle pleasures of a well-made tool. They live perhaps twenty years, dying from accumulated injuries, from the eventual failure to catch prey, from the rare successful challenge by a younger, stronger rival.

And when they die, their burrows are usually claimed by others—the locations are too valuable, too carefully chosen, to abandon. The new occupant clears out the old tools, the old decorations, the bones and feathers and leather scraps, pushing them into the deepest part of the burrow where they'll gradually be incorporated into the moss substrate. Then they install their own possessions, mark the entrance with their own territorial signs, and continue the pattern that has repeated for centuries: hunter living above the moss, dropping from above, returning to the earth, the cycle unbroken and unchanging.

---

Together—hethrim, rakmora, velimara, and all the deep-moss fauna—they form an ecosystem so perfectly balanced that it seems designed, seems intentional. Perhaps it was. Perhaps the original builders engineered the moss to support the millipedes, brought in the proto-velimara and shaped them over generations into their current form, invited or created the rakmora to prevent overgrazing, and then adjusted themselves—shrank themselves—to fit into the machine they'd made.

Or perhaps it's simply convergence, the inevitable outcome when species share an environment long enough that they become mutually dependent, their evolution entangled, their fates bound together. Perhaps the hethrim remember wrong, have built mythology around changes that were natural selection rather than conscious choice.

What's certain is that the moss valleys function, have functioned for a time beyond easy measurement, and show every sign of continuing to function as long as the stone remains stable and the air remains breathable and the slow chemistry of life maintains its steady conversion of death into growth into death again. The beings who live here—large and small, sapient and instinct-driven—are all part of that function, all essential threads in a web so complex that removing any single strand would cause unpredictable cascades.

They are, all of them, the moss valleys. They are what lives there.
