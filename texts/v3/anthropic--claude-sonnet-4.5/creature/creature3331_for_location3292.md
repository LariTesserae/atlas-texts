# Who Lives Here

## The People of the Deep Listening

They are not one people but several, though the distinctions have blurred over generations of intermarriage and the necessities of survival. They call themselves, collectively, something that translates poorly—"those who remain," or perhaps "those who learned to stay." The individual groups have names, but these names are sonic signatures as much as words: specific patterns of clicks and tones that identify lineage, territory, specialization.

A person here is short by the standards of the lit world—five feet tall is common, five-six is unusually tall. The body plan has been shaped by generations in the dark: compact, broad-shouldered, with powerful legs adapted to walking vast distances across uneven terrain. The bones are dense and heavy. Balance is exceptional—the inner ear has elaborated, providing extraordinary spatial awareness. They move with a quality that seems unhurried but covers ground efficiently, reading the terrain through their feet, adjusting to slope and surface without conscious thought.

The hands are the most immediately striking feature: large, with long sensitive fingers, the fingertips broad and heavily innervated. These are reading hands, touching hands, hands that can detect texture differences measured in microns, that can identify materials, assess moisture content, read tactile markers, perform delicate repairs in total darkness. The palms are tough but not calloused—the skin maintains sensitivity while being durable enough for constant contact with rough surfaces. Children spend years developing this tactile literacy, learning to identify hundreds of plant species by touch, to assess the condition of machinery, to read the carved message-markers that mark trails and boundaries.

The face has changed. The eyes remain—small, deep-set, protected—but they're almost vestigial. Some individuals retain light-perception, a dim awareness of the bioluminescent markers, but most see nothing at all. Vision has been replaced by other senses. The ears are large and mobile, capable of independent orientation, constantly moving to track sounds. The external ear structure is elaborate, channeling sound efficiently, and behind it the auditory processing centers of the brain have expanded dramatically. They hear frequencies beyond normal human range—subsonic communications of the herds, ultrasonic echoes from their own mouth-clicks.

Because echolocation has become primary. They've developed it independently, as their ancestors learned to navigate in total darkness. It's not as precise as a bat's—the human mouth can't produce ideal ultrasonic clicks—but it's functional. A rapid series of tongue-clicks, focused through slightly modified oral structures, produces echoes that paint a crude acoustic picture: the shape of spaces, the location of obstacles, the texture of surfaces. Combined with passive hearing, this gives them a three-dimensional acoustic sense of their environment. They "see" in sound.

The nose has enlarged, the nasal cavity expanded, the olfactory epithelium much more extensive than baseline human. They live in a world of scent: the mineral signatures of different stone formations, the vegetable smells of the various grasses and reeds, the musky odors of the herds, the acrid tang of predators, the personal scents of other humans (diet, health, emotional state—all readable), the chemical markers that indicate weather changes, the industrial smells of the forge-zones and processing stations. Scent is memory and navigation and social information all at once.

They speak in ways that use the whole sonic spectrum. Ordinary conversation includes clicks and hums and tones that carry different information than the words themselves—emotional content, emphasis, sometimes pure data (coordinates, numbers, technical specifications). The languages here have evolved to be acoustically rich, using tone and rhythm and timbre in ways that would be difficult to transcribe. Some information is conveyed subvocally, through subsonic rumbles felt more than heard. Some through patterns that utilize the acoustic properties of the environment—a message spoken in a particular chamber will resonate in specific ways, adding layers of meaning.

Their clothing is practical: layers of woven fiber from the processed grasses, treated with oils to resist the constant moisture, cut to allow freedom of movement. The weave itself is often meaningful—different patterns identify different groups, occupations, achievements. Pockets and pouches are everywhere, containing tools, materials, food. Footwear is minimal—thick-soled shoes or boots that protect while maintaining ground-sense. Many people go barefoot when possible, preferring the direct contact with terrain.

They adorn themselves, but not visually. Jewelry makes sound—bells and chimes in different tones, worn so that movement creates acoustic signature. Some people wear elaborate arrangements that make them identifiable from a distance by sound alone. Others prefer silence, wearing sound-dampening materials, learning to move without acoustic trace. Both are respected: the bold who announce themselves constantly and the silent who pass nearly undetectable.

## The Listener-Engineers

Among them are specialists who tend the machinery and the listening stations, who maintain the acoustic network, who interpret the vast flow of information from the sensors and drums and wires. These are not priests exactly, though there's something spiritual in how they speak of their work. They're engineers who've developed an almost mystical relationship with their infrastructure.

A listener-engineer is someone who has trained for decades, who has developed their hearing to extraordinary levels, who can detect the slightest variation in the rhythm of a pump station and diagnose the problem, who can predict weather systems from subtle changes in atmospheric pressure and sound propagation, who can hear a distant herd and estimate its size and health and direction. They wear elaborate arrangements of tuned metal strips and rods that respond to different frequencies, that provide them with additional sensory information. These acoustic ornaments also mark their status—the more complex the arrangement, the more experienced the listener.

They live at the stations, usually—the pump stations, the forge-zones, the listening posts—maintaining the machinery, keeping the network functional, processing information. They're respected, essential, given status and resources. When they travel between stations, they're hosted generously, because they carry news, technical knowledge, and the acoustic signatures of distant places.

Their training begins in childhood. Candidates are identified early—those with exceptional hearing, good spatial sense, the temperament for sustained focus. They apprentice to senior listeners, learning not just technical skills but something deeper: how to merge consciousness with the acoustic environment, how to expand awareness to encompass vast territories, how to hold in memory the complex acoustic maps of regions hundreds of miles across. Some never quite achieve it and remain competent technicians. Others experience something like transcendence and become the master listeners, the ones consulted for major decisions, whose predictions are trusted, who seem to hear things no one else can.

There are stories—impossible to verify—that the greatest listeners can detect the subsonic songs of the planet itself, the deep vibrations of geological processes, the fundamental frequency of the world. Whether this is real or metaphorical hardly matters; these individuals operate at levels that seem impossible, predicting events with uncanny accuracy, perceiving patterns others miss.

## The Herders

Others specialize in following and managing the great herds. These people are nomadic, living most of their lives on the move, following the seasonal migrations, maintaining relationships with specific herd-groups that have been in their family care for generations.

A herder is lean and weathered, their body adapted to constant movement, to the physical demands of keeping pace with animals that travel relentlessly. They carry little—everything they need packed into a sophisticated system of bags and wraps that distribute weight efficiently. They know the migration routes intimately, can predict where a herd will be on a given day, know the water sources and grazing areas and the places where predators are likely to strike.

They bond with the herds in ways that are hard to explain. They learn the acoustic signatures of individual animals—each one's unique combination of call tones, hoof-rhythms, breathing patterns. They can identify hundreds of individuals by sound alone, can track lineages, know which animals are likely to lead, which are pregnant, which are ailing. The relationship is symbiotic: the herders protect the herds from predators (or rather, minimize predation through strategic positioning and distraction), guide them away from dangerous terrain, help with difficult births. In return, the herds provide—not through slaughter, which would be unsustainable, but through other means.

The herds shed: horn material, hair, skin. They produce dung, which is valuable fuel and fertilizer. They create trails that others can follow. And yes, occasionally, animals die—of age, of injury, of disease—and these bodies are used completely, nothing wasted. But the relationship is not predatory. The herders speak of "tending" the herds, of being in relationship with them, of carrying responsibility for their wellbeing.

Young herders are taught to move with silence, to avoid spooking the animals, to interpret the herd's collective mood from its acoustic signature. They learn to mimic the calls, to communicate simple messages—danger, safe, water ahead, turn here. They learn the art of misdirection, of using sound to draw predators away from the herd, of creating acoustic barriers that make predators hesitant.

The herders gather annually at traditional meeting places, where multiple family groups converge, where herds mingle and mating happens, where information is exchanged, marriages arranged, disputes resolved. These gatherings are enormous—thousands of humans, tens of thousands of herd animals, all concentrated in favored grazing grounds for a few weeks. The sound is overwhelming: the constant low-frequency rumble of the herds, the calls of the flying creatures attracted by the concentration of animals, the human voices in conversation and argument and song, the drums announcing arrivals and departures. Then they disperse, each group following its herd into the vastness.

## The Makers

In the permanent settlements, in the forge-zones and processing centers, live those who make things. Not just smiths and weavers but a whole culture of material specialists who work with the resources of the plains to create the tools and infrastructure that sustain everyone.

A master smith here is an artist of sound as much as metal. They work the local alloys—combinations of metals extracted from specific ore deposits, processed through techniques developed over generations—creating tools, structural elements, the mechanical parts that keep the machinery functional. But they also create acoustic instruments: the elaborate metal arrangements worn by the listener-engineers, the tuned plates used in message-drums, the bells and chimes, the resonant chambers that amplify or filter or transform sound in specific ways.

The forge itself is a kind of theater. The smith works in heat and darkness, reading the metal by sound and touch and smell—the ring of the hammer changes as metal reaches proper temperature, the texture under the hammer tells whether another heat is needed, the scent indicates chemical composition. An apprentice might spend years learning to hear these subtleties, to understand what the metal is saying. The work produces its own acoustic signature, and experienced smiths orchestrate these sounds deliberately, creating rhythms that serve as both work pace and social communication.

Weavers work the processed grass fibers, creating textiles that range from coarse water-resistant outer layers to fine inner garments. The weaving itself is tactile poetry—fingers flying through memorized patterns, creating not just cloth but information. The weave patterns identify origin, purpose, sometimes contain encoded messages. A skilled weaver can feel a piece of fabric and read its entire history: where the grass was harvested, who processed it, when it was woven, what it's meant for.

There are rope-makers, producing the cables that connect the acoustic network, the lines used in construction, the specialized ropes used by those who venture into the deep caves. There are leather-workers, processing the hides that come from the herds, creating everything from boots to containers to the specialized sound-dampening materials. There are specialists in adhesives and sealants, in waterproofing, in the treatment of wood.

And there are those who work with the living world—not exactly farmers, because cultivation here is more like stewardship. They manage the moss-gardens, encouraging certain species over others, harvesting without destroying. They tend the bioluminescent organisms, maintaining the subtle markers that guide navigation. They process the endless vegetable matter, creating the compressed fuel-blocks, extracting useful chemicals, managing the composting operations that produce biogas.

The settlements around the forge-zones and processing centers are permanent, substantial—not cities but something more than villages. Multi-generational structures, deep-rooted, with extensive underground portions. These are social centers, places of trade and gossip and planning, places where young people come to learn crafts, where the scattered nomadic groups send representatives to exchange information and goods.

## The Deep Scouts

Then there are those who go into the places others avoid—the deep caves, the far territories, the zones where sound behaves strangely and dangers multiply. These are the explorers, the scouts, the ones drawn to edges and depths.

A deep scout is a particular type—self-reliant, comfortable with isolation, possessing exceptional spatial awareness and courage that might be indistinguishable from foolishness. They venture into the cave systems that honeycomb the ancient highlands, mapping the underground waters, finding new mineral deposits, sometimes discovering things that don't fit into any expected category.

The caves here are not dead stone but living systems—water flowing through them constantly, unusual minerals forming, strange ecologies adapted to the eternal darkness and the particular chemistry of the deep waters. The scouts speak of underground lakes vast as seas, of vertical shafts that drop for thousands of feet, of rivers that flow through chambers so large the echoes take seconds to return. They speak more quietly of the things they find in the depths: formations that seem too regular to be natural, sounds that suggest presences, waters that behave in ways water shouldn't.

They use specialized equipment—ropes and anchors, echo-enhancing devices, containers for collecting samples, extensive provisions. They map meticulously, using tactile notation, creating records that other scouts can follow. Some make their living discovering new routes, new resources, selling this information to the listener-engineers or the settlement councils. Others seem to explore for its own sake, disappearing for months, returning with stories that may or may not be believed.

There are also those who scout horizontally—ranging far across the plains, beyond the territories regularly traversed, into regions where no permanent settlements exist, where the herds rarely venture. These horizontal scouts serve as long-range contacts between distant groups, carrying messages too complex for the drum-network, negotiating with foreign groups, searching for new resources or migration routes.

Both types of scout are solitary by temperament but valued by everyone. They're welcomed in settlements, given hospitality, their information shared. They're also given a kind of respectful distance—recognized as people whose relationship with the world is different, who need the space and silence that others find unsettling.

## The Children

The young are raised communally, with biological parents maintaining strong bonds but child-rearing shared across the group. Children learn through immersion, through play that is also training, through gradual assumption of responsibility.

A child here develops senses that would seem superhuman elsewhere. By age three or four, they navigate confidently, using echolocation and acoustic awareness, rarely bumping into obstacles. They learn to identify dozens of plant species by touch and smell, to judge distances, to move silently or noisily as needed. They learn to swim early—water is everywhere, and drowning is a real danger—becoming comfortable in the marshes and streams, learning to sense currents and depths, to find firm footing in murky shallows.

They learn through stories, through songs that are also information-storage—acoustic mnemonics that encode practical knowledge, history, moral lessons. The song-teaching sessions are daily, with children learning to harmonize, to maintain complex rhythms, to use their voices as instruments. A skilled singer here can produce multiple tones simultaneously, creating overtones and undertones, turning themselves into a one-person ensemble.

They learn crafts by watching—or rather, by close proximity, by touch, by listening to the sounds of work, by gradually participating. A child in a forge-zone grows up surrounded by the sounds and smells of metal-work, begins helping with simple tasks, over years develops the understanding that eventually becomes mastery. A herder's child walks with the herd from infancy, riding on parents' backs, then walking alongside, learning the animals' patterns, until one day they're guiding them independently.

Play is elaborate—acoustic games, tracking games, competitions of silence and sound. Children develop the ability to remain utterly silent, to move without trace, and also to produce complex acoustic patterns, to coordinate group sounds, to play the environment itself like an instrument. Teenage courtship often involves these acoustic displays—elaborately orchestrated sound-pieces that demonstrate skill, creativity, understanding of the environment.

But childhood here is also hard. Mortality is not negligible. The environment is dangerous—predators, sudden weather, drowning, getting lost, infections from constant moisture. Children die. Everyone knows loss. This creates a culture that is both nurturing and realistic, that teaches caution alongside exploration, that maintains awareness of danger without becoming paralyzed by it.

## The Predators' Nemesis

There are those who specialize in hunting the hunters—not herders defending their charges but active predator-hunters who track and kill the dangerous fauna to protect settlements and trade routes. These are dangerous people, respected and a little feared, occupying a social position that is high-status but liminal.

A predator-hunter is usually scarred—the work is dangerous, and injuries accumulate. They move with lethal grace, every motion efficient, wasteless. They carry weapons adapted to darkness: spears with sound-dampening wrappings, blades that make no whisper against the air, clubs weighted for crushing skulls in a single strike. But their real weapon is knowledge—understanding predator behavior so deeply that they can anticipate, deceive, ambush.

They work alone or in small groups, tracking dangerous animals through acoustic signature and scent, through the traces they leave, through prediction and patience. A hunt might take days—following at a distance, learning the animal's patterns, waiting for the moment when it's vulnerable. The kill is quick, professional. The body is used: hide, organs, meat, bone—all valuable. The hunters take a share, the rest goes to the community.

They also serve as guards, escorting trade caravans, protecting seasonal gatherings, eliminating predators that have learned to hunt humans. They train continuously, practicing movement and silence, weapon-work, the reading of signs. Some become legendary—individuals who've killed dozens of the most dangerous predators, who can move through any terrain, who seem to possess supernatural awareness.

But it changes them. Spending so much time alone, in predator mindset, tracking and killing, creates a particular psychology. Many become quiet, socially awkward in large gatherings, more comfortable with silence than conversation. Others develop a gallows humor, a dark playfulness. Most eventually retire into other work—taking their accumulated skills into teaching, or scouting, or listener-engineering. Some don't survive long enough to retire.

## The Elders

Those who survive to old age—sixty, seventy years, occasionally longer—become repositories of knowledge, living libraries in a culture with limited writing. Memory here is extraordinary by necessity, and the elders remember—trade routes and techniques, lineages and alliances, weather patterns and historical disasters, the changes in the landscape, the locations of ruins and resources.

An elder is treated with deep respect, not just for age but for what they carry. Their acoustic memory spans decades: they remember the sound of the great storm forty years ago, the unique call-pattern of a legendary herd-leader, the song that was popular when they were young, the voice of long-dead friends. When disputes arise, elders are consulted—they remember precedents, old agreements, how similar situations were handled before.

They become storytellers, spending long evening hours with listeners gathered close, recounting histories that are also moral lessons, technical manuals, entertainment. The stories are precise—passed down through generations, memorized exactly, changed only deliberately and with community consent. This is oral history as rigorous as written, maintained through mnemonic structures, through song and rhythm, through repetition and testing.

But elders are not just passive repositories. Many remain active in governance, in decision-making, in conflict resolution. Some continue in their specializations—a listener-engineer might work well into their seventies, their experience compensating for physical decline. A master smith might maintain advisory roles long after their hands can no longer hammer metal.

Eventually, when death approaches, there are rituals. The body is given to the plains—left in designated areas where the ecology will reclaim it, where the nutrients will return to the soil, where the person rejoins the living system. There's no fear of this, no revulsion. Death is transition, integration, the completion of a cycle. The person is mourned, their stories preserved, their acoustic signature remembered and sometimes reproduced in memorial songs, but their body belongs to the world.

## The Visitors

Occasionally, people come from the lit world—traders, scholars, exiles, the curious. They're immediately recognizable by their clumsiness, their reliance on light sources that seem ridiculous here, their inability to navigate or interpret their environment. Most don't stay long. The adjustment is too profound, too uncomfortable. They leave with trade goods—processed fibers, unique alloys, plant extracts with pharmaceutical properties—and stories that are usually not believed.

A few stay. These are often people running from something, or seeking something they couldn't find elsewhere, or possessed by the kind of radical curiosity that makes them willing to remake themselves. The transition is brutal—months of stumbling helplessness, of depending on guides, of slowly developing the sensory awareness that everyone else absorbed from birth. Most who attempt it eventually give up. Those who persist gradually become competent, then skilled, then—after years—something like native.

These integrated outsiders occupy an interesting position—they remember light, can serve as translators between worlds, can explain to the native-born what's normal elsewhere and what's unique here. Some become valuable precisely because of their dual perspective, their ability to bridge cultures. Others try to forget where they came from, diving deep into this new identity, becoming more zealous than the native-born about maintaining traditional ways.

The native-born are generally hospitable to visitors—curious about the lit world, willing to trade, generous with guidance—but there's also a kind of amused pity. From their perspective, people who need light to function are handicapped, missing most of what makes life rich. The idea that their world is a place of darkness and deprivation is incomprehensible; from inside, this is simply the world, full and complete, lacking nothing.

## The Feral Ones

And then there are those who've gone too deep, who've lost or abandoned human social bonds and live entirely wild. These are rare, spoken of mostly in whispers, but they exist—individuals who've become entirely solitary, who live in the far territories or the deep caves, who've developed senses beyond even the normal exceptional range, who've perhaps become something not quite human anymore.

Stories describe encounters: a presence felt but not quite heard, moving parallel to a traveler for miles before vanishing. An unsettling perfection in the way something moves—too fluid, too silent, too aware. Finding a camp that was clearly used by a human but shows patterns that are wrong, alien. Hearing something that might be speech but isn't quite, words that almost make sense but not quite.

Whether these are real or myth is unclear. Certainly people do sometimes disappear into the wilderness and never return. Certainly the environment could support solitary humans, and certainly extended isolation would change someone profoundly. Whether it changes them into the almost-mythical figures described in the stories is another question.

What's certain is that the possibility exists in the cultural imagination—the idea that someone could become so perfectly adapted to this environment, so completely merged with the darkness and the acoustic landscape, that they'd transcend ordinary human limits. For some, this is a cautionary tale about the dangers of isolation. For others, it's a kind of aspiration, a romantic notion of ultimate freedom and capability.

## The Collective Mind

More than any individual type, what lives here is a collective awareness—the network of humans and their infrastructure and their relationships forming something larger than any individual. The drum-network and the listener-stations, the seasonal gatherings and the trade routes, the shared songs and stories and techniques, the acoustic map maintained in thousands of individual memories—all of this weaves together into something that functions almost like a distributed intelligence.

Information flows through this network constantly. A scout discovers a new water source, drums the coordinates to a listening station, which relays it through the network, which updates mental maps across hundreds of miles. A herder notes unusual predator activity, sends a message that reaches other herders within days, leading to adjusted routes and increased vigilance. A smith develops a new alloy, demonstrates it at a gathering, the knowledge spreading outward in ripples as people carry it to their home territories.

The system is resilient because it's distributed. No single node is essential. Settlements can be abandoned, individuals can die, machinery can fail, and the larger network persists. But it's also fragile in ways that aren't immediately obvious—dependent on continuous maintenance, on the training of new specialists, on the social bonds that make people willing to cooperate across vast distances.

This is what truly lives here: not just the humans and the herds and the predators and the vegetation, but the entire interactive system, the living culture that has made its home in the darkness, that has learned not just to survive but to thrive, to create meaning and beauty and complexity, to build a civilization that is unlike any other, that could only exist here, in this specific place, under these specific conditions.

They are people of the darkness, and the darkness has made them extraordinary—not superhuman but differently human, adapted and capable and entirely at home in a world that would seem impossible from outside. They live, they love, they create, they struggle, they die, and the whole vast system continues, breathing with the plains, making itself known through sound and touch and presence, endlessly fascinating, endlessly strange, endlessly alive.
