# What Lives There

## The Pneumatic Ungulates (*Pneumatica ungulata*)

They have existed here longer than the industrial remnants, though how much longer remains uncertain. Fossil evidence is sparse in shield rock that offers few depositional environments, but genetic analysis suggests deep time—hundreds of thousands of years at minimum, possibly millions, of adaptation to precisely these conditions.

Each individual is a study in controlled mass. An adult stands roughly 110 centimeters at the shoulder, extends 180 centimeters from blunt nose to abbreviated tail. Mass varies dramatically with air-sac inflation state: deflated, perhaps 140 kilograms; fully inflated for maximum buoyancy, effectively half that through displacement. The skeletal structure is dense, reinforced—necessary ballast against the pneumatic system that might otherwise make them helpless in any significant wind.

Their heads are broad and low-slung, with eyes positioned laterally but angled slightly forward, providing nearly 300-degree vision with a narrow binocular overlap. The eyes themselves are large relative to head size, adapted for low-light function: pupils that dilate enormously, retinas dense with rods, a tapetum lucidum that reflects back what little light exists. In the dimmest hours, their eyes shine if you catch them with artificial light—a green-gold eyeshine scattered across the dark steppe like distant signals.

The mouth is wide and equipped with a dental arrangement specialized for their diet: forward incisors for scraping lichen, complex grinding surfaces behind for processing the gritty material. They have no upper incisors, just a hard keratinized pad—a common ungulate configuration, but here developed to unusual toughness for working against stone surfaces. They can peel lichen from rock with remarkable precision, taking the biological material while leaving the underlying stone largely intact.

Their digestive system is elaborate and slow. Four stomach chambers, though the divisions are not quite analogous to terrestrial ruminants. The first chamber houses symbiotic bacteria and fungi capable of breaking down the complex compounds in lichen and moss—organisms that would be toxic to most herbivores due to their chemical defenses. Digestion takes days. An individual may eat for an hour in the morning (as much as this place has morning) then spend the next several days processing that meal while walking slowly across stone. They produce waste infrequently: compact pellets, nearly dry, dense with undigested minerals that will eventually break down and feed the microbial crusts.

The air-sac system ramifies through their entire body. Primary sacs lie along the flanks, beneath the shoulders, around the haunches—visible as potential swellings when inflated. Secondary sacs extend into the legs, even into the tail. Tertiary sacs branch into microscopic structures that interpenetrate muscle tissue. The entire system connects to the lungs through a series of valves that the animal controls with precision developed from birth.

Inflation serves multiple purposes. For locomotion, they can bound across terrain with inflated sacs providing both lift and shock absorption—useful for crossing the rare broken stone fields or navigating channel edges. For water crossing, even the shallow channels, they can achieve enough buoyancy to essentially walk on the surface, hooves barely breaking through, body supported by pneumatic flotation. For temperature regulation, air trapped in the sacs provides insulation against the persistent cold. For communication, air forced through specialized chambers produces their characteristic tones.

These tones are more complex than they initially seem. Field recordings, subjected to analysis, reveal harmonic structures, subtle frequency modulations, patterns that vary by individual. Whether this constitutes language in any meaningful sense remains debated, but it clearly conveys information. A sharp exhalation seems to signal alarm—others nearby will raise their heads, sometimes inflate partially in preparation for movement. A longer, more modulated tone might indicate location—useful in mist when visual contact is impossible. Some tones seem to carry emotional content: a particular low frequency appears correlated with contentment, often heard when an individual has found good grazing or water.

They rarely vocalize through their mouths. That channel seems reserved for breathing and eating. The air-sac system has become the voice.

Social structure is diffuse but real. They do not form tight hierarchical groups with clear leaders. Instead, they operate through what behavioral ecologists call "distributed decision-making." An individual begins moving in a particular direction; nearby individuals may follow or may not. If several begin moving similarly, a trend establishes itself. More join, momentum builds, and a migration pattern emerges—but it has no single origin point, no alpha individual commanding movement. It crystallizes from collective tendency.

Within this loose structure, there are persistent associations. Certain individuals are often found within a few hundred meters of each other, day after day, week after week. These might be kin groups—mothers and offspring maintain proximity for years—or they might be preference-based affiliations. Juveniles tend to cluster more tightly than adults, groups of adolescents forming temporary cohorts that roam together before gradually dispersing into the broader population.

Mating happens during the slightly warmer periods, if those can be called seasons. Males develop enlarged neck sacs and produce deeper tones, spending hours calling across the steppe. Females approach or don't. There is little fighting—males assess each other through tone contests, deep reverberating exchanges that seem to communicate size, health, air capacity. The individual with the lowest sustainable tone usually prevails without physical contact.

Gestation lasts approximately thirteen standard months. Births occur alone—females separate from the loose herds, find sheltered depressions, deliver single offspring rarely, twins occasionally. The newborn can walk within hours, a necessity on terrain that offers no safe stationary position. The juvenile's air-sac system is functional but underdeveloped; full pneumatic capacity takes years to mature. Mothers and young rejoin the broader population within days, the juvenile learning to keep pace, to find water, to identify edible growths, to modulate its developing air-sacs through observation and instinct.

Mortality is quiet and often invisible. There are no predators—none have been documented, at least. Deaths come from accumulated deficits: an individual unable to find sufficient water during a dry span, gradually weakening. Disease, sometimes—respiratory infections can spread through groups in close proximity, though the spacing of the population limits epidemics. Injury, occasionally—a leg broken in a fall from a swell's edge, leaving the individual unable to keep up, eventually unable to rise. The cold takes the weakened.

Bodies are processed by the landscape itself. The tough hide resists decay initially, but eventually desiccates, cracks open. Insects—small, pale, specialized—move in. There are beetles that feed only on ungulate keratin, their larvae developing in the hollow of a horn or hoof. Flies of peculiar morphology lay eggs in body cavities, their maggots reducing flesh to liquid that drains into stone. Fungi colonize from within, having entered through the respiratory system as spores, waiting for death to activate their saprophytic phase. Within months, a body becomes a low mound of deflated hide over scattered bones. Within years, even the bones fragment, their organic components consumed, their mineral components scattering as gravel that eventually becomes indistinguishable from the substrate.

The population seems stable—no boom and bust cycles evident in the years since detailed observation began. This suggests a system in equilibrium: birth rates matching mortality rates, resource consumption matching resource regeneration. The lichen and moss grow slowly but persistently; the ungulates crop but don't eliminate; nutrients cycle through organism to waste to stone to organism. It is an old balance, worn smooth.

## The Microfauna

Beneath notice, sometimes literally beneath the surface, other lives persist.

In the films of water that cover stone after condensation events, protozoans bloom: single-celled organisms that can desiccate to near-indestructibility, then rehydrate and become active within minutes of water contact. They feed on bacteria, on algae, on each other. Under microscopic examination, these temporary pools contain astonishing diversity—dozens of species in a single hand-sized puddle, each pursuing strategies for maximum reproduction before the water vanishes. Some form cysts that blow across the steppe as dust, settling into the next depression, waiting for the next water. Some have evolved to process the mineral-heavy water, sequestering metals in vacuoles, creating internal galleries of toxic beauty.

The moss hosts an entire community. Between the individual moss stems, arthropods smaller than rice grains maintain territories. Springtails—hexapods with furca that propel them in explosive jumps—graze on moss surfaces, eating the algae and fungi that colonize the stems. Mites patrol the understory, predatory species hunting the grazers, herbivorous species eating the moss itself. Pseudoscorpions, barely visible even with magnification, hunt among the moss holdfasts, their pedipalps seizing springtails and smaller mites.

In the lichen, the diversity becomes almost absurd. Each lichen is already a symbiosis—fungus and alga or cyanobacterium, sometimes both—but dozens of other organisms have evolved to exploit that partnership. Lichen-specialist mites feed only on particular lichen compounds. Beetles with sculptured exoskeletons matching the lichen's texture graze on its surface, invisible against their food source. Parasitic fungi attack the lichen fungi, creating galls and deformations that themselves become habitat for yet other organisms.

The industrial ruins host their own specialists. In the oil spill around the functioning pumpjack, bacteria have evolved to metabolize petroleum. They form mats—visible as rainbow sheens on the black surface—processing the hydrocarbons, slowly, slowly, converting them into forms other organisms can use. The mats grow thicker each year. Eventually, perhaps, they will remediate the spill entirely, but that process will take decades at current rates.

In the crevices of rusted metal, lithophytic bacteria establish themselves—rock-eating species that weren't restricted to natural stone, that found industrial alloys equally suitable. They contribute to corrosion, secreting acids that dissolve metal, extracting iron and other elements for their metabolic processes. The rust itself becomes ecosystem: fungi colonize the flaking oxides, drawing moisture from air, creating microhabitats where yet smaller organisms shelter.

Birds are rare. Perhaps twice a month, a high-altitude flyer appears—a silhouette against the clouds, circling slowly, watching the steppe with eyes adapted for spotting small movements from great heights. They might be raptors, if that term applies on this world. They have hooked beaks and taloned feet. They descend occasionally to take something—perhaps the juvenile of an ungulate, perhaps some microfauna aggregation that became dense enough to warrant attention. They don't land for long. There are no trees, no cliffs, no perching sites. They are visitors to the steppe, not residents.

Flying insects exist but are seldom seen. The cold suppresses them. They emerge during the warmest hours, flying low and fast, seeking the temporary pools where they lay eggs. Their larvae develop rapidly—ten days from egg to adult—feeding on the protozoans and algae, transforming water into wings before the water vanishes. Most pools dry before the cycle completes. Evolution's answer to this has been massive egg production: lay thousands, accept that only a tiny percentage will complete development, rely on sheer numbers and the vast quantity of temporary pools across the steppe's extent.

## The Cryptic Underground

Beneath the visible surface, in the porous stone and crack systems, other communities persist.

Water that vanishes into boreholes and fissures doesn't disappear—it percolates downward, flowing through channels too small or too inconsistent to map. In these dark, cold, perpetually damp spaces, specialized organisms have evolved. Colorless amphipods—crustaceans that lost pigmentation across evolutionary time—navigate through water-filled voids. They feed on detritus washed down from above: fragments of lichen, moss spores, the dissolved components of ungulate waste.

In certain deeper channels, fish exist—or fish-like organisms at least. Elongated, scaleless, with reduced eyes or no eyes at all. They navigate by lateral line, sensing pressure changes in water. They feed on the amphipods, on worms that also inhabit these spaces, on each other. How they arrived in this deep habitat is uncertain—perhaps the channels connect to surface water features that no longer exist, ancient lakes now dried, leaving only the underground remnants of their ecosystems.

Fungi extend through the substrate, mycelia threading through cracks, connecting distant sites. Some are saprophytes, feeding on dead plant material wedged in crevices. Others are parasites, their spores settling on lichen or moss, sending hyphae into the plant tissues. A few are mutualists, forming associations with the roots of wire-grass, exchanging soil nutrients for photosynthetic sugars. The mycelia can extend for hundreds of meters, a single fungal individual spanning vast areas, though its body remains hidden, thread-thin, detectable only by the fruiting bodies it sends up occasionally: small mushrooms, pale or gray, appearing after mist-events, releasing spores, then collapsing into slime within days.

## The Seasonal Lives

In the variations that might constitute seasons—difficult to define but statistically present in long-term data—certain organisms become more active.

During the relatively warmer spans, lichen growth accelerates slightly. Reproductive structures appear: apothecia, small cup-like formations at a lichen's surface, releasing spores in quantities that briefly color the air if disturbed. The wire-grass flowers, if "flowers" is the right term—tiny structures, barely differentiated from the vegetative parts, wind-pollinated, producing seeds so light they blow across kilometers before settling.

The moss produces sporophytes: thin stalks rising from the moss cushions, each topped with a capsule of spores. These appear suddenly, thousands of them across a moss bed, giving the growth a fuzzy appearance. The capsules mature, split, release spores that the thin wind carries. Most land on inhospitable stone. A tiny fraction finds a crack with sufficient moisture and begins the slow process of establishing a new moss colony.

The ungulates, as mentioned, mate during warmer periods. But they also grow thicker insulation then—the short fibers of their hides elongating slightly, creating a denser layer against the coming cold. This suggests some environmental cue they're responding to, some pattern in temperature or light or atmospheric pressure that signals seasonal change even when the change itself is subtle.

During the coldest spans, activity drops. The ungulates move less, conserving energy, relying more on stored body fat. Many lichen species become dormant, their metabolic processes slowing to near-halt. The wire-grass yellows slightly, its already-minimal growth stopping entirely. Even the microfauna become scarcer—protozoan cysts predominate over active cells, springtails enter diapause in the moss holdfasts.

But nothing stops completely. The world continues its patient functioning even at coldest. Water still moves through underground channels. Bacteria still metabolize, though slowly. The ungulates still need to eat, still crop the lichen, their bodies requiring fuel to generate warmth. It is a dimmed version of an already-dim ecology, but it persists.

## Intelligence and Awareness

The question of what thinks here, what understands, is more complex than it might initially seem.

The ungulates have brains of moderate size—smaller than terrestrial cattle relative to body mass, but with cortical folding that suggests complexity. Behavioral observations reveal problem-solving capacity: an individual encountering a channel too wide to cross comfortably will walk along it until finding a narrower section, rather than attempting a dangerous leap. They remember locations—water sources, good grazing areas, sheltered sites—and return to them appropriately. Mothers teach juveniles through example: which plants are edible, how to modulate air-sacs for different purposes, how to respond to the rare environmental hazards.

Is this intelligence? It is adaptation, certainly. It is behavioral flexibility. Whether it crosses some threshold into actual cognition—into subjective experience, into something that could be called thought—remains unknowable from outside. They have inner lives, certainly. Whether those lives include reflection, anticipation, understanding beyond immediate stimulus-response is a question that observation cannot definitively answer.

Their communication system suggests complexity. The tonal patterns vary by context, by individual, by social situation. New patterns emerge and spread through populations—what some researchers call "acoustic culture." A particular call pattern might appear in one group, prove effective for some purpose (coordinating movement? signaling water discovery?), and gradually propagate to neighboring individuals, spreading across the steppe at speeds measurable in weeks or months. This implies learning, imitation, social transmission of information.

Some individuals seem more—"intelligent" is the wrong word—more something than others. More responsive, more flexible, more successful in navigating the steppe's challenges. These individuals often have more juveniles nearby, suggesting others recognize and affiliate with capability. Natural selection operating on behavioral variation, creating gradients of cognitive capacity across the population.

Whether they have awareness of death is unclear. They show interest in bodies of their own kind—approaching, investigating, sometimes calling—but whether this represents grief, confusion, or simple curiosity cannot be determined. After investigation, they move on. The body becomes landscape, gradually processed by smaller lives into substrate.

## The Ecology of Silence

What emerges from all these lives—the millions of ungulates, the uncountable lichens and mosses, the hidden underground organisms, the rare birds, the microfauna—is an ecology of profound quietness.

There are no dawn choruses because there is no dawn. There are no territorial displays because territories are not defensible on terrain this vast and uniform. There are no alarm calls because there are no predators requiring alarm. The sounds that exist—hoof on stone, wind through ruins, the occasional low tone from an air-sac—punctuate silence rather than creating soundscape.

This quietness is not absence. It is the acoustic signature of an ecology that has minimized energy expenditure across all its components. Sound production requires energy; in an environment where energy is persistently limited, where plant growth is slow and nutrition is marginal, evolutionary pressure favors silence. The ungulates communicate when necessary but not otherwise. The insects are too small to produce sound we can hear. The plants have no need for acoustic signaling.

The quietness creates disorientation in human visitors. Our species evolved in environments rich with sound, where acoustic information provided constant updates on surroundings. Here, that information channel is nearly empty. Distance becomes difficult to judge—sound doesn't carry reliably, so the usual cues (how loud, how clear, how delayed the echo) become unreliable. Time feels different when not marked by regular sounds. Some observers report almost hallucinatory experiences: phantom sounds, auditory pareidolia, the mind generating noise to fill the vacuum.

The ungulates don't seem troubled. They have adapted across deep time to this acoustic environment. Their large eyes provide the primary information channel. Their sense of smell is acute—the thin air carries scents far, and they can detect water, good grazing, other individuals by chemical cues. Their spatial memory is excellent—they navigate by recognizing stone formations, channel patterns, subtle variations in terrain that to human perception seem identical.

## Temporal Ecology

The different organisms here operate on radically different timescales, creating an ecology that layers rapid and slow in the same space.

The bacteria in the water films live and die in hours. A single day encompasses generations—bloom, reproduction, encystment. Their experience of the steppe, if "experience" applies to bacteria, is of a world that constantly vanishes and reappears: water exists, we grow, water disappears, we sleep, water returns, we wake.

The springtails and mites have lifespans of weeks to months. For them, the steppe is constant but slowly changing—moss beds grow slightly, lichen patches expand, the locations of reliable moisture shift gradually. Their behavior shows no awareness of larger patterns; they respond to immediate conditions, perpetually present-focused.

The ungulates live decades—twenty to thirty years seems typical based on tooth-wear analysis of dead specimens. For them, the steppe has history. An individual remembers routes established in youth, sees the same channels deepen over years, watches lichen formations expand across remembered stone. They accumulate knowledge, become repositories of spatial and ecological information, living libraries of where and when and how.

The lichen individuals live longer still—centuries, potentially. The largest formations are likely older than any ungulate alive, older than the industrial installations, possibly older than human presence in this system. Their temporality is almost geological. The stone beneath them weathers measurably during their lifespans. They change the substrate that supports them, gradually, chemically, creating their own future conditions.

These timescales coexist but rarely interact directly. The bacteria don't affect the lichen's century-long growth except in aggregate—billions of bacterial blooms across hundreds of years contributing to nutrient cycling that eventually feeds lichen. The lichen doesn't affect the bacteria except by providing, slowly, the organic compounds that eventually become food for saprophytes that eventually break down into nutrients that eventually reach the water films.

The ungulates bridge scales: long-lived enough to experience slow changes, active enough to interact with fast processes. They crop lichen, affecting growth patterns that persist beyond their individual lifespans. They distribute nutrients through waste, feeding bacterial and fungal communities. They create paths through repeated walking, patterns that will persist for decades, perhaps centuries if the population maintains these routes.

## The Failed Colony

Among the industrial ruins, one installation stands out: a habitat module, heavily corroded but still recognizably architectural. It sits on a slight rise, visible from several kilometers. Solar panels, most broken, cant at various angles from its roof. An airlock door hangs partially open, jammed in position by rust and the subtle shifting of the foundation.

Inside—accessible through the broken airlock—evidence of brief human presence remains. Equipment racks, empty now, their contents either removed during evacuation or scavenged later. A water recycling unit, non-functional, its membranes dried and cracked. Sleeping quarters, narrow bunks built into walls, still fitted with degraded textiles. A common area with a table surface and bench seating. Viewports, scratched and cratered by windblown particles but still transparent enough to see out.

Graffiti on the walls, scratched or written: tallies of days, names, crude drawings. One that appears repeatedly: "How long?" No answers provided.

The module housed fifteen people for approximately eight months—the duration indicated by log entries on a data storage device that somehow survived. They were resource assessment personnel, evaluating the economic viability of expanded extraction operations. They lived in this module, conducted surveys, drilled test boreholes, analyzed samples. Their logs describe a gradual accumulation of problems: equipment failures, supply delivery delays, communication difficulties, interpersonal tensions, and—increasingly prominent in later entries—psychological effects.

"The quiet is wrong," one entry reads. "It's not that it's too quiet. It's that it's quiet in the wrong way. Silence should feel empty but this feels full. Like something is listening but there's nothing there to listen. Like the whole steppe is paying attention but it doesn't have attention to pay."

Another: "Saw an ungulate today with a broken leg. It was lying down in a depression about half a kilometer from here. I walked out to it thinking maybe we could help—we have medical supplies, some of it might work. But when I got close it just looked at me. Just looked. Its eyes were clear, not panicked, not in obvious pain, just looking. It knew it was dying. I know animals aren't supposed to know that but this one knew. I couldn't think of what to do so I walked back. It's probably still there."

Later entries show deterioration: "Third week without supply drop. Communications still down. Jensen says the satellite is fine, it's the orbital relay that's malfunctioning, but who knows if that's true. We have food for another two months if we ration. Water indefinitely from the processor if it keeps working. But I don't know if I can do two more months. It's not about supplies. It's about being here."

The final entry: "Evacuation shuttle confirmed for tomorrow. We're leaving most of the equipment. No point taking it—the whole operation is shutting down, not just this site. The economics don't work, they say, but I think it's not about economics. I think people just don't want to be here. We've been here eight months and I don't understand this place any better than when we arrived. Maybe less. The ungulates, the lichen, the stone—it all just continues. It doesn't care that we came. It won't care that we left. Something about that is unbearable."

The module has been empty since. The ungulates ignore it mostly, though occasionally one will peer through a viewport, its dark eye appearing in the scratched transparency, looking at the abandoned human space, then moving on.

## What Endures

The dominant fact, the overwhelming truth of the Pneumatic Steppe, is persistence.

The lichen persists through cold that would kill most organisms, through drought periods lasting weeks, through grazing pressure from millions of ungulates. It grows slowly but reliably, converting stone and air and water into living tissue, millimeter by millimeter, year after year.

The moss persists in its scattered colonies, holding moisture in its interwoven stems, creating microhabitats in a landscape largely hostile to complexity. It dies back during the coldest periods, returns during the warmer spans, maintains its positions in favorable locations across decades.

The ungulates persist through collective adaptation: their pneumatic systems, their efficient metabolism, their ability to process low-quality forage, their distributed social structure that requires no complex coordination. They have been shaped by this landscape across evolutionary time and now fit it precisely, moving across it in vast numbers without depleting it, dying at rates that exactly balance births, maintaining equilibrium.

The industrial remnants persist through simple durability: metals that corrode slowly in dry cold, synthetics that degrade but don't disappear, structures that settle and crack but don't collapse. They will remain recognizable for centuries, monuments to economic calculations made by people who never visited, who saw only data, who decided there wasn't enough profit to justify continued presence.

The landscape itself persists in its slow geological patience. The shield has existed for billions of years, worn by time into its current configuration. It will continue essentially unchanged—the same rolling swells, the same exposed stone, the same channels and depressions—for millions of years more, barring geological catastrophe. Everything else—the organisms, the industrial objects, even the atmospheric conditions—is superficial, temporary, occurring in the thin skin between stone and sky.

Humans came, briefly, and found the place hostile not in any dramatic way but in a cumulative one—the cold that never relented, the distances that confounded, the silence that disturbed, the feeling of being irrelevant to a system that had been functioning long before their arrival and would continue unchanged after their departure. They left.

The ungulates remain. They will likely remain indefinitely, until some external change—climate shift, asteroid impact, the eventual expansion of the star into red giant phase—disrupts the conditions that support them. They are at equilibrium with this place. They are, in a sense, the place made flesh: cold-adapted, patient, quiet, persistent, distributed across vast distances, living in the narrow margin between stone and sky, processing the steppe's minimal biological production into their own mass, then returning that mass to the system when they die, maintaining the cycle.

Watching them—standing on a rise as dim light reduces them to silhouettes scattered to every horizon, hearing occasionally the low tones they produce, seeing them move slowly over stone polished by millions of hooves—produces an odd feeling. Not exactly awe, not exactly loneliness. Something like recognition of a system that has achieved stability, that has solved the equation of how to persist in a place that offers minimal resources, that has found sufficiency in limitation.

They continue. The lichen continues. The moss continues. The stone continues. The dim sky continues. The cold continues.

This is what lives here: everything that has learned not just to survive but to endure, to outlast, to remain. This is an ecology of the permanent, the patient, the quietly persistent. It offers no drama, no spectacle, no moments of intensity or transformation. It offers only continuation—the same processes today as yesterday as a thousand years ago, functioning reliably, requiring nothing external, self-contained and self-perpetuating.

And that, perhaps, is enough. That is what it means to truly inhabit a place: not to conquer it or transform it or make it yield, but to fit into it so precisely that you become inseparable from it, part of the same pattern, subject to the same rhythms, participant in the same quiet ongoing.

The Pneumatic Steppe continues. What lives there continues. The two are, finally, the same thing.
