# Those Who Remain Level

They are few, and they know it. The number hardly varies—somewhere between forty and sixty souls across the seasons, across the years. Enough to maintain what must be maintained. Not so many that the terraces cannot feed them, that the intermittent water becomes crisis rather than constraint. The population has found its ceiling, or the ceiling has found the population. Births are infrequent events, widely spaced. Deaths come slowly, usually to the very old. The arithmetic stays roughly constant.

They move with economy. You notice this first—how someone crosses a terrace without hurry but without dawdling, how hands go to tasks with minimal wasted motion, how even speech seems edited down to what's necessary. It's not efficiency in the driving, optimized sense. It's something older: the learned choreography of people who understand that energy, like water, arrives in finite quantities and must be allocated with thought. A young woman scraping grain from stalks doesn't rush. The rhythm is steady, sustainable, a pace that can be maintained hour after hour. An old man repairing a gap in a retaining wall tests each stone for fit with patient repetition, setting aside those that don't sit right, waiting for the one that does. No one here performs urgency.

Their bodies tell the terms of this life. Lean, most of them, in the way of people who work steadily but never quite have abundance. Not starved—the grain yields what it yields, supplemented by what can be gathered or occasionally caught—but held at a sustainable thinness. Muscle exists where it's needed: in forearms that grip stone tools, in legs that navigate terraces daily, in backs that lift and carry. Faces are weathered young, the skin taking on the quality of the place—dried, toughened, lined early by the clean light and the dry air. Hands are callused in patterns specific to their work: the outer edge of the palm from grinding grain, the fingertips from flaking stone, the base of the thumb from gripping tool handles wrapped in leather cord.

They wear layers, always. The innermost is usually wool—felted or loosely woven, depending on what fiber they have—holding warmth against skin. Over this, woven cloth, muted in color: the pale gray-brown of undyed fiber, the darker brown of plant-dyed thread, occasionally the deeper gray of wool from darker animals. Outer layers might be leather, stiff and smoke-cured, patched at elbows and knees with careful stitching. Footwear is simple: wrapped leather bound with cord, or wooden soles with leather uppers, or for those doing delicate work on walls, bare feet that know every angle of stone. Everyone has a head covering of some kind—woven cloth bound around the temples, or leather caps, or hoods attached to outer garments—donned when the midday sun grows strong or when wind picks up. They dress for the eternal cool, and for the small variations within it.

Ages distribute unevenly. There are more older people than you might expect, and fewer young children. Several individuals have reached what must be sixty, seventy years—difficult to count precisely when faces age fast and no one tracks years with precision. These elders move more slowly but are still moving, still contributing what they can: sorting seeds, watching over grain as it dries, working leather with patient hands, tending fires. The middle-aged—thirty to fifty, in rough estimation—do the heavier work of wall repair and water management and the endless labor of cultivation. Younger adults are fewer, perhaps six or eight in total, and they carry a disproportionate share of the physical burden. Children are rare enough to notice individually: maybe five or six across the whole settlement, ranging from infants carried in slings to those near adolescence who work alongside adults at tasks within their strength.

They do not gather often, but when they do, it's in the paved area before the larger communal building. Not daily—that would be unnecessary—but when something requires collective decision or shared labor. Perhaps a section of terrace wall has degraded beyond individual repair and needs coordinated rebuilding. Perhaps stored grain needs redistributing. Perhaps there is news—someone has died, or someone has seen something unusual beyond the settlement's edge, or a decision must be made about water allocation in a particularly dry span. They come then, in ones and twos, assembling without announcement, as if some shared sense of timing operates among them.

The gatherings are quiet affairs. People stand or sit on the stone paving, forming a loose cluster rather than organized rows. Whoever has something to say speaks, standing in place, not raising voice much above conversation. Others listen. If there's disagreement, it surfaces as alternative proposals, stated plainly. "The north wall needs repair first." "The east terrace has less grain; we should redistribute from storage." The tone stays level. Arguments, when they happen, are about logistics, about allocation, about the practical questions of maintaining equilibrium. They resolve not through voting or decree but through talking until a consensus emerges, or until the most practical option becomes obvious to enough people that others acquiesce.

Leadership exists but sits lightly. There are those whose judgment carries more weight—usually the older ones who've maintained this place longest, who remember more dry seasons and more repairs, whose hands have shaped more of the infrastructure that everyone depends on. An old woman, perhaps seventy, her hair gone entirely white, her hands twisted with age but still capable, speaks and people listen with attention. A man in late middle age, his face deeply lined, who seems to understand water better than anyone—where it will flow, how the channels should be cut, when the cisterns need sealing compound renewed—offers opinions that are rarely questioned. But they don't command. They suggest, they advise, they offer memory of what worked before. If someone has a better idea, and can articulate why it's better, it will be considered. Authority here is earned through demonstrated competence and renewed through continued contribution.

The children, when you watch them, seem oddly quiet. They play, certainly—you might see two of them balancing on a low retaining wall, or arranging small stones in patterns, or chasing the occasional bird—but the play is muted, contained. They don't shriek or run wild. Perhaps they learn early that noise carries in the thin air and serves no purpose. Perhaps they absorb the settlement's baseline quietness the way they absorb its cool air. They work, too, earlier than children elsewhere might. A child of perhaps eight hauls water from cistern to building, the bucket sized to her strength. Another, slightly older, helps winnow grain, learning the wrist motion that separates chaff from seed. They watch adults at their tasks with serious attention, and adults include them in the work without making much ceremony of it. Childhood here is not separate from the settlement's labor; it's apprenticeship from the earliest possible age.

Speech, when it comes, is sparse but not unfriendly. The language they speak is old—you hear it in the phonemes that seem worn smooth, in the grammar that bends in archaic ways, in words for stone and water and grain that sound like they've been spoken on these terraces for centuries. Conversations are brief. "The east cistern is low." "I'll seal the gap tomorrow." "The grain on terrace nine is ready." Information transfers efficiently. There's little small talk, little elaboration for its own sake. This isn't coldness—watch two people meet on a pathway and you might see a nod, a slight smile, a hand briefly touching a shoulder as they pass. Affection exists, but it doesn't require constant verbal expression.

They eat together sometimes, but not always. Meals are simple, taken in individual dwellings or occasionally shared among two or three families in one of the larger buildings. The grain they grow—ground to coarse flour, mixed with water, cooked on flat stones over small fires—forms the base of most meals. It's supplemented by what grows wild: the seeds of native grasses, gathered and parched; the waxy leaves of certain bushes, chewed for their moisture and slight nutrient value; occasionally roots, dug from terrace edges where they've worked down into accumulated soil. Protein is rare. Perhaps once a week, someone manages to catch one of the small brown birds, or a lean mammal from beyond the settlement's edge, and the meat is shared, distributed across multiple families in portions that seem small but are received as abundance. They eat without complaint, without much comment on flavor or variety. Food is fuel, carefully allocated, consumed with the same steady attention they bring to other tasks.

Water drinking is ritualized in small ways. You don't gulp here. You don't waste drops. Someone drinking from a clay cup does so slowly, in measured sips, making the water last. Children learn this early: the cup is held with both hands, raised with deliberation, lowered carefully. Spilling is rare and visibly distressing when it happens—a child whose hand slips, sloshing water onto stone, will stop and stare at the spreading wetness with something like grief before an adult comes to reassure them quietly that it's alright, these things happen, but be more careful. The spilled water is sometimes gathered with rags if there's enough of it, the rags then wrung into a cistern or a clay vessel, recovering what can be recovered.

They sleep in cycles adjusted to light and temperature. Retiring early after the sun drops, when the cold begins its earnest work—into the thick-walled buildings where body heat and small fires create warmth enough to sleep. Rising early, before dawn, when the stone has spent its last stored warmth but before the day's coolness sets in hardest. The hours around sunrise and late afternoon are the most active. Midday brings a slowing, people working in shade where they can find it or pausing tasks that aren't urgent. No one pushes against the rhythms the place imposes.

Illness, when it comes, is managed quietly. You might see someone moving more slowly, or not emerging from their dwelling for a day or two, and others bringing them water, bringing them food. The settlement has someone—usually one of the elders—who knows plants, who knows poultices, who knows what can be done with the limited pharmacopeia available at this elevation. Most ailments are respiratory: coughs that linger in the cool air, chest infections that come after rain when damp creeps into buildings. Some are digestive, from food that wasn't stored quite right or water that got contaminated. Some are injuries: a fall from a terrace, a stone that slipped during wall work, a cut that became infected. The healer does what can be done. Sometimes it's enough. Sometimes it isn't. When death comes, it's accepted with the same level pragmatism that governs everything else here.

The dead are buried beyond the settlement's edge, in ground too rocky for terracing. Simple graves, marked with stone cairns. The burial is communal—whoever can come, does, standing in a loose circle while the body, wrapped in cloth, is lowered into a cavity that took someone most of a day to excavate from the resistant soil. Words are spoken, brief and formulaic: acknowledgment of the person's work, of what they maintained, of how they contributed to the settlement's continuance. Then stone is piled over the grave, and people return to the terraces. Grief happens privately, in individual dwellings, expressed in quietness rather than wailing. Life continues with one fewer set of hands, and the remaining hands adjust their labor accordingly.

Children are born in the thick-walled buildings, attended by women who've done this before, who know what can go wrong and sometimes how to prevent it. Birth is dangerous here—no medical technology beyond traditional knowledge, no intervention available if something goes seriously wrong. Some births go well. Some don't. When a child survives the first year, there's quiet relief, though not celebration. The child is simply incorporated into the settlement's routines, carried in a sling while a parent works, gradually given small tasks as capability develops. Names are given, simple and phonetically straightforward: sounds that carry well, that don't confuse in the thin air, that distinguish individuals without elaborate meaning.

Relationships form, though not with ceremony. You might notice two people spending more time near each other, working adjacent terraces, entering and leaving the same building. Partnerships happen, usually monogamous from what you can observe, though there's no formal bonding ritual. They simply become a pair, move into a dwelling together, combine their labor. Sometimes partnerships involve children eventually. Sometimes they don't. If a partnership dissolves—rarely, but it happens—one person moves to another dwelling and life continues. The settlement is too small for elaborate drama, too dependent on collective function to allow extended conflict.

Status differentials are minimal. Everyone works. Even the elders contribute what they can. The person who's best at flaking stone tools isn't treated differently from the person who's best at predicting rain. Competence is noted and utilized, but it doesn't translate to privilege. The larger dwelling near the center stores communal grain, not the surplus of a chief or leader. When food is distributed, it's distributed by need—those doing heavier labor get slightly more, small children get less not because they're valued less but because they need less, the ill or injured get what they require to recover. The arithmetic is practical, not hierarchical.

They maintain the old knowledge carefully. The elders teach the younger ones: how to read the stone for the right kind to use in tools, how to cut channels that will carry water without eroding, how to build a wall that will last generations, how to felt wool, how to identify the few edible plants, how to time the grain planting by the angle of sun and the pattern of mist. This knowledge transfers slowly, through demonstration and repetition, through years of watching and then doing under supervision. There's no written record—no one here writes, or if they once did, they've stopped—so everything exists in memory and practice. When an elder dies, some knowledge dies too, unless it was transferred in time. This is understood. It makes the teaching relationships essential, almost sacred, though no one would use that word.

They have stories, certainly, though they're told infrequently. On occasional evenings, when work is done and people have gathered for some reason, someone might tell one—sitting near the fire in the communal building, speaking in the old language, recounting how the terraces were first built (by ancestors whose names have been forgotten, who worked for years beyond counting), or how the settlement survived a particularly long dry period (by rationing, by discipline, by collective will), or about the plateau itself and what lies beyond (lower lands, other settlements perhaps, though no one here has traveled to them in living memory). The stories are short, unembellished, told in the same even tone used for conversation. They serve as shared history, as moral instruction (work together, waste nothing, maintain what you inherit), as entertainment in a place where entertainment is scarce.

Music is almost absent. Perhaps someone has a simple flute, carved from wood or bone, and plays it occasionally—simple melodic lines, repetitive, not quite songs. Perhaps there's rhythmic chanting during the rare communal work efforts, a way to coordinate labor. But there's no tradition of elaborate music here, no instruments beyond the most basic, no performances. Sound, like everything else, is used sparingly.

Art is functional. The baskets woven tight enough to hold grain show patterns—not decorative patterns added for beauty, but the patterns inherent in the weaving technique, subtle variations in over-under rhythm that create visual texture. The clay vessels are smooth and well-formed but undecorated. The stone walls show the aesthetic of careful fitting but nothing added beyond structural necessity. If there's beauty here, it's the beauty of right proportion, of appropriate technique, of things made to last and to work. No one paints images on walls. No one carves decorative figures. The aesthetic is one of refined utility.

They mark time loosely. There's no calendar in any formal sense, no written tracking of days or years. Seasons are minimal at this elevation—wet and dry more than hot and cold, and even those not dramatically different. People know roughly how many rainfalls have passed, roughly how old they are, roughly how long ago something happened. "Many rains ago" is precise enough. "Before I was born" is precise enough. The lack of formal time-keeping reinforces the sense of suspended duration, of days that blur into each other, of change happening so slowly it hardly registers as change.

Outsiders are almost theoretical. The settlement sees no trade, no visitors, no contact with other populations. Whether other settlements exist elsewhere on the plateau or in the valleys below is unknown or at least never discussed. The people here are turned inward, focused entirely on the terraces they occupy and the systems they maintain. If someone arrived from outside—exhausted traveler, lost wanderer—they would likely be received with cautious hospitality, given water and food, allowed to rest, then expected either to integrate into the settlement's labor or to move on. But this doesn't happen, or hasn't happened in anyone's memory. The settlement exists in isolation, complete unto itself.

They experience weather intimately. Everyone can read the sky—the particular quality of light that means rain is coming, the shift in wind direction that precedes the brief storms, the thickness in the air on mornings when mist will be heavy. When rain comes, people pause work to watch it, to ensure the channels are flowing right, to check that cisterns are catching properly. Children are allowed to stand in it briefly, lifting faces to the cold drops. Adults might extend a hand, letting water pool in the palm, feeling its weight before drinking it or pouring it into a vessel. Rain is respected, welcomed, managed. Dry periods are endured without panic—this is expected, this is normal—but you can see the tension in how often people check the cisterns, in how carefully water is distributed.

The rare hot day, when sun breaks through and the usual cool recedes briefly, makes everyone uncomfortable. People retreat to the shade of buildings, slow their work even further, drink more water than usual. They're adapted to cool, and warmth throws off the calibration. But these days are infrequent enough not to require significant adaptation. By evening, the baseline cool returns, and everyone relaxes back into their normal rhythms.

Wind is common enough to be unremarkable. It comes from the west usually, steady rather than gusting, carrying the smell of distance—dry stone, sparse vegetation, the plateau's particular mineral scent. Strong wind makes certain tasks harder—trying to winnow grain becomes frustrating when gusts scatter chaff back into seeds—so people wait for calmer hours. Wind at night makes sleep harder, the sound of it working into the gaps in stone walls, whistling and moaning softly. But it's part of the place's vocabulary of sound, as familiar as the drip of water or the tap of footsteps.

They take injuries seriously because they must. A broken bone means someone can't work, can't contribute, becomes a drain on resources until healed. The healer does what's possible—splinting, immobilizing, administering poultices that might reduce infection—and the injured person rests, cared for by others, while bone knits. Recovery is slow. People resume work before they're fully healed, doing what they can with limited mobility, because the settlement's margin is too thin for prolonged incapacity. Those who become permanently disabled—an elder who can no longer walk steadily, a younger person whose injury didn't heal right—shift to whatever tasks they can manage: sitting work, fine detail work, teaching.

Hair is kept short or bound back, practical rather than decorative. Men and women both—there's no gender differentiation in hairstyle. Long hair is a liability, getting in the way of work, requiring water to wash. Most people cut it themselves with stone blades, roughly and infrequently, resulting in uneven lengths that speak to function over form. Beards on men are usually short, trimmed or plucked, though some of the older men let them grow longer, gray and wiry.

They smell of smoke and stone dust, of wool and leather, of grain and occasionally of the waxy plants they sometimes handle. No perfumes, no scented oils—those would be absurd extravagances here. Body odor is present but not overwhelming; the cool air and the dry climate limit sweating. Cleanliness is maintained pragmatically: occasional washing with water when it can be spared, more often just brushing off dust, scraping dried mud from skin and clothing, combing fingers through hair to dislodge debris.

They experience solitude regularly, necessarily. The spatial distribution of the settlement means people spend hours each day working alone or in pairs, separated by terrace walls and distance from others. Some individuals seem more comfortable with this than others. One young man, perhaps twenty, is often seen working terraces on the settlement's edge, furthest from the center, speaking to no one for days at a time. He's not shunned—when he comes to gather water or collect grain from storage, people nod to him normally—but he prefers distance. Others cluster more, finding reasons to work adjacent terraces, to walk together to the communal building, to share evening fires. The settlement accommodates both tendencies.

At night, lying in the thick-walled buildings on sleeping platforms covered with felted wool and leather, they rest in the dark—the firelight died down to embers, the cold seeping through stone, their breathing slow and steady. Some sleep alone. Some sleep paired or in small family groups, sharing warmth. Dreams are rarely discussed, but surely they dream: of water flowing endlessly, of terraces extending beyond counting, of skies that open and pour rain for days. Or perhaps they dream of the past, of ancestors cutting these terraces, of days identical to their own stretching backward beyond memory. Or perhaps they dream of nothing, sleep too deep for images, the exhaustion of constant maintenance pulling them down into darkness as complete as the night sky.

Morning comes with the first pale light—not dawn's drama but its quiet arrival, the eastern sky lightening by degrees. People wake, add fuel to fires that have burned low, breathe visible clouds into cold air, wrap themselves in layers before emerging. Another day begins, indistinguishable from the day before and the day that will follow. Someone walks to a cistern to check the water level. Someone begins grinding grain. Someone inspects a terrace wall where a stone has shifted. The light strengthens. Birds call. Wind moves across open spaces.

They are continuous with this place. Their rhythms match its rhythms. Their scarcity matches its scarcity. Their patience is the patience stone requires. They have been here long enough—generations long enough—to be shaped by the terraces as much as they shape the terraces through their maintenance. They are not fighting the place, not trying to transform it into something lusher or easier or more abundant. They are living within its terms, accepting its constraints, surviving in the narrow margin it allows.

They will continue. Children will grow into the labor. Elders will die and be buried under cairns. Walls will need repair and will be repaired. Water will come and be collected. Grain will grow to its modest height and be harvested. The population will stay roughly constant, adjusted by some invisible mechanism to what the terraces can support. Seasons—such as they are—will cycle. Years will pass, uncounted and unremarkable.

They do not hope for change. They do not expect rescue or transformation. They are not waiting for anything. They are simply here, maintaining what was given to them, doing the work that allows this particular form of human life to persist in this particular place. That is enough. It has to be enough. And in the way they move through their days—steady, economical, patient—you see that it is enough. They have made their peace with scarcity, with cool thin air, with stone and sparse grain and intermittent water.

They remain. They remain level. That is what they do.
