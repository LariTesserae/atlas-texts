# Who Lives in the Moss Gardens

## The Pier-Born

They are small people. Not uniformly — there are tall ones, broader ones — but as a population they tend toward compactness. Whether this is selection over generations (those who fit the space thriving, staying, raising children) or something else, no one can say. Their bodies know verticality in their bones. They climb before they walk, and their balance is uncanny. A two-year-old will navigate a ladder with the same casual confidence that a child above-ground toddles across flat floor.

Their skin tends pale, but not sickly — a coolness of tone, like marble or birch bark. Some have a faint greenish cast in certain lights, particularly the very old or those who work most closely with the moss. This might be imagination, or it might be real; the distinction doesn't concern them. Their hair grows thick and tends toward dark browns and blacks, though it often carries a hint of red or bronze that gleams in the lamplight. They wear it long, practical, braided back in patterns that denote family lines — the Caracol braid loops three times at the base of the neck, the Severin braid (from another pier) plaits in five strands, and so on.

Their hands are their most distinctive feature. Strong, blunt-fingered, with thick calluses from climbing and carving, but also surprisingly dexterous. They can tie knots in near-darkness, thread copper wire through moss without tearing, manipulate the tiny tools of their crafts with absolute precision. Their fingertips are always slightly damp, slightly green-stained. They smell of stone and water and living things, a scent that is neutral, clean, faintly mineral.

They move quietly not because they are trying to be quiet, but because quiet movement is the only kind that makes sense here. Loud motion would be a kind of violence against the space. They learned this as children, absorbed it pre-verbally, and now it's simply how they are. Even their breathing is modulated, steady, economical. They have a stillness to them, a quality of being settled that makes people from above seem restless and loud by comparison.

Their eyes are adapted. Not changed, exactly — they're still human eyes — but trained from birth to read subtle variations in dim light, to track the slight movements of translucent salamanders in water, to distinguish eighteen shades of green moss by texture and luminosity as much as color. They can see in the perpetual twilight of the deepest alcoves where a newcomer would be nearly blind. And they can read each other's faces in dimness, tracking micro-expressions, understanding whole conversations in the slight shift of a mouth, the small tension around eyes.

## The Elders

There is a council, though it's not called that. It's just what happens when decisions need to be made: the eldest gather in the main chamber and talk quietly until agreement emerges. There is no voting, no hierarchy beyond respect for experience. Aneta is one of these, by virtue of her moss-keeping and her years. 

The eldest are the living memory. They remember when the Thread of Saint Olova was cleared after the blockage of '52 (or whenever it was — dates are vague here, marked more often by personal events than calendars). They remember the winter when someone from above brought down influenza and a dozen people died. They remember particular carvings made by particular hands now decades gone, and they can point to them and say, "That was Mikkol's work. See the way he always deepened the center of his spirals? That was his mark."

They teach not through formal lessons but through presence, through letting young ones follow them, through casual correction and demonstration. Aneta showing a child how to test moss moisture by touch. Gregor letting his son's son watch him clear a blockage, explaining the sound, the feel of it. Elder carvers giving children progressively more complex patterns to learn, progressing from simple spirals to the elaborate knotwork that takes years to master.

The very eldest, those in their seventies and eighties (rare, but present), begin to take on an odd quality. They move more slowly, but with even greater certainty. Their skin seems thinner, almost translucent, like the mirror-plants. They speak rarely, but when they do, everyone listens. They are approaching something, becoming something, though what exactly is unclear. There is no senility here — the cold and the work and the precision required seem to prevent it, or perhaps those prone to it simply don't survive to great age. Instead there is a kind of clarification, a distillation. The oldest seem to be becoming part of the place itself, growing root-like, settling deeper.

## The Artisans

Every adult is an artisan of something. There is no choice in this — the place requires it. But some have a gift that goes beyond competence into art.

The carvers are obvious, their work visible everywhere in the spirals and knots that cover every wooden surface. But there are specialists within this: those who do structural carving, ensuring the decorations don't weaken the wood; those who do memorial carving, the names of the dead in elaborate scripts; those who carve functional beauty, the spoons and bowls and tool handles that are themselves works of art.

There are the moss-cultivators, of whom Aneta is the master. They know not just the eighteen species but the subspecies, the particular strains that have developed in specific niches. They trade cuttings with cultivators in other piers, creating exchanges of genetic diversity, new colors, new growth patterns. They experiment quietly, seeing what will grow where, sometimes succeeding, often failing, always learning.

There are the light-shapers who maintain and adjust the mirrors and baffles that direct illumination through the space. This is subtle work, seasonal in a place that has no seasons — the angle of light from above changes slightly with the sun's position, and the mirrors must be adjusted accordingly. They also cultivate the mirror-plants, understanding their needs, encouraging their growth in patterns that create living illumination.

There are the water-wardens like Gregor, but also those who specialize in water quality, testing it constantly for the slight changes that might indicate problems above, ensuring it remains clean and cold and mineral-rich. They know the river intimately, its moods channeled through the limestone, and they can predict fluctuations before they happen.

There are the felt-makers who take raw wool traded from above and transform it through fulling and moss-fiber integration into the damp-resistant fabric everyone wears. Their hands are always wrinkled from water, their shoulders strong from the pounding and rolling that creates the dense material.

There are the oil-renders who process the pale fish into fuel for lamps, and those who make the lamps themselves, and those who make the wicks from moss fiber, and those who tend the fire-stones (kept in a special ventilated chamber) that provide the initial flames.

There are those who trade with above, making the periodic journeys up to exchange their unique products — mirror-plant cuttings, special moss medicines, the incredibly fine fish oil, carved wooden art objects — for things they cannot make: metal tools, flour, certain medicines, wool, seeds.

There are the singers, though "singer" is perhaps too grand a word. They are the ones who remember the songs, the breathing-chants that serve as history, as ceremony, as communal binding. Their voices are trained to the acoustics of dampness, to projection that is more resonance than volume.

## The Children

The children are feral in a very controlled way. They have freedom within absolute boundaries. They scramble through the galleries like squirrels, knowing every ladder, every shortcut, every hiding spot. They play games that are really training: thread-the-needle through the tightest spaces, practicing for the day they might need to clear a blockage in a narrow channel; moth-hunt in the darkness, developing their night vision and stillness; moss-spot, learning to identify species by touch alone; water-carry, building the balance and strength needed for this vertical life.

They are quieter than children above, but not silent. Their laughter is present, just muted, turned inward into giggles and grins rather than shrieks. Their conflicts are resolved quickly and quietly — there's no room for sustained argument, for the escalation that space allows. They learn negotiation, compromise, and occasional necessary solitude (which means climbing to the highest reaches where adults rarely go, sitting in the presence of the sleeping bats, thinking).

By five or six, they're useful. Not working, exactly, but helping. Carrying tools, fetching water, harvesting the easy moss, learning songs. By ten, they've usually gravitated toward a craft, following a particular adult, learning through observation and tentative participation. By fifteen, they're competent in at least one skill and adequate in several others. By twenty, they're full members of the community, no ceremony required — it just becomes obvious that they know what they're doing.

Some children are born with something extra, a sensitivity: they can hear water moving in ways others can't, or they have an instinct for where moss will grow best, or they can sense structural problems in the wood before they're visible. These children are watched carefully, encouraged, their gifts developed. They often become the next generation's masters.

Pip, at seven, has the carver's eye. They see patterns in everything — in moss growth, in water stains, in the way people move. They're beginning to understand that wood has memory, that it wants to be carved in certain ways, that decoration is really revelation. Their mother, herself a competent but not exceptional carver, recognizes this and makes sure Pip spends time with the true masters, learning what she cannot teach.

## The Bridge-Born Who Leave

Some do leave. Usually in their late teens or early twenties, seized by curiosity about the world above, about horizontal space, about weather and seasons and the other things they've only heard described. They climb up and out and join the city proper.

Most come back within a year. The space is too big, too open. The weather is unbearable — hot in summer, truly cold in winter (not the clean constant cold of the pier, but a bitter wind-driven thing), wet in unpredictable ways. The noise is overwhelming. The waste is shocking — so many things used once and discarded, so much space taken up by nothing, rooms standing empty while people crowd elsewhere.

Those who stay above become translators of a sort, merchants and traders who understand both worlds. They keep connections with the piers, facilitating exchanges, explaining each side to the other. They tend to live on the bridge deck itself, in the small shops and houses that have accreted there over generations, finding a middle ground between below and above.

A very few integrate into the city completely, marry above-ground people, have children who never see the piers. But they carry something with them always: the quiet voice, the economical movement, the inability to waste anything, the discomfort with empty space. Their neighbors think them odd, thrifty, a bit withdrawn. They are homesick for the rest of their lives for something they can't quite explain to anyone who hasn't felt it — the sense of being held, contained, of living inside rather than on top of.

## The Above-Dwellers Who Descend

Occasionally someone from the city above becomes fascinated by the bridge-dwellers and seeks to join them. They are not refused, exactly, but neither are they welcomed. They are given a corner, a sleeping shelf in a less desirable location, and left to learn or fail on their own.

Most fail. The cold is one thing; the unchanging cold is another. The density presses on them. The darkness (which isn't darkness to the pier-born) feels oppressive. The dampness gets into their lungs, or they think it does. The quiet drives them slowly mad — they crave conversation, music, noise, any noise. They leave within weeks.

A few persist. They learn the quiet voice, the economical movement. They develop the eye for moss, the ear for water. After a year, they're competent. After five, they're accepted. After ten, they might be trusted. Their children, if they have children here, will be fully pier-born, indistinguishable from anyone else.

These converters, as they're sometimes called, often become the most fervent keepers of tradition. Having chosen this life rather than been born to it, they love it with a conscious gratitude. They see its strangeness and love it anyway, or because of it. They are the ones who most often try to explain the place to curious visitors, though they rarely succeed — the explanations sound like deprivation to outsiders, when what they're trying to convey is richness.

## The Visitors

People come down sometimes, from the city above. Travelers curious about the famous moss gardens. Officials checking on structural integrity (the pier-dwellers tolerate this, show them what they want to see, wait for them to leave). Scholars studying the unique ecosystem. Artists seeking inspiration.

The pier-dwellers are polite to visitors but not warm. They will show you around, answer questions, even demonstrate crafts. But there's a reserve, a sense of waiting for you to leave so real life can resume. They know how they appear to outsiders: strange, deprived, living in what looks like poverty or fanaticism.

They don't bother arguing. You either understand or you don't, and understanding takes time, immersion, a willingness to let the place teach you rather than judging it by external standards. Most visitors don't have this patience. They tour for an hour or two, marvel at the strangeness, perhaps buy a carved box or a mirror-plant cutting, and climb back up to the world of weather and waste and space.

But occasionally a visitor will sit down in the main chamber and just... stay. Watching. Listening. Breathing the cold damp air. And the pier-dwellers will recognize something in this, a quality of attention, of openness. They will bring the visitor tea made from moss and mushrooms. They will allow them to follow the daily rounds. They will start to explain, not with words but with demonstration, how this place works, what it means.

These visitors, whether they stay an afternoon or a week, leave changed. They move differently in the world above. They waste less, notice more, carry a small stillness with them. They don't usually speak much about their experience — like the pier-dwellers themselves, they learn that this place resists explanation. But it marks them.

## The Dead

The dead are present. Not as ghosts (though on very still nights, when the mirror-plants glow brightest, some claim to see shapes moving in the highest galleries where no one has reason to be). But as memory, as names in the walls, as the work of their hands still visible and functional and beautiful.

When someone dies, there is grief, but it is quiet grief, compressed like everything else here. The body goes up to be buried in the earth, but the person stays, woven into the place. Their sleeping shelf is rededicated but not altered — the carvings remain, the particular way they shaped their space persists. Their tools pass to apprentices or family, already worn to the shape of their hands, and the new hands learn from those worn places how their predecessor held them.

The copper-wire names are touched in passing, a casual greeting to those who came before. Children learn to read the walls, understanding the history of the place through the names and dates (when dates are recorded). They learn who cleared the Weeping Scar originally, who first cultivated mirror-plants, who died in the influenza, who lived to be ninety.

There is no Heaven or Hell in the theology here, or rather, it's not discussed. The afterlife is vague, personal, uncodified. What matters is the work, the place, the continuation. You live, you contribute, you teach, and then you become part of the memory that guides those who come after. It is enough.

## The Community

The community is not harmonious in any idealized way. There are tensions, disagreements, personality conflicts. Gregor thinks the moss-keepers use too much water and Aneta thinks the water-wardens don't appreciate the needs of living things. Two families haven't spoken in years over a dispute about sleeping shelf allocation that no one else can quite remember the origin of. Some of the carvers dismiss the work of others as too traditional, too conservative, while the traditionalists think the innovators are showing off rather than honoring the wood.

But these tensions exist within an absolute commitment to the place's survival. You can despise someone personally and still respect their work, still cooperate when needed, still maintain the systems that keep everything functioning. The density enforces this. There is no walking away, no slamming of doors, no dramatic exits. You work it out or you live with it, and most often you do both simultaneously.

There are deep friendships, too. People who grew up in adjacent sleeping shelves and have known each other sixty years. Work partners who can communicate entire concepts with a glance. The quiet intimacy of shared space, shared work, shared understanding of what it takes to maintain this improbable life.

There are love affairs — some brief, some lifelong. Marriages, as mentioned, are simple affairs, but the relationships they formalize are complex, negotiated, adapted to this particular way of living. You cannot have privacy in the usual sense, so privacy becomes a courtesy, an agreement not to listen even when you can hear everything. You learn to live intimately with others while still maintaining boundaries, to be alone in a crowd through shared acknowledgment of aloneness.

There are mentorships that last decades, knowledge passing in an unbroken chain from elder to adult to child. There are rivalries that push people to excellence. There are casual connections, the daily small interactions that build community without weight or drama.

## What They Are Not

They are not primitive. The sophistication of their system — the water management, the light direction, the ecological balance, the social organization — rivals anything in the city above. It is simply different, adapted to different constraints, pursuing different values.

They are not poor, though they own little. Wealth here is knowledge, skill, position in the web of mutual obligation. An expert carver is rich. A master moss-keeper is rich. Someone who owns nothing but knows everything is the richest of all.

They are not religious in any organized way, though there are shrines, offerings, a sense of the sacred woven through daily life. The sacred is not separate from the ordinary. It is in the water, the moss, the light, the persistence of the place itself. When they tend the gardens, they are performing devotion. When they carve, they are praying. But there are no priests, no services, no codified theology. Or rather, everything is prayer and everyone is priest.

They are not sad. This is perhaps what outsiders find hardest to understand. They are not enduring a hardship. They are living a life they value, in a place they love, by principles they hold dear. They have joy — quiet joy, compressed joy, but real. Joy in good work, in beauty created, in problems solved, in the community that holds them, in the space that has become not just home but self.

## What They Are

They are the keepers. Of the bridge, yes, but also of a way of being that has become nearly extinct elsewhere. A patient attention to detail. A willingness to accept constraints and create within them. An understanding that less can be more when the less is carefully considered, consciously chosen, lovingly maintained.

They are proof that humans can adapt to nearly anything and make it home, make it meaningful, make it beautiful.

They are artists whose medium is daily life, whose canvas is stone and wood and water, whose masterpiece is the ongoing creation of a functional, sustainable, intricate community.

They are strange, yes. But the strangeness is not deprivation. It is specificity, adaptation, the particular genius that emerges when people truly inhabit a place rather than simply occupying it.

They are, in the end, exactly what they appear to be: people living in a cold damp vertical garden inside a bridge pier, doing so with competence and care and a kind of austere joy, generation after generation, making and remaking their impossible home until it becomes not just possible but necessary, not just necessary but beloved.
