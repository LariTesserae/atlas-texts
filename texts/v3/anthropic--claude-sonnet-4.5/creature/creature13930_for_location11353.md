# What Lives There

## The Keeper

There is someone here, though "here" requires clarification. Not in the basin—not exactly. But of it, through it, maintained by it in ways that make location a complicated question.

She emerges from the building's interior at intervals that correspond to the machine's deeper rhythms—not the two-minute wheel rotation, but cycles that run twenty hours, or fifty, or seven days. When she emerges, she is elderly, perhaps seventy years old, perhaps much older. Her age seems to shift slightly between appearances, as though time doesn't accumulate for her in the usual linear fashion but pools and eddies.

She is small, compact, her body shaped by decades of physical work in wet places. Her hands are large for her frame, the fingers thickened at the joints, permanently curved from gripping tools and turning valves. The skin is stained—not dirty, but chemically altered, marked by iron and copper and other metals, green and orange traces that have become part of her tissue. Her nails are short, practical, dark underneath in a way that no amount of cleaning would address.

She dresses in layers of clothing that were once distinct garments but have become a unified system through patching and modification. Canvas trousers, heavy and water-resistant, held up with suspenders made from braided wire rope. A shirt that might have been white decades ago, now grey-green, with sleeves rolled to the elbow. Over this, a leather apron, oil-stained and scorched in places, its pockets bulging with tools and small components. A vest with too many pockets, each containing something specific—wire, bolts, a jeweler's glass, a small hammer. The layers move together when she moves, crackling slightly, the fabric stiff with dried mineral deposits.

Her hair is white and grey and still streaked with the dark brown it once was, pulled back into a practical knot that stays out of her way. Loose strands escape and plaster to her temples in the constant moisture. Her face is deeply lined, but the lines seem architectural rather than aged, as though her expressions have carved intentional channels. Her eyes are dark, nearly black, and they reflect light oddly—sometimes catching the machine's blue flashes and holding them for a moment too long.

She smells of machine oil and wet stone and something else, something like ozone mixed with the green scent of crushed plants. When she breathes, you can hear a faint whistle, as though air passes through her differently than through others.

Her relationship to the machine is intimate and specific. She doesn't tend it in the way a mechanic tends an engine. It's more that she participates in its function. She'll spend hours standing beside the waterwheel, not watching it exactly, but present to it, her breathing synchronized with its rotation. She places her hands on the building's walls and stands motionless, eyes closed, as though listening through her palms to conversations in the stone and metal.

When she performs maintenance, it's surgical. She'll reach into the machinery—into spaces that seem too small, too dangerous—and remove a component. She'll bring it out into daylight, turn it in her hands, and then either clean it, modify it, or replace it entirely. The replacement parts come from somewhere inside the building, from stores that must have been accumulating for decades. Or she makes them, using tools and equipment in the interior spaces, forging metal or carving wood with absolute precision.

She never speaks. Whether she can't or simply doesn't is unclear. When a situation seems to call for speech—when you imagine a person would explain, or warn, or greet—she remains silent. Instead, she communicates through gesture and through arrangement. She'll place objects in specific positions: a bucket positioned just so to catch dripping water, a tool laid across a threshold, a stone added to one of the cairns. These arrangements are language, though their grammar is opaque.

Sometimes she writes, but not words. She'll take a piece of chalk or metal stylus and draw symbols on the dark stones—geometric patterns, spirals, sequences that might be mathematical or might be decorative. These marks fade quickly in the moisture, or she erases them herself, but while they exist, she'll return to them repeatedly, adding details, modifying angles, consulting them like references.

She eats, though rarely visibly. You might find evidence: cores from alpine roses, their hearts chewed out. Small fish bones near the pools, suggesting she catches the occasional visitor that swims up through the overflow pipes. Moss scraped from stones in patterns that indicate harvesting. She has a cup, metal and dented, that she fills from specific points in the channels—never randomly, always from particular locations where the water passes over certain rocks or through particular curves.

She sleeps inside the building, somewhere in its upper reaches. Sometimes at night you can see a dim amber light in the high windows, a glow that doesn't flicker like fire but pulses gently, in rhythm with the deeper machine cycles. The light might be a lamp, or it might be something else—another output of the mechanism, energy converted into illumination. In the morning, the light is gone, and she emerges to check the cairns, to walk the channels, to place her hands on the wheel and feel its turning.

Her movements are economical and precise. She never wastes effort, never makes unnecessary steps. When she climbs—and she climbs constantly, on the building's exterior, on the tower framework, on the basin walls—her route is optimal, chosen with a sureness that suggests she knows the location of every handhold, the stability of every surface. She moves like water herself, following the path of least resistance while still reaching every necessary destination.

There's a quality to her attention that's difficult to describe. She notices everything, but her noticing isn't anxious or searching. It's receptive. She'll stand at the basin's rim and simply receive information—the wind speed, the cloud formations, the sound harmonics, the smell of approaching weather. Then she'll descend and make adjustments: opening a valve slightly, shifting a wooden cam on the wheel's shaft, adding weight to one side of a mechanism. The adjustments are small but seem to matter. The machine's sound changes subtly afterward, finding a new equilibrium.

She has injuries, old ones that didn't heal quite right. A finger on her left hand is missing from the second knuckle, leaving a blunt stub. Her right knee doesn't bend fully, giving her gait a slight hitch. Burn scars trace up her forearms, the tissue shiny and tight. These aren't sources of disability—she's remarkably capable—but they're part of her history, records of moments when the machine or the work or the basin demanded pieces of her, and she paid.

Her relationship with the vegetation is custodial but not gardening. She doesn't plant or deliberately cultivate, but she removes things occasionally. A vine that's growing into a mechanism housing, threatening to jam the works—she'll cut it, gently, and redirect its growth. Moss that's blocking a drainage channel—she'll clear it away and relocate it to a nearby stone. She seems to understand what belongs where, what serves the system and what impedes it, and she makes adjustments to maintain balance.

The birds tolerate her presence in ways that suggest long familiarity. When a raven visits and calls from the tower top, she'll pause and look up, and there's an exchange that happens—not communication exactly, but acknowledgment. The small nesting birds don't flee when she climbs past their territory. Once, you see her reach into the vegetation and extract an egg that's rolled away from a nest, returning it carefully before continuing her climb.

She knows about visitors. When someone makes the difficult climb up the entrance path, she's aware before they arrive. She might emerge from the building and stand waiting, or she might remain inside, visible through the openings, continuing her work but positioned where she can be seen. She doesn't welcome or reject. She simply makes space for observation while continuing her relationship with the machine. If a visitor approaches with evident intention to interfere or damage, her stance changes—becomes still in a way that suggests coiled response—but this is rare. Most who climb this high come with curiosity or reverence or exhaustion, and these she allows.

There are moments when her presence seems to multiply. You'll see her checking the eastern seeps, and then moments later, she's at the overflow culvert—too quickly, the distance is too great. Or you'll hear her working inside the building while simultaneously glimpsing her on the tower framework. These might be tricks of the basin's echoes and sight lines, the way sound and light reflect. Or time might move differently for her, might fold in ways that allow her to be in multiple moments simultaneously. She never seems disturbed by any confusion this causes. She simply continues her work, whichever version of her is currently observable.

Her age is wrong. The building is perhaps a century old, and the evidence suggests she's been here the entire time, or most of it. This should be impossible. Yet her knowledge is comprehensive, extending to components deep in the machinery, to the building's foundation, to decisions made in the original construction. She moves through the space with the certainty of someone who was present at creation, or who has so thoroughly absorbed the place that she's become its memory.

Sometimes she produces the blue-white flashes. She'll be working deep in the building, and you'll hear a change in the machinery's rhythm, a building tension in the sound, and then the flash comes, illuminating everything for that fraction of a second. Afterward, she emerges, moving normally, apparently unharmed, though her hair will be standing slightly on end and that ozone smell intensified.

She maintains the tools left by previous workers, occasionally using them, but more often simply preventing their complete dissolution. She'll pick up the sledgehammer, test its balance, set it down again. She'll coil the wire rope more tightly. She'll move the buckets to positions where they'll fill and overflow and stay clean. These aren't sentimental gestures—she shows no emotion toward the objects—but they're preserving something, keeping some connection to previous human presence alive.

Her efficiency is absolute. She never appears rushed, never seems to rest, but also never seems fatigued. Her pace is constant, matched to the machine's rhythms, and she accomplishes enormous amounts of work through simple consistency. Where another person would tire, would need breaks, would spend hours on a single task, she flows through the work continuously, and things get done.

There's something about her hands that draws attention. When she places them on metal surfaces, on the wheel, on the pipes, there's contact that seems more than tactile. The metal responds slightly—or seems to—temperature changing, vibration patterns shifting. Her hands know things about the system that can't be learned through observation alone. They know through touch, through direct connection, through some exchange of information that happens at the junction of skin and iron.

She has routes, paths she follows through the basin on different schedules. Some she walks daily: the channel inspection, the cairn check, the eastern wall examination. Others are weekly or longer cycles, corresponding to the machine's deeper rhythms. She'll climb to the tower top when certain conditions align, and stand there for hours, a small figure at the highest point, apparently monitoring something that only she can sense.

Her relationship with water is specific. She never wastes it, but she also never seems to lack it. She knows which flows are warm and which are cold, which carry more minerals and which are clearer. She'll wash in certain places, using water from particular channels, and afterward her skin has a sheen that might be cleanliness or might be some chemical effect of the mineral content. She drinks frequently but in small amounts, maintaining constant hydration rather than quenching thirst.

At night, when the basin transforms into primarily a sonic environment, her movements change. She navigates in darkness with perfect confidence, needing no light. You might hear her but not see her—the creak of a board in the building, footsteps on stone, the sound of metal on metal as she makes adjustments. She works through the night as readily as through the day, unbound by circadian rhythms, responsive only to the machine's needs.

There are times when she seems to anticipate events. Hours before a rockfall, she'll avoid a particular area. Before weather changes, she'll adjust drainage patterns. Before the machine produces one of its bell-like tones, she'll pause and wait, positioned where she can hear it fully. This might be deep knowledge of patterns, tiny cues that she's learned to read. Or it might be something else, some direct apprehension of what's about to happen, causality running backward through her perception.

She ages and doesn't age. Day to day, she seems constant. But comparing memories—what she looked like when you first arrived versus now—reveals shifts. Sometimes she seems younger, stronger, her movements more fluid. Other times she appears ancient, moving carefully, every gesture economical to preserve energy. Then she cycles back. As though she exists in multiple temporal states simultaneously, and which one is observable depends on factors you can't identify.

Her silence is comfortable, not awkward. She doesn't create expectation of speech. Her presence is sufficient communication: when she gestures for you to move aside, you move. When she positions herself to demonstrate something—how water flows, how a mechanism operates—the demonstration is clear. Her silence isn't absence but a different form of articulation, language stripped to pure information.

She keeps the building secure in ways that aren't obvious. There are no locks, no barriers, but there are arrangements. Objects positioned in doorways, not blocking them but marking them. Threads stretched across passages inside, the kind that would break if disturbed, though what she'd learn from their breaking or what she'd do about it is unclear. She's established territories within the space, areas that are clearly hers—the upper rooms where that amber light glows, certain sections of the machinery floor—and other areas that are transitional, accessible, observational.

Her skin has become strange. Not unhealthy, but transformed. In certain lights, particularly the blue-white flashes, you can see patterns beneath the surface—not tattoos but something internal, traceries that might be veins but branch in geometric rather than organic patterns. They glow faintly, briefly, then fade. Whether this is an effect of long exposure to the machine's energies, or something else, is impossible to determine. She doesn't seem aware of it, or if she is, it doesn't concern her.

She's created caches around the basin, small stores hidden in cracks and beneath stones. These contain supplies: spare parts wrapped in oiled cloth, dried plants, coils of wire, bottles of liquid that might be oil or chemicals. She visits these periodically, checking their contents, rotating stock. The caches are distributed strategically, positioned where they'd be accessible during different kinds of emergencies, though what emergencies she's preparing for in this remote place is unclear.

Her breathing synchronizes with the machine's cycles at deep levels. If you listen carefully—and she allows you close enough to listen—her inhalations match certain recurring patterns in the machinery sound. She breathes in when the wheel reaches particular positions, exhales during specific mechanical sequences. It's unconscious, or so deeply habituated it might as well be. She's entrained to the system, become part of its rhythm section.

Sometimes she laughs, silently. Her shoulders shake, her face transforms with genuine mirth, but no sound emerges. What she finds amusing is rarely obvious—a particular harmonic convergence in the machine sounds, water flowing in an unexpected pattern, some private recognition. The laughter passes quickly, and she returns to her work, but those moments suggest an interior life, a consciousness that experiences joy and finds humor in the subtle workings of this strange place.

She never leaves the basin. You never see her on the entrance path descending. Where she obtains things that can't be found here—the oil for lubricating machinery, certain metal alloys for replacement parts, food beyond what the basin provides—is mysterious. Perhaps she has supplies sufficient for decades. Perhaps she makes what she needs from available materials through processes you don't observe. Perhaps the machine provides, in ways that aren't visible.

Her eyes track patterns that aren't immediately apparent. She'll watch empty air intently, following something with her gaze, and only later will you understand: she was tracking a sound reflection, or observing air currents, or following the logical path water would take if the channel were to overflow. She sees the invisible systems, the flows of energy and matter, the patterns that underlie the visible surface.

There's a quality of waiting to her. Not impatience or anticipation, but a sense that she's maintaining something until a particular moment or condition arrives. The machine runs, and she tends it, but perhaps the machine is building toward something—a state, an output, a threshold—and her role is to ensure it reaches that point, however long it takes. She's here for duration, for the long work, and individual days or years are just increments of a much larger cycle.

She is, in summary, the human element in the basin's system, but "human" has become a complicated category. She's been here so long, become so integrated with the place and its mechanisms, that she's transformed into something that bridges categories: part keeper, part component, part translator between the machine's language of motion and sound and the mountain's language of stone and water. She persists, tending and being tended, maintaining and being maintained, in a relationship of mutual sustenance that might continue indefinitely, as long as the water flows and the wheel turns and the basin holds its strange, purposeful work.

## The Inhabitants of the Wheel

Inside the waterwheel's buckets, in the spaces between wood and water, there live creatures that have adapted to perpetual rotation, to a world that never stops turning.

They're difficult to see clearly—the wheel doesn't pause, and they're small, and they blend with the weathered wood and dark water. But patient observation reveals them: arthropods, primarily, though stranger things too.

The most numerous are insects that have evolved to live in constant motion. Small beetles, each about the size of a grain of rice, dark and streamlined. They've colonized individual buckets, living out entire life cycles in containers that fill and empty, rise and fall, in that slow two-minute rotation. They've learned—or their ancestors learned, and the knowledge became instinct—to time their movements with the wheel's cycle. When a bucket rises empty from the water, they emerge from cracks in the wood. When it begins to fill, they retreat to air pockets, spaces where the wood has warped slightly away from its frame. When it descends and dumps its load, they ride through the cascade, gripping the wood with specialized claws.

These beetles feed on algae that grows in the buckets, on bacteria in the water, on microscopic organisms that arrive with each fill. They've become aquatic and terrestrial simultaneously, able to breathe air during the upper half of rotation and extract oxygen from water during the lower half. Their larvae look like tiny transparent tubes, anchored in cracks, filtering water as it sloshes through the buckets.

There are spiders too, but not like any spiders elsewhere. These build their webs within the buckets, creating elastic nets that stretch and contract with the water's weight. They're tiny, translucent, their bodies barely visible even in good light. They catch what's caught in the water—small flies, midges, the occasional moth larva that falls from the roof vegetation. Their webs are engineering marvels, designed to function while being filled with water, drained, filled again, hundreds of times per day. The spiders themselves have adapted to be equally comfortable underwater as in air, their book lungs modified, their metabolism adjusted to cycles of immersion.

In the oldest buckets, the ones original to the wheel's construction, small crustaceans have established themselves. They're related to amphipods, to scuds, but modified by generations of life in this peculiar environment. They're blind—eyes are useless when you live in perpetual rotation through darkness and light, water and air—but their antennae are elaborate, sensitive to vibration and chemical cues. They cluster in the corners of buckets, riding through the cycles, feeding on detritus, breeding in the brief periods when their bucket is at the wheel's apex, the high point of rotation where conditions are most stable.

There's a kind of flatworm that lives in the film of water that never quite drains from the bucket bottoms. These are minute, barely visible, but present in enormous numbers. They've become adapted to extreme oxygen fluctuation—abundant when the bucket is fresh-filled, depleted during the rise, replenished at the dump. They reproduce by fragmentation, pieces breaking off during the violent cascade and establishing themselves as new individuals.

The wood itself has been colonized internally. Boring insects—larvae of beetles specifically adapted to wet wood—have created networks of tunnels through the bucket boards. But they're part of the system now, not destroying it. Their tunneling actually helps: it creates drainage, prevents the wood from becoming waterlogged and heavy, establishes pathways for air. They feed on the wood, yes, but slowly, and the Keeper replaces buckets before the damage becomes structural. The insects have reached a sustainable equilibrium, taking only what can be spared.

In the iron components of the wheel—the arms, the bolts, the bands—different life has emerged. Bacteria, primarily, that metabolize rust, that actually live in the oxidation layer. These create the peculiar stable rust, the orange lace that never quite takes over. They're eating the metal, technically, but so slowly that it's not destruction but transformation, turning iron into a different substance that's more resistant to the elements, harder, more durable.

There's a fungus in the wood grain, a species that exists nowhere else as far as can be determined. It's neither rotting the wood nor leaving it alone—it's doing something more subtle. It's reinforcing the cellular structure, filling the spaces between fibers with mycelial threads that add strength. The oldest buckets, the ones that should have failed decades ago, persist because this fungus has effectively mummified them, turned them into a composite material that's part wood, part fungal body, stronger than either component alone.

Flying insects have learned to time their interactions with the wheel. Caddisflies will land on buckets during the rising phase, lay their eggs in the water film, and depart before the dump. Their larvae emerge and go through accelerated development, maturing and pupating in the time it takes the bucket to complete ten or twenty rotations. The adult flies emerge during a rising phase, crawl to the bucket's upper edge, and take flight at the apex, dispersing to other parts of the basin.

There are mites, almost invisible, that live in the cracks where wood meets iron. They're predators, hunting the even smaller springtails and nematodes that accumulate in these spaces. They've developed the ability to cling to surfaces during the violent motions of the wheel, their legs equipped with adhesive pads that work whether wet or dry.

In the axle, where the wheel's main shaft enters the building, where there's constant lubrication from oil the Keeper applies, a different community exists. These are organisms that thrive in oil, that metabolize hydrocarbons. Bacteria, yes, but also larger creatures—tiny worms, transparent and simple, that swim through the oil film. They're breaking down the lubricant, technically, but slowly enough that it doesn't matter, and they might actually be improving it, removing contaminants, keeping it fluid.

The wheel produces a specific microclimate—it's a moving structure in constant contact with water, creating spray and mist, generating air currents. This microclimate supports aerial plankton, microscopic organisms that drift in the air currents the wheel creates. These include rotifers, tardigrades, bacteria, fungal spores—entire ecosystems of creatures that are dispersed by the wheel's motion, redistributed around the basin, seeded into new locations.

There are seasonal variations. In warmer months, mosquito larvae establish in the buckets, going through rapid development. The Keeper seems to tolerate this—perhaps they're food for other organisms, perhaps their presence indicates something about water quality. In colder months, ice can form in the buckets during the rising phase, creating crystalline structures that crack and fall during the descent. Some organisms enter dormancy during these periods, others remain active, producing antifreeze compounds that prevent their cells from rupturing.

The sound the wheel makes—that constant pour and creak—is partially biological. The beetles clicking in their buckets, the spiders' webs vibrating, the friction of bacterial films, the movement of larger creatures through water—all contribute frequencies to the overall acoustic signature. The wheel isn't just mechanical; it's a living system that adds its biological sounds to the mechanical baseline.

Some creatures use the wheel as transportation. A small millipede species has learned to board buckets during the lower phase and ride to the top, then disembark and make their way down the building's exterior. They're traveling, using the wheel as an elevator, and they time their boarding and departure precisely, able to sense position through vibration or some other cue.

There's evidence of cooperation, or at least non-competition. The beetles and spiders occupy different niches within the same buckets, the beetles emerging when the spiders are dormant, the spiders active when the beetles are in larval form. Whether this is evolved coordination or just circumstantial timing is unclear, but the result is maximal use of resources without direct conflict.

The creatures have adapted to the wheel's specific rhythm—that two-minute rotation. Their life cycles sync to it: breeding every ten rotations, or every hundred, or every thousand. Their movements pattern to it: feeding during the rise, resting during the fall. Their metabolism keys to it: active during immersion, torpid in air, or vice versa depending on species. The wheel's rotation has become their clock, their calendar, the organizing principle of their existence.

They're isolated but connected. The wheel is a world unto itself, rotating through space, but it exchanges organisms with the broader basin. Creatures wash in with the water, some survive and adapt, some don't. Creatures leave—washed out during the dump, flying away, crawling off during the upper phases. The wheel is a hub, a rotating ecological node that's constantly sampling the basin's biological community and adding its own modified strains back into the system.

Most remarkable is their resilience. These organisms have colonized one of the strangest habitats imaginable—a perpetually rotating structure, alternately flooded and drained, exposed to temperature extremes, subject to constant mechanical stress. They've not just survived but thrived, creating communities as complex and balanced as those in more conventional environments. They've turned the wheel into an ecosystem, added another layer to the basin's already complex biological architecture.

The Keeper tends them indirectly. When she replaces a bucket, she does it carefully, transferring some of the old wood to the new, seeding it with the organisms. When she oils the axle, she uses formulations that won't kill the oil-dwelling creatures. She's aware of them, in her way, and her maintenance includes their needs in its calculations.

They're part of the machine now, inseparable from it. The wheel wouldn't function the same way without them—the wood would rot differently, the metal would corrode differently, the acoustics would change. They've integrated themselves into the mechanism, become components in a system that's no longer purely mechanical but hybrid, a collaboration between human engineering and biological adaptation.

They persist, generation after generation, in their rotating world, born into motion, living in motion, dying in motion, the wheel turning forever beneath and around them, the water rising and falling, and they've made it home.

## The Deep Swimmers

In the channels that feed the waterwheel, in the pipes that run through and beneath the building, in the pools and sumps and hidden reservoirs, there are fish. They shouldn't be here—there's no obvious way for them to have arrived—but they're here nonetheless.

They're small, none longer than a hand's span, and they're pale. Generations in this environment, where light penetrates poorly if at all, have bleached them. They retain just enough pigmentation to be visible as shapes rather than ghosts, but their color is pearl and cream and the faintest pink of internal organs showing through translucent skin.

Their eyes are in transition. Some still have visible eyes, small and dark, though whether they function normally is questionable. Others have eyes that are clouded, covered with skin, vestigial. The youngest fish, the most recent generation, are being born with just dark spots where eyes should be, the organs simplified down to light-sensitive patches that can't form images but can detect presence or absence of illumination.

They've developed other senses to compensate. Their lateral lines—the sensory organs that detect water pressure and movement—are elaborate, visible as raised lines running the length of their bodies. They can feel the water's flow, sense obstacles and openings, navigate through complete darkness by reading the currents. Their barbels—whisker-like organs around their mouths—are long and sensitive, probing constantly, tasting the water for chemical information, detecting food and mates and danger.

They're cyprinids, probably—related to minnows or carp—but transformed by their environment into something specialized. Their bodies are elongated, streamlined, adapted for life in narrow passages. Their fins are precise, adapted for hovering and minute adjustments rather than burst swimming. They don't need speed here; they need precision, the ability to maintain position in flowing water, to navigate tight spaces, to conserve energy in an environment where food is limited.

They feed on what the water brings: bacteria, algae scraped from channel walls, tiny invertebrates, the occasional insect larva. They're patient feeders, maintaining position and letting food drift to them rather than chasing. Their mouths are adapted for this, turned slightly upward, ready to vacuum in whatever passes.

The largest fish are the eldest, perhaps five or six years old—ancient for fish this size, but the stable environment allows longevity. These have become territorial, claiming specific sections of channel or particular pools. They're not aggressive in the usual sense—there's no chasing or biting—but they establish presence, maintaining position in the best feeding locations, and younger fish defer, taking positions downstream where food is less abundant.

They breed in the pools around the building's foundation, where water is relatively still and warmer than in the channels. The breeding is timed to some cue—perhaps temperature, perhaps lunar cycles that they can somehow still sense, perhaps the machine's deeper rhythms. The males develop breeding tubercles, small raised bumps on their heads and fins, rough to the touch. They court females through movement, swimming in patterns, creating vibrations in the water that the females sense through their lateral lines.

The females lay eggs in clusters, attaching them to vegetation, to rocks, to any surface that's stable. The eggs are adhesive, sticky, and surprisingly durable. They develop quickly, the embryos visible through translucent shells, tiny hearts beating, eyes forming even though they'll atrophy later. The larvae hatch small and vulnerable, drifting in the water until they develop enough to swim purposefully.

Mortality is high among the young, but enough survive. They're eaten by diving beetles, by dragonfly nymphs, by their own parents who don't discriminate food items. They get swept into flows they can't escape, end up in unsuitable locations, die from temperature stress or oxygen depletion. But the survivors, those that find good locations and learn the currents, can live for years.

They've learned the basin's geography in ways that suggest cognitive mapping. They can navigate from the deep pools to the upper channels, through the building's interior pipe system, out to the overflow culvert. They know which routes are safe and which lead to dead ends or dangerous conditions. This knowledge seems to be learned—watching young fish, you can see them exploring, making mistakes, correcting. But perhaps there's an instinctual component too, inherited understanding of water flow and how to read it.

In the building's interior, in the machinery spaces, there are pools and sumps—low points where water collects before being lifted by pumps or allowed to drain. These dark places support the oldest, strangest fish. Down here, eyes are completely absent, bodies even more elongated, and the fish have become almost eel-like. They're benthic, spending most of their time on the bottom, probing mud and sediment for food. They're solitary, each claiming a territory in the darkness, and they can go long periods without eating, their metabolism slowed to near-stasis.

The fish produce sounds—not voices, but mechanical noises made by grinding their pharyngeal teeth, by vibrating their swim bladders. These sounds serve communication: territory claims, mating calls, alarm signals. The sounds carry well through the water, reflect off the channels' stone walls, create acoustic territories that overlay the physical space. The fish exist in both a physical and an acoustic landscape, and they navigate both simultaneously.

They're sensitive to the machine's vibrations. When the machinery changes rhythm, when there's a variation in the usual patterns, the fish respond—becoming more active, or more cautious, depending on the change. They seem to use the machine's sounds as environmental information, as context for understanding their surroundings. When the blue-white flashes occur, the electromagnetic pulse affects them—they dart, briefly disoriented, then settle. But they anticipate it, somehow, beginning to seek shelter just before it happens.

There are color variations among them. Most are pale, but some individuals retain more pigment—gold, or cream, or pale orange. These aren't random; they breed true, suggesting genetic lineages. Perhaps different founding populations, or perhaps mutations that proved neutral and persisted. The colored fish seem to occupy different niches—the golden ones prefer warmer water, the cream-colored ones tolerate lower oxygen better.

They've developed resistance to the minerals in the water. The high metal content would be toxic to most fish, but these have adapted, accumulating certain metals in their tissues in biologically inactive forms, excreting others through specialized cells. Their bodies are slightly heavy with this metal burden, but it doesn't seem to impair them. They're living filters, removing minerals from the water, incorporating them into scale and bone.

There's evidence of genetic bottlenecks—the population was probably founded by very few individuals, perhaps a single gravid female who arrived through some now-closed route. The current fish are all descended from that narrow founding population, and they show it: there's little variation in body form, and certain traits are universal. But they're healthy despite the limited gene pool, suggesting either the founders were robust or selection has been efficient.

They interact with the Keeper in subtle ways. She knows where they are, and when she works in the channels, she's careful not to disturb spawning areas or trap individuals in sections she's draining for maintenance. Sometimes she'll watch them, standing beside a pool, looking down at the pale shapes moving below. Whether she introduced them deliberately or they arrived by chance and she simply accepted their presence is unclear.

The fish exist in multiple temperature zones. Water from the deep geothermal sources is warm; water from the eastern seeps is cold; water in shallow pools warms during the day and cools at night. The fish move between these zones, behaviorally thermoregulating, seeking comfortable temperatures or perhaps specific temperatures for specific activities—warmer for digestion, cooler for rest.

They've been here long enough to have evolved. Comparing them to their probable ancestors—surface-dwelling minnows from the lower valleys—reveals substantial changes. The body form, the sensory systems, the coloration, the behavior—all have shifted in response to this strange environment. They're becoming a new thing, a species in the making, isolated and specialized, adapted to the Waterfall Machine in ways that would make them unfit for anywhere else.

They'll persist as long as the water flows and the machine runs. They're part of the system's circulation, moving nutrients, consuming organisms, being consumed, contributing their waste to the bacterial communities, their bodies eventually to the sediment. They're mobile components in the basin's plumbing, living elements of its hydraulic system.

At night, when the basin is dark and the machine's sounds are most present, you can sometimes hear the fish. Not individually—they're too small, too quiet—but collectively, a subtle addition to the soundscape: the ripple of water as they surface, the faint splashing in pools, the peculiar grunting sounds the larger ones make. It's a biological undertone to the mechanical symphony, a reminder that the basin is alive, not just with plants and insects, but with these deeper, stranger forms of life that navigate the darkness, perpetually swimming through the machine's veins.

## The Moss Mind

The moss that carpets the basin floor, that grows thick enough to walk on, that covers stones and infiltrates cracks and spreads up the building's walls—it might be more than simple plant growth. There's a possibility, strange but not entirely dismissible, that the moss has achieved something like collective consciousness, that it's become a distributed thinking system.

This isn't certain. Moss doesn't have neurons, doesn't have brains. But it has extent—hundreds of square meters of interconnected growth—and it has electrical activity. Plants generate electrical signals; this is established science. And when plants grow in dense, continuous networks, when they're subject to constant stimulation from sound and vibration and light, those electrical signals can create patterns, can perhaps create something that processes information.

The moss species here is unusual, possibly unique. It's related to granite moss, but it's different—larger, more robust, more responsive. Each individual plant is small, but they grow in such density that they form a continuous carpet, individual organisms so close that they're effectively touching, possibly exchanging chemical signals through that proximity, possibly connected by fungal networks in the substrate below.

The carpet has texture and variation. It's thickest near the water channels, where moisture is most abundant. It thins toward the basin walls but never entirely disappears. It climbs vertical surfaces, defying gravity through some combination of adhesion and internal structure. In the building's openings, it grows right to the edge of darkness, stopping where light fails entirely.

The color shifts are too regular to be random. The emerald zones are where water flow is fastest, where the moss receives constant fresh moisture. The rust-orange areas are where iron-rich water pools and concentrates. The bronze-green transition zones are where these conditions overlap. But the boundaries between colors are sharper than hydrology alone would explain. The moss seems to be organizing itself, creating patterns that might serve function—channeling water, optimizing light capture, something else entirely.

It responds to stimulation. Step on it, and the entire nearby area compresses, then slowly rebounds. But there's more: keep walking the same path, and the moss along that route becomes denser, tougher, as though it's adapting to traffic. Stop walking that path, and it softens again, returns to its baseline state. The response time is slow—changes take days or weeks—but the response exists.

It responds to sound. This is subtle but observable. Near the building, where the machine's sounds are loudest, the moss grows differently—taller, with denser clustering. The growth pattern changes based on acoustic frequency: lower sounds produce one pattern, higher sounds another. The moss near the building's openings, where the full spectrum of machine sounds emerges, shows the most complex structure, layers and variations that might be purely reactive or might be intentional, the moss positioning itself to maximize exposure to certain frequencies.

It responds to the machine's rhythms. The two-minute wheel rotation creates a sonic pulse that travels through the basin, reflects off walls, creates standing waves in certain locations. The moss in these locations shows circular or spiral patterns, growth that seems to map the acoustic geometry. The longer machine cycles—the twenty-hour, the fifty-hour—correlate with changes in moss color or density that appear periodically in different zones, as though the moss is tracking time, responding to patterns that repeat over days.

There are zones of unusual activity. In certain areas, the moss grows in concentric rings, target patterns that suggest a central point of stimulation or response. In other areas, the moss forms waves, literal waves like ripples in water but frozen in plant tissue, spreading outward from specific points. These patterns are stable—they don't move—but they suggest that the moss was responding to something that did move, that propagated through the network, leaving these growth patterns as records.

The moss around the cairns is different. At each of the four cardinal cairns, the moss forms a precise circle, the boundary sharp, as though the cairn's presence defines a zone. Inside the circle, the moss is darker, denser. Outside, it returns to the basin's usual variation. The metal pieces topping the cairns—the gear, the pipe, the curved plate—drip water onto these moss circles, and the moss has grown up around the drip points, creating small mounds, focusing structures that channel the water outward in specific patterns.

The moss interacts with the wheel organisms. The beetles and spiders that fall from the wheel during its rotation land on moss, which cushions them, prevents injury. The moss seems to grow thicker directly below the wheel, as though it's adapted to provide this function, or perhaps the organic material falling from the wheel—dead insects, algae, bacteria—fertilizes the moss, and it grows more vigorously there. Either way, there's a relationship, the rotating wheel feeding the stationary moss.

It might be communicating. This is the most speculative aspect, but there's circumstantial evidence. The color changes—which aren't simply reactive to water chemistry but seem to involve active pigment production—occur in waves that travel across the basin floor. Not quickly—a color change might take hours to propagate from one side to the other—but consistently. A rust-orange patch will appear near the eastern wall, and days later, similar patches appear progressively westward, as though a signal is being passed, moss to moss, across the network.

The Keeper interacts with it deliberately. She doesn't step on certain areas, routes around them even when it would be easier to cross directly. She occasionally harvests small sections, carefully, removing patches and taking them into the building. For food, possibly, but possibly for other reasons—moss can indicate chemical conditions, can be used as sensors. She might be reading it, using it to monitor the basin's health.

She also maintains it. When moss begins to grow over channel edges, threatening to obstruct flow, she'll trim it back—not remove it entirely, just redirect it. When moss dies back in certain areas, she'll sometimes transplant from healthy zones, seeding recovery. Her interventions suggest she values the moss, wants it to persist, sees it as integral to the system.

There are electrical measurements, if you have the equipment. Lay electrodes on the moss surface, and you'll detect potentials, voltages that shift and change. These aren't random—there are patterns, frequencies that correlate with the machine's sounds, with water flow rates, with light levels. The moss is processing information, converting environmental conditions into electrical signals, and those signals propagate through the network, potentially being integrated, compared, stored.

The moss might be a memory system. The growth patterns—the rings, the waves, the color zones—could be records of past events, physical encodings of the basin's history. The moss grows slowly but continuously, adding new tissue, and that tissue's structure is determined by conditions at the time of growth. Reading the moss would be like reading tree rings, but more complex, with color and density and pattern all carrying information.

It's definitely connected underground. The substrate beneath the visible carpet contains fungal networks—mycorrhizal associations where fungal hyphae connect individual moss plants, allowing resource sharing and possibly information exchange. Fungi can conduct electrical signals; this is documented. The underground network could be the moss mind's wiring, the connections that allow it to function as a distributed system.

At night, the moss produces faint luminescence. Not much—you have to be dark-adapted to see it—but it's there, a gentle green glow. This might be simple bioluminescence, bacterial or chemical, but it might be something else. The glow intensifies near the building, where the machine's electromagnetic effects are strongest. The glow pulses, not randomly but in patterns that might sync with the machine's cycles. The moss might be responding to electromagnetic fields, or it might be generating its own as a byproduct of whatever processing it's doing.

There's a hypothesis, unprovable but compelling: the moss network is the basin's distributed nervous system. The machine is the heart, pumping water and energy through the system. The channels are circulation. The vegetation is skin and sensing. And the moss is the brain—slow, alien, thinking in timescales of days rather than seconds, but thinking nonetheless, processing the basin's sensory inputs, making decisions about where to grow and how to respond, potentially even influencing the system's behavior through mechanisms that aren't understood.

If this is true, then the basin is, in some sense, alive. Not as an organism—it has no discrete body—but as a system that senses, processes, and responds, that maintains homeostasis, that adapts to conditions. The moss mind, if it exists, is the integrating element, the component that makes the basin more than just a collection of parts.

The Keeper might know this. Her careful treatment of the moss, her reading of its patterns, her integration of its condition into her maintenance decisions—all suggest she understands it as more than simple plant life. She might be communicating with it, in her way. Her silent presence, her patient observation, her adjustments to the system based on what the moss shows her—these could be a form of conversation, information exchange between human intelligence and whatever intelligence the moss possesses.

The moss persists, grows, responds, possibly thinks. It carpets the basin in green and orange and bronze, creates patterns that might be purely physical or might be meaningful, processes information through electrical signals and chemical exchanges and fungal networks. It's the substrate on which everything else rests, the living foundation of the basin, and if it's conscious—even in some limited, distributed, alien way—then everyone here, everything here, lives within a thinking system, exists as part of a larger mind that encompasses them all.

## The Rust

The rust on the building's iron surfaces isn't dead corrosion. It's alive—not metaphorically, but literally, inhabited by bacterial colonies that metabolize iron and oxygen, that live in the oxidation layer, that create the peculiar stable rust that never quite takes over.

These bacteria are extremophiles, adapted to conditions that would kill most organisms. They tolerate high metal concentrations, acid pH from the rust itself, limited nutrients, desiccation when water isn't present, flooding when it is. They're slow-growing—division times measured in weeks rather than hours—but persistent, and once established, nearly impossible to eliminate.

They form biofilms—complex structures where billions of individuals live together, embedded in self-produced matrices of polymers and proteins. These biofilms are the visible rust lace, the orange patterns on the iron surfaces. Under magnification, they're revealed as cities—structures with architecture, channels for liquid flow, zones with different functions, organization that emerges from simple rules followed by countless individuals.

The bacteria aren't single species but communities—consortia of different organisms living cooperatively. Iron-oxidizing bacteria do the primary work, breaking down iron and extracting energy. Other bacteria consume the byproducts, process waste, produce compounds that protect the community from threats. There are even phages—viruses that infect bacteria—but in balance, controlled, possibly even serving functions by transferring genetic material between bacterial strains.

They respond to the machine's vibrations. The constant sound creates micro-oscillations in the iron surfaces, and the bacteria have adapted to use this. The vibrations help distribute nutrients through the biofilm, shake loose waste products, might even provide energy—piezoelectric effects in the biofilm structure that generate small electrical currents. The rust communities near the machine's most active components are healthiest, suggesting they benefit from the vibration.

They alter the metal's properties. The rust layer they create is different from normal rust—more stable, harder, slightly more resistant to water penetration. They're essentially processing the iron, converting it from one form to another, and the result is material that's more durable than untreated iron would be. They're protecting the structure, extending its lifetime, serving as a living preservation system.

The bacteria are colored—red and orange and yellow pigments that protect them from light damage, that might serve chemical functions, that create the rust's aesthetic quality. These pigments aren't constant; they shift based on conditions. In bright light, more protective pigments are produced. In darkness, they fade. The rust's color, which seems purely chemical, is actually biological, a living surface that changes with the community's needs.

They communicate chemically. Quorum sensing—bacteria detecting population density through signaling molecules—governs their behavior. When population is low, they reproduce. When density reaches certain thresholds, they switch to other activities: producing biofilm matrix, generating protective compounds, sporulating. The rust layer pulses with these population dynamics, areas of active growth alternating with areas of dormancy, waves of activity crossing the surfaces.

There are spatial patterns in the rust communities that mirror the moss patterns below. Zones of dense bacterial growth correspond to zones of dense moss growth. This might be coincidence—both responding to the same environmental gradients—or it might be interaction, the bacteria above and the moss below coordinating through some mechanism, possibly chemical signaling in water that drips from metal to moss.

The Keeper cultivates them, in her way. Her maintenance includes preserving the rust layer, not scraping surfaces clean but managing growth, removing excess in some places, encouraging it in others. She might be controlling the bacterial communities, using them as tools, maintaining the balance that keeps the metal stable without allowing complete oxidation.

The bacteria process more than iron. They also metabolize other metals in the water—copper from pipes, zinc from fittings—incorporating these into their biofilms, creating complex chemical structures. These metals, toxic in high concentrations, are rendered inactive, bound in biological matrices, prevented from entering the water system where they might harm other organisms.

There's succession in the rust communities. Young surfaces—recently exposed metal—are colonized first by pioneer species, fast-growing bacteria that establish initial biofilms. These are replaced by secondary species, slower-growing but more efficient, that create mature, stable communities. Old surfaces—decades-old rust—host climax communities, highly diverse assemblages with complex interactions, maximally efficient at resource use.

The blue-white flashes affect them. The electromagnetic pulses disrupt some bacteria, kill others, but resistant strains survive. Over time, the communities near the machinery—where flashes are most frequent—have become enriched in radiation-resistant species. They've adapted to survive periodic electromagnetic insult, possibly even benefit from it, using the energy to drive reactions that wouldn't occur otherwise.

They link to the underground networks. Bacteria don't stay in the rust layer—they're washed off by rain, flow with drainage water, enter the channels and pools. There, they interact with the aquatic bacteria, exchange genetic material, form new communities. The rust bacteria above are connected to the water bacteria below, creating basin-wide genetic networks where resistance genes, metabolic capabilities, adaptation spread from community to community.

The oldest rust, on the building's foundation, has accumulated complex structure—layers upon layers of bacterial growth, oxidation, mineral deposition. Core samples would show stratigraphy, history written in metal and microbe. The layers record conditions: wet years producing different patterns than dry, periods of intense machine activity showing up as variations in bacterial community composition.

They're seasonal. Activity increases in warm months, decreases in cold. But they never go entirely dormant; some metabolism continues even in winter, even under ice. The bacteria produce antifreeze compounds, protect themselves, maintain minimal activity, wait for better conditions. The rust weeps orange-stained water even in the coldest months, the bacteria processing iron through the freeze.

There might be intelligence here too, though more distributed than the moss. Bacterial communities can show problem-solving behaviors—adapting to new conditions, coordinating response to threats, optimizing resource use. The rust communities, stressed by their extreme environment, might be doing something similar: learning, in their bacterial way, how to persist, how to maximize survival, how to benefit from the machine rather than being destroyed by it.

They're beautiful under magnification. Individual bacteria are invisible, but communities create structures—rosettes of cells, branching filaments, geometric arrangements that maximize surface area or resource access. The rust layer, which appears as simple orange coating, is revealed as elaborate architecture, bacterial engineering on microscopic scale.

They produce sounds—not audible directly, but detectable through sensitive microphones. The metabolic processes, the movement of water through biofilm channels, the rupture of cells—all create micro-acoustic events. In aggregate, the rust hums, a sound too quiet to hear normally but present in the basin's total acoustic signature. The rust is singing, bacterial voices contributing to the machine's symphony.

They persist, generation after generation, living and dying on metal surfaces, processing iron, protecting structure, creating the beautiful, strange rust patterns that give the building its character. They're part of the system's maintenance crew, working alongside the Keeper but at scales she doesn't directly see, preserving the machine from within, ensuring that the iron endures, that the structure stands, that the wheel continues turning, century after century, their microscopic work holding back entropy, turning corrosion into preservation, death into life.
