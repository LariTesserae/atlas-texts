# What Lives There

The mountain keeps them, but they are not kept.

## The Maintained

They are not monks, though that word surfaces in the minds of those who glimpse them. They are not hermits, though they live alone together. They have no name for themselves that translates cleanly into purpose or philosophy. If pressed—and they are never pressed, for who would press them here?—they might say they are tenders, though even that implies too much intention. They maintain. They witness. They remain.

There are three at present. There have been as few as one, as many as seven. The number seems to matter, but none of them could articulate why. When one leaves—through death or departure, and the two are not always distinguishable on the terrace—another arrives within a span of time that might be weeks or might be months. The terrace calls, in its way. Or perhaps it's simpler: those who would come, come. Those who would stay, stay.

**Kael** is eldest in tenure, though not in years. Seventeen years on the terrace, or possibly nineteen—time's fluidity here makes precise counting a habit he abandoned early. He is spare in the way of long-weathered wood, his body pared down to essential function. Sixty years old, perhaps. Perhaps older. His hair, once black, has transmuted to the color of wet stone: dark gray, touched with green where the terrace's biofilm has found microscopic purchase. He doesn't wash it away. He is tall, but moves in a compact way, folded into economy, each gesture precisely sufficient.

His hands are what you notice: long-fingered, always damp, the skin softened by constant moisture until it has taken on a quality somewhere between leather and moss. He can hold water cupped in his palms for improbable durations, barely losing a drop. He can touch the spiral-growers and they cease their creaking for a span of heartbeats, then resume. He knows every pool, every seep, every fern cluster. He walks the terrace three times daily—dawn, noon, dusk, though these divisions are more felt than seen—and notes changes that would be invisible to anyone else: a new orchid spike, a shift in a pool's depth, a variation in the cliff face's seeping pattern.

Kael speaks rarely and slowly, his words emerging with the same patient timing as water from stone. When he does speak, his voice has taken on the terrace's acoustic properties: it carries oddly, seems to come from slightly beside where he stands, echoes faintly even in open air. He is teaching himself to be silent in the way the terrace is not-silent, to be present in the way stone is present, to maintain in the way that maintenance becomes indistinguishable from existence.

He sleeps in the northern section, in a shallow concavity in the cliff face where the stone's curve creates a space almost like shelter. No roof, no walls, just a curved hollow that deflects the heaviest moisture. He has a blanket woven from some fiber that might be plant-based or might be something else—it's the color of moss and never quite dry and never quite wet, maintaining its own equilibrium. He owns the clothes he wears: loose pants, a long shirt, both gray-green, both worn soft. He owns a cup carved from spiral-grower wood, its interior following the wood's natural helical grain so that liquid poured into it swirls slowly even when held still. He owns nothing else, needs nothing else.

His task—self-appointed or terrace-appointed, the distinction is unclear—is observation and response. He watches. When a fern frond breaks under its own weight, he removes it, carrying it to a specific pool where it will decompose. When a spiral-grower's growth threatens to crack the stone in a way that seems wrong (and he can feel wrongness here, a discordance in the terrace's ambient hum), he carefully prunes it with the stone blade, then returns the blade to its place. When the stone circles shift—and they do shift, minutely, over seasons, the stones creeping outward with frost-heave or inward with settling—he adjusts them, moving each stone back to where it seems to want to be, where the terrace's geometry feels correct.

He is becoming the terrace, slowly. Or the terrace is becoming him. The distinction grows less meaningful as years accumulate. His breathing has synchronized with the mountain's rhythmic seeping. His heartbeat has slowed to match some deep pulse in the stone. He dreams the terrace's dreams: slow spirals of growth, patient accumulation of moisture, the weight of cloud pressing down and lifting, pressing and lifting, a tidal rhythm measured in hours instead of seconds.

**Inara** has been here three years, or possibly four. She is younger—forty, maybe forty-five—but her youth is relative, already tempered by the terrace's slow erosion. She is small, compact, her body dense with a coiled energy that the terrace hasn't yet unwound, though it's working on her. Her hair is cropped short, practical, already graying though it was brown when she arrived. Her eyes are the most startling thing about her: pale gray, almost colorless, and they've developed a quality of stillness, of long focus, as if she's always looking at something just beyond normal sight.

She came fleeing something. Most of them do, in one way or another. She doesn't speak of it, and the others don't ask—words here are too valuable to waste on past pain. But in her first months, she moved with a flinching quality, a readiness for impact. The terrace has slowly dissolved that. You can't maintain hypervigilance in the face of such patient, indifferent constancy. The mountain doesn't care what you're fleeing. It offers only its eternal present: this drip, this breath, this moment of moisture condensing on your skin.

Inara tends the orchids. Not in any horticultural sense—they need no tending, grow according to their own deep schedule—but she knows them. She has named them, privately, though she'd deny this if asked. The cluster near the southern circle is "the sisters." The solitary bloom in the middle section is "the witness." The small one growing from an improbable crack ten feet up the cliff face is "the aspirant." She checks them, learns their cycles, celebrates silently when new growth appears, mourns quietly when one fades.

She has developed a practice: each morning, she collects condensation from moss-mounds, pressing them gently and catching the released water in a small wooden bowl (carved from spiral-grower wood by Kael, years before her arrival, waiting for whoever would need it). She does this until the bowl is full—it holds perhaps a cup—and then she slowly pours it back onto the moss, onto the stone, into the pools, redistributing it with deliberate care. It is pointless. The terrace has more water than it needs, cycles it constantly without human intervention. But the practice grounds her, gives her hands something to do while her mind learns stillness.

She sleeps in the middle section, on a flat expanse of stone near the largest pool. No shelter, just openness and the sky's constant overcast. She likes to wake in deep mist, to open her eyes to whiteness, to exist for those first moments in a world that consists only of stone beneath her body and cloud around it. It reminds her that she is small, that her concerns are smaller, that there is relief in insignificance.

She is learning patience. It's hard for her—her nature is quick, reactive, adaptive. But the terrace rewards only slowness. You cannot hurry a fern's unfurling. You cannot rush the spiral-growers' helical climb. You cannot force the mist to clear or stay. You can only wait, watch, breathe, and slowly—so slowly—your own rhythms adjust.

She talks to herself, sometimes, in whispers the other two pretend not to hear. Working through something, processing something, unwinding the coiled springs of her pre-terrace life. Kael thinks she'll stay, eventually. There's a quality to her stillness, on good days, that suggests she's finding what the terrace offers. But she's not settled yet. She could still leave. The mountain holds lightly, never grasping.

**Mirren** is newest—six months, possibly eight. They are young, which is unusual. Most who find the terrace have lived enough life to need what it offers. Mirren is perhaps twenty-five, lean and awkward, all joints and uncertainty. Their gender is their own business, and the terrace, genderless itself, accepts them without complication. They have dark skin that the constant moisture keeps luminous, and hair in dozens of small braids that they re-do obsessively, a fidgeting habit not yet worn away by the terrace's slow insistence on economy of motion.

Mirren talks more than the others, which means they talk occasionally, in short bursts, usually to themselves. They are mapping the terrace's sounds. They arrived with perfect pitch—a gift and a curse—and they cannot help but hear the terrace's drone as music. They've identified seventeen distinct frequencies in the cliff face's seeping. They've catalogued forty-three types of drips, each with its own tonal quality. They're trying to understand the spiral-growers' creaking as rhythm, though the rhythm is so slow, so extended, that holding it in human memory is nearly impossible.

They sit for hours by pools, listening. They move along the cliff face with their ear pressed to wet stone, following the paths of internal water flow by sound alone. They're developing a theory—they won't call it that, it's too presumptuous—a sense that the terrace is performing something, some vast slow composition that operates on a temporal scale humans aren't built to perceive. They're trying to perceive it anyway. It's probably impossible. They don't care.

At night, in darkness, they hum. Soft, sustained tones that match frequencies they've heard in the stone, in the water, in the breathing air. Sometimes Kael or Inara hums with them—never planned, never discussed, just a spontaneous harmony emerging from separate throats. The terrace seems to respond, or seems to seem to respond, its own sounds shifting slightly, accommodating the human contribution, creating momentary resonances that fade as soon as you notice them.

Mirren is struggling. Six months is not long enough to settle into the terrace's pace. They are still quick, still anxious, still carrying the speed of the outside world in their nervous system. They sleep poorly, wake often, disoriented by darkness and sound. They eat sporadically—there is food here, brought up from below by... someone, they're not clear who, it simply appears at irregular intervals in the northern circle, basic things, dried fruit and hard bread and nuts, enough for three people for a span of days, then more appears when it's needed. They forget to eat, then eat too much at once, then forget again. Their body hasn't regulated to the terrace's rhythms.

But they're trying. They've stopped wearing shoes—the others went barefoot, so they did too, and now they understand why. The stone's warmth, the direct contact, the textural information: dry stone here, wet there, biofilm-slick in this spot, rough in that. You learn the terrace through your soles, build a map in your feet. They're learning.

They sleep, when they sleep, in the southern section, near the mysterious staining that might be writing. They lie on their back and study it in the dim light, looking for patterns. Sometimes they see meaning. Sometimes just stains. They haven't decided which perception is correct, or if correctness matters. The ambiguity troubles them. The terrace is teaching them to sit with ambiguity, but they're a slow student in this particular lesson.

## The Others

**The salamanders** are more conscious than they should be. Not intelligent—that's the wrong frame—but aware in a way that exceeds their tiny amphibian brains' apparent capacity. They watch the humans. Not with fear or curiosity, but with something like assessment. They emerge more often when Inara is present, as if they prefer her company, or find her less disruptive to their activities. 

There are seven, or possibly nine—counting them is difficult, they're rarely all visible simultaneously. Each is hand-length, black as the deepest pool water, with eyes that reflect lamplight (when there is lamplight, rarely) in shades of gold and green. They make no sound. They move with liquid precision, each gesture exactly sufficient, nothing wasted. They are what Kael is trying to become: perfectly efficient, perfectly integrated, absolutely present.

They eat dampflies, but selectively. Not every dampfly—particular ones, chosen by criteria invisible to human observation. They mate, presumably, though none of the humans have witnessed it. Young salamanders appear sometimes, smaller versions, same black coloration, moving with the same liquid certainty. The population stays stable: seven, or possibly nine, never more, never fewer.

At night, sometimes, they gather. All of them, in a single pool, arranged in a pattern—a circle, or a spiral, or something between. They're absolutely still, absolutely silent, just small black shapes in dark water. Are they sleeping? Communing? Performing some amphibian ritual? The humans watch from a distance and don't interfere. There are boundaries here, unspoken but clear. The salamanders tolerate the human presence, even seem to accept it as part of the terrace's ecosystem. But there are spaces—temporal and physical—that are theirs alone.

**The dampflies** exist in clouds of varying density, thickening and thinning according to patterns that might be temporal or might be responsive to humidity, temperature, or factors too subtle for human perception. They're each about the size of a large bee, but elongated, more delicate, their bodies segmented and iridescent: blues and greens and purples that shift with viewing angle and light quality.

They have long legs—six, as proper insects should—that hang down when they hover, creating the impression of dangling threads. They land on every surface: stone, moss, fern fronds, human skin. They're gentle, their touch barely perceptible, and they do no harm. They seem to drink moisture—you can watch them on moss, their mouthparts (too small to see clearly) moving minutely, and the moss darkening slightly where they feed, becoming just a bit drier, then re-saturating from internal reserves once the fly moves on.

They hum. Individually, the sound is nearly inaudible, a high thin note at the edge of hearing. Collectively, when they swarm in higher densities, they create a drone that adds depth to the terrace's constant susurrus. Mirren has determined that the collective hum is actually multiple frequencies, slightly out of phase, creating beat patterns that cycle with a period of roughly seven seconds. Whether this is intentional—whether the dampflies are coordinating their wing-beats to produce these patterns—is unclear. Probably not. Possibly yes. The ambiguity is characteristic of the terrace.

They live perhaps a week, each individual, though measuring this requires patience and the ability to distinguish one dampfly from another, which is nearly impossible. They die silently, falling to stone or into pools, their iridescent bodies quickly breaking down, consumed by processes too small to see. Their biomass becomes part of the terrace's general circulation. Nothing is wasted.

**The birds** that visit are ravens, usually. Sometimes a hawk. Very rarely, something smaller—a thrush once, disoriented, resting on a spiral-grower for half an hour before departing. The ravens are pragmatic: they come for water, for the occasional drowned dampfly in a pool, for reasons of their own. They're loud, their calls harsh against the terrace's soft acoustic texture. They don't stay. 

There's one raven that returns more often than others—Inara thinks she recognizes it by a damaged feather in its left wing, a break in the vane that creates a small gap. It's warier than the others, lands farther from the humans, watches with more intensity. It has taken food from the cache in the northern circle—dried fruit, carried off to some nest or cache of its own. The humans don't begrudge it. There's always enough. There's always exactly enough.

The hawk is hunting, when it comes. It perches on the cliff's edge, above the terrace, and watches the dampfly clouds with predatory patience. Occasionally it drops, takes a fly (or tries—they're more maneuverable than they look), then labors back up to altitude. It's beautiful in a way that feels wrong here, too sharp, too fast, too decisive. The terrace is soft edges and slow change. The hawk is angles and speed and sudden death. It doesn't belong, quite, but it comes anyway, and the terrace tolerates it as it tolerates everything.

## The Visitors

People come, very occasionally. Perhaps twice a year someone climbs to the terrace from the lower trails, or descends from above, and stays for a span of hours or days. The three residents—Kael, Inara, Mirren—greet them with silence and small nods, neither welcoming nor forbidding. There is water. There is space. There is stone to sit on. Stay if you need to. Leave when you're ready.

Some visitors are hikers, accidentally arrived, looking for a route up or down the mountain. They stay minutes or hours, made uncomfortable by the strange warmth, the constant sound, the presence of the three silent residents. They take water, rest briefly, leave. They tell stories, later, of the weird shelf where hermits live, where the rock is always wet, where time feels wrong. The stories are embellished or dismissed, according to the listener's temperament.

Some are seekers, intentionally arrived, looking for the terrace they've heard about in whispered conversations, in marginal notes in climbing guides, in folktales told by valley dwellers. They come with questions or needs: grief to process, decisions to make, spiritual crises to resolve. The terrace offers them what it offers everyone: moisture, warmth, sound, slowness, patience. Some find what they need. Some don't. The residents don't guide them, don't counsel, don't intervene. This isn't a monastery. There are no teachings. There is only the place itself, and what it does to time and perception and the busy human mind's constant generation of noise.

A woman came, once, while Kael was alone, before Inara. She was old, dying, cancer eating her from inside. She climbed the mountain with a determination that must have been agony. She stayed three days, lying on the warm stone, breathing the thick air, listening to the mountain breathe. She died on the fourth day, quietly, near the middle pool. Kael sat with her body for a full day and night, then buried her—not in soil, there isn't enough, but in a cairn of stones at the terrace's edge, overlooking the cloud. He added one of the spiral-growers' fallen branches, its wood still warm. He sang something—he didn't know he remembered songs, but one came, old and wordless. The terrace's acoustics carried it, strange and echoing. Then silence. Then the constant drip and creak resumed, and he returned to his work, which is watching, which is being present, which is exactly what he was doing.

The woman's cairn is still there, near the southern end. Moss grows on it now. A small fern has found purchase between two stones. The terrace is reclaiming her, slowly, folding her into its cycles. Kael visits the cairn sometimes, not in mourning but in acknowledgment. She stayed. In the most permanent way, she stayed. There are worse fates.

## The Unseen

There is a sense, persistent but unconfirmable, of other presence. Not the salamanders, not the dampflies, not the occasional birds. Something else. Something in the stone, or of the stone, or expressed through the stone. The residents don't speak of it to each other—some experiences are too tenuous for words—but each has felt it.

For Kael, it comes as a quality of attention. Sometimes, when he's working—clearing a broken frond, adjusting a circle-stone—he feels watched. Not by eyes, exactly. More like the terrace itself is aware of him, is noting his actions, is assessing whether his maintenance serves its needs. He can't tell if it approves. The feeling is neutral, observational, patient. It's not comforting, exactly, but it's not threatening either. It simply is.

For Inara, it's a sense of presence in the mist. When the cloud rolls in thick and she's isolated in her small sphere of visibility, there's sometimes a feeling that the white around her contains something, someone, shapes that move at the edge of perception. Turn to look and there's only mist. Look away and the sense of presence returns. She's learned not to look. Let it be what it is. The terrace has its own life, its own inhabitants, and not all of them need to be visible to be real.

For Mirren, it comes through sound. Sometimes, in the terrace's constant acoustic tapestry, they hear something like a voice. Not words—nothing so distinct—but a tonal quality that suggests intentional communication, meaning being conveyed, though the meaning itself remains opaque. They're trying to respond, with their humming, their careful matching of frequencies. They don't know if anything is listening. They don't know if anything is there. But the attempt feels necessary, a reaching toward possible communion with whatever the terrace is or contains.

None of them would call it a spirit. That word carries too much baggage, too many specific cultural meanings. But "presence" fits. "Awareness" fits. The terrace is alive—that much is obvious—but its life exceeds the sum of its plants and salamanders and dampflies. There is something systemic, something emergent, something that watches through the wet stone and breathes with the mountain's exhalations and knows, in its slow patient way, what occurs within its boundaries.

The stone blade might be relevant here. It was left by someone, clearly. But by whom, and when, and for what purpose? Kael uses it, carefully, when pruning is needed. But it was placed there long before his arrival, placed with ceremony suggested by its position, by the way it sits on its flat rock like an offering or a tool deliberately provided. For whom? By whom?

Perhaps by the terrace's previous residents, whoever they were. Perhaps the three are not the first to tend this place, to live in slow rhythm with its cycles. Perhaps there have been others, in decades or centuries past, and they left the blade as they departed, a tool for those who would come after. A continuation of stewardship.

Or perhaps the blade was placed by something else. By whatever presence inhabits the stone, made physical through processes unknown. A gift, or a test, or simply a necessary tool placed where it would be needed. The terrace operates on its own logic. Tools appear when needed—food arrives in the northern circle with no visible bearer. Why not a blade, left on stone, waiting for the hand that would need it?

The residents don't speculate aloud. Speculation feels presumptuous. Better to simply accept: there is a blade, it's useful, use it with care, return it when finished. The how and why can remain mysterious. Mystery is acceptable here. Mystery is maybe the point.

## The Rhythm

What the residents have in common—what anyone who stays more than a few days begins to develop—is a sense of rhythm, of being synchronized with the terrace's deep pulse. It's not conscious. You can't force it. But slowly, if you're receptive, if you settle into the place's slowness, your body begins to match its patterns.

You wake with the subtle brightening that marks dawn, though there's no sun visible, just a slight warming of the gray light. You walk the terrace's length, your pace matching some internal periodicity that aligns with how long the walking should take—not measured in minutes but in rightness, in the feeling of proper duration. You tend what needs tending, not by schedule but by awareness, by noticing what the terrace presents as needing attention.

You eat when hungry, but hunger becomes subtle, rarely urgent. The body, given steady warmth and moisture and gentle movement, needs less fuel than it did in the faster world below. You sleep when tired, but fatigue too becomes gentle, a soft settling rather than exhaustion's collapse. Dreams, when they come, are slow: long sequences of water moving through stone, of ferns unfurling over days compressed into sleep's strange duration, of walking the terrace's length in darkness, guided by sound and the warmth of stone underfoot.

Sex is absent here, or dormant. Not forbidden—nothing is forbidden—but irrelevant. The drives that operated in the faster world quiet in the terrace's patient atmosphere. Bodies become functional, appreciated for their utility: hands that can adjust stones, ears that can distinguish drip-patterns, feet that can read texture. The urgent needs fall away. What remains is presence, maintenance, observation.

Conversation becomes rare and precious. Inara and Mirren sometimes talk, short exchanges about what they've noticed: a new orchid bud, a change in the spiral-growers' creaking pattern, a salamander behaving oddly. Kael rarely speaks, but when he does, they listen. His words carry weight accumulated through years of silence. He'll say "The middle pool is deeper" and they'll go look, and he'll be right—somehow, it has deepened, by an inch or two, the stone beneath having settled or eroded minutely, and they'll watch the new depth, observe how it changes the way light moves in the water, how it affects the dampflies' behavior.

Or Mirren will say "The stone is singing lower" and Inara will listen, and hear it or not hear it—her ears are less trained—but she'll accept the observation, add it to her internal model of the terrace's state. Or Inara will say "The sisters are about to bloom" and within two days the orchid cluster will produce flowers, and no one will remark on her accuracy because of course she's accurate, she watches them, she knows.

This is community, here: shared attention, shared maintenance, shared rhythm. Not intimacy in any conventional sense, but a deep quiet companionship of people who are learning to be part of the same slow system, to serve the same patient needs, to breathe together in the mountain's exhaled warmth.

## The Leaving

They will leave, eventually. All of them, even Kael. The terrace keeps no one against their will. When the time comes—and it comes for different reasons, at different times, measurable only in internal seasons—they'll gather what little they own and descend or ascend, returning to the faster world with its sharp edges and urgent demands.

They'll be changed. You cannot live on the terrace for years—or even months—and return unchanged. The slowness gets into you, becomes part of your operating system. The patience learned here doesn't easily dissolve. The attention to small changes, the ability to hear complex soundscapes, the comfort with ambiguity and mystery—these persist.

Some adapt back to normal speed. They carry the terrace inside them, a still place they can return to mentally when the world's rush becomes overwhelming. They're better at waiting, better at noticing, better at being present. The terrace was a gift, and they unwrap it slowly over the remaining years of their lives.

Some can't adapt back. The world is too fast, too bright, too loud. They struggle in cities, in conversations, in the press of human urgency and need. They might return to the terrace, or they might find other slow places: monasteries, remote cabins, quiet work that allows for patience. They need the rhythm they learned, can't function without it.

And some stay until they die, like the woman Kael buried. They give their years to the terrace, become so integrated into its ecology that leaving would be a kind of violence, a tearing away. They age on warm stone, their bodies slowing further, their minds settling into deeper synchrony with the mountain's pulse, until one day they simply stop, as naturally as a fern frond that's reached its span, and their bodies are buried or burned and their matter returns to the cycle, and the terrace continues, unchanged, patient, breathing its slow breaths, waiting for whoever comes next.

Mirren will leave first, probably. Six months, or eight—they'll reach a year, maybe two, but probably not more. They're young. They have fast years ahead of them, music to make, relationships to form, a life to live in the swift world. The terrace will give them what it gives: a foundation of stillness, a reference point of slow, a sense of rhythm that operates on scales beyond the human. They'll carry it forward, use it, be grateful for it. But they'll leave.

Inara might stay longer. Three years, or four, or seven. She's healing something, processing something, and the terrace is patient therapy. When the healing is complete—if healing is ever complete—she might go back, rejoin the world, carry forward whatever wisdom the terrace has taught about patience and presence and the value of small attentions. Or she might stay, become another Kael, give decades to the wet stone and constant sound. It's not decided yet. She doesn't need to decide. The terrace asks nothing, demands nothing. Stay or go. Both are fine.

Kael will stay until he can't, which might be another ten years, might be twenty. His body will age, will eventually fail to navigate the terrace's length, will need more help than the place provides. Then he'll leave, descend to the valleys, live out whatever remains in some quiet place with more amenities, more support. Or he'll stay until the end, and whoever's there with him will bury him—another cairn, another fern finding purchase in piled stones. Either way is acceptable. The terrace is indifferent to human choices, generous with its allowances.

When they go, others will come. The pattern holds. The terrace is always tended by someone, by a few someones, by people who need its particular gifts and can offer in return their attention, their maintenance, their presence. It's a fair exchange. More than fair. The terrace gives far more than it asks.

## What Remains

When all the current residents are gone—when Kael and Inara and Mirren have departed or died, when new residents have come and gone in their turn, when decades have passed—the terrace will be unchanged. The ferns will still unfurl. The spiral-growers will still creak. The dampflies will still hum. The salamanders will still glide through pools in their liquid silence. The stone will still weep. The mountain will still breathe.

The stone circles will still be there, adjusted by countless hands, each resident adding their small corrections to the patient geometry. The blade will still wait on its flat rock, used and cleaned and returned, used and cleaned and returned, an unbroken chain of careful stewardship extending back to whoever first placed it and forward to whoever will use it last.

The cairns will accumulate, perhaps. More people choosing to stay in the ultimate sense, giving their bones to the mountain. Or perhaps the terrace will claim them completely, break down the cairns, fold the stones back into its general structure, erase all evidence of individual death in favor of the larger continuity. Either way is consistent with the place's character.

The mystery will remain. The warmth rising from below, source unknown. The water too pure, too constant. The time that moves strangely. The light in the mist. The presence that watches without eyes. The patterns that might be patterns or might be randomness interpreted as meaning. The terrace will keep its secrets, will continue to offer them openly while never explaining, never resolving into simple understanding.

And people will continue to come, drawn by rumor or accident or need. Some will stay hours. Some will stay years. A few will stay forever. They'll all be changed by it, marked by the slow rhythm, taught patience by the patient stone.

The terrace lives. Not as an organism, exactly—though it has organismal qualities—but as a system, a process, an ongoing event that includes stone and water and plant and animal and human and time and sound and light and something else, something harder to name, something that suffuses it all and makes it more than the sum of components.

It breathes. It teaches. It maintains itself and accepts the maintenance others offer. It exists in balance, settled but alive, strange but not hostile, mysterious but not withholding. It is what it is, fully and completely, and what it is is sufficient: a small high place where the rules are slightly different, where time moves differently, where humans can learn to be slower, to be quieter, to be more present, to be—for a span of days or years—part of something larger, older, deeper, and endlessly, patiently breathing.
