# Who Lives in the Breathing Hollow

The **singers** are the hollow's most evident presence, though "evident" means something different here where nothing is seen.

Each singer is approximately the size of a human head, though their bodies compress and expand slightly with their vocalizations, making exact measurement meaningless. The radial symmetry of their bodies means they have no forward or backward—six legs emerge from a central mass at sixty-degree intervals, each leg containing its own neural cluster that processes local sensory information. They are not hive-minded, but each leg knows what the others know through chemical signals that travel faster than their centralized nervous system could manage. Touch one leg and all six respond, but not identically—each responds according to its position and circumstance.

Their bodies yield under pressure like a water balloon, but the interior is not liquid—it's a dense matrix of muscle, resonating chambers, and something like lung tissue that fills most of their body cavity. They breathe through their skin, but they sing through specialized chambers that loop through their core in mobius-strip configurations, allowing them to sustain tones continuously without pause. They can sing while feeding, while moving, while sleeping (they do something like sleeping), the tone varying in complexity with their level of consciousness but never stopping completely. To stop singing is to die, or perhaps to stop singing is what defines death for them.

Their covering is not fur or feathers but something intermediate—filaments that branch at microscopic scales, creating surfaces of immense area that wick moisture, sense vibration, and produce the oils that keep their skin-breathing functional. The filaments are iridescent, though this means nothing in darkness—it's a holdover from some ancient ancestor, or perhaps they can perceive the iridescence through non-visual means, the way certain fish sense electric fields. The filaments respond to touch by stiffening slightly, creating a ripple of texture-change that spreads across the body, allowing singers to communicate through direct contact in ways more nuanced than sound alone permits.

They have eyes—six of them, one above each leg—but the eyes do not see light. They are sensitive to pressure waves, functioning as directional hearing organs, allowing them to locate sound sources with uncanny precision. The eyes can also detect the electromagnetic emissions from the pillars, perceiving them as something like warmth-at-a-distance, a gradient they navigate toward or away from depending on their needs. When a singer "looks" at you, all six eyes orient toward you simultaneously, and you feel it as a sudden intensity of attention, a focus that is almost weight.

The singers are not intelligent in the tool-using, problem-solving sense, but they are deeply aware. They recognize individuals—other singers, the walkers, regular human visitors—and adjust their behavior accordingly. They have preferences, habits, relationships. Pairs bond, though not monogamously, creating duets that can last for days before one or both singers drift into other configurations. They mourn when companions die, producing tones that descend into infrasound, barely audible groans that make the air itself feel heavy. They celebrate, though what they celebrate is unclear—sometimes the blooming of particular flowers, sometimes the arrival of a walker, sometimes nothing perceptible, just sudden eruptions of complex harmonics that spread through the colony like laughter.

They build nests from moss and the silk-like threads, weaving structures that are part shelter, part instrument—the nests resonate with their songs, amplifying certain frequencies, dampening others, each nest tuned to its builder's voice. Nests last for years, maintained and modified continuously, growing more acoustically sophisticated over time. Old nests become landmarks, reference points in the soundscape, and when a singer dies, its nest is gradually deconstructed by others, the materials redistributed, the acoustic signature absorbed into new constructions.

They reproduce through something like budding crossed with sexual recombination. Two or more singers will press together for days, their bodies partially merging, exchanging genetic material through direct cellular transfer. Then they separate, and one of them—always only one, regardless of how many participated—develops a growth that swells over weeks until it detaches as a fully-formed juvenile, already singing, already mobile, though its voice won't mature for months. The juvenile is raised communally, any adult will feed it, any nest will shelter it, until it develops its own voice-signature and establishes its own territory within the soundscape.

They eat constantly but in small amounts, processing the tough vegetation slowly through grinding plates that line what might be called a gizzard. They have no teeth, no biting capability—they pull leaves into their central mouth-opening through muscular action, the opening capable of stretching to accommodate surprisingly large items. Digestion is slow and thorough, extracting nearly all available nutrients, excreting compact pellets that crumble quickly into soil. They seem to prefer certain plants at certain times, showing seasonal variations in diet that correlate with the plants' chemical compositions—eating more of the mineral-accumulating species when preparing to grow new filaments, more of the aromatic species before breeding, more of the high-calorie fungi when air temperature drops to its lowest ranges.

The singers have territories, but the territories are sonic rather than spatial. Each singer maintains a characteristic frequency range, a tonal signature that says "this acoustic space is mine," and others respect it, singing around and through it but not over it. Conflicts occur when two singers' ranges drift too close together, resolved through volume competitions that can last hours—both singers increasing amplitude until one yields and shifts frequency. Physical violence is rare, reserved for genuine threats like a predator that doesn't exist here, or protection of juveniles from the very occasional predator-behavior displayed by a sick or injured walker.

They are curious about visitors but not needy. A singer might approach and touch you with its legs, reading your texture and temperature and chemical signature, then lose interest and move away. Or it might stay near you for hours, adjusting its song to harmonize with your breathing rhythm, your heartbeat, the sounds your joints make when you move. Some singers become habituated to regular visitors, greeting them with specific tone-patterns, guiding them to particular locations as if showing off favorite spots. But the relationship remains optional, initiated by the singer, never demanding.

The **walkers** are fewer, more alien, more difficult to know.

They appear perhaps once a week, perhaps less—their territories extend far beyond the hollow, and they pass through rather than residing permanently. Each walker is an individual, distinguished by size, gait, the particular subsonic frequency of its groan, but whether they form social groups elsewhere is unknown. They might be solitary. They might congregate in some other hollow over the next ridge. They leave no traces except the slight polish on the floating platforms where they rest, the specific plants they choose to browse, the occasional manipulation of threads that serves purposes unclear.

A walker is mostly leg, four legs that are each nearly two meters long, jointed in three places, ending in feet that spread like hands, six digits each, capable of remarkable delicacy despite their size. The body suspended between the legs is compact, barrel-shaped, perhaps half a meter long and thirty centimeters in diameter, covered in skin that is somewhere between reptilian scales and bark, rough and deeply textured. The head extends forward on a short neck, and the head is nearly all jaw—a wide, gentle mouth that opens to reveal grinding plates rather than teeth, designed to process the toughest vegetation.

Above the jaw are sensory organs that might be eyes but probably aren't, bulges covered in the same rough skin as the body, unresponsive to light but tracking movement through some other mechanism—perhaps heat, perhaps the subtle air currents that moving objects create, perhaps direct perception of the bioelectric fields that all living things generate. The walkers navigate with extraordinary precision despite their apparent blindness, stepping over obstacles with millimeters to spare, reaching their heads into dense vegetation to browse specific leaves without disturbing surrounding growth.

They move with the slow deliberation of enormous things, though they are not enormous—their care comes from attention rather than mass. Each step is placed precisely, weight transferred gradually, the leg lifting only after the foot is positioned exactly where it needs to be. Crossing the hollow takes them hours, and they pause frequently, standing motionless for periods that might be rest or might be some form of meditation or information processing. When they stand still, they are almost impossible to detect except for their subsonic groan, which continues regardless of movement, a constant vibration that travels through the ground as much as through air.

The groan serves multiple functions. It is echolocation—the low frequencies reflect off surfaces, off vegetation, off other fauna, and the walker processes the returns to build a sonic map of its environment. It is communication—other walkers at distance can hear it, and the modulations contain information, warnings or invitations or simple declarations of presence. It is also, perhaps, self-comfort, the way humans hum while working, a baseline of sound that says "I am here, I am functioning, the world continues."

Walkers feed on vegetation that nothing else eats—the oldest moss, turned hard and nearly fossilized; the thick bark of the rare dead trees; woody stems of shrubs that have become nearly mineral; fungal shelves so tough they resist breaking even when dry. The walkers' grinding plates can process this material, extracting nutrients other fauna cannot access, performing a service to the ecosystem by breaking down the most recalcitrant organic matter. They are eating not just food but the hollow's memory, consuming decades and centuries of accumulated growth, reducing it to fertile excrement that enriches the soil.

They rest on the floating platforms, which seem designed for them—the right height, the right size, stable enough to support their mass without perceptible movement. A walker will approach a platform slowly, test it with one foot, then another, then carefully lower its body until it sits, legs folded, weight distributed across the platform's surface. They rest for periods that might be an hour or might be three, the groaning continuing but shifting to deeper frequencies, slower modulations. Other fauna avoid platforms occupied by walkers, giving them a wide berth, though not from fear—more like respect for something large and old and entitled to space.

The walkers occasionally interact with the structures in ways that suggest purpose rather than accident. They will stand before a pillar for extended periods, their groan synchronizing with the pillar's hum, creating beats and harmonics that are mathematically complex and possibly meaningful. They will touch the threads, pulling them gently, listening to the tones transmitted to pillars, adjusting the threads' tension in ways that change the transmitted frequencies. They will approach the folded structure and reach into its impossible interior spaces with their long tongues—yes, tongues, surprisingly delicate organs that extend from the grinding-plate mouth—as if retrieving or depositing something, though what they retrieve or deposit remains invisible and intangible to human observers.

Are they intelligent? They show problem-solving behavior—navigating complex terrain, finding specific plants, manipulating threads and platforms with clear intent. They show individual variation—each walker has preferences, habits, characteristic behaviors. They show something like emotion—one walker observed over multiple visits displayed what appeared to be joy when certain flowers were blooming, returning repeatedly to a particular patch, groaning in higher frequencies, moving with more energy than usual. But they do not communicate with humans in any recognizable way, do not show interest in human visitors beyond simple awareness, do not modify their behavior based on human presence.

They might be caretakers. They might be the structures' creators, or descendants of the creators, maintaining the technology through instinct or training or genetic memory. They might be pets or tools of some other intelligence, biological robots performing functions they do not understand. They might be exactly what they appear—herbivores browsing a habitat, occasionally interacting with interesting elements of their environment, no more mysterious than deer investigating a garden sculpture.

They arrive without warning and leave the same way, their subsonic groan fading to imperceptibility over distance, the hollow returning to its walker-less soundscape until the next one passes through. The singers do not change their behavior much when walkers are present, suggesting long familiarity, deep integration. The smaller fauna continue their activities around the walkers' legs as if around trees. Only humans seem to find the walkers remarkable, which might say more about humans than about walkers.

The **moss-dwellers** are invisible unless you search for them, and even then they are more texture than organism.

They are small—thumb-sized at largest, many much smaller—and segmented, their bodies composed of articulated sections that move independently, allowing them to navigate through the dense moss layer like swimmers through water. Each segment has legs, tiny paired appendages ending in hooks, bristles, or adhesive pads depending on species and function. They use these legs to pull themselves through moss stems, between moss leaves, through the spaces where water collects, where the moss meets soil, where spores accumulate and decay begins.

There are dozens of species, maybe hundreds—no one has catalogued them completely, and the darkness makes identification challenging even when you manage to capture one. Some are elongated, centipede-like, built for speed and distance travel through the moss canopy. Others are compact, pillbug-like, armored against predation that doesn't exist here but existed somewhere in their evolutionary past, the armor now serving mainly as protection against desiccation during rare dry periods. Still others are delicate, almost translucent, visible only when backlit by your hand's warmth, their internal organs faintly luminescent with the bioluminescence of gut bacteria.

They feed on decay—fallen leaves, dead moss fronds, fungi, the bodies of their own dead, anything organic that has begun breaking down. They are the hollow's primary decomposers, reducing complex organic molecules to simpler forms, excreting nutrients that the moss and other plants reabsorb, closing loops that might otherwise remain open. Watch a moss patch where a singer dropped a leaf weeks ago and you'll see the leaf slowly disappearing, not rotting visibly but being carried away piece by piece by the moss-dwellers, disassembled and distributed through the ecosystem at scales too small to observe directly.

They have sensory organs but limited nervous systems—most of their behavior is programmed, reflexive, stimulus-response rather than decision-making. They move toward chemical gradients that indicate food, away from textures that indicate barriers or inappropriate substrate, toward moisture, away from excessive heat. They congregate in areas where decay is active, sometimes dozens of individuals working the same leaf, but they don't cooperate—they simply respond to the same stimulus, their individual actions coincidentally supporting each other.

Some species have specialized relationships with plants—they live only in the moss cushions near the pools, where the water-retaining properties create specific microclimates. Others live only in the moss that grows on the pillars, feeding on something in the pillar material that flakes off at molecular scales, too slow to measure but sufficient to support small populations over time. Still others are generalists, moving through multiple moss species, multiple microhabitats, opportunistic and adaptable.

They reproduce through fission, mostly—an individual grows to threshold size, then divides into two individuals, each containing half the original's segments plus new segments grown to replace the missing halves. Some species also reproduce sexually, individuals meeting at random, exchanging genetic material through brief contact, then continuing on their separate paths, the exchange resulting in offspring produced days later through parthenogenesis that incorporates the exchanged genes. The balance between fission and sexual reproduction depends on environmental stability—more fission when conditions are constant, more sexual reproduction when conditions vary, the genetic mixing providing adaptability insurance.

They have lifespans measured in weeks or months, far shorter than the singers or walkers, their populations turning over rapidly, evolution operating at speeds that allow real-time adaptation. A change in plant chemistry can produce a new moss-dweller species within years, populations diverging and specializing, finding niches within niches, increasing overall diversity without increasing competition. The moss layer likely contains more genetic diversity per cubic meter than any comparable volume elsewhere, thousands of species compressed into the space beneath your feet, most of them unknown, all of them essential.

Humans rarely notice them unless they hold very still in the moss and wait, feeling the subtle tickle of tiny legs traversing skin, the occasional pinch of hooks gripping fabric, the sense of minute presence moving through the environment at scales beneath conscious perception. They are the hollow's foundation in many ways—without them, dead vegetation would accumulate, nutrients would lock up in undecayed organic matter, the plants would starve, the singers would have nothing to eat, the walkers would find no browse, the entire system would collapse. But they work invisibly, at night-scales even in perpetual darkness, their importance inverse to their visibility.

The **pool-dwellers** are almost not-fauna, their animal characteristics so reduced that you might mistake them for mobile plants or animate mud.

They live in the pools, or perhaps they are part of the pools, the distinction unclear. Each individual is formless, a volume of tissue without defined shape, adapting its geometry to the space it occupies. In shallow water they spread wide and flat, like fleshy pancakes ten centimeters across and one centimeter thick. In deeper pockets they extend downward, forming columns or fingers that reach into the substrate. When the pools contract and they're exposed to air, they ball up, reducing surface area, waiting.

They are filter-feeders, drawing water through their bodies via cilia that cover their surfaces, countless microscopic hairs beating in coordinated waves, creating currents that pull water in through one region and expel it through another. The water passes through internal channels that branch and rebranch, maximizing contact between water and filter-tissue, extracting bacteria, algae, dissolved organic compounds, minerals, everything small enough to enter the channels and nutritious enough to be worth processing.

They have no sensory organs that are distinct from their general tissue—their entire body surface is sensitive to light (they avoid it when rare bioluminescence occurs), to chemicals (they move slowly toward higher nutrient concentrations), to vibration (they contract when large objects like walkers approach pools), to temperature (they position themselves in thermal gradients to optimize metabolism). They have no brain, no centralized control—their behavior emerges from their tissue's distributed sensitivity, local responses to local stimuli creating coherent whole-organism behavior without executive function.

They reproduce through fragmentation and fusion both. A pool-dweller that grows too large might split into pieces, each piece becoming a new individual if conditions support it, or reabsorbing into the parent if not. Two pool-dwellers that encounter each other might fuse, merging tissues, combining genetic lineages, becoming one larger individual with mixed characteristics. There are probably also sexual processes—release of gametes into the water, external fertilization, development of larvae—but observations are difficult, the water too opaque with suspended material, the organisms too amorphous to track reliably.

They have a symbiotic relationship with photosynthetic bacteria that live in their tissues, bacteria that can harvest energy from the pillars' electromagnetic emissions just as the pillar-coral do. The bacteria provide the pool-dwellers with supplemental nutrition, glucose synthesized from ambient energy, while the pool-dwellers provide the bacteria with shelter, minerals, optimal positioning in the pools. The relationship is ancient—the bacteria's genes show up in the pool-dwellers' genomes as incorporated sequences, suggesting millions of years of co-evolution, possibly lateral gene transfer, the boundary between host and symbiont becoming porous at molecular scales.

They are essential to pool health. By filtering constantly, they clarify the water, prevent bacterial blooms, cycle nutrients between water and sediment. They also stabilize the pool margins—their tissues bind particles together, their metabolic byproducts cement sediments, creating the subtle structure that prevents the pools from simply spreading across the hollow as the water expands. Without pool-dwellers, the pools would be murky, chaotic, their boundaries undefined, their capacity to support other life much reduced.

They are also possibly the oldest fauna in the hollow. Their reproductive flexibility, their ability to survive fragmentation, their symbiotic energy supplementation—all suggest organisms that can persist through extreme environmental changes. They might have been here before the mountains eroded to their current state, adapting through each epoch, surviving whatever produced the hollow and the structures, continuing through millions of years while around them other species arrived, evolved, specialized, and became the ecosystem's more visible elements.

They have no interaction with humans beyond mechanical—they contract if you touch them, pull away if you disturb their water, return when you leave. They do not learn, do not remember, do not distinguish between a human foot and a falling branch. You could visit the hollow a thousand times and the pool-dwellers would respond identically to your presence each time, no recognition, no adaptation, pure stimulus-response. Yet they feel somehow aware, the way simple organisms can feel aware, their behavior so appropriate to circumstances that you suspect more going on than mechanics, some form of minimal subjectivity, experience without reflection.

The **pillar-coral** are not coral, not plants, probably not fauna, but they are alive and they act like animals sometimes, so they belong in this accounting.

They anchor to the pillars, to the folded structure, occasionally to the larger rocks at pool margins. Each colony is small—ten centimeters across when mature—but dense with complexity. The base attaches through adhesive that is partially biological, partially chemical bonding with the pillar material, forming a connection so strong that removing a colony would require removing pillar substrate. From the base grow feeding structures, fractal branches that subdivide again and again until they terminate in surfaces measured in square meters despite the colony's small volume.

The feeding structures extend into air, which is unusual—most filter-feeders require water. But the air here is nearly water, saturated enough that the pillar-coral can extract dissolved nutrients, atmospheric bacteria, the aerial plankton of spores and pollen and microorganisms that drift through the hollow's moisture. The branches undulate slowly, creating currents, drawing nutrient-rich air through their surfaces, absorbing what they need, releasing purified air back to the environment.

They photosynthesize, but not with light. They have photosynthetic pigments, chlorophyll analogs, but these have evolved to capture the pillars' electromagnetic emissions, which are strongest in the very-low-frequency range, wavelengths measured in kilometers. The pigments' molecular structure is bizarre, incorporating metals that shouldn't be biological, resonating at frequencies that shouldn't produce chemical changes, yet somehow harvesting energy and converting it to sugars through pathways that are recognizably photosynthetic in architecture.

They are sessile most of the time, anchored and growing, but they can move. Once established, a colony can detach, leaving its base behind, crawling across the pillar's surface on hundreds of tiny cilia at rates of millimeters per day. They move toward optimal electromagnetic field strengths, toward areas where their feeding structures can extend without competing with neighbors, away from areas where mineral deposits or moss growth are overwhelming them. The movement is purposeful—colonies encountering barriers reverse course, colonies finding good substrate stop and re-anchor, suggesting sensory capability and decision-making, though the mechanisms remain unclear.

They reproduce through budding and through broadcast spawning. A healthy colony will produce buds that detach as mobile larvae, crawling away from the parent to find their own anchoring spots. Multiple colonies will also synchronize spawning events, releasing gametes into the air, where the moisture holds them suspended long enough for fertilization to occur, the resulting larvae settling onto pillars and beginning new colonies. The synchronization happens across all pillar-coral in the hollow simultaneously, suggesting chemical communication or shared sensitivity to environmental cues, the hollow's moisture acting as a medium for coordination.

They are probably engineered. Their electromagnetic-photosynthesis is too unlikely to have evolved naturally, too specifically adapted to the pillars' emissions, suggesting they were designed to live here, possibly by whatever built the pillars, possibly by the pillars themselves if the pillars are autonomous in some way. They show design elegance—they fill a niche that otherwise wouldn't exist, harvesting an energy source nothing else can use, converting it to biomass that enters the food web when they die and decay. They are living interface between technology and biology, assuming the pillars are technology, which seems likely but isn't certain.

They live for years, possibly decades—no one has tracked a colony long enough to observe natural death. They grow slowly, accumulating mass at rates suggesting individual colonies might be centuries old, their calcified bases showing growth rings when broken and examined. They are attacked by nothing, disease appears rare, physical damage is usually survived through regeneration. They simply persist, anchored to their pillars, feeding and growing and reproducing, stable components of a stable system.

When you touch one, it contracts slightly, pulling its feeding structures closer to its base, a defensive response that suggests sensitivity but not intelligence. The texture is somewhere between soft coral and firm gelatin, yielding under pressure, cool to the touch despite the pillar's warmth. Some visitors report that touching the pillar-coral while the pillar is humming produces a sensation—a tingling, a vibration felt deep in hand bones, possibly the electromagnetic field interacting with nerve tissue, possibly just the power of suggestion. The colonies themselves seem unharmed by the contact, expanding again within minutes of being released.

The **smaller singers** are not juvenile singers but a different species, possibly several species, much harder to observe because they are quieter and avoid contact.

They are smaller—perhaps one-third the size of the common singers—and more delicate, their leg joints visible through translucent skin, their bodies compressible to sizes that let them hide in spaces between branches, under moss layers, inside the folded structure's impossible geometries. They sing at higher frequencies, often above easy human hearing, their tones blending with the common singers' harmonics in ways that make them hard to distinguish. You know they're present mainly by inference—sounds that don't quite match the common singers' patterns, movements that seem wrong for the larger species, occasional direct encounters when one fails to hide quickly enough.

They might be nocturnal, which is absurd in perpetual darkness, but they might be active during the pools' contraction phases when the common singers rest, occupying temporal niches instead of spatial ones. They might feed on different plants—the higher, thinner branches that the common singers can't reach, the flowers that are too delicate for larger mouths, the pollen that drifts through the air. They might be specialists where the common singers are generalists, targeting specific resources in specific conditions.

They are more solitary, or at least they don't form the dense colonies that common singers do. You might encounter one every few visits, always alone, always wary, never remaining in one place long enough for detailed observation. They sing briefly—a few seconds of high-frequency tone—then fall silent and move. Whether they maintain the continuous singing that common singers do is unknown; possibly they can stop singing, or possibly their frequencies are simply too high to hear continuously, only occasionally dropping into human range.

They might be a recent evolutionary split from common singers, populations diverging in response to resource competition, body size decreasing as an adaptation to unexploited niches. Or they might be older, the ancestral form from which common singers evolved, persisting in reduced numbers as the larger species came to dominate. Or they might not be closely related at all, convergent evolution producing similar body plans for similar functions, the radial symmetry and continuous vocalization arising independently.

They have relationships with the structures that differ from common singers—they seem to prefer the folded structure specifically, using its interior spaces as shelter, emerging only to feed. Whether they're protecting something inside, being protected by something inside, or simply using it as convenient real estate is unclear. The walkers largely ignore them, suggesting they're too small to be interesting, though one walker was observed carefully not stepping on a small singer that was crossing its path, suggesting awareness and consideration even for fauna too insignificant to usually warrant attention.

The **fungal gardens** might not be fauna at all, but they move, slowly, and they seem to make decisions, so they occupy a category somewhere between vegetation and animal, or outside both categories entirely.

They grow on dead wood, on the underside of particularly old moss cushions, on certain rocks near the pools. Each garden is a colony of multiple fungal species living in coordinated relationship, the species' mycelia interwoven so thoroughly that they form a single functional organism. The visible portion is fruiting bodies—mushroom-like structures in miniature, none larger than your fingernail, most much smaller, in clusters that can cover areas the size of your hand.

The gardens move by growing directionally—the mycelium extends in specific directions based on nutrient availability, moisture levels, competition from other gardens, producing new fruiting bodies at the growth edge while allowing old fruiting bodies at the trailing edge to die. A garden can cross a meter of substrate over the course of a year, which is fast enough to qualify as behavior rather than just growth. The movement is purposeful—gardens route around barriers, redirect when they encounter unsuitable substrate, move toward areas where decay is active.

They reproduce through spores, obviously, but they also reproduce as coordinated systems—a garden reaching threshold size can fragment, each fragment retaining representatives of all the component species, each fragment continuing as a fully functional garden. The coordination mechanism is probably chemical, signals moving through the mycelial network, but it might be electrical, fungal tissue capable of conducting signals, creating networks that function similarly to neural tissue without being neural.

They are critical decomposers for woody material, breaking down lignin and cellulose that even the moss-dwellers struggle with, reducing centuries-old wood to nutrients over decades. They have relationships with the walkers—walker droppings contain woody material partially processed by the walkers' grinding plates, and fungal gardens preferentially colonize these droppings, suggesting either the walkers are preparing food for the gardens or the gardens have evolved to exploit walker feeding behavior. The relationship might be mutualistic—gardens breaking down the droppings might release nutrients that benefit plants the walkers browse, creating a feedback loop.

They glow, some of them, producing bioluminescence so faint it's invisible to dark-adapted human eyes but measurable with instruments. The light is blue-green, pulsing slowly, possibly serving as communication between gardens, possibly attracting microscopic fauna that disperse spores, possibly just a metabolic byproduct with no function. The pool-dwellers avoid areas where glowing gardens are dense, suggesting the light serves as a mild deterrent, protecting the gardens from being overgrown.

They can survive being eaten—their mycelial network is so distributed that losing fruiting bodies barely affects the organism. The common singers occasionally feed on them, cropping the fruiting bodies, and the gardens respond by producing more fruiting bodies, suggesting herbivory might actually benefit them through stimulation of reproductive structures. Or the singers are farming them unconsciously, maintaining gardens through pruning, receiving food in return, another mutualism arising from simple interactions repeated over evolutionary time.

The **air plankton** are barely organisms—single-celled or simple-multicellular, drifting through the hollow's saturated air like oceanic plankton drift through water.

They are bacteria, archaea, microscopic fungi, protists, algae, things that don't fit any category, most of them unnamed. They float in the moisture droplets that constitute the air here, metabolizing dissolved organics, photosynthesizing using the various energy sources available (faint bioluminescence, pillar emissions, chemical gradients), reproducing through division or budding or spore formation. They are the base of the food web in some sense—they convert inorganic resources and waste products into biomass that the filter-feeders harvest, which the larger fauna then consume.

Some are parasites, infecting the singers, the walkers, the moss-dwellers, usually without causing obvious harm—the infections are ancient, the hosts adapted to them, the parasites providing services that blur the line between parasitism and mutualism. Some are decomposers, specializing in particular types of dead tissue, particular chemicals. Some are photoautotrophs, some chemoautotrophs, some heterotrophs, some combinations that don't fit standard categories. The diversity is extreme—a single breath contains thousands of species.

They form biofilms on every surface—invisible films just molecules thick, communities of microorganisms living in structured relationship. The biofilms on pillar surfaces differ from those on plants which differ from those on pool margins which differ from those on moss. Each biofilm is a microecosystem, species competing and cooperating, consuming and producing, creating localized chemistry that affects the substrate beneath. The pillars might be maintained partially by biofilms, microorganisms consuming corrosion products or building protective layers or consuming materials that would otherwise degrade the structure.

They are probably responsible for the air's smell—complex organic volatiles produced by metabolic processes, each species contributing characteristic compounds, the mixture creating the hollow's signature scent profile. The variations in smell across the hollow map to variations in microbial populations—more bacterial near the pools (earthy, mineral scents), more fungal near the fungal gardens (musty, sharp scents), more algal where the pillar-coral are dense (green, slightly sweet scents).

They evolve rapidly—generation times measured in hours or days, populations turning over constantly, mutations arising and spreading or disappearing based on immediate selective pressure. The air plankton of today are probably significantly different from those of a year ago, a decade ago, definitely different from those of a million years ago, yet the community structure is probably stable, the same niches filled by slightly different species, the functional ecology unchanging even as the participants change.

The **visitor fauna** are those creatures that pass through occasionally from outside—birds (rarely, as few can navigate darkness), insects (more commonly), other small vertebrates (occasionally), usually lost, sometimes intending to be here.

The birds that arrive are disoriented, calling anxiously, fluttering against barriers they can't see, exhausting themselves. Most leave quickly if they can find the way out. A few stay, dying quietly in the darkness, their bodies processed by moss-dwellers and fungi, contributing brief pulses of nutrients to the system. None are adapted to the hollow's conditions—they cannot echolocate effectively here, cannot feed on the available resources, cannot navigate the acoustic environment. Their presence is accidental, tragic, brief.

The insects are more successful. Certain moths find the hollow attractive, possibly drawn by scents from the flowering plants, possibly by the pillars' electromagnetic emissions. They navigate well in darkness, feed on nectar and pollen, serve as pollinators for plant species that might otherwise depend entirely on wind. They are uncommon but regular—you might encounter one every dozen visits, recognize them by wing-buzz frequency, by the way the singers' tones modulate in response to their presence. They are too small to threaten, too useful to repel, tolerated by the resident fauna, integrated into the ecology without being of it.

There are also soil invertebrates that drift in from outside—worms, beetles, centipedes, things that tunnel through substrate, that followed moisture gradients or chemical trails and ended up here. Most don't survive—the hollow's chemistry is different enough from surrounding areas that imported species struggle. But some adapt, their populations establishing in margin areas, creating transitional zones where outside ecology grades into hollow ecology. These margin populations might be sources of evolutionary novelty, genes entering the hollow's ecosystem from outside, maintaining connection to the broader biosphere.

Occasionally a mammal arrives—something small, a rodent or shrew, following insect scent or escaping a predator or just wandering. Mammals do poorly here. They are too visually dependent, too stressed by the darkness, too reliant on resources the hollow doesn't provide. They retreat quickly or die trying to find the exit. Their bodies when found show starvation usually, sometimes injuries from falling into pools or walking into structures, sometimes just stress-related failure. The singers investigate mammal bodies with what seems like curiosity, touching them with legs and filaments, producing unusual tone-patterns, possibly recognizing similarity—bilateral symmetry, four limbs, the architecture of vertebrate life—or possibly just interested in the novelty.

You are visitor fauna too, of course—a mammal, poorly adapted, requiring technological support (light sources, navigation tools, food from outside) to survive here. But you return, which makes you different from the lost birds and doomed rodents. You are visitor fauna that chose to visit, that accepted the hollow on its terms, that tried to understand rather than escape. The hollow tolerates this. The singers learn your sound, the walkers acknowledge your presence, the ecosystem absorbs your temporary addition to its mass and metabolism, then continues when you leave, unchanged by your having been here, ready for your return or indifferent to your absence, ancient and patient and complete.
