# The Inhabitants

No one *lives* here in the way the word is usually meant—no permanent resident maintains a nest or den, no creature calls this their exclusive territory. Instead, the Breath Engine is inhabited the way a lung is inhabited: by what passes through it, by what depends on its rhythm, by what has become so integrated into its function that removal would mean collapse.

## The Bats

The colony is the engine's purpose, though they would never know it.

They are *Myotis enigmaticus*—a designation that exists only in three unpublished field journals and one doctoral thesis that was never defended. Mouse-eared bats, technically, but changed by generations in this place. Smaller than their surface cousins by a third. Their fur has gone strange—not albino-white but a silvered gray that seems to catch and hold the dim electrical light, giving the roosting colony a pewter gleam. Their eyes are larger than they should be, adapted to function in luminal conditions that are neither true darkness nor anything approaching day.

They understand the chamber system as a three-dimensional map of air currents and acoustic reflections more detailed than any human visualization. Each individual bat holds in its neural structure a sonic map of every surface, every archway, every obstruction, updated nightly with the precision of echolocation. The colony's collective knowledge is vast—they know which pipes weep sweetest water, which fungi blooms attract the fattest moths, where the air flows fastest toward the exit shafts.

Their social structure has become eerily organized over generations. The ceiling is divided into districts that shift only marginally year to year. The western section, where the warmest pipes run, hosts the nursery colony—females heavy with pups, then nursing mothers, then the pups themselves learning to hang and groom. The central vault belongs to the mature males, a roosting zone that buzzes with low-frequency territorial calls. The eastern reaches, cooler and damper, are for the elderly and sick, a hospice zone where bats come to die, their bodies falling to feed the rats below.

They have a relationship with the place that goes beyond simple habitat use. The bats are the primary distributors of guano—the nitrogen-rich waste that feeds the plants, that creates the soil-like substrate for the fungi. Without them, the vegetation would eventually exhaust itself. But it's more specific than that: different bat species in the colony have different diets, different roosting preferences, and their guano is deposited in patterns that have shaped which plants grow where. The ferns beneath the nursery colony are lusher, fed by the richer waste of lactating females. The fungi under the male zones are different species—those that prefer waste from insectivorous diets heavy on beetle chitin.

Some of the older bats—and they can live surprisingly long here, protected from predators and weather—have behaviors that border on the inexplicable. There are individuals who roost always in the same spot, above specific machines, as if guarding them. Others make flights through the chamber during the rest period when the rest of the colony is sleeping, solo patrols checking the boundaries, visiting the water pools, hanging briefly in spots where no food or water exists, as if inspecting, as if maintaining.

When the colony departs for nightly feeding, they don't just leave—they spiral upward in a pattern, a vortex that seems to pull air with it, creating a pressure differential that affects the entire ventilation system. The exit is timed to the second by some collective decision-making process no one has ever documented. It begins with a few scouts, then a cascade, and within three minutes ten thousand bats are airborne and gone.

What they find outside, in the countryside above, is unclear. The ventilation shafts emerge in ruins and thickets and forgotten places. The bats disperse across miles of territory, feeding on the insects of the surface world, and return before dawn with bellies full of moths and midges and mosquitoes, bringing with them the energy that will be metabolized, converted to guano, and fed back into the system below.

They are not intelligent in any anthropomorphic sense, but the colony has a kind of emergent intelligence—a distributed cognition that manages the population, culls the weak, adjusts roosting density, times the breeding season to the availability of food. They are the central nervous system of this place, and they have optimized it without ever knowing they were doing so.

## The Rat Collective

If the bats are the nervous system, the rats are the maintenance crew, the demolition team, the recycling plant, and the distribution network all at once.

These are brown rats—*Rattus norvegicus*—but only in the way that wolves are dogs. The colony has been here long enough to have diverged noticeably. They're sleeker, longer-limbed, with oversized ears that swivel constantly, tracking sounds in the acoustic chaos. Their eyes have not gone pale like the moths or colorless like the salamanders; instead they've gone darker, larger, absorbing every photon of available light. In the dimness they move with absolute confidence, a surety of step that comes from knowing every centimeter of territory.

The population is perhaps two thousand individuals, organized into what can only be called neighborhoods. The coal wagon communities are the most established—multi-generational nests built in the rusted steel bodies, insulated with shredded moss and fungi, structured with genuine architectural logic. Some wagons house single large families, thirty or forty rats across three generations. Others are more communal, divided into chambers by scent-marked boundaries, with different families occupying different sections.

They have roads—actual maintained pathways through the vegetation, worn smooth by thousands of transits, marked at intersections with urine posts that convey information about traffic, food sources, and territorial boundaries. The main thoroughfares are wide enough for two rats to pass without slowing, and they do, constantly, moving with purposeful direction that suggests errands, tasks, destinations.

Their economy is complex. They are omnivores here, but specialists have emerged. Some rats focus on insect hunting, patrolling the root mat for beetles and roaches, dispatching them with quick bites and dragging them back to communal feeding sites. Others are fungus harvesters, gnawing shelf fungi from the beams and walls, storing pieces in caches for later consumption. Still others are scavengers of the highest order, specializing in finding and processing bat corpses, salamander eggs, or the occasional windfall of a large moth.

But strangest are the rats that tend the plants.

Not all of them, and not in any coordinated fashion that suggests training or culture, but there are individuals—always the same individuals, recognizable by their behaviors—who actively cultivate certain vegetation. They clear competing growth from around favored fruiting fungi. They drag nesting material to spots where it will decay and feed specific plants. They've been observed moving seeds—actual seed dispersal behavior—carrying the pods from one area and depositing them in prepared spots elsewhere.

Whether this is instinct refined by generations or genuine innovation is impossible to say, but the result is that certain patches of the engine's flora exist only because rats maintain them. There's a section of the eastern gallery where a particular species of pale flowering moss grows nowhere else, fed by a rat family that has incorporated its care into their territorial routine for reasons that might be as simple as "it smells good" or as complex as "our grandmother did this."

The rats are loud not in volume but in constant presence. Their communication is incessant—squeaks and chirps and ultrasonic calls that blend with the bat chorus, territorial disputes that escalate into chases but rarely actual violence, mothers calling pups, males advertising status. The sound of their movement is even more pervasive: the skitter of claws on metal, the rustle through vegetation, the splash when one swims across a water basin, the gnawing sound that never entirely stops as they process food, wear down their ever-growing teeth, and reshape their environment.

They have predators here—owls sometimes make it down the ventilation shafts, and the rats respond with alarm calls and a wholesale retreat into the deepest vegetation. But mostly they're apex in their weight class, bold enough to move freely, to investigate anything new, to approach the strange without much caution.

The rat collective has shaped this place as much as any other force. Their tunneling through the root mat has created drainage channels that prevent waterlogging. Their nesting has redistributed organic matter from the bat-heavy zones to the periphery. Their seed dispersal has created the current plant distribution. They are gardeners, engineers, and citizens of this underground city, and they have made it work for themselves with industrious brilliance.

## The Moth Generations

The moths are ghosts—literally colorless, nearly translucent, moving through the dim spaces like animate fog.

There are several species, but they've all converged on the same solution to lightlessness: abandon pigmentation entirely, grow larger to maintain body temperature in the cool underground air, and adopt a lifestyle that's almost entirely sedentary. The largest species—wingspan approaching twenty centimeters—are likely *Thysania agrippina* derivatives, white witch moths that found their way down decades ago and never left, adapting generation by rapid generation.

They don't so much fly as drift, their movements languid, almost aimless. But there's purpose beneath the apparent randomness: they're tracking chemical gradients, following scent plumes from the fungi and plants, seeking the pale flowers that bloom in the darkness and the sweet exudations from certain molds.

Their lifecycle is visible everywhere once you know to look for it. Eggs laid in careful clusters on the underside of fern fronds. Caterpillars—white, translucent—moving in slow processions along the vines, consuming vegetation with mechanical efficiency. Cocoons wrapped in silk and plant fiber, hanging from every available surface in the quiet zones where air movement is minimal. And adults emerging damp and crumpled, taking hours to pump fluid into their wings, then joining the slow aerial ballet.

The population explodes and crashes in cycles tied to the fungi fruiting, which is itself tied to the bat guano deposition, which cycles with the bat breeding season. But it's a gentle explosion, a slow crash—nothing as violent as surface boom-bust cycles. The moths simply become more or less common, filling more or less of the air with their silent flight.

They are prey—essential prey. The bats don't feed on them here (some ancient instinct keeps the colony from predating in their own roost), but the moths that drift too close to the ventilation shafts during exodus time are sometimes caught in the turbulence and consumed. More significantly, they're food for the salamanders in their larval stage, caterpillars falling into the water pools and being snapped up. The rats take adults occasionally, leaping to snatch them from the air in displays of surprising agility.

But the moths' real role is pollination. The pale flowers that bloom here—on vines, on certain fungi, on creeping plants that shouldn't flower at all without sunlight—are adapted for moth pollination. Long tubular structures, fragrant in ways that are unpleasant to humans (musty, fungal, with undertones of decay) but apparently irresistible to moths. The moths crawl inside, emerge dusted with pollen, move to the next flower, and complete a cycle that keeps the plant diversity stable.

Some of the moths cluster in groups—hundreds gathering on a single patch of wall or ceiling, overlapping like shingles, breathing collectively. These clusters are temporary, forming and dissolving over hours, but while they exist they create zones of moving texture, the wall itself seeming to breathe as hundreds of wings slowly open and close in unsynchronized rhythm.

The strangest behavior is their attraction-repulsion relationship with the electric lights. They circle the bulbs in the classic moth spiral, but then veer away before contact, as if they've learned or evolved a response to heat that's more sophisticated than the usual phototaxis. They orbit at a safe distance, dozens around each functioning bulb, creating a halo effect, a living lampshade of translucent wings that filters and diffuses the already dim light into something softer, more organic.

## The Salamander Elders

In the deepest pools, in the water that collects beneath the weeping pipes and in the old condensation basins, the salamanders exist in something close to geological time.

They are *Proteus anguinus*-adjacent—olm-like, cave salamanders that have lost all pigmentation and all eye function, grown pale as grubs and twice as long as they have any right to be. The largest are perhaps thirty centimeters, thick-bodied, moving with the slow confidence of creatures that have no natural predators and all the time in the world.

They are nearly invisible until they move—their skin matches the pale stone and fungal growth around the pools so perfectly that they disappear when still. But when they surface to breathe (they have lungs, unlike their fully aquatic relatives, an adaptation to the variable water quality here), they make a small splash, a ripple that spreads across the still surface, and for a moment you can see them: pale, eyeless, with external gills waving like small pink feathers around their heads.

They hunt by waiting. The pools are rich with invertebrates—aquatic isopods, the larval stages of various insects, occasionally a rat pup that falls from a nest above. The salamanders lie motionless on the pool bottom, which is not stone but centuries of accumulated sediment and organic matter, soft as mud. When vibration or chemical cue indicates prey is close enough, they lunge with shocking speed, jaws opening to create a vacuum that sucks the prey in rather than biting it.

Their metabolism is so slow that a single large meal—a drowned rat, a cluster of caterpillars—can sustain them for weeks. They don't need much, and the pools provide enough. They are survivors in the most fundamental sense, capable of enduring conditions that would kill surface salamanders: periods of low oxygen when the water stagnates, temperature fluctuations when the heating pipes cycle, even partial drying of their pools during dry periods, during which they burrow into the mud and enter a state of near-suspended animation.

They breed infrequently—perhaps every few years—and when they do, it's with a ritual. The males gather in one particular pool (always the same one, beneath the old boiler in the side chamber) and perform a slow undulating dance, releasing pheromones that cloud the water. Females arrive over the course of days, assess the males, and eventually pick up spermatophores from the pool bottom, fertilizing their eggs internally.

The eggs are laid in clear gelatinous strings wound around submerged roots and pipes, and the adults abandon them immediately. The larvae hatch weeks later, miniature versions of their parents, and begin the long slow process of growth that will take years to reach maturity. Most die—eaten by their own species, by rats that fish for them, by simple failure to thrive—but enough survive to keep the population stable at perhaps fifty adults across all the pools.

They make sounds—not often, but sometimes. A kind of clicking, produced in the throat, that seems to be territorial or possibly communicative. When multiple salamanders are in the same pool (usually only during breeding), the clicking creates rhythms, call and response, slow conversations in the dark water.

The salamanders are the deep memory of this place, the slowest-moving component, changing least over time. Individual salamanders might be twenty, thirty years old, having lived their entire existence in a single pool, knowing nothing of the surface, nothing of sunlight or seasons, only the eternal dim present of the water, the slow rain of organic matter from above, the rare disturbance of a rat drinking or a bat swooping too close to the surface.

## The Invertebrate Foundation

Beneath all the larger life, foundational and essential and overwhelming in sheer biomass, are the invertebrates—not a single community but dozens, hundreds of interlocking micro-ecosystems, each specialized, each essential.

The springtails are everywhere, billions of them, microscopic to nearly visible, processing the fallen plant matter and bat guano at the base level, converting it to soil, to usable nutrients. They cluster in patches so dense that the surface seems to shimmer with their movement, a crawling texture that resolves into individual jumping specks only when you look closely.

Millipedes as long as a human finger patrol the root mat in slow processions, armored in chitinous segments, consuming decaying wood from the old support beams and producing frass that feeds the plants. They're blind, navigating by chemical trail, following paths laid down by their predecessors in lines that sometimes circle the entire chamber in spirals that take days to complete.

Isopods—sowbugs, roly-polies—have grown large here, some the size of a human thumb, their segmented armor gone pale gray. They cluster under every piece of wood, every stone, processing the dead vegetation with tireless efficiency. When disturbed they curl into armored balls and drop, bouncing off the root mat, and within minutes they've uncurled and resumed feeding.

Beetles of every description: ground beetles hunting springtails, rove beetles patrolling the fungi, carrion beetles appearing within hours when a bat corpses falls, processing it to bones within days. The beetles are the cleanup crew, the rapid response team, and they're everywhere, clicking and rustling and going about their essential business.

The cockroaches are emperors—huge, slow-moving, ancient in their design. *Blaberus giganteus*-sized, perhaps descendants of escaped pets or lab colonies from decades past, but adapted now, integrated. They're detritivores on a massive scale, capable of consuming wood, fungi, carrion, guano, anything organic. They move across the walls with regal slowness, antennae waving, processing their environment chemically. They're unafraid, unconcerned, going about their business with the confidence of creatures that have no predators capable of handling their size and armor.

When they molt, leaving behind translucent shells perfect in every detail, the rats gather the shells and eat them—a source of calcium, chitin, essential minerals. The molting sites are regular, the same spots used repeatedly, and the floor beneath them is littered with shell fragments and the smaller invertebrates that gather to feed on the organic residue.

The spiders are few but significant—cave spiders with legs spanning five, six centimeters, weaving webs in the corners and across the smaller passages. Their webs catch moths, catch the smaller bats occasionally (which they can't consume but which die and fall, feeding the floor), catch everything that flies in the dim spaces. The spiders are pale, eyeless or nearly so, hunting by vibration, patient beyond measure.

In the water, in every pool and puddle, the aquatic invertebrates form their own kingdoms. Dragonfly nymphs—here in darkness, adapted to prey on salamander larvae and each other. Water beetles. Mosquito larvae by the thousands, wriggling at the surface, metamorphosing into adults that feed the bats in turn. Flatworms, leeches, things without names, all processing, all feeding, all dying and being consumed.

The invertebrates are the foundation, the substrate on which everything else depends. They turn death back into life, they process the energy that enters the system, they maintain the cycles. Remove them and the system collapses within weeks—the guano accumulates, the plant matter piles up, the pools become septic, the bats starve. But they're here, they've always been here, and they perform their functions with mindless perfection, each species in its niche, each filling its role in the great underground machine.

## The Fungi Network

The fungi are not fauna, but they're not quite flora either—they're something else, and in the Breath Engine they've achieved a presence that borders on sentience.

The mycelial networks are everywhere, invisible threads connecting every part of the system. They web through the root mat, linking plant to plant in chemical communication networks. They penetrate the wood of the old support beams, digesting it slowly, converting it to nutrients while somehow maintaining its structural integrity—the beams are more fungus than wood now, but they still hold the ceiling.

The fruiting bodies are the visible portion—shelf fungi, puffballs, the luminous species that glow in the deepest dark. But beneath them is a single interconnected network (or maybe several, boundaries unclear) that has infiltrated every organic surface. The mycelium is the internet of this place, carrying chemical signals about nutrient availability, about moisture, possibly about the movement of the larger creatures.

Some research (the unpublished kind, the field notes that end abruptly) suggests the fungi here have developed a relationship with the bats that goes beyond simple decomposition. Certain species fruit only when the guano deposition reaches specific concentrations, their fruiting timed to attract the maximum number of insects, which feed the bats, which produce more guano—a feedback loop of mutual benefit.

The luminous fungi—several species, glowing in blues and greens—are the strangest. Their bioluminescence cycles in patterns that change over days, waves of brightness moving through the colony in ways that don't correspond to any external trigger. They might be communicating, signaling to each other chemically while the light is merely a side effect. Or the light might be functional, attracting insects to spread spores. Or it might be something else entirely, some purely aesthetic expression of fungal metabolism.

They grow in specific patterns—rings, spirals, grid-like arrangements on the walls that suggest either response to environmental geometry or some internal organizing principle. The patterns are too regular to be random, not regular enough to be obviously purposeful, and they shift over months as fruiting bodies die and new ones emerge in slightly different positions.

The fungi are the digestive system, the communication network, and possibly the decision-making substrate of the Breath Engine. If the place has consciousness—and sometimes it feels like it might—it would be distributed through the mycelial network, a vast slow intelligence processing information at chemical speed, adjusting growth patterns, guiding the system toward equilibrium.

## The Visitors

Rarely, very rarely, other things enter this place.

Owls sometimes follow the bats down the ventilation shafts, seeking easy prey in the confined space. They learn quickly that it's a mistake—the acoustic chaos disorients them, the confined spaces limit their flight advantage, and the bats mob them viciously, driving them back toward the surface with sonic assault. But occasionally one stays, trapped or injured, and the rats find it within days, process it to bones within weeks.

Snakes have been found—preserved in the dry sections, desiccated—suggesting they sometimes enter seeking the rats or salamanders and become lost, unable to find the exit, dying slowly in the maze. They don't establish populations; the environment is too strange, too confined.

Insects from the surface drift down constantly—moths that aren't the pale local species, beetles that don't belong, flies and wasps and bees that stumble into the ventilation shafts and emerge into the dim gallery. Most die quickly, unable to navigate, unable to find food. Some are eaten. A very few integrate, joining the local invertebrate communities if their niche is unfilled.

Plants sometimes send roots down from above—tree roots following moisture through cracks in the stone, penetrating into the upper regions of the chamber. They never last. Something about the air composition, or the fungi, or perhaps active sabotage by the established vegetation, causes them to die back, leaving dried remnants that hang from the ceiling like wooden stalactites.

And once, according to one of the field journals, humans.

There are signs—initials scratched into brick, a glass bottle from the 1950s, a leather shoe so desiccated it's nearly petrified. Maintenance workers, explorers, or something else, people who entered when the system was more accessible, when the ventilation shafts had ladders, when the passages hadn't been consumed by vegetation.

They left, or they didn't. There are no skeletal remains visible, but in a place where organic matter is consumed this efficiently, absence proves nothing. If someone died here, the rats and beetles would have reduced them to scattered bones within weeks, and the bones themselves would eventually be dragged away, gnawed for calcium, incorporated into nests.

The place doesn't prevent entry, but it doesn't encourage it either. The ventilation shafts are climbable, theoretically, but they're long and dark and slick with moisture and growth. The old access tunnels are mostly collapsed. The electrical conduits might still connect to the surface, but they're too narrow for human passage.

Mostly, the Breath Engine remains unvisited, alone, cycling through its rhythms with only its own inhabitants to mark the time. It is a place that has closed itself off from the world, that has achieved a stability that requires nothing from outside except the water that seeps down, the electricity that somehow still flows, and the bats that travel nightly to bring back the energy of the surface world.

It breathes in, it breathes out, and what lives there has learned to breathe with it.
