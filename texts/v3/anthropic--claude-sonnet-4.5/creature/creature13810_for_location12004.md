# What Lives There

## The Slurry-Swimmers

Most of what lives in the Breathing Plain doesn't surface. It doesn't need to.

They move through the saturated substrate like thoughts moving through a mind too dense to have clear distinctions between ideas. You see the wake before you understand there's something causing it—a raised line traveling across the breathing ground, matter displaced upward and to the sides, collapsing back sluggishly. The creature responsible might be the width of your forearm or it might be something vast, sinuous, kilometers long, and what you're seeing is just a segment, one coil of a body that threads through the entire compressed mass of this section of the plain.

When they do surface—rarely, and seemingly without purpose—you see sections of body that don't resolve into wholeness. A segment that's too smooth, grey-brown slick with the substrate's moisture, featureless except for a textured pattern that might be scales or might be the result of pressure from moving through dense matter. No eyes visible, no mouth, no obvious direction of travel. It breaks the surface, exists there for a moment—ten seconds, thirty—then submerges again with a wet sound that's immediately swallowed by the plain's constant roar.

Some are segmented like enormous worms, but the segments don't quite match—one section ridged, the next smooth, a third bearing protrusions that might be vestigial limbs or might be sensory organs or might be tumorous growths that couldn't be selected against because there's no predation pressure. They look like evolutionary compromises that were never forced to resolve.

Others seem to have given up on consistent form entirely. You see something that's more cavity than body—a hollow tube of flexible tissue moving through the ground by collapsing and expanding, sucking substrate through itself, extracting whatever nutrients exist in the chemical soup, expelling compressed waste behind it. It's less a creature than a mobile digestive tract, all function and no structure beyond what function requires.

They don't interact with each other as far as you can tell. The plain is vast enough that encounters must be rare even for creatures that might span kilometers. When two wakes do cross, there's no deviation, no acknowledgment—just two disturbances passing through the same space at different depths, indifferent to each other's presence.

They've been here longer than the artifacts. Their ancestors were swimming through stone when it was still soft from the planet's formation. They've adapted to the compression, the density, the gradual transition from geology to this saturated matrix-state. They've simplified along with the landscape—lost eyes in the permanent dimness of the substrate, lost specialized organs in favor of general permeability, lost the distinction between swimming and burrowing because the medium doesn't admit the difference.

Some of them might be colonial organisms, thousands of small units operating as one distributed body. You'd never know—the substrate hides everything, reveals nothing except the wake.

## The Surface Crawlers

What moves on top of the breathing ground does so with the exhaustion of refugees who've arrived somewhere and can't go any further.

They're arthropodal, mostly—jointed limbs, segmented bodies, but built on plans that seem provisional, unfinished. A creature the size of a small dog with twelve legs, but only eight of them functional, the other four dragging uselessly or held curled against the carapace. Another with legs of different lengths, listing to one side as it hauls itself across the wet surface, leaving a track like a drunk signature. A third that seems to be mostly shell, the actual body a soft, minimal thing barely visible in the gaps between armored plates, the shell doing most of the work of locomotion by levering against the ground.

They move slowly, always slowly. The thick air resists them, or they resist the thick air—it's hard to tell which. Each step is deliberate, mechanical, as if walking requires conscious decision rather than instinct. They pause frequently, sometimes for minutes at a time, motionless except for the ground breathing beneath them, then resume with no visible stimulus.

Their carapaces are universally dark, slick with moisture—the air is too saturated for anything to dry. Some have textures: pitting, ridging, barnacle-like encrustations that might be parasites or might be the creature's own tissue responding to this environment's wrongness. Others are smooth, almost polished, reflecting the dim light in streaks and highlights that move as they move.

Feeding happens but you rarely see it. Sometimes one is crouched over a mat, mandibles or proboscis or some less identifiable feeding apparatus engaged with the bacterial-fungal-moss tissue. The mat doesn't react—it has no nervous system to react with—just gradually depletes in that location, a bare patch spreading as the crawler consumes. More often you find them motionless near plant-things, positioned as if waiting, though for what isn't clear. Do they drink condensation from the stems? Feed on something the plant exudes? Simply rest in the slightly-different microclimate?

Some carry shells that aren't theirs, fragments of larger creatures' armor, broken and repurposed as mobile shelter. They've wedged themselves partially inside these borrowed housings, their own bodies too soft or too vulnerable for the plain's openness. The shells scrape against the ground as they move, adding a small grinding sound to the world's roar.

Others have built shells from substrate—compacted matter glued together with secretions, lumpy and irregular but functional. These constructed shells bear the marks of long use: repairs, additions, erosion where moisture has gotten between the layers. The creatures emerge from them partially to move, dragging the shells along, then retract completely when they stop, becoming indistinguishable from stones.

They don't seem to have territories. You see them wherever the ground happens to be in that moment, scattered at the same statistical density as the plant-things—one every few hundred meters in any direction you look, more or less, with clusters and gaps that suggest randomness rather than choice.

Occasionally two meet. They touch antennae or chemoreceptors, exchange some information in a language of molecules and vibration, then separate and continue in their original directions. No aggression, no mating behavior, no social structure. Just acknowledgment that another thing exists, then return to the private struggle of moving through this dense, wet world.

Very rarely—once in long observation—you see one die. It simply stops, mid-stride or mid-rest, and doesn't move again. The body remains for a time, slowly settling into the breathing ground, then one day the ground's motion has taken it, pulled it partially under, and the substrate's chemistry is dissolving it, reclaiming the organized matter. Within weeks there's no trace except a slightly darker patch where nutrients are concentrating, and even that disperses as the ground breathes and the water migrates.

They reproduce somehow—there are young, smaller versions moving with the same exhausted deliberateness—but you never see eggs or larvae or any vulnerable stage. Perhaps they birth live young already encased in shell. Perhaps they lay eggs deep in the substrate where the slurry-swimmers can't reach them. Perhaps they reproduce so rarely that witnessing it would require more patience than observation permits.

They are adapted to this, native to it, but they don't seem to thrive. They persist. That's all. Generation after generation moving slowly across the breathing plain, feeding minimally, reproducing rarely, dying incrementally, locked in equilibrium with a landscape that offers just enough to prevent extinction but not enough to encourage abundance.

## The Colony Makers

These are different.

When you see an occupied plant-thing—the lumpy additions of mud and saliva and compressed fiber, the chambers and passages that transform a simple stem into an architecture—you're seeing something that hasn't given up. Something that still remembers, or never forgot, that purpose exists.

The creatures themselves are rarely visible. They're small, insect-like but not insects—six-limbed, low-bodied, with manipulatory appendages that seem too dexterous for something without obvious eyes. When one does emerge—to gather material from the mat, to make adjustments to the structure's exterior, to interact with some element of the plant-thing itself—it moves with precision that the crawlers lack. This isn't exhausted persistence. This is intention.

They're pale, almost colorless, as if they've spent so long inside their structures that pigmentation has become unnecessary. Their carapaces are thinner than the crawlers', more flexible, allowing them to squeeze through the narrow passages of their own architecture. Some have bioluminescence—faint, greenish, visible only when they're deep in the structure's interior and you're looking at the right angle. The glow serves some purpose: communication, navigation, cultivation of something that grows better in light.

The colonies vary in size. Some occupy structures barely larger than the host plant-thing—a swelling around the base, a few chambered additions to the stem. Others have expanded until the plant is barely visible, encased in a construction twice its height and three times its width, a entire complex of galleries and storage chambers and something that might be nurseries or might be cultivation spaces for the mat-fungus they harvest.

Inside—and you only know this from abandoned structures, from colonies that have died or moved on—the architecture is intricate. Not sophisticated in any technological sense, but carefully organized. Chambers of different sizes connected by passages that curve and branch in ways that suggest traffic flow, resource distribution, social hierarchy. Some chambers have been lined with secretions that harden into smooth surfaces. Others are left rough, the compressed substrate visible. Still others have been cultivated—the walls thick with mat-growth, creating surfaces that are more alive than structural.

The sounds from active colonies are distinct from the plain's general roar. That rhythmic thumping—is it construction? Communication? Something being processed in repetitive cycles? The scraping might be mandibles on substrate, building or excavating. The steam venting suggests they're generating heat somehow, possibly through metabolic density—hundreds or thousands of individuals in close proximity, their combined body heat needing release.

You never see them en masse. Even when a colony is clearly active—steam rising, sounds emerging, fresh material being added to the structure's exterior—only one or two individuals are visible at a time. The rest remain inside, performing whatever functions the colony requires. Are there specialists—builders, gatherers, cultivators, reproducers? Or is each individual capable of all functions, and the architecture is just what emerges from hundreds of creatures making local decisions that somehow integrate into coherent structure?

They seem to prefer certain plant-things, though the criteria isn't obvious. Not the largest, not the healthiest, not positioned in any special location. Sometimes a plant-thing stands alone and ignored while its neighbor fifty meters away hosts a thriving colony. Perhaps it's chemical—the plants produce varying secretions and some are more compatible with colonization. Perhaps it's historical—the colonies inherit preferences from previous generations, continuing to occupy the same plant-lineages across time. Perhaps it's random and the appearance of preference is false pattern recognition.

They do abandon colonies. You find empty structures frequently—the mud-and-fiber construction beginning to dissolve in the moisture, the passages empty, the plant-thing recovering or dying beneath its burden of vacated architecture. Why the evacuation? Resource depletion seems unlikely—the mat is everywhere, the water inexhaustible, the chemical soup of the ground constantly replenished. Disease? Structural failure? Simple wanderlust, an instinct to move periodically whether necessary or not?

Sometimes the abandoned structures have been emptied completely—no bodies, no debris, no indication of catastrophe. Just a clean vacancy, as if the entire colony decamped in an organized migration. Other times there are corpses—dozens of small, desiccated bodies that somehow dried despite the saturated air, piled in chambers or scattered in passages, the colony dead but not departed.

And very rarely, you find colonies that have built beyond any functional requirement. Structures that rise three times the height of the plant-thing, with chambers that extend down into the substrate, creating entire underground complexes. These massive constructions seem to serve no additional purpose—the colony occupying them is no larger than those in modest structures. But they've built anyway, added chamber after chamber, extended passages into elaborate labyrinths, as if size itself has become the goal.

These super-colonies never last. You find them abandoned more often than active, the excessive architecture collapsing under its own weight or being pulled down by the ground's breathing. As if there's a maximum sustainable scale and these colonies tried to exceed it, succeeded briefly, then failed catastrophically.

The colony-makers are the only fauna that seem to acknowledge the artifacts. Not in any reverent way—they're not building cairns or leaving offerings—but pragmatically. Sometimes a colony has incorporated artifact-stones into its structure, using the ancient worked pieces as foundation elements or wall components. Sometimes they've built adjacent to a platform, using it as a base for expansion. They treat the artifacts as just more material, more substrate to build with, no different from the mud and fiber except more durable.

This suggests intelligence, or at least flexibility—the ability to recognize and utilize unusual resources. Or it suggests the artifacts are so old and so integrated into the landscape that they've become just another geological feature, no more special than stones.

## The Ambiguous Ones

Then there are presences that might be creatures or might be phenomena, and the plain's wrongness makes the distinction uncertain.

Movements in the mist that could be wind-patterns or could be something navigating through the dense air. Shapes that resolve and dissolve too quickly to categorize—eight-limbed, many-limbed, limbless, changing configuration as if form is optional. They appear most often near the geometry-anomalies, the places where mist makes cubes or lines, as if they're either drawn to wrongness or are themselves the source of it.

Once, something that might have been a swarm—dozens or hundreds of small flying things, except they weren't flying so much as existing in sequence, appearing in one location then appearing in another without traversing the space between. The swarm made a sound like crystal ringing, high and clear, audible above the plain's roar for the few minutes it existed before dissipating or departing or ceasing.

The light-at-the-bottom-of-pools might be biological. Might be organisms that have adapted to living submerged in the temporary ponds, generating luminescence for purposes unknown. Or might be something else, something that uses water as a medium to manifest in, peering up through the liquid at the dim sky.

Occasionally the substrate-surface develops what look like tracks—patterns pressed into the breathing ground that suggest something massive moved across it. But the tracks are wrong. They appear fully-formed rather than accumulating from repeated passage. They lead nowhere, starting and stopping arbitrarily. They're too regular, too geometric—perfect circles or exact spirals or straight lines that run for hundreds of meters. Sometimes they're raised rather than depressed, as if something moved across the surface by adding material rather than displacing it.

These could be geological—pressure variations from the ground's breathing, water migration creating patterns, chemical processes that happen to form regular shapes. Or they could be the marks of creatures too large to see as creatures, bodies that span kilometers and move so slowly that their passage seems like terrain formation. Or they could be something experiencing the plain through different dimensions, and what you're seeing are cross-sections, the places where they intersect with this reality's three-dimensional space.

There are sounds without sources. Not just the general roar but specific sounds—a whistling that stays in one location but has no hole or passage to generate it, a rhythmic pounding that comes from underground but isn't regular enough to be geological, voices that are definitely not voices because nothing here has vocal cords but sound voice-like anyway, words almost forming in the roar before dissolving back into noise.

Some crawlers have passengers—smaller creatures riding on their shells, positioned carefully to avoid the legs' movement. The passengers are motionless, might be dead, might be symbionts or parasites, might be a different life-stage of the crawler itself. They're never alone—when one crawler has passengers, it has dozens, clustered across the carapace like barnacles. Other crawlers are completely passenger-free. It might be a seasonal thing, except seasons don't exist here. It might be infection, except the infested crawlers move no differently than the clean ones.

And sometimes—more often as wrongness accumulates—there are things that exist only partially. A leg without a body, moving across the ground with full articulation. A shell that's empty but behaves as if occupied, responding to obstacles, changing direction. Antennae waving from substrate that has no crawler attached. These partial presences don't act injured or incomplete. They function as if the rest of their body is simply elsewhere, in some adjacent reality, and only this portion has manifested here.

The fauna might be increasing in strangeness, or observation might be failing to maintain category boundaries. After long enough in the plain, you start to question whether "creature" means anything when forms are this provisional, when presence is this ambiguous, when the distinction between living and non-living has been eroded by the same forces that eroded the mountains.

## The Ecosystem That Isn't

Nothing eats anything else with the clarity of predation.

The slurry-swimmers filter-feed, extracting nutrients from the substrate they move through. The crawlers graze on mats. The colony-makers cultivate and harvest. But there's no predator-prey relationship, no population cycling, no trophic cascade. Energy enters the system from the ground's warmth, from chemical gradients in the substrate, from the dim light that some things might still be able to utilize. It circulates in ways too diffuse to map, then dissipates into the general entropy.

The dead are recycled but not through scavenging—through chemistry, through the substrate's dissolution of complex molecules back into simple ones, through the mat-growth that spreads across abandoned bodies and breaks them down into usable components.

Competition doesn't exist because resources aren't scarce. The plain is vast, the water endless, the chemical soup perpetually replenished from below. There's no territory worth defending, no mate worth fighting over, no survival pressure that would generate conflict.

So the fauna exists in a state of distributed negligence, each organism pursuing its minimal requirements with minimal interaction. They've achieved equilibrium not through dynamic balance but through mutual irrelevance.

This should produce evolutionary stagnation—without selection pressure, forms should freeze or drift randomly. But the wrongness is a pressure of its own kind. The physical laws' approximateness, the reality-fraying that's accelerating, the ground's breathing and the water's impossible behaviors and the light's sourceless directions—these might be selecting for adaptability-to-wrongness, producing creatures that can function when causation becomes negotiable.

The partial presences might be the leading edge of this adaptation—organisms that have learned to exist discontinuously, spreading their being across multiple states or locations or dimensions because the plain's wrongness has made that possible or necessary.

Or it's all dying, slowly, the final fauna of a terminal landscape, each generation slightly less viable than the last, locked in a millennial decline that ends when the last slurry-swimmer stops moving and the last crawler ceases and the last colony abandons its structure and the plain breathes on, lifeless, perfect in its flatness, worn down past the point where even biology can assert itself.

## The Watchers

There might be intelligence.

Not in any individual creature—they're too simple, too mechanical in their behaviors, too locked in minimal survival patterns. But in aggregate, or in emergence, or in ways that don't map to consciousness as recognizable feature.

The colony-makers come closest. Their architectures are too intricate to be purely instinctive, their material choices too pragmatic, their occasional use of artifacts too adaptive. They might have something—not language, not self-awareness, but perhaps a kind of collective problem-solving, a distributed intelligence that exists in the colony rather than any individual.

But there are also times when it seems like the plain itself is watching.

You feel it in the way wrongnesses appear—not randomly but in response to observation, as if being witnessed generates them. In the way patterns emerge in the mist only when you're looking for patterns. In the way the sound's occasional harmonics seem to answer questions you haven't asked.

Perhaps consciousness isn't a feature possessed by organisms here. Perhaps it's environmental, a property of the dense, saturated, event-filled substrate itself. The plain is alive not biologically but informationally—so packed with happenings, with interactions, with chemical exchanges and pressure variations and thermal fluxes that awareness emerges from pure complexity.

The artifacts might be evidence of previous watchers—beings that achieved enough intelligence to build, to shape stone, to create platforms and rings and structures, then either departed or evolved beyond need for artifacts or died so completely that even their bones have been dissolved into the substrate.

Or the artifacts were never made by fauna at all. They're the plain's own creations, moments when it achieved enough self-organization to express intention, to place stones in rings, to create platforms, before subsiding back into its roaring, breathing, unconscious state.

The colony-makers building super-structures might be responding to this intermittent planetary consciousness—receiving instructions or inspiration or patterns from the plain itself, building according to plans that aren't their own, then abandoning the results when the communication ceases.

Or there's no intelligence anywhere. The appearance of watching is false—consciousness desperately seeking itself in a landscape that's purely mechanical, purely chemical, purely physical despite its wrongnesses. The plain seems aware only because its complexity mimics awareness's patterns, the way turbulent fluid mimics intentional flow.

But still. Sometimes. In certain lights (that come from nowhere). In certain sounds (that resolve briefly into almost-language). In certain moments when the ground breathes in a rhythm that feels like response rather than mechanism. It seems like something is paying attention.

Not benevolent. Not malevolent. Not interested in any personal sense. Just aware, vast, ancient, eroded down to near-nothing but not quite nothing, watching with whatever remains after everything else has been worn away.

## The Continuance

They persist, all of them.

The slurry-swimmers thread through the dense substrate, generation after generation, changing so slowly that change is invisible, simple and eternal and sufficient.

The crawlers haul themselves across the breathing surface, exhausted but never stopping, minimal but never extinct, scattered across the vast flatness in perpetual sparse distribution.

The colony-makers build and abandon, occupy and evacuate, generation after generation maintaining their mysterious threshold between simple and complex, between instinct and something more.

The ambiguous ones flicker in and out of definite existence, more frequent as wrongness deepens, suggesting futures where solid presence becomes optional, where the fauna adapt to reality's dissolution by dissolving themselves.

And the plain breathes beneath them all, warm and wet and dense with events, roaring its constant multilayered thunder, moving through rhythms that might be mechanical or might be choice, hosting this spare, strange, terminal fauna in its terminal landscape.

They don't thrive. They don't expand. They don't evolve toward complexity or intelligence or any recognizable goal.

They simply continue, generation after generation, adapted perfectly to a place that's wearing down past the point where adaptation should be possible, native to wrongness, at home in an ending that never quite ends.

And they might be the last fauna that will ever exist here—the final organisms before the plain completes its erosion into lifelessness.

Or they might be the early forms of whatever comes after biology, after chemistry, after physics, when the wearing-down has gone so far that new categories of existence become necessary.

They move. They feed. They build occasionally. They die incrementally.

And the plain accepts them all back, eventually, pulling their organized matter into its dense substrate, redistributing their components through its breathing, making them part of itself until the distinction between fauna and landscape dissolves completely, as all distinctions here eventually dissolve, given enough time, in this place that has nothing but time, this terminal flat infinity where even ending takes forever.
