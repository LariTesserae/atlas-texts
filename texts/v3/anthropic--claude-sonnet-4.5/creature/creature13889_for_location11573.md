# The Warrenfolk

They are a people made compact by stone, their bodies and their gestures shaped by generations of living inside the carved dark. Not stunted—that would imply something taken away—but refined toward efficiency, every proportion calibrated to a life of ducking through passages and reading stone by touch and making do with less light than the sun-dwellers think necessary.

## Bodies Shaped by Stone

They are slightly shorter than the peoples of the open lands, though the difference is only a matter of inches—enough that a tall warrener might reach five and a half feet, while most settle comfortably around five foot two or three. But it's not the height that marks them. It's the way they're built: compact through the torso, with broad shoulders that taper to narrow hips. Their arms are long relative to their height, developed from childhood spent pulling themselves up carved stairs and bracing against passage walls for balance on sloping floors.

Their hands are their most distinctive feature. Broad palms, fingers slightly shorter than surface-dwellers but notably stronger, with pronounced calluses across the pads and the sides. The fingertips are blunt and highly sensitive—they read stone the way others read faces, detecting temperature shifts that indicate a water channel behind a wall, feeling the grain that tells them where stone will split cleanly or crumble. Their nails are kept very short, filed smooth against stone. Long nails would break in the first hour of passage travel or carving work.

The children's hands are softer, but even at five or six years old, a warrener child has calluses forming, hands already learning stone's language.

Their feet are similarly adapted. Broad, with pronounced arches and splayed toes that grip carved stairs instinctively. They go barefoot in the warrens more than not—the stone is cool but not cold, and bare feet give better purchase on slopes and stairs. Their soles are thick with callus, able to walk across the faceted, chisel-marked floors without discomfort. When they do wear foot coverings, they're soft leather, thin enough to maintain feel for the surface beneath.

Eyes are their compromise. Larger than average, with pupils that dilate wide in the limited light. The irises tend toward lighter colors—gray, pale brown, occasionally a washed-out blue—as if the darkness has bleached them. They see well in low light, their eyes adapted to making the most of lamp glow and the shafts of daylight that portion out through the settlement. But in bright sun, on the surface, they squint and shade their faces. Children sometimes cry the first few times they're brought into full daylight; it's overwhelming, painful even.

There's a particular way they hold their heads, slightly tilted, listening. The warrens teach you to navigate by sound as much as sight—the echo of your footsteps telling you if a chamber is near, the quality of air movement indicating a shaft ahead. They listen constantly, unconsciously, building a sound-map of the space around them.

## Movement and Presence

They move differently than surface peoples. More deliberately, with an economy of motion that comes from living in spaces where a careless gesture might crack your knuckles against stone. There's a gliding quality to their walk, a smoothness—they've learned to move without bouncing, keeping their heads level even when navigating irregular floors.

In narrow passages, they turn sideways automatically, sliding through gaps without thinking about it. They know exactly how wide their shoulders are, can judge instantly whether a space will admit them. And they're flexible—able to squeeze through openings that look too small, contorting with a practiced efficiency.

They're good at moving in darkness. Not blind navigation—they're not cave-adapted creatures—but comfortable movement in very low light, using memory and touch and sound. A warrener returning home at night doesn't need a lamp for the familiar passages. Their feet know the path; their hands trail the walls reading the landmarks: this groove, this patch of smooth stone, this place where two passages meet at a particular angle.

They're quiet. Not from intention but from habit. Loud noise in the warrens is uncomfortable—something about the acoustics, or maybe just the density of habitation making everyone conscious of being overheard. Children are taught early to modulate their voices, to walk softly. A tantrum in the warrens is not just annoying but transgressive; it violates the acoustic peace that makes the closeness bearable.

But they're not silent. They hum while working, sing soft songs to themselves, talk continuously in low voices when in company. The quietness is about volume, not absence of sound. The warrens murmur with human presence.

## Senses and Perception

Their sense of touch is highly developed, especially in the hands and feet. They read texture constantly—the difference between limestone that will carve cleanly and limestone that will fracture, between dry stone and stone that's damp, between old work and new. They can tell, touching a wall in darkness, roughly when it was carved. Old stone feels different: smoother from the oil of countless hands, the edges rounded, the tool marks softened.

They're sensitive to air movement. A slight current means a shaft or passage nearby. Still air means you're in a dead end or a chamber with only one entrance. The direction of air flow tells them something about the warren's geometry, about where ventilation shafts draw from and exhale to.

Temperature differences matter. Cool air sinking from a light shaft. Warmer air where bodies have been working. The particularly cold touch of stone that has water running just behind it. They dress in layers because the temperature varies—not dramatically, but enough that you want to add or remove a layer as you move from a deep, cool chamber to a shallow one warmed by afternoon sun filtering through.

Their sense of smell is acute, tuned to the warren's particular palette. They can smell damp stone versus dry, the clay scent of cistern water versus the mineral tang of spring water, the difference between lamp smoke from animal fat and lamp smoke from seed oil. They smell people too—not unpleasantly, but recognizably. In darkness, you might know who's approaching by their particular combination of wool and leather and lamp smoke and self.

Taste is less emphasized. The food here is simple, often bland—grain porridge, root vegetables, dried meat from trade, occasional fresh greens from the surface gardens. Seasoning exists but sparingly. They drink a lot of water, plain, and seem content with it.

## The Life Cycle

Children are born in the warrens, in chambers where the mothers labor attended by other women who've done this before. Birth is matter-of-fact—not hidden or mysterious, but not public either. A private thing among women, in spaces made temporarily sacred by the enormity of what's happening.

Infants are carried constantly. Strapped to mothers' bodies in slings of soft leather and wool, they move through the passages experiencing the rhythm of warren life from the beginning. They sleep against the sound of stone being carved, voices murmuring, water dripping. The warrens are their first world; the surface comes later.

As they grow, children are everywhere underfoot, learning navigation through play. They chase each other through passages, hide in niches, develop games that use the three-dimensional space. Adults tolerate this patiently; children need to learn the warrens, and play is how they do it.

By five or six, children are contributing small labor: carrying messages, holding lamps for working adults, gathering stone chips for disposal. By ten, they're learning craft—sitting beside a parent or other relative, trying their hand at the loom or the carving or the pottery wheel. Not all children follow their parents' work, but most start there.

Adolescence is when the serious carving begins for those who'll become excavators. The physical strength is developing; the stone-reading skills are ready. They work alongside experienced carvers, learning where to strike, how to read a fracture line, when to stop before you go too thin.

Courtship happens in the warrens but also on the surface. Young people make excuses to go above—to work the gardens, to check the light shafts—and there, in the relative privacy of the brush and grass, they court. Some families arrange marriages; others let young people choose. Either way, partnership is serious here. You'll be living close to your spouse, probably in a chamber you carve together or inherit, in a settlement where everyone hears everything. Compatibility matters.

Adults work. That's the center of adult life: the work of maintaining, expanding, provisioning. The work is varied enough that most people learn several things—some carving, some craft, some surface work. Specialization exists but isn't rigid.

Old age comes gradually. The work shifts—less physical carving, more fine craft, more teaching. The elderly are respected for their knowledge of stone and custom. They remember when certain passages were carved, why chambers are arranged as they are. They teach children, tend the small shrines, help settle disputes.

Death is handled simply. Bodies are taken to the deepest chambers, the burial rooms, and laid in niches carved for the purpose. Sometimes the niche is carved by the person before death; sometimes family does it after. The body is wrapped in cloth, placed inside, and the niche is sealed with a thin stone slab. Often the slab has the person's name carved into it, and their family mark. Sometimes just a handprint, pressed into wet clay that's been plastered over the slab.

The burial chambers are quiet, deep places. People visit them sometimes, to remember, to speak to the dead. But they're not morbid spaces. They're just another part of the warrens, where the dead continue their residence in darkness.

## Knowledge and Skill

The deepest knowledge here is stone-knowledge. The good excavators know limestone intimately—know it better than they know people, maybe. They understand its formation, its grain, its moods. They know that stone carved in damp weather chips differently than stone carved dry. They know which bands of the limestone are reliable and which are treacherous with hidden flaws.

This knowledge is transmitted through apprenticeship. You learn by watching, then doing under supervision, then doing alone while someone checks your work. Eventually you're trusted to choose where to carve, and that's when you've truly learned.

Tool-making is crucial knowledge. How to shape a bronze chisel so it holds an edge but doesn't chip. How to haft an antler pick so the angle is right for striking upward at a ceiling. How to maintain tools—sharpening, cleaning, storing them properly so the metal doesn't corrode in the damp.

Water-knowledge is similarly essential. Where water collects, where it flows, how to channel it, how to recognize when a passage is at risk of flooding. The good water-readers can predict when a chamber will start seeping based on the season and the recent rain. They maintain the cisterns, manage the spring, keep the drainage channels clear.

Craft knowledge varies. Weaving—the setup of the loom, the patterns possible with two colors or three, how tight to beat the weft. Pottery—the right consistency for clay, how long to let pieces dry in this humidity, which forms are strongest. Leather working, woodworking, all the small skills needed to maintain a material culture with limited resources.

There's also deep knowledge of the warrens themselves. The old people know the layout like a language they were born speaking. They can direct you: "Take the left passage, then down the spiral stairs, then through the chamber with the crystal wall, then right where the passage forks three ways—take the middle fork—and you'll find her." And they're right. The warrens are legible to them.

Some people specialize in medicine. They know which herbs from the surface treat which ailments, how to set a broken bone—common injuries from carving accidents or passage falls—how to draw infection from a wound. They know the traditional treatments: certain clay poultices, moss for packing wounds, steam inhalation for chest sickness.

There's little written knowledge. Some people mark stone—simple tallies for trade goods, family marks to indicate ownership. But there's no developed writing system. Knowledge lives in people and practice, not in texts.

## Social Architecture

The social structure here is built on family but extended beyond it. A household might be parents and children, or might include grandparents, or might be a partnership of siblings, or several related families sharing a large chamber and a network of smaller ones. The boundaries are permeable.

Family lines matter for inheritance—chambers and tools pass down—but they're not rigid hierarchies. A talented carver from a poor family can earn respect and resources. A person from an old family who's lazy or incompetent loses status.

There's a loose sense of district identity. People from the eastern quarter think of themselves as the original warreners, carrying the oldest traditions. People from the western quarter are the innovators, less bound by custom. The northern district has its peculiar pride in the crystal-lined chambers. The southern district is scrappier, hungrier, pushing into new stone.

Decisions happen through discussion. When something affects the whole settlement—should we open this new ventilation shaft, should we trade this much cloth for this much grain—people gather in one of the large communal chambers and talk. Not everyone comes; not everyone speaks. But enough people to represent the range of perspectives. They talk until consensus emerges, or at least until no one objects strongly enough to block the decision.

Disputes are handled similarly. If two families are in conflict—say, about whether one is digging too close to the other's chamber—elders mediate. They examine the situation, hear both sides, render an opinion. The opinion is usually followed, not because elders have enforcement power but because defying consensus makes you an outsider, and being an outsider in this density is unbearable.

There are specialists—the particularly skilled carvers, the medicine-knowers, the best water-managers—and they have status. People seek their advice, trade favors for their help. But they're not a separate class. They do other work too. Status is real but fluid.

Gender roles exist but aren't rigid. Carving tends toward men because it requires upper body strength, but women who have that strength carve too. Weaving tends toward women, but men weave. Children are raised primarily by mothers when they're young, but fathers are present and involved. As children grow, both parents teach them, and so do aunts and uncles and grandparents and unrelated elders who take interest.

The culture values patience, precision, care. Carelessness with stone—striking too hard, misjudging a wall's thickness—can collapse a chamber or injure someone. Carelessness with water can flood a district. Carelessness with fire—a lamp knocked over—is unthinkable. Children are taught early to move thoughtfully, to check before acting.

But there's also a value placed on cleverness, on the elegant solution. A carver who figures out how to extend a chamber using a natural cavity, saving weeks of work, is admired. Someone who devises a new channel system that captures more rainwater is celebrated. Innovation within the constraints is prized.

## Relationships to the Outside

The surface is part of their world but not the center. They go above daily—to tend gardens, to check light shafts, to meet traders at the agreed spots. But it's a transitional space, not home. They squint in the bright light, feel exposed under the sky, and are relieved to descend back into the stone's embrace.

Traders come irregularly, following routes that connect various settlements. The warreners produce goods for trade—cloth, pottery, carved bone tools, leather work. The traders bring grain, dried meat, bronze tools, occasionally luxuries like dyes or spices. The trading happens on the surface, at established spots, with payment in goods. There's no money here, no abstract exchange. This many pots for that much grain, negotiated until both sides feel it's fair.

Some young people leave. The warrens don't hold everyone. Those who feel stifled by the closeness, who want more light or more space or just difference, trade for passage with the merchants and go to live in surface towns. They're remembered but not resented. The warrens can't grow indefinitely; there's only so much limestone. People leaving is part of the balance.

Occasionally outsiders join. Someone from a surface town, drawn by curiosity or fleeing something, asks to stay. If they can adapt—learn to navigate the darkness, tolerate the closeness, contribute labor—they're accepted. But many try and leave. The warrens aren't for everyone.

There are other settlements, though far enough apart that contact is limited to traders' tales. Different stone-dwelling peoples, each adapted to their particular geology. The warreners hear about the cliff-dwellers who carve into vertical sandstone faces, about the deep-miners who tunnel into igneous rock for copper. There's a sense of kinship with these other stone-peoples, but it's abstract. The warrens are the world that matters.

The surrounding peoples—farmers and herders on the open land—regard the warreners with a mixture of respect and unease. Respect for their craft, their ability to create livable space from stone. Unease because there's something uncanny about people who choose to live in darkness, who seem more comfortable underground than under the sky. Trade relations are cordial but not warm. There are occasional partnerships—a surface farmer's daughter marries a warrener, or vice versa—but it's uncommon. The cultural gap is significant.

## Belief and Meaning

They're not unreligious, but their religion is understated, woven into practice rather than declared in doctrine. Stone is sacred, not as a god but as a partner, a living substance that allows itself to be shaped if you approach it correctly. They make small offerings before starting major excavation work—a drop of oil, a pinch of precious salt—pressed into a natural cavity or placed in a carved niche. It's respect, acknowledgment that they're asking something of the stone.

Water is similarly honored. The spring chamber has become a shrine not through decision but through accumulation—generations of people leaving marks, smoothing the walls with touch, understanding that this water, which comes up unbidden, is a gift.

Light is precious and therefore somewhat sacred. The daily rhythm of light moving through the shafts structures time. There are songs sung at particular moments—when the first shaft-light of morning strikes, when the last light fades in evening. These aren't prayers exactly, more acknowledgments. The day has come; the day is ending. We witness this.

The dead are present. They're in the burial chambers, yes, but also in the passages they carved, the chambers they lived in. Grandmothers who chose this route for a corridor, grandfathers who figured out this water channel—their decisions persist in the stone. The warreners live inside an accumulation of ancestral choice. The ancestors are the walls around them.

There's little sense of an afterlife. You die; your body goes into a niche; you're remembered. Eventually you're forgotten as individuals but you remain as the work you did. The passages you carved are still there, still used. That's immortality enough.

They mark the seasons, but differently than surface peoples. Less about planting and harvest, more about water and light. The wet season when the cisterns fill. The dry season when water is careful. The long-light time when the summer sun penetrates deepest into the shafts. The short-light time of winter when lamps burn longer.

There are celebrations, but they're not grand. A family finishes carving a major chamber; neighbors come, share food, admire the work. A child is born; there's a small gathering, quiet blessings, gifts of useful things. A couple partners; they carve their mark together in their chamber entrance, and there's a meal, witnesses.

There's a festival after the first major rain of the wet season, when the cisterns fill. People gather in one of the large chambers, share food and tell stories and sing the water-songs. It's not frenetic celebration but deep satisfaction—the water has come; there will be enough. The relief is profound.

## The Texture of Daily Life

Morning is whenever you wake, which varies. There's no sun to dictate it. But most people rise when the light shafts begin to glow, when the first daylight filters down. They emerge from sleeping platforms, wrap in wool against the cool, splash face and hands with water from a chamber pot or a basin.

Breakfast is simple. Porridge, usually, made from grain boiled with water, maybe some dried fruit stirred in if the family can afford it. Herbal tea, weak and bitter but warming. Bread if there's flour, though bread-making is less common—it requires an oven, and ovens require ventilation and fuel.

Then to work. For carvers, that means moving to wherever they're currently excavating, gathering tools, lighting a lamp if the chamber doesn't have a shaft. The work is physical: striking stone, clearing rubble, checking angles and thickness. Regular breaks, because you can't sustain that intensity all day.

For crafters, it's different rhythms. A weaver might work for hours steadily, hands moving in practiced patterns. A potter waits for clay to reach the right consistency, works intensely during the throwing, then waits again for drying.

Children's work is lighter but constant. Fetching water, carrying messages, sweeping stone dust from passage floors, helping with food prep. Old enough to help, not old enough yet to have a specialized craft.

Midday meal is often eaten while working, or in small groups. Bread and cheese if you have them, dried meat, vegetables from the surface, water to drink.

Afternoon continues the work, but the quality of light shifts. Different shafts illuminate. People move to follow the light if their work requires it.

Evening is when things slow. The light in the shafts fades to orange, then gray, then gone. Lamps are lit. People finish up, put tools away, move toward their chambers. There's more socializing now—neighbors visiting, sitting together in a chamber talking, children playing quietly.

Dinner is the main meal. Stew, usually, made in ceramic pots over small fires in chambers with good ventilation. Grain and vegetables, sometimes meat, seasoned with herbs. The family eats together, sitting on the carved benches or on the floor, using carved wooden bowls and bone spoons.

After dinner, people work on small things. Mending clothes, sharpening tools, weaving by lamplight if eyes are good enough. Stories are told. Children are taught songs, traditional tales about the first carvers, about the spring's origin, about what lives in the deepest, oldest cavities in the earth.

Then sleep. The lamps are extinguished or turned very low. People wrap in wool and leather, lie on sleeping platforms covered with furs or woven blankets. The warrens settle into their night sounds: distant dripping, the far-off percussion of someone still carving, the soft breathing of sleepers, the occasional murmur of voices from a passage.

## The Children Especially

The children here are remarkable for their spatial intelligence. By three, they navigate familiar passages confidently in low light. By five, they've developed a mental map of a significant portion of their district. By eight or nine, they know large sections of the warrens, can guide an adult to a specific chamber without hesitation.

They play games that seem impossible to outsiders. Hide and seek in three dimensions, using the entire vertical and horizontal space. Tag through passages where the pursued can suddenly climb up or drop down. They invent games with pebbles and shadows, games that use the acoustic properties of different chambers.

They're surprisingly quiet, even when playing. They've learned that volume carries, that being too loud brings adult attention and perhaps censure. So they modulate, keep their excitement to urgent whispers and suppressed giggles.

They're also remarkably mature about safety. Children as young as six understand that certain passages are dangerous, that certain walls are thin and shouldn't be touched carelessly. They've seen the consequences of carelessness—a scrape, a bruise, once in a rare while something worse. They learn to be careful.

But they're still children. They test boundaries, sneak into passages they're not supposed to explore, dare each other to go deeper than they should. Adults tolerate this within reason; exploration is how you truly learn the warrens.

They ask endless questions. Why is this passage curved? Who carved this chamber? Where does this water come from? The adults answer patiently, knowing that a child who understands the warrens will be a competent adult who makes good decisions.

The adolescents develop a particular restlessness. The warrens feel too small suddenly, too known. They spend more time on the surface, ostensibly doing useful work but really just wanting space, wanting to see horizon. Some of this resolves as they mature and find their place in the work. Some of it doesn't, and those are the ones who eventually leave.

## The Particular People

There's Mira, an old woman who remembers the carving of the western district's major gathering chamber, who was a child when that work began and a young woman when it finished. She knows more passages than anyone living, can navigate the oldest sections in complete darkness. She tells stories to children, her voice soft and steady, hands demonstrating on stone how the first carvers worked.

There's Keth, a mid-aged man who's the best water-manager currently working. He's figured out several clever channel systems, including one that captures water from three different seep-sources and combines them into a single cistern. People seek his advice when they're planning new chambers and want to know if they'll hit water.

There's young Dara, sixteen, who's brilliant at carving but who stands at the surface entrances sometimes, staring out at the horizon with an expression of such longing that everyone knows she'll leave soon. Her parents are trying to accept it, trying not to take it as rejection.

There's Tovin and Nell, a partnered couple in their thirties with three children, who run a pottery workshop in a large chamber they've carved to have perfect humidity. They're teaching their oldest daughter the craft; the middle child wants to be a carver instead; the youngest is still too young to have strong preferences.

There's Resh, a boy of eight, small for his age, who's developed an uncanny ability to hear water behind walls. Other children tease him, calling him "fish ears," but adult water-workers are starting to bring him along, trusting his sense.

There's Old Ferris, who's mostly blind now, eyes finally failing after eighty years of low light, who still works leather by touch in his chamber, producing bags and straps and garments of extraordinary quality. People bring him leather and describe what they need, and his fingers create it.

There's the triplets—Senna, Sorra, and Sill—who confused everyone as children and still do as adults, who've carved adjacent chambers and who finish each other's sentences, who weave together on a large loom and create patterns no one else can quite replicate.

There's Jek, who's considered odd because he insists on bathing daily—a waste of water, most people think—and who keeps his chamber almost obsessively organized, everything in its place, who gets anxious when things are disordered.

There are hundreds more, each with their particular skills and quirks and histories, each contributing to the ongoing life of the warrens. They're not generic stone-dwellers but specific people, living specific lives, interconnected and individual simultaneously.

## The Strangeness They Don't See

An outsider notices things they don't. The way they all unconsciously duck slightly when passing through doorways, even when the doorway is tall enough that ducking isn't necessary. It's a protective habit, learned early.

The way they touch walls constantly. Not consciously—it's automatic. A hand trailing along stone as they walk, fingers reading texture, maintaining orientation through touch.

The way they can identify individuals in darkness by the sound of footsteps. Different people have different gaits, different rhythms, wear different foot coverings or go barefoot differently.

The way they're comfortable with pauses in conversation. Surface people find the silences awkward; warreners just wait until someone has something to say. Sound is precious; why fill it with unnecessary words?

The way they share space that surface people would find intolerably intimate. Sleeping platforms might be in the same chamber, separated by a few feet and maybe a hanging. Private conversations happen in voices low enough that you can't quite hear them from ten feet away, but that's as much privacy as you get or need.

The way they have almost no personal possessions. What would be the point? There's nowhere to put them, and everything here is functional. A bowl, a knife, a few clothes, maybe a special tool or a piece of jewelry if you're wealthy. That's it.

The way they perceive time differently. Without sun, without seasons in the same way surface people have them, time is more about rhythm and cycle than about linear progression. They know how old they are approximately, but it matters less than where they are in life's progression: child, working adult, elder.

The way they're genuinely happy here. That's perhaps the strangest thing to outsiders. People who visit the warrens, who experience the closeness and darkness and coolness, can't imagine choosing this. But the warreners aren't settling or enduring. This is good. The stone around them is comforting. The darkness is peaceful. The closeness is community. They wouldn't want to live any other way.

## The Future They're Carving

They don't think much about distant future—what the warrens will be like in ten generations. But they think constantly about immediate future: the chamber that will be finished next season, the new ventilation shaft planned for next year, the passage that needs widening, the section of wall that's getting thin and needs a supporting pillar carved.

Every family with children thinks about what chambers those children will inhabit. Will they inherit the family chamber, or will they need to carve new ones? Where is there good stone available? What's the trade-off between carving deep (more work, but cooler and quieter) versus shallow (easier work, more light, but less stable temperature)?

Young people planning to partner discuss where they'll live. Will they expand one family's existing chamber, or carve entirely new space? The decision has implications—living with or near parents means help with children but less independence. Carving new means years of hard work but complete autonomy.

The community as a whole discusses expansion. Should they push further west, where the stone is good? Or south, where the stone is harder but there's room? Or should they focus on filling in the gaps in already-settled districts, carving chambers in the thick walls between existing spaces?

There's an awareness that they can't expand forever. The limestone layer has limits. Eventually they'll reach its edges, hit clay or harder stone. What happens then? Some people think they'll just stop, live in what they've carved, maintain rather than expand. Others think some families will have to leave, found new warrens elsewhere. No one knows. It's a problem for future generations.

But for now, the work continues. The sound of carving, the slow accumulation of space, the ongoing transformation of stone into home. They're building something that will outlast them, that will be inhabited by people they'll never meet, who will move through passages they carved and think about them, perhaps, the way they think about the first carvers whose work they still use.

That continuity—past, present, future linked through stone—is central to how they understand their lives. They're not just living; they're contributing to something larger than individual existence. Every chamber they carve is a gift to descendants. Every passage they maintain is honoring ancestors. They're links in a chain that extends back through time and forward into it, bound together by the patient work of carving stone.

---

The warreners are not one thing but many people, various and particular. But they share this: a comfort with darkness, a patience with stone, a tolerance for closeness, a pride in craft, a deep attachment to the place they're continuously creating. They are becoming, always, just as their warrens are becoming. Each generation slightly different from the last, adapting, learning, adding their particular contribution to the long accumulation of human life in carved stone.

They are, in the end, exactly what they need to be for the life they've chosen: compact, skillful, patient, and content in the carved dark.
