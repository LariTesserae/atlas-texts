# The Qesh-Ma'ari

They move through the terraces with the unselfconsciousness of deer, if deer carried baskets and tied branches and knew the names of seventeen kinds of moss. The people here—they call themselves something that translates poorly, perhaps "the tenders" or "those who stay" or simply "us," as distinct from the people who live anywhere else—have bodies shaped by the work and the place.

Their hands tell the story most clearly. Every adult's hands are callused in specific patterns: the outer edge of the palm thickened from gripping wet tools, the fingertips both tough and sensitive from constant contact with plants, small scars mapping out decades of thorns and splinters and the occasional rabbit bite. They can tie a graft in the dark, by feel alone. They can detect the difference between healthy sap-flow and diseased by texture, by the slight difference in stickiness. When they gesture while speaking—and they gesture constantly—their hands move with that particular grace of people whose fingers have performed ten thousand precise movements, whose muscles remember the exact angle needed to train a branch or harvest a crop without damaging the plant.

They're not tall, these people. The average height clusters around five and a half feet, with variation but not enormous. The terraces themselves might explain this—the living architecture was trained when whoever first shaped it was a certain size, and the doorways and passages and working heights all reflect that ancestral build. Taller people exist but move through the settlement in a permanent half-stoop, learning to duck without thinking. The culture unconsciously selects for compact frames that can climb easily, slip between narrow spaces, work for hours in positions that would exhaust larger bodies.

They're strong in specific ways. Not the strength of lifting heavy things—there are no heavy things here, really, nothing that can't be moved by two or three people working together. But the strength of endurance, of working all day in the cool damp air without tiring. The strength of grip, of hanging from branches while tying them. The strength of legs that walk the terraced slopes a hundred times daily, that can stand on a six-inch-wide root-wall and work with both hands occupied. Their bodies are lean, defined, marked by visible muscle in the forearms and calves and shoulders. They carry almost no excess fat—not from deprivation but from constant movement in a climate that's always slightly cold, always demanding the body maintain its warmth.

Their skin tends toward brown in a dozen shades, though the constant moisture and lack of harsh sun means they don't darken much from childhood to adulthood. What changes is the texture. Everyone over thirty has skin that's slightly rough, almost weathered, from the perpetual dampness and the wind and the work. Their hands and faces especially—the parts always exposed—develop a quality that's somehow both soft and tough, like well-used leather. The younger children have smooth skin that marks them immediately as not-yet-fully-working, still in the phase where play dominates.

They smell of the place because the place is in them, on them, constantly. The green smell of crushed plants soaks into their clothing and won't wash fully out—not that they try particularly hard. The bird-smell becomes invisible to them but visitors notice immediately: they carry a faint musk of feathers and guano, of living in close quarters with thousands of avians. Wood-smoke, inevitably, from the cooking fires and the evening heating. Their own sweat has a particular quality, maybe from the diet, maybe from the constant low-level exertion, slightly sharp but not unpleasant. And under everything, the smell of damp wool, because their clothing is always slightly wet and never quite dries.

The clothing itself is pragmatic to the point of uniformity. Trousers or skirts of woven wool or plant fiber, the colors natural—cream and grey and brown—because dyes are precious and saved for special pieces. Tunics or wrapped shirts, simply constructed, designed to be patched endlessly. Every adult's clothing shows repairs, panels of different fabric integrated where the original wore through. They wear layers in the morning cold, strip down as they work and warm up, layer again in the evening. Feet are protected by wrapped cloth or simple leather shoes when they're available, but many people work barefoot, feet toughened until they can navigate the root-matted floors and stone steps without difficulty.

Their hair is black or very dark brown universally, straight or with a slight wave. They wear it practically: short on men and women both, or if longer, always tied back, braided, wrapped, kept absolutely out of the way. Loose hair catches on every branch, every protruding twig in the living walls. Small children have their hair cropped close until they're old enough to maintain it themselves. The elderly sometimes let it grow, wear it wrapped under cloth, marking their transition from primary workers to advisors.

Look at their faces and you see the lineage clearly—not because they're all related, though many are, but because certain features dominate. Strong noses, pronounced cheekbones, deep-set eyes that might be adaptation to the filtered light or might be coincidence frozen by isolation. Their eyes are brown or very dark hazel. No blue eyes exist here, no green—hasn't been that kind of admixture in generations if ever. Their expressions at rest tend toward serious, focused, the look of people whose attention is habitually on their work. But they smile easily, laugh often, the seriousness breaking into warmth without transition.

They age in a particular way. Young adults look ageless, could be anywhere from twenty to thirty-five. The work keeps them fit but also wears at them in small increments. Then quite suddenly, usually in the forties, the aging becomes visible—lines appearing around eyes and mouth, hair greying, the shoulders beginning to round from decades of hunching over crops and looms and detailed work. By fifty they look old, though they continue working until body or mind fails. The genuinely elderly—sixty and beyond—are rare enough to be notable, treated with elaborate respect, given the lightest tasks and the warmest spots near fires.

The children are small, active, loud within the general din, covered in scrapes and plant stains and dirt that's actually compost. They're nursing until two or three, common for cultures without easy alternate nutrition. They're underfoot constantly until around five, when they begin to be genuinely useful, can be trusted with simple tasks. The transition from child to working member of the community is gradual, not marked by ceremony but by progressive inclusion in adult work. By twelve or thirteen they're doing real labor, not make-work, and they know it.

The adolescents have a particular energy, a restlessness that the terraces can barely contain. This is when some people leave—go down to the river settlements to trade and never come back, or join up with trading caravans, or marry out to other communities. But most stay. The work is known, the place is secure, leaving means losing the elaborate knowledge that makes you competent here. The adolescents who stay become noticeably more settled around sixteen or seventeen, as if they've accepted the bargain: stay and you'll always have work, food, community, the beauty of this place. Leave and you're starting over.

Partnerships form with practical consideration and personal attraction in roughly equal measure. Cousins marry freely—the community is too small for elaborate incest taboos—but siblings don't, nor half-siblings. The partnerships are mostly between one man and one woman but not rigidly—sometimes a household has three adults, or two of the same sex, or other configurations that work for those people. What matters is the labor contribution, the ability to maintain a dwelling and its terraces. Love is valued but not considered sufficient without complementary skills.

Pregnancy is common, constant, and dangerous. Women give birth attended by other women who've accumulated knowledge across births. Many babies die in the first year, from diseases that sweep through the dense population, from accidents, from causes no one fully understands. The women who survive multiple births develop a status, a gravitas, having proven their bodies' resilience. Infertile women and men are absorbed into the community without particular stigma—there's plenty of work regardless.

The children who survive are intensely socialized, raised as much by the community as by their biological parents. A child might sleep and eat in their parents' dwelling but spend the day following another adult who has relevant skills to teach. The biological tie is recognized but not considered as important as the practical ties of who teaches what. "My parent" can mean the person who bore you or the person who taught you to read plants or the person who feeds you most often. The language has different words for these relationships but they're all translated as "parent" inadequately.

Speech happens at volume, necessity in a place where the background noise is constant. Their voices are pitched to carry, trained from childhood to project without straining. They interrupt freely, overlap, finish each other's sentences—in close quarters with this much shared work, the boundaries between individuals become porous. The language itself is elaborate in vocabulary around plants, animals, water management, kinship relations, and fairly simple about everything else. They have seventeen words for different types of plant training but only three colors—light, dark, and the green-blue of copper corrosion which is also the word for sacred/valuable/preserved.

They laugh at physical comedy, at incongruity, at the endless small dramas of living in such density. Someone slips on wet stone and catches themselves—everyone nearby laughs, including the person who slipped, immediate and unself-conscious. A cat steals food from a basket and is chased by three children and two adults in an elaborate chase through the terraces—everyone's laughing even while trying to recover the food. The humor is gentle but constant, a release valve for the pressures of proximity.

They sing while working, wordless melodies or simple repeated verses, the songs functioning to coordinate labor and pass time. Different tasks have different associated rhythms. The weaving song is different from the harvesting song is different from the water-management song. The songs are ancient, probably, or maybe they regenerate constantly—no one thinks about their origin, they're just what you sing while doing this work.

They argue regularly, voices rising, hands gesturing emphatically, over the proper way to do something, over resource allocation, over personal slights, over anything. But the arguments rarely escalate to physical violence. In a place this dense, where cooperation is mandatory for survival, the culture has developed elaborate conflict-resolution patterns. Someone respected intervenes, or the parties agree to defer to expertise, or they simply stop arguing and return to work because the work doesn't stop for hurt feelings. Grudges exist but are held quietly, expressed in subtle ways—helping someone slightly less quickly, sharing food but not the best portions.

Physical affection is common and casual. People touch each other constantly—a hand on an arm while speaking, passing close enough to brush shoulders, children climbing on adults indiscriminately. The personal space that would be normal elsewhere doesn't exist here, isn't possible. They've adapted by making touch meaningless until it becomes significant through intensity or context. Sexual touching happens privately in theory, but in practice everyone knows what's happening behind the thin walls of living wood, just as they know everything else.

They bathe irregularly by outside standards, but the constant moisture means they're never entirely grimy. They wash hands and faces frequently in the ever-present water channels, rinse off after particularly dirty work, and occasionally do a full wash standing in a water channel during the warmer part of the day. The cold water is shocking, the process quick. Soap is made from rendered animal fat and ashes, precious enough to use sparingly. Hair is washed even less frequently, more often just combed through and re-tied.

Their health is generally good but punctuated by epidemics. Living this densely, when one person catches something, everyone catches it. Childhood diseases sweep through the settlement, killing some, conferring immunity on survivors. Adults have weathered multiple waves and are relatively robust, but new diseases arriving with traders can devastate. Respiratory ailments are common—the constant dampness affects lungs, especially in the elderly. Parasites are managed through plant medicines that are somewhat effective. Broken bones are set with reasonable skill but often heal badly. Infected wounds are treated with poultices that sometimes work. Pain is managed with plant medicines and stoicism in roughly equal measure.

The old people accumulate knowledge that becomes precious. They remember the great fungal blight twenty years ago, can advise on its early signs. They remember which plant lineages produce the best fiber, which family lines are prone to which troubles. They remember the stories—not written down, just told—of how the settlement was founded, how the first trees were trained, who did what when. The stories are practical and mythological simultaneously. The first planter is also maybe a god, maybe just a person, maybe both. The distinction isn't important.

They accumulate possessions slowly, carefully. A good knife passed down through three generations. A particularly beautiful basket woven by a grandmother. A clay vessel with a perfect spout. A blanket made from the wool of a long-dead sheep, patched and repatched until it's more patches than original but still warm. The lack of space means possessions are minimal, valued for usefulness and beauty in equal measure. Displays of wealth are subtle—slightly finer clothing, slightly more or better tools, being able to give away resources in the complex networks of obligation.

The menstrual cycle is managed with cloth pads washed and reused, or with absorbent moss during particularly heavy work days. The blood is composted like everything else, returned to the gardens. Menstruation is not hidden but also not discussed much—it's simply another bodily function, managed practically. Pregnancy is obvious early in a culture where everyone's body is known through constant proximity. Pregnant women work until they can't, rest briefly, return to work as soon as they're able. The culture can't afford long lying-in periods.

The dying are kept in their homes if possible, attended by family, given what comfort is available. Pain management is reasonable if there are herbs for it, minimal if there aren't. Death is common enough to be familiar—children die, adults die in accidents or from sudden illness, the elderly die predictably. Bodies are composted, literally, returned to the terraces in designated spots where they'll break down and feed the plants. This is described matter-of-factly as going back to the soil, and it's meant literally. The dead person's possessions are redistributed, their dwelling reassigned if needed, their name used for the next baby born who needs one. Grief is expressed openly but briefly—there's too much work for extended mourning.

They have what outsiders would call superstitions but they consider practical knowledge. Don't cut the oldest trees, they hold the terraces together. Do leave offerings at the spring, it responds to respect with better flow. If a cat brings you a bird, accept it gracefully, the cats are watching. These might be metaphor or might be literal belief—the distinction is unclear and probably not important to them. They do the things that work, that have always worked, and debate about why they work is for people with more leisure time.

The few who learn to read and write—taught by a trader or a visitor—are valued but also slightly suspect. Writing things down suggests you don't trust memory, don't trust the oral transmission. It's useful for trading records, for marking measurements that need to be exact, but not for the real knowledge of how to live here. That knowledge is in hands and eyes and muscle memory, in the body's understanding of when a branch is ready to tie, in the feel of soil that has the right moisture content.

They dream, presumably, but don't share dreams much. Sleep is practical, necessary, happens when you're tired. The dream life is private in a culture where almost nothing else is. They wake with the light, sleep when it's dark, and don't think much about it. Some people remember dreams vividly and are consulted about them—is dreaming of flood a warning? But most just sleep, wake, begin the work again.

They love the place, clearly, though they wouldn't phrase it that way. They love it in their hands' knowledge of every plant, in the way they pause when the light shifts beautifully, in the pride when a visitor admires the trained architecture, in their horror at the thought of leaving. The place is identity. To be Qesh-Ma'ari is to know these terraces, these plants, these practices. Away from here, who would you be?

The outside world exists but distantly. Traders bring news, sometimes travelers come through with stories from far away, but it all seems faintly unreal compared to the immediate, pressing, constant reality of the terraces. Kingdoms rise and fall in the lowlands—so what? The rabbits need managing. Wars happen elsewhere—terrible, truly, but here the water channels need clearing. They're not indifferent to suffering but their circle of concern is necessarily tight, focused on the community and the place that sustains it.

They are not simple people living simple lives. They're sophisticated, operating a system of extraordinary complexity, making thousands of decisions daily based on accumulated knowledge and observation and experience. But the sophistication is focused entirely on this place, these practices. About other things they're incurious, uninformed, and untroubled by it. They know what they need to know.

When they're happy—and they are, often, despite the hardship—it shows in their ease with each other, in the laughter, in the songs, in the way someone will stop work to show a child a bird's nest, in the quiet satisfaction of a well-maintained terrace. When they're unhappy—and they are, also often—it shows in tension, in shorter tempers, in the arguments lasting longer, in people working in slightly more isolated ways. But both states are temporary, surfacing and submerging in the flow of daily work.

They'll continue, probably. The knowledge is deep, the practices are robust, the place provides. Children are being born, learning, becoming competent. The terraces are maintained, the balance holds. They could continue for another thousand years like this, generation after generation, tending the same living walls, managing the same dense populations of plants and animals, living in close proximity with the same tensions and joys.

Unless something changes. Unless the climate shifts. Unless a disease arrives that they can't manage. Unless the young people stop learning, stop caring, start leaving in numbers too great to sustain the work. Unless the knowledge breaks, the oral transmission fails, the hands forget what they've always known.

But today, this evening, as the lamps glow and the birds settle and the water whispers and the people gather for their meals, Qesh-Ma'ar continues. And the people who are Qesh-Ma'ar continue with it, woven into the place as thoroughly as the vines are woven into the walls, impossible to extract, impossible to imagine anywhere else, carrying in their bodies and their hands and their knowledge the same ancient bargain: work always, tend constantly, and this place will keep you, will feed you, will be beautiful, will be home.
