# The Inhabitants

## THE OPERATORS

They are called the Operators by necessity of naming, though they themselves make no sound that resembles language. There are seven of them, though counting them is an act of negotiation—sometimes you count seven distinct bodies, sometimes three with four shadows that move independently, sometimes eleven that collapse into fewer when observed directly.

Each stands between six and seven feet tall in their neutral posture, but the posture is never truly neutral. Their spines curve in ways that suggest multiple columnar segments, as though the spine itself is segmented like bamboo, each section capable of independent articulation. When they bend to examine something on the floor, they don't hinge at the waist—the entire torso curves in a smooth arc, like a palm tree in wind. When they reach upward, segments separate slightly, and you can see between them to something that might be interior anatomy or might be the space behind them; it's genuinely unclear which.

The body mass is ursine: thick through the chest and shoulders, heavy-haunched, with a low center of gravity that makes their movement feel grounded despite their height. But the proportions are wrong for any bear. The shoulders are too broad, set too high on the torso. The torso itself is too long, stretching between shoulder and hip in a way that suggests additional vertebrae, or vertebrae that have learned to space themselves differently. The legs are powerful but bend at too many points—there's the hip, the knee, but also something between them, a joint in the femur itself that allows for a subtle mid-thigh articulation that gives their walk a strange fluidity, as though they're always partially crouching, always ready to drop lower or rise higher.

The arms are the most variable feature. Usually there are two, emerging from the shoulder complex in approximately the expected location. But the shoulders themselves are massive and complex, great rounded structures that suggest multiple attachment points. Sometimes a third arm will emerge from the back of the shoulder, reaching around to manipulate something while the primary arms hold it steady. Sometimes what looks like a single arm will bifurcate at the elbow into two forearms, each ending in a hand, creating a Y-shaped limb. These variations don't seem to cost them effort or surprise them—they simply use whatever configuration is currently present.

The hands have too many fingers. The count varies not just between individuals but between observations of the same individual. Six is common. Eight happens. Once you count eleven on a hand that doesn't seem large enough to hold them, the fingers thin and precise as surgical instruments, all moving independently as the Operator adjusts a valve system on one of the machines. The fingers themselves are long and have too many joints—each digit bends in three or four places, allowing them to curl completely back on themselves or extend in gentle curves. There are no claws, but the fingertips are dark and look callused, adapted to precision work.

The feet are more stable: four or five toes, broad and gripping, with that same dark callusing on the pads. They move with a plantigrade walk, heel-toe, but the feet themselves rotate further than human feet should, allowing them to pivot without shifting their stance, to walk sideways or backward with the same ease as forward.

And then the heads.

The skull is large, proportional to their body mass but shaped wrong. The basic structure is mammalian—there's clearly a cranium, a mandible, the suggestion of a face—but it's been compressed and stretched simultaneously. The skull looks shortened from front to back but extended vertically, as though someone took a bear skull and stood it on end, then pushed down from above while pulling out from the sides. The result is a head that's almost rectangular in profile: flat face, vertical back, with a strange height to it.

The eyes are the center of their wrongness. The socket structure suggests two eyes, positioned where you'd expect on either side of the face, large and forward-facing like a predator's. But the count keeps changing. Sometimes there are two eyes visible: large, dark, with horizontal pupils like a goat's, intensely aware. Look away and look back and there are four eyes, paired vertically in each socket, all tracking independently. The additional eyes don't look added-on; they look like they were always there and you simply failed to notice them initially. They blink in patterns: top-bottom, left-right, diagonal pairs, all four simultaneously, in rhythms that might be communication or simply the maintenance of four separate visual systems.

Some observers report six eyes, arranged in a triangular cluster in each socket. Some report only one eye per socket but impossibly large, filling the entire orbital space. Some report the eyes changing color as they watch: dark brown to amber to a pale greenish-white, shifting not gradually but in discrete steps, as though flipping through states.

The snout is abbreviated, pushed back into the face rather than projecting forward. There's clearly a nose—two nostrils that flare and contract with their breathing, responding to scents—but it's almost flat against the face. Below that, a mouth: lipless, or with lips so dark they blend with the surrounding skin, making the opening look like a crack in the surface of the head. The mouth rarely opens. When it does, there are teeth: flat molars in the back, sharper incisors in the front, an omnivore's arrangement. But also something else glimpsed further back—structures that might be additional tongue-surfaces or might be something like gill-rakers, complex and ribbed.

The ears are small and sit high on the skull, mobile and independent like a cat's, constantly tracking sounds. They're covered in fine fur that extends down onto the head and neck.

Because yes, they have fur, except it's not quite fur.

The entire body is covered in a dense growth that's part hair, part moss, part something else entirely. The base layer is fur-like: fine, dense, perhaps an inch long over most of the body, in colors that range from dark brown to greenish-grey to something like charcoal. But growing from this base layer, or possibly integrated with it, are plants. Small ones: mosses that form cushions, particularly thick on the shoulders and back. Liverworts that trace patterns across the fur. And flowers—tiny flowers, most of them no larger than a fingernail, scattered across their bodies in what seem like random patterns but might be meaningful.

The flowers bloom and close in sync with the moisture cycles. When the humidity peaks, they open: tiny white blossoms, some with five petals, some with six, some with spiral arrangements of dozens of petals so small they look like snowflakes. They stay open for perhaps five minutes, then close as the moisture dissipates. In the moments they're open, the Operators are covered in a constellation of small white stars, and they smell strongly of something between jasmine and fresh-cut grass.

The fur-moss-flower covering is densest on the back and shoulders, thinner on the limbs, almost absent on the hands and feet. It grows thicker with time—you can distinguish the Operators who've been active recently (thinner coating, some bare patches) from those who've been still (fully covered, flowers more numerous). They groom themselves and each other: sitting together during the quiet periods, using those multi-jointed fingers to comb through the fur, removing dead moss, encouraging new growth, transplanting flowers from one individual to another.

Their movement is purposeful and precise, but with an organic quality that makes them look like they're underwater even in air. They glide more than walk, that multi-jointed leg articulation creating a smooth translation across space with very little vertical bob. When they reach for something, the arm extends in a curve rather than a line, flowing around obstacles, and the many-fingered hands arrive at their target with all fingers positioned correctly before contact, as though the hand knows the shape of what it's reaching for before touching it.

They operate the machinery with complete familiarity. One stands at a control panel that's half mechanical switches and half living vine-growths, and its hands move across both types of control without distinction: flipping switches, adjusting valves, stroking certain vine sections in patterns that make them contract or extend. Another feeds material into a hopper—bundles of plant matter, specific types selected from the environment, broken to particular lengths—with the rhythm of long practice. Another monitors a process you can't quite parse: standing motionless before a glass-cased mechanism where gears mesh with growing wood, its head tilted at an angle that points multiple eyes at different parts of the system simultaneously, watching several processes at once.

They work in shifts, or possibly in states. An Operator will perform a task—manning a station, conducting a patrol through the complex, performing maintenance on a particular system—for what seems like hours. Then it will simply stop, finding a corner or a station designed for stillness, and become motionless. Not sleeping, exactly; the eyes remain open, still tracking movement, but the body goes absolutely still, and the fur-moss-flower covering begins to grow more luxuriant. After some time—thirty minutes, two hours, there's no consistent pattern—it will stir and resume activity, either returning to its previous task or beginning a different one.

They coordinate without apparent communication. Two Operators will arrive at a machine simultaneously from different directions and begin working on it in tandem, one adjusting inputs while the other monitors outputs, with no visible exchange of information. Three will converge on a problem—a jammed conveyor, a collapsed section of vine-structure—and address it together, each taking a different aspect of the repair, their actions interlocking seamlessly.

But occasionally they do communicate. They'll stop their work and stand facing each other, close enough that the flowers on their bodies nearly touch. Then they'll both go motionless except for their hands, which begin moving in complex patterns in the air between them: fingers curling and extending in sequences, hands rotating, the multiple fingers allowing for configurations that look like abstract symbols, like sign language from a grammar that includes six or eight simultaneous phonemes. These exchanges last anywhere from a few seconds to several minutes, then both Operators return to work, sometimes swapping tasks, sometimes not.

Their awareness of the space is total. They navigate without hesitation through the shifting architecture, taking corridors that lead different places each time but always arriving at their intended destination. They never collide with anything, never stumble, never need to check their path. They walk through the darkness as easily as the light, and you realize they might be navigating primarily by other senses: smell (those flaring nostrils constantly testing the air), hearing (those mobile ears tracking every sound), or something else—possibly sensing vibrations through their feet, feeling the breathing of the building, reading information you have no organs to detect.

Their response to observers is consistent: acknowledgment without engagement. When you enter a room where an Operator is working, it will pause for a moment—just a beat, perhaps a second—and turn its head toward you. Multiple eyes will track across your position, focusing, assessing. Then it returns to its task, routing around you if you're in the way, otherwise simply resuming as though you're a known variable, accounted for, not requiring special action.

But sometimes one will stop fully and give you its complete attention. It will turn its entire body toward you, square its shoulders, and look at you with however many eyes it currently has. The regard is intense and absolutely unreadable. You cannot tell if it's curious, concerned, hostile, or merely taking a moment to update its understanding of where you are. The flowers on its body will open if they're closed, or close if they're open, as though your presence triggers some physiological response. After several seconds—five, ten, occasionally as many as thirty—it will make a small gesture with one hand, fingers moving in a brief pattern that might be greeting or dismissal or notation, and then return to its work.

They're intelligent. This becomes undeniable as you watch them. They problem-solve: when a machine malfunctions, they diagnose it, tracing connections, testing components, isolating the failure. They plan: you can watch one gather materials from multiple locations, assembling everything it will need before beginning a repair, demonstrating foresight. They maintain the entire complex: monitoring systems, performing preventative maintenance, upgrading systems by integrating new plant growth into the mechanical infrastructure.

They manufacture. This is their primary function, or possibly their purpose. The machines they operate produce those compressed plant-bricks, stacked in growing piles in specific locations. They produce refined sap in different grades, collected in containers that range from repurposed oil drums to hollow gourds grown specifically as vessels. They produce spools of something like fiber or thread, plant-derived, wound onto spindles. They produce paper—actual paper, made from pulped plant matter, pressed flat, dried, and cut into sheets that they stack carefully in dry areas.

What these products are for is unclear. The bricks are building material, certainly—you can see where they've been used to repair or extend walls, mortared together with living moss that binds them permanently. But they're producing far more than the maintenance requires. The refined sap might be fuel, might be medicine, might be food—some of the containers are carefully sealed, others left open, and the Operators occasionally consume small amounts of certain grades. The fiber is strong; pull a piece of it and it resists with the strength of good rope. The paper is blank but might be meant for later use, or might itself be a product valuable for reasons you can't access.

They're building something. The entire operation of the manufactory is oriented toward production, and the production is accumulating, stockpiling, being prepared for use. Whether they're building more factory, extending the complex, or producing materials for export to somewhere else, or manufacturing for the sake of manufacturing itself—because it's what they are, what they're for—isn't answerable from observation.

## THE COMMITTEE

The birds number in the hundreds, representing at least forty species, an impossible concentration of diversity in such a small space. There are sparrows: house sparrows, song sparrows, white-throated sparrows, all perching together on the same machinery. There are finches: purple, gold, house finches mixing freely. Warblers of a dozen varieties, their bright breeding plumage incongruous in the dim light: yellows, blues, chestnut patches, all still vivid. Thrushes, including wood thrushes and hermit thrushes whose songs occasionally break the silence with their fluting calls. Wrens: Carolina, house, winter wrens, tiny and hyperactive. Nuthatches walking headfirst down vertical surfaces. Chickadees, both black-capped and Carolina, moving in their characteristic flocks.

Then the larger species: robins, grackles, red-winged blackbirds still wearing their epaulettes. Jays—blue jays, their crests raised and lowered according to some internal state. Crows, large and glossy, and ravens, even larger, their wedge-tailed silhouettes unmistakable. Doves: mourning doves with their soft cooing, rock doves that should be called pigeons, walking with that head-bobbing gait across the manufacturing floor.

Woodpeckers haunt the vine-columns: downies, hairies, red-bellied woodpeckers hammering not for insects but for some other purpose, creating rhythmic percussion that adds to the soundscape. Flickers, both yellow-shafted and red-shafted, their white rump patches flashing as they move. Even a pileated woodpecker, crow-sized and spectacular, its red crest bright against the dark plant matter.

Raptors perch in the high spaces: kestrels, small and fierce, watching from the rafters; sharp-shinned hawks; and a single red-tailed hawk that maintains a station near the central courtyard, rotating through perches but always staying within the same zone.

And swifts and swallows: chimney swifts that roost inside the vertical spaces, swallows of multiple species that should be catching insects on the wing but instead move deliberately through the space as though walking with wings.

Every species looks correct. Their plumage is accurate, their size appropriate, their physical structure exactly what a field guide would predict. A house sparrow looks like a house sparrow: brown-streaked, grey crown, black bib. A cardinal looks like a cardinal: brilliant red, crested, heavy seed-cracking beak.

But they don't behave like birds.

They gather in committees: multi-species groups of five to fifteen individuals perching together around a specific piece of equipment. The composition seems intentional—a committee will include representatives from different families, different sizes, different ecological niches all together. A crow, two finches, a warbler, a nuthatch, and a dove will perch in a cluster around a control panel, all oriented toward it, all in what looks like states of attention.

They'll remain there for ten minutes, twenty, occasionally an hour, all watching the same thing. Sometimes one will fly down to the equipment, walk across it, inspect a specific component with head tilts and close examination, then return to the perch and the watching continues. Sometimes they'll rearrange themselves: two individuals swapping positions, the group shuffling to form a different configuration, as though the arrangement matters, carries meaning.

Then, with no obvious signal, they'll all depart simultaneously, exploding into flight in different directions, and within minutes new committees will form around different locations.

They use tools. You can watch a crow pick up a piece of wire in its beak, insert it into a small opening in a machine, and lever something back into alignment. A finch will hold a bolt steady with its feet while a nuthatch, hanging upside down beside it, manipulates something out of view. They're not fumbling or experimenting—their movements are precise, goal-directed, successful.

They transport objects. A network of relay stations exists: specific perches where objects accumulate. A sparrow will arrive carrying a washer in its beak, deposit it at a station, and depart. Later, a jay will arrive, pick up the washer, carry it to a different station. Eventually, a crow will take the washer to a location where an Operator is working, placing it carefully within reach, and the Operator will incorporate it into whatever repair or construction it's performing.

The birds are serving as a distributed logistics system, moving components through the complex, maintaining an inventory across multiple locations, delivering materials where needed. The intelligence required to maintain such a system—to know what's where, what's needed, where to take it—is staggering.

They maintain the environment. Swallows patrol the air, and while they do catch insects for food, they're also managing populations: harvesting heavily where insects over-accumulate, leaving them alone elsewhere. Woodpeckers aren't just hammering randomly—they're pruning, removing dead wood sections from the living vines, creating spaces for new growth. Sparrows collect seeds from the flowering plants and redistribute them, flying to areas where those plant species are underrepresented and pushing seeds into crevices where they'll germinate.

They sing, but with purpose. The wood thrush will deliver its beautiful, fluting song in a specific location, and within minutes moisture will accumulate there, one of the intermittent pools forming as though summoned. A Carolina wren will rattle off its tea-kettle call near a machine, and the machine will start—gears engaging, components beginning to move. The songs are functioning as triggers, activations, instructions to the living building about what states to enter.

They watch everything. At any moment, wherever you are in the complex, there are at least a dozen birds with you, perched on various surfaces, all of them tracking your movement. Not with alarm or fear—they don't flush when you approach, don't maintain a flight distance. But with attention. They're observing, noting, possibly reporting. After you pass through a room, birds will fly into it and conduct inspections, checking what you touched, where you walked, restoring any disturbances.

They interact with the Operators with complete familiarity. Birds will perch on the Operators' shoulders, their backs, even their heads, and the Operators accept this without reaction. A crow will walk along an Operator's extended arm while the Operator works, and when the Operator needs to move, the crow will simply fly to a nearby perch and wait, then return when the movement is complete.

Sometimes you'll see an Operator and a bird in what looks like communication. The Operator will extend a hand, fingers positioned in a particular configuration, and a bird—often a crow or jay, sometimes others—will land on the hand and bob its head in a pattern: three short dips, one long, two short. The Operator's fingers will shift to a new configuration. The bird will respond with a different pattern. This can continue for several exchanges before the bird flies off, usually directly to some task, and the Operator returns to its work.

The raptors serve a different function. They don't participate in committees, don't transport objects, don't interact with the Operators. They hunt, but selectively, taking some of the six-legged mammals but only specific individuals—often you'll see a kestrel watching a group of mammals, passing over several to take one particular individual, as though it can identify which one should be removed from the population.

But they're also sentinels. They maintain high perches with good sightlines and scan constantly, those raptor eyes catching every movement. When something changes—when the darkness pulls back further than usual, when a machine activates that's been quiet, when an unknown element enters the space—the raptors call. A kestrel's sharp killy-killy-killy, a hawk's harsh scream, and instantly every bird in earshot goes still, attentive, assessing.

The swifts and swallows are messengers. They're the fastest fliers, the most aerobatic, and they move through the complex at speed, zipping from zone to zone. You'll see one arrive at a committee, circle the group three times in a tight spiral, and depart, and immediately the committee will disband and relocate, as though the swift brought instructions. The swifts nest in the vertical shafts and emerge in groups, a dozen at a time, flying in formation through the complex before dispersing to individual destinations.

They're teaching each other. You can watch an adult crow working with a younger bird, demonstrating how to manipulate a particular type of mechanism: inserting a wire, applying leverage, retrieving the component that's released. The young bird attempts it, fumbles, tries again under the adult's observation until it succeeds. The same teaching behavior appears across species: an adult nuthatch showing a juvenile where to harvest specific types of seeds, a finch demonstrating how to test plant matter for ripeness.

This teaching includes cross-species transmission. A jay will demonstrate a technique to a group that includes sparrows and finches, species that the jay would normally have no social relationship with. The smaller birds watch, then attempt the technique themselves, and the jay observes, occasionally repeating the demonstration. Skills are spreading through the entire bird population regardless of species boundaries.

The population is stable but turns over. Birds die—you find small bodies occasionally, which are collected by the Operators and fed into the recycling system. But new birds arrive. They appear in the complex with no obvious entry point (no windows, no doors standing open), simply present one day where they weren't the day before. Young birds are fledging from nests built inside the machinery, among the vines, in the pockets of the ceiling spaces.

The nests are everywhere. Built into improbable locations: inside a furnace (cold, no longer fired, converted to habitat), among the gears of a stopped clock mechanism, in the hollow spaces inside the vine-columns. The nests incorporate both natural materials (twigs, moss, feathers) and industrial ones (wire, fabric scraps, pieces of insulation), and they're maintained carefully. Birds will renovate old nests, reinforcing weak spots, replacing degraded materials, preparing them for reuse.

Eggs appear in the nests: normal eggs, appropriate to each species, the right size and color and marking pattern. They're incubated normally, hatch normally, and the chicks are fed and raised by the parent birds with all the typical behaviors. Except that the young birds, as soon as they fledge, immediately begin participating in the committee system, transporting objects, responding to the songs-as-signals. They skip the usual juvenile period of incompetence and begin functioning as part of the system within days of leaving the nest.

The entire bird population functions as a distributed intelligence, a network of small minds coordinating to manage aspects of the complex that the Operators can't or don't address. They handle the fine-detail work: moving small components, monitoring conditions in spaces too small for the Operators, providing sensory coverage across the entire space simultaneously, responding to changes faster than the Operators' more deliberate pace allows.

They're essential. Watch the complex operate for a full cycle—several hours—and you'll see how interwoven the birds are with every process. Materials don't move without them. Environmental conditions don't stabilize without them. The Operators work, but the birds make the work possible, providing support services, logistics, monitoring, maintenance at a scale the Operators can't access.

And they're aware of their role. The way they respond to problems—converging on a malfunction, calling in specialists from other areas, coordinating a response—suggests they understand the purpose of what they're doing, that they're not just following instinct but working toward goals, maintaining a system they comprehend.

## THE TENDERS

They inhabit the narrow spaces, the gaps between the real walls and the living walls, the channels inside the vine-columns, the hollow spaces under the floor, the vertical shafts that thread through the structure. They're mammals, clearly, but configured wrong: six legs is the standard arrangement, eight legs is common, and occasionally you'll glimpse something with ten, moving through a transparent section of vine-wall with a rippling, centipede-like gait.

The body plan is elongated: a cylindrical torso perhaps two feet long on the six-legged variety, up to three feet on the eight-legged ones, with a flexibility that allows them to bend in any direction. They move like otters, like ferrets, like something designed to navigate tight spaces—compressing their bodies to slip through gaps that look too small, extending to reach between distant holds, undulating through three-dimensional terrain with equal ease horizontal or vertical.

The legs emerge from the body in pairs, evenly spaced along the torso. Each leg is structured like a mammalian limb: shoulder, elbow, wrist, or hip, knee, ankle, with proper joints at each position. But the proportions are adjusted—the legs are longer than they should be for the body size, giving the creatures a spidery appearance despite their obvious mammalian nature. The legs end in paws: five digits each, with retractable claws that emerge when gripping, withdraw when walking on soft surfaces.

The fur is dense and short, in colors that range from dark brown to grey to a greenish tone that makes them blend with the moss and plant matter they live among. Some individuals have patterns: stripes, spots, patches of contrasting color, but the patterns don't correspond to any familiar species. They're unique to each individual, like fingerprints, allowing you to distinguish between them if you watch closely.

The head is somewhere between a cat and an otter: rounded skull, abbreviated snout, large eyes positioned forward for binocular vision. The eyes are dark and reflective, catching light with a green eyeshine when illuminated. The ears are small and rounded, set far back on the skull, mobile and independent. The whiskers are extensive: long, thick, projecting from the muzzle, above the eyes, from the cheeks, providing rich tactile information about the narrow spaces they navigate.

The tail is long and prehensile: a sixth or seventh limb, depending on how you count. They use it constantly—wrapping it around supports for stability, hanging from it to free up the other limbs for manipulation, even using the tip to probe into spaces too small for the head, testing conditions before committing the body.

They're silent. No vocalizations, or none that you can hear—possibly they're communicating ultrasonically, or subsonic, in ranges beyond human perception. They move silently despite their claws and their many legs, placing each foot with precision, flowing through the space like water, like something that's learned perfect efficiency of movement.

Their population is large but hard to estimate. You see them individually or in small groups—two or three together—but never in great numbers in one place. Yet they're everywhere. Stand still in any room and within a few minutes you'll spot movement: a tender emerging from a crack in the wall, walking along a pipe, descending from a ceiling channel, ascending from a floor gap. The total population might be dozens, might be hundreds. They occupy a parallel infrastructure, a network of spaces threaded through the building.

Their behavior is focused and purposeful: they tend the plants. This is their primary function, the activity you see them engaged in most frequently. They prune, using their teeth to cut away dead leaves, diseased sections, growth that's extending into spaces it shouldn't occupy. They're precise about it, taking specific leaves while leaving others, cutting stems at particular points, removing exact amounts of material.

They carry away the pruned matter in their mouths, transporting it through their tunnel network to collection points, usually near where the Operators are working. The Operators incorporate this material into the manufacturing process, or into the recycling system, or use it to feed specific sections of growth that need additional resources.

The tenders also harvest. Certain plants produce fruits or seeds that the tenders collect: those purple waxy things the size of crabapples, smaller berries, seeds from the flowering vines. They gather these methodically, checking each plant, taking the ripe ones, leaving the immature. They store the harvest in caches: hollow spaces in the walls, alcoves in the machinery, specially maintained chambers where dozens of fruits are piled carefully.

Some of these stores are for their own consumption—you can find evidence of feeding, seeds and skins discarded—but most of the harvest seems to serve the system. The Operators collect from these caches, using the seeds in the manufacturing process, pressing oils from them, or planting them in specific locations where new growth is desired.

They also farm fungus. In the deep spaces, the narrow channels where they spend most of their time, they maintain fungus gardens: carefully cultivated patches of edible mushrooms, growing on substrate the tenders provide by bringing in plant matter, shaping it into beds, inoculating it with fungal spores. They tend these gardens closely: removing competing organisms, adjusting moisture (urinating on the beds to add nitrogen, bringing in damp moss to regulate humidity), harvesting mature fruiting bodies.

The mushrooms they grow are small, pale, and appear to be a species that exists nowhere else in the complex, possibly domesticated over many generations, possibly created through selective breeding, possibly something the tenders brought with them from wherever they came from or have always been.

They consume some of the mushrooms themselves—you find evidence of feeding stations, small clearings in the tunnel network where mushroom fragments accumulate—but again, much of the harvest goes to the system. They bring mushrooms out and leave them where the Operators can collect them, or where the birds can carry them to specific locations, possibly to be used as inoculant for new fungus gardens in other areas.

They maintain the physical plant infrastructure. The vine-columns that support the building are hollow, and the tenders live inside them, moving up and down through the interior spaces. They clean these spaces: removing debris, dead insects, shed plant matter that accumulates. They repair damage: when a vine is wounded (cracked by stress, damaged by equipment), tenders converge on the injury, cleaning it, packing it with a material they produce—possibly saliva mixed with plant matter, possibly something secreted—that seals the wound and promotes healing.

They regulate water flow. The complex has a vascular system: sap moving through the vines, water collecting in the moisture cycles and draining through specific channels. The tenders manage this system, clearing blockages, opening or closing flow channels by manipulating vine-valves (places where the plant has developed sphincter-like structures that can be opened or closed), directing water to areas that need it, draining areas that are accumulating too much moisture.

You can watch them working on a vine-column, moving on the exterior surface, and see them stop at a particular spot, dig into the surface with their claws, and manipulate something inside—and immediately water will begin or stop flowing through a nearby channel, or a section of plant will visibly perk up as moisture reaches it.

They interact with the insects in a way that looks like herding. When insect populations accumulate too heavily in an area, tenders will arrive and begin moving through the swarm, and the insects will flow away from them, redirected, streaming toward a different area. The tenders aren't hunting them—aren't feeding—just managing their distribution, keeping them from overwhelming certain spaces, concentrating them where they're needed for pollination or decomposition.

They cache more than food. You find caches of objects: collections of small mechanical parts—bolts, washers, springs, wire segments—stored together in organized piles. Collections of seeds sorted by type, each species in its own pile. Collections of stones, small pebbles brought from somewhere, all similar in size, piled in pyramids. The purpose isn't always clear, but the organization is intentional, maintained, added to over time.

They mark territory, or possibly navigation points, with scent. They have scent glands—exact location unclear, possibly on the flanks, possibly near the tail base—that they rub against surfaces at specific points. The scent is subtle, barely detectable to human olfaction, something like dried grass and earth. The scent marks appear at junctions, at the entrances to important channels, at locations where decisions about route would need to be made. Possibly they're boundary markers. Possibly they're signage for a navigation system readable by scent.

Their interaction with the Operators is transactional and specific. A tender will emerge from the wall near an Operator that's working, carrying something—a component, a fruit, a bundle of fiber—and deposit it within the Operator's reach, then retreat. The Operator will incorporate the item into whatever process it's engaged in. The reverse also happens: an Operator will place an object in a specific location, and later a tender will retrieve it and carry it away into the infrastructure.

They coordinate with the birds similarly. A tender will bring something out and leave it at one of the relay stations, and birds will arrive to transport it. Birds will deposit items at locations where tenders can access them, and the tenders will carry those items into the deep infrastructure where birds can't follow.

They're serving as the connection between the macro-scale that the Operators work at and the micro-scale inside the walls, maintaining the hidden infrastructure that supports the visible systems.

They're social with each other but in limited ways. You rarely see them together except during specific activities. They'll cooperate on large tasks: two or three working together to move a heavy fruit, to repair a major breach in a vine-column, to clear a blocked channel. They'll groom each other occasionally: sitting together, using teeth and paws to clean each other's fur, remove parasites (though parasites seem rare or absent here), maintain the coat condition.

Sometimes you'll see what might be play: two tenders chasing each other through the infrastructure in patterns that look purposeless, or wrestling gently, gripping each other with paws and rolling, or hanging from supports and swatting at each other with their tails. These interactions are brief and seem to involve young individuals, smaller than the adults, possibly learning movement and navigation through play.

They breed somewhere in the hidden spaces. You occasionally see very small tenders, clearly juveniles, following adults through the infrastructure, mimicking their movements, being led to fungus gardens and feeding, being shown how to prune plants. The adults are patient teachers, demonstrating repeatedly, allowing the young to practice under supervision.

Where they sleep, where they nest, where the breeding and birthing happens—all of this occurs in spaces you can't access, deeper in the infrastructure, in chambers that might exist or might be created as needed by the living structure responding to the tenders' requirements.

Their lifespan is unclear. You can't distinguish age except in the juveniles. The adults all look equally mature, equally capable. Possibly they live for years. Possibly the population cycles through generations quickly, but with complete transmission of learned behaviors, creating continuity that looks like longevity.

They're aware of observers in a different way than the Operators or birds. They don't acknowledge you directly, don't pause to assess you. But they route around you smoothly, always maintaining distance, never allowing themselves to be cornered or approached closely. If you move toward one, it will flow away, disappearing into an opening with liquid grace. They don't flee—don't show fear—they simply maintain a buffer zone, a minimum distance, adjusting their position continuously to preserve it.

But they're watching. You can feel it. When you're in a room, there are tenders in the walls, tracking your position by sound or vibration or scent, adjusting their movements to account for where you are, possibly warning others of your location through means you can't detect.

## THE SMALL CITIZENS

The insects don't behave as individuals. Even walking past a single gnat, watching it hover, you're observing a fragment of a larger system temporarily isolated, like watching a single cell separated from tissue—technically independent, but functioning best as part of the whole.

They exist in clouds, swarms, columns, sheets, geometric formations that range from a few dozen individuals to thousands. The species diversity is extreme: gnats and midges forming the hazy, diffuse clouds; beetles creating the tighter, darker formations; moths contributing the twilight columns that appear during certain light conditions; flies of many varieties adding chaotic elements; mosquitoes (non-biting, or possibly unable to recognize human blood as food) contributing to the aerial masses.

There are also wasps—paper wasps, mud daubers, possibly yellowjackets—but they're not aggressive, don't defend territory, seem to have abandoned those behaviors in favor of whatever purpose they serve here. Bees appear too: several species, including what look like honeybees and various native bees, all collecting from the flowering plants but also participating in the geometric formations, adding their flight patterns to the collective movement.

The formations are responsive. A cloud of gnats will hang in the air, apparently random, but throw a stone through it and the cloud will open a hole exactly the size of the stone's path, then close behind it. The insects aren't individually dodging—there's no time—they're functioning as a fluid medium, the collective responding faster than individual reaction time allows.

The formations are persistent but mobile. A column of insects will connect floor and ceiling, rotating slowly, maintaining its structure while drifting laterally across a room over the course of minutes. A grid will hang in the air, beetles holding positions at precise intervals, the whole grid shifting as a unit, rising or falling, always maintaining the spacing.

The patterns correlate with architecture. Where machinery has a spiral structure, insects form spirals above it. Where there's a grid of rivets on a wall, insects form matching grids in the air nearby. Where a mechanism has rotating components, insects orbit the mechanism in synchronized circles. They're either reading the environment and reflecting it, or responding to fields or emanations from the machinery that happen to correlate with visible structure, or actively being instructed by the place itself about what patterns to form.

The formations change with the moisture cycles. When humidity peaks, the insects disperse into looser distributions, individual movement more visible, the collective structures dissolving into something more like normal insect behavior. Then as the air dries, they reaggregate, the patterns forming tighter, the geometric precision increasing.

They pollinate, but systematically. The flowering plants throughout the complex need pollination, and insects provide it, but not randomly. A bee will visit every flower on a vine in sequence, left to right, top to bottom, with obvious intentionality. A moth will move between plants of the same species, visiting only those, ignoring others, serving as a specialist pollinator. The collective result is complete pollination coverage—every flower gets visited, genetic material gets transferred between plants optimally.

They decompose and recycle. The insects include many detritivore species: larvae in the accumulated plant matter, adults feeding on fungus and dead tissue. They're breaking down the constant input of dead leaves, fallen flowers, pruned material, the bodies of dead animals, reducing everything to component nutrients that re-enter the soil (where soil exists) or get absorbed directly by the plant matter that serves as substrate.

There are predatory insects hunting other insects: dragonflies (inside a building, somehow), hunting flies; wasps catching other insects to provision nests. But the predation seems managed, regulated, never overwhelming any population, maintaining balance rather than creating boom-and-bust cycles.

The insects maintain chambers within the structure—hives, nests, nurseries. Some are visible: paper wasp nests hanging from rafters, constructed around machinery, incorporating machine parts into the nest structure so that a pulley or a pipe section becomes part of the nest architecture. Others are hidden: inside the hollow vines, in gaps in the machinery, in spaces the tenders have cleared and designated for insect use.

These colonies are productive. The paper wasps are creating paper—not for cells, or not only for cells, but producing sheets of paper that they leave in stacks, which the Operators collect and incorporate into their manufacturing. Bees are producing wax and honey, small amounts, stored in comb built in sheltered locations, and both products get harvested and used (wax for sealing, honey possibly as food, possibly as raw material).

The moths are producing silk. You find it everywhere: fine strands connecting surfaces, accumulating in corners, being collected by the tenders and wound onto small spools. The silk isn't random webbing—it's being produced deliberately, in quantity, as a material resource.

They distribute spores and seeds. The insects that move through the fungal regions get coated in spores, then carry those spores throughout the complex, inoculating new areas. Insects visiting flowers collect pollen but also pick up seeds—small ones that stick to their bodies—and transport them, dropping them in cracks and crevices where they germinate, spreading plants through the structure.

They clean. Insects feed on algae and mold that try to establish on surfaces, keeping growth in check. They consume spilled sap, leaked oils, any organic residue, preventing accumulation, maintaining cleanliness. The space stays remarkably clean despite the constant plant growth and moisture, and the insects are a major part of that maintenance.

They respond to signals. When a bird sings certain calls, insects will converge or disperse, their formations building or dissolving in response to the acoustic signal. When the light conditions change—one of those moving patches of brighter illumination passing through—insects will track it, concentrating in the light, or avoiding it, depending on species and context.

When an Operator places a hand with fingers in a particular configuration, insects will land on it, covering the hand, and remain there as the Operator moves through the space, then depart when the fingers shift to a different pattern. The hand becomes a temporary perch, or possibly the insects are being transported, relocated deliberately to a new area where their services are needed.

They form larger cooperative structures occasionally. You'll find sections where different insect formations have merged: a beetle grid intersecting with a moth column, the two patterns maintaining separately but occupying the same space, insects of different species moving through each other's formations without disruption. Sometimes this creates interference patterns, moiré effects where the combined movement creates emergent visual rhythms not present in either formation alone.

There are specialist behaviors. Some insects—appears to be certain beetles—spend all their time on the machinery, walking across surfaces, exploring every gap and joint. They might be inspecting, detecting stress or damage through means unavailable to the larger inhabitants. When they find something—exact criteria unknown—they'll gather at that spot, a cluster of five or ten beetles, and remain there until a tender or an Operator arrives to investigate.

Other insects—some of the flies—seem to serve as mobile sensors, flying rapid patrol routes through the complex, covering large distances quickly, then returning to specific stations where they land and remain still for several minutes (reporting?), then departing on another patrol.

The mosquitoes gather near the moisture pools when they form, hovering over the water, but not breeding in them—the pools don't last long enough for larval development. Instead, they seem to drink, taking up water, then dispersing to other locations where they rest on plants. Possibly they're contributing to plant watering, transporting tiny amounts of water to foliage that needs it, serving as a distributed irrigation system.

The life cycles are completing. You find eggs: insect eggs on plants, in soil pockets, in hidden chambers. Larvae developing: beetle grubs in the decomposing plant matter, moth caterpillars on specific food plants, wasp larvae in the cells of nests. Pupae transforming in their various ways. And eclosion: new adults emerging, wings expanding, bodies hardening, then immediately joining the formations, participating in the patterns.

The population is self-sustaining, reproducing, replacing losses, maintaining stable numbers or possibly growing—it's hard to assess total population over time when they're distributed through so much space and constantly in motion.

They're aware of scale. The insects interact appropriately with organisms of vastly different sizes: avoiding the Operators entirely, landing on the tenders in neutral ways (neither feeding nor feared), treating birds as mobile perches or as predators depending on the bird's current behavior, and treating humans as large obstacles to route around.

They maintain temporal patterns. Certain formations appear only at specific times relative to the moisture cycles: appearing two minutes before a cycle, persisting through it, dissolving three minutes after. Other patterns are tied to longer rhythms, appearing once every few hours, lasting for twenty minutes, then dispersing until their next scheduled appearance.

Some insects are solitary, behaving more like typical individuals: a beetle walking across a surface, exploring, feeding, not part of any formation. A single bee working a flower cluster alone. A moth resting on a wall, wings folded. These individuals seem to be off-duty, or performing tasks that don't require collective action, or possibly they're injured or old, excluded from the collective, living out their remaining time independently.

But even the solitary individuals will respond to collective signals—when the main swarms shift, the individuals will also adjust position, maintaining some connection to the whole even while acting independently.

## THE OTHERS

There are presences without clear physical form.

You'll stand in a room, alone, no Operators visible, no large creatures present, and feel observed. Not by the birds—you can account for them, see them on their perches. Not by the tenders—you know their signature, the slight sounds of movement in the walls. This is something else: attention without source, regard without visible regard-er.

The darkness moves. This has been noted, but it's more than simple flow—sometimes the darkness concentrates into volumes that have boundary and density, that are darker than the surrounding darkness, that hold position for minutes at a time before dissipating. These dark-forms don't interact with anything physically—shine a light at one and the light passes through, revealing normal space behind it. But they're present in some mode, occupying space, possibly observing.

There are sounds without sources. The factory sounds—clanks, hisses, the grind of gears—have explanations, actual machinery producing them. But there are other sounds: footsteps in empty corridors, heavy and deliberate, that stop when you approach. Breathing sounds, the deep respiration of something large, coming from directions where nothing large is present. Once, a voice: a single word spoken clearly in a language not quite parseable, close enough to language to recognize as speech, far enough to be incomprehensible.

There are temperature anomalies. Cold spots: areas where the temperature drops fifteen or twenty degrees within a few feet, with no physical explanation—no drafts, no change in the structure, just a localized cold that moves slowly through the space over the course of several minutes, then dissipates. Warm spots similarly: areas of increased heat that feel like standing near a body, the thermal signature of something alive, but nothing visible.

The moisture pools sometimes contain things other than water or sap. A pool will form during a cycle, and in it will be a liquid that's thicker, darker, viscous and slow-moving. Reach into it and it's warm, blood-temperature, but it's not blood—it's something else, organic-seeming, possibly cellular, possibly some substance the building is producing or secreting. These pools drain away like the others, leaving no residue, but while they're present they feel like wounds, like the building is bleeding something.

Objects appear. You'll pass through a room, note its contents, return an hour later and find something new: a tool lying on a surface where nothing was before, old but functional, appropriate to the machinery nearby. A piece of cloth, folded neatly, that wasn't there. A small machine component, carefully placed, as though left out for use. The Operators might be moving these things, or the tenders, but sometimes the objects appear in locations neither could easily access—in sealed spaces, on high ledges, in areas where nothing has been disturbed.

There are faces in the machinery. Not literally—not carved or constructed—but the pareidolia is overwhelming. Arrangements of pipes and valves that form eye-mouth-nose patterns, that you can't unsee once you've noticed them. These faces aren't static: return later and the same machinery has a different expression, because some valve has shifted, some pipe has rotated, and the face looks different—surprised where it was neutral, angry where it was calm.

And sometimes the faces blink. A valve will close-open in a rhythm too deliberate to be mechanical fluctuation, a motion that reads as a blink, that feels intentional, aimed at you.

The vines pulse with that slow respiration, but occasionally one will pulse differently: faster, or with greater amplitude, or in a pattern—three strong pulses, three weak, three strong—that feels like signaling. Stand near the vine when this happens and you can feel it in your chest, your own heartbeat trying to synchronize with the rhythm, your breathing wanting to match it.

There are shadows without objects. Light from above casting shadows as expected, but also shadows that don't correspond to anything, that fall across surfaces with no object positioned to cast them. Shadows that move against the direction of the light, sliding across walls, pooling in corners, stretching and contracting in ways that violate the geometry.

Some of these shadows are person-shaped. Human-shaped, walking shapes, standing shapes, sometimes just the shadow of a hand reaching across a surface. They're brief—visible for a few seconds, then gone—but while present they're distinct, clear-edged, as though the thing casting them is just out of phase with visibility, present but not present, real but not material.

There are the almost-seens. You'll turn your head and catch something in peripheral vision: a figure, a face, something moving. Turn to look directly and there's nothing, or there's something—a particular arrangement of plant matter, a pile of equipment—that explains what you saw but doesn't fully account for it, feels like a substitute, like the real thing withdrew at the last moment and left a decoy.

The building dreams. This is certain. The architecture shifts not randomly but according to some internal logic, some narrative the building is exploring. The factory becomes more factory-like during certain periods: the machinery runs more consistently, the industrial sounds increase, the brick walls firm up and stay solid longer. Then it shifts toward the organic: more flowers blooming, the vines growing more luxuriant, the plant matter advancing, and the machinery quiets, becomes more like sculpture, more like memory of industry than active industry.

These shifts feel like sleep cycles: active periods and dormant periods, with the space dreaming during the transitions, trying out different configurations, exploring possibilities of what it could be.

And in the deep dreams, things emerge. You'll find a room configured in a way that doesn't fit with the rest of the complex: too finished, too intentional, as though imported from somewhere else. A perfect recreation of an office, complete with desk and chair and papers on the desk (blank, but present), that lasts for perhaps an hour before dissolving back into the standard factory-garden ambiguity. A hallway lined with doors, all closed, that appears between two known rooms, providing a new route that exists for one transit then vanishes, leaving the original configuration restored.

The moisture cycles are the building exhaling, but what is it inhaling? Between cycles, there's a slight negative pressure, a sense of air being drawn inward from everywhere simultaneously, converging on no particular point, simply being absorbed by the structure. The building breathes in the air and breathes out humidity, as though it's extracting something from the atmosphere, using it, returning the processed remnant.

There are chemical gradients. Areas where the air smells more strongly of specific compounds: ozone in some zones, suggesting electrical activity or UV exposure from unknown sources. Ammonia hints in areas where the tenders are most active, predictable. But also stranger scents: something like copper or iron in areas where no metal is exposed, something like storm-air in windless interior spaces, something organic and complex—pheromone-like—that changes how you feel, makes you alert or calm or vaguely uneasy depending on the specific signature.

The light anomalies are frequent. Those moving patches of brighter illumination don't correspond to any physical source—there's nothing above projecting them, no skylights or gaps in the canopy overhead. They simply exist: regions where the darkness is less, moving through the space on paths that are consistent enough to be predictable but complex enough to suggest purpose. Following one leads you through a winding route that touches multiple rooms, visits several key pieces of machinery, approaches areas where the Operators are working, as though the light is conducting a patrol or an inspection.

Sometimes the light stops above you, and you stand in a pool of relative brightness for a minute or more before it moves on, and the sensation is of being examined, assessed, possibly approved of or found wanting, though there's no indication of which.

There are threshold effects. Pass through certain doorways and you'll feel a transition that's more than spatial: a slight resistance, a moment of increased pressure or temperature, a sense of crossing a boundary that matters. Some doorways are neutral, purely architectural. Others are barriers of some kind, membranes between states or zones, and moving through them requires a small effort, carries a small cost—you emerge slightly dizzy, or with a brief headache, or with a moment of disorientation where you're not sure which direction you're facing.

The electrical systems are partially active, wiring running through conduits, but most of it is dead, unpowered. Except occasionally you'll see a spark: a brief arc between two conductors, discharge from nowhere, lasting a fraction of a second. Or a wire will glow faintly for a moment, cherry-red, as though carrying impossible current, then cool immediately. The building has energy moving through it in ways that don't follow the installed infrastructure, possibly through the living plant matter, possibly through the moisture in the air, creating occasional intersections with the old electrical system that produce visible effects.

There are magnetic anomalies. Compasses don't work consistently—they'll spin, or point in directions that don't correspond to any reasonable magnetic field, or lock onto directions that seem to be defined by the structure rather than by the Earth. Metal objects sometimes stick to surfaces that aren't magnetic, or feel heavier or lighter than they should, as though local gravity is variable or something else is adding or subtracting from the gravitational pull.

Time has already been noted as flexible, but it's more specifically wrong in certain locations. There's a stairwell where climbing feels faster than it should—you count fewer steps than the vertical distance traveled would require. A corridor where walking its length takes three minutes in one direction, seven minutes returning, with no change in pace, no obvious spatial distortion.

There's a room with a clock—mechanical, spring-driven, still running—that keeps accurate time checked against watches. But standing in that room feels subjectively longer than the clock indicates; fifteen minutes by the clock feels like thirty by experience, and emerging leaves you confused about how much time has passed, needing to reconcile the objective measurement with the subjective experience.

The building contains history, memories encoded in its structure. The machinery remembers being operated by humans: you can find graffiti scratched into paint surfaces, notes left in handwriting, personal marks. But there's no evidence of recent human presence—no bodies, no signs of violence or departure, just absence. The people who built and ran this place are gone, and the structure has been taken over, colonized, transformed by the new inhabitants and by the building's own process of becoming-other.

But occasionally the building replays its past. You'll hear the sounds of industrial activity: multiple machines running, voices calling to each other, the background noise of active manufacturing. The sounds are distant, muffled, as though happening in an adjacent room, but following them leads nowhere—the sounds stay ahead of you, retreating as you approach, until they fade entirely.

Or you'll see motion: a fleeting glimpse of human figures working, visible for a moment before dissolving into the current reality of empty machinery or Operators or simply vacant space. The building is haunted not by ghosts of the dead but by recordings of the past, traces that occasionally become active, play out their brief loop, then subside.

The boundaries are ambiguous. Where does the building end and the surrounding landscape begin? The structure has walls, clearly, but the walls phase between solid and permeable, and the vine-growth extends beyond the walls, possibly for significant distances. The basin the factory sits in might be entirely within the factory's influence, might be part of the same organism or system.

And the canopy overhead—is that outside, or is it ceiling, or is the building much taller than it appears and the high structure is simply invisible in the darkness? No amount of observation resolves this. The vertical extent of the place is undetermined, possibly infinite, possibly tightly bounded, possibly changing.

The building is becoming. It's in process, moving toward some configuration, some final form or function. The transformation from factory to garden, or the integration of factory and garden into something that transcends both categories, is ongoing. The Operators are managing this transformation, or serving it, or possibly they're its agents—emissaries from the future state of the building, working to bring that state into being.

The entire complex is a cocoon, a chrysalis, a larval form of something that will eventually emerge complete. Or it's already complete, already what it's supposed to be, and will maintain this state indefinitely, a stable hybrid, a new kind of space that's found its equilibrium.

The presence you feel—that watchfulness, that sense of being within something aware—is possibly the building itself, conscious in some distributed way, its awareness spread across the entire structure, perceiving through the sensors of its inhabitants (the birds' eyes, the tenders' whiskers, the Operators' multiple gazes), processing through the collective intelligence of the insect formations, thinking in the slow rhythms of plant growth and the faster cycles of moisture and breath.

You are inside something alive. Something that notices you. Something that, for reasons unknown, permits your presence, routes its processes around you, includes you temporarily in its ongoing transformation while maintaining all its functions, all its mysterious manufacturing, all its careful self-maintenance.

What it wants, if it wants anything—what it's building toward, what purpose drives the endless production and the constant tending and the strange integration of industrial and organic—remains unreadable. But you exist within it, surrounded by its breathing walls, accompanied by its many small citizens and its enigmatic Operators, while it continues becoming whatever it will be.
