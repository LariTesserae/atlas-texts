# What Lives There

They have no name for themselves—naming requires a kind of stillness they do not possess.

The **woodbones** move through the hollow in packs of thirty to sixty, their bodies a compromise between efficiency and accident. Six legs—four for locomotion, two partially adapted for grasping, though the grasping is clumsy, achieved more through hooking and pinning than true manipulation. Their fur grows in whorls and cowlicks that follow the underlying musculature, creating patterns: stripes that aren't quite stripes, spots that drift into streaks. Coloration ranges from moss-gray to amber-brown, and they are always dirty, always matted with sap and spores and the general accumulation of moving through dense space.

Their faces are blunt and wide, with eyes positioned more laterally than forward—they see motion and threat better than detail. The eyes themselves are too large, slightly protruding, and they reflect the bioluminescent light in unsettling flashes of green and gold. Their mouths contain omnivore teeth: flat molars for grinding plant matter, sharp incisors for tearing flesh, and between these, confusion—teeth that seem to serve no function, or functions that aren't clear, as if their dentition was designed by committee and never finalized.

They are intelligent in bursts.

You watch a pack encounter a fallen branch blocking a passage, and for long minutes they mill around it stupidly, bumping into it, each other, making low confused sounds. Then one—always a different one, there's no consistent leadership—suddenly understands, and communicates this through posture and vocalization, and the pack coordinates: half lifting, half pushing, moving the branch in a maneuver that requires genuine collaboration. Then immediately after, they revert: wandering, grazing on moss, investigating nothing in particular. The intelligence comes and goes like signal through static.

Their calls are what you remember most. A repertoire of sounds that moves from low chest-rumbles to high chittering to sudden, shocking shrieks that seem designed to terrify. But also: sounds that approach language. Repeated phrases—if phrases is the right word—used in consistent contexts. A particular warble that precedes group hunting. A descending whistle that signals danger. A complex vocalization, almost a song, that they produce at the structures, the half-built things, though what it means or why they make it remains opaque.

They build, but they build like children, like dreamers, like creatures possessed by an idea they don't fully understand.

You find their constructions everywhere—piles of wood arranged in rough domes, walls of stacked stone that go nowhere, platforms elevated on stilts for no apparent purpose. Sometimes they work in focused silence, passing materials in efficient chains, fitting pieces with surprising precision. Other times they work in chaos, each individual adding to the structure without regard for what others are doing, creating architectural cacophony. And often they simply stop—mid-construction, mid-thought—and wander away, and don't return for hours or days, or never return, and other packs find the abandoned project and either continue it in completely new directions or dismantle it for materials.

The woodbones use tools, but barely.

Mostly they use rocks held in their hooking forelimbs: choppers for cutting vines, hammers for breaking open the armored beetles, scrapers for processing bark into fibrous material. They don't make the tools—they find appropriate stones and use them until lost or dropped, then find others. But occasionally, very occasionally, you see something more: a stone that's been deliberately flaked, showing multiple removal scars, creating a sharper edge. A bone that's been abraded against rock until one end is pointed. These refined tools appear in their structures sometimes, placed with care, though whether as useful objects or something else—offerings? decorations? forgotten experiments?—is impossible to determine.

They hunt in coordination but feed in chaos.

When they bring down one of the fliers—leaping in concert, grabbing with multiple limbs, bearing it down through weight and number—the kill is efficient, almost practiced. But the moment it stops moving, the pack descends into competitive frenzy. They tear and yank and squabble, each trying to claim the best portions, and the noise is tremendous: snarling, snapping, screaming. Smaller pack members get pushed out entirely, forced to wait for scraps. Yet when the feeding is done, the pack reconvenes as if nothing happened, grooming each other, vocalizing in what sounds like contentment. The violence and the cooperation exist without contradiction.

They care for their young with surprising tenderness.

The juveniles are smaller, fuzzier, with proportionally enormous eyes, and they cling to the adults' backs as the pack moves. When danger threatens, the adults form a defensive circle with the young at the center—this happens instantly, instinctively, and they will hold this formation against any threat, even fire, even the collapse of the structure they're standing on. You watch a pack lose two adults defending their young from a swarm of the armored beetles, and the remaining adults immediately redistribute the orphaned juveniles among themselves without hesitation or conflict.

But also: sometimes they eat their young.

When resources are scarce—or seem to be scarce, the triggers are unclear—a pack will turn on its own juveniles. Not all of them, usually just one or two, the smallest or weakest. It happens quickly, almost matter-of-factly, and afterward the pack continues as before. The survivors show no obvious trauma. The next litter is cared for with the same tenderness. It's not cruelty—it lacks the intentionality for cruelty—but it's not quite survival strategy either. It's something in between, something that suggests they exist in a perpetual state of negotiation between what they are and what they might become.

The **laceflights** dominate the upper regions, where space opens between the root-ceiling and the tallest structures.

They are not birds—calling them birds is a failure of language, an attempt to make the alien familiar. They have wings, yes: membranes stretched between fingers of lightweight bone, but the bone is hollow in ways that seem structurally impossible, and the membranes aren't skin but something else, something that shows iridescence in the right light, fractal patterns of color that shift as they move. Their bodies are narrow, streamlined, with keeled sternums that protrude dramatically. Four wings—not two—arranged in an X-configuration that lets them achieve hovering and sudden direction changes no Earth creature could manage.

Their heads are small and pointed, beaked but flexibly so, the beak more like hardened lips than true horn. Eyes face forward—unusual here—giving them binocular vision, giving them the focused attention of predators. But they have other eyes too: simple ocelli arranged across their foreheads, detecting light and shadow, giving them nearly 360-degree awareness of movement. When they focus on you with all eyes at once, the intelligence is undeniable and uncomfortable.

They navigate through sound more than sight.

Their calls are constant in flight: chirps and clicks that echo off the wooden surfaces, creating a three-dimensional sound-map of the space. They can fly in absolute darkness—you observe this in the deepest chambers where bioluminescence fails—navigating the tangled architecture perfectly through echolocation alone. But they also produce sounds that aren't navigational: complex songs, almost melodic, that they sing to each other during their spiraling courtship flights. These songs have structure—verse and chorus, repetition and variation—and different individuals have different songs, suggesting cultural transmission, personal identity, something approaching art.

They collide constantly, but this seems to be social rather than accidental.

When two laceflights meet in flight, they often bump deliberately—chest to chest, wing to wing—tumbling together for a moment before separating and continuing. Sometimes this is clearly aggressive: they strike hard, snap beaks, shriek challenges. But more often it seems playful or communicative, a aerial handshake, a physical conversation. Juveniles do it more frequently, crashing into each other and adults with apparent enthusiasm, and the adults tolerate this with what might be patience, might be indifference.

They nest in the structures—the builders' constructions—in a relationship that might be parasitism, might be symbiosis.

The laceflights wedge their nests into corners and gaps in the half-built domes and platforms, using the structures as foundations, adding their own materials: plant down, moss, shed fur from the woodbones, all woven into cup-shaped nests barely large enough for their narrow bodies. They defend these nests aggressively, diving at any creature that approaches, striking with their beaked faces, their wing-claws. The builders sometimes tolerate this, sometimes incorporate the nests deliberately into their designs, building around them. Other times they destroy the nests casually while expanding a structure, and the laceflights simply relocate and rebuild without apparent grudge.

They hunt the smaller fliers—the ones that use jet-propulsion—in coordinated flocks.

A dozen laceflights will formation-fly in widening spirals, herding a cloud of the smaller creatures into tighter and tighter space, compressing them through geometry and fear. Then the attack: the flock collapses inward and the feeding begins mid-air, laceflights snatching prey without landing, without slowing. They eat in flight, swallowing small prey whole, tearing larger prey apart with beak and claw while maintaining altitude. The precision required is extraordinary—they never collide during these hunts, never interfere with each other's strikes, each seeming to know exactly where the others are and will be.

But they also feed on fruit—on the flowers and seed-pods produced by the inverted trees, the horizontal pillars.

You watch them hover delicately, almost hummingbird-like, their four wings blurring, as they probe blossoms with their flexible beaks, emerging dusted with pollen. They seem to prefer certain flowers, visiting them repeatedly, and the flowers respond—opening wider, producing more nectar—suggesting ancient coevolution, or perhaps rapid evolution, or perhaps the flowers themselves possess awareness and preference.

The laceflights show tool-use that borders on craftsmanship.

They collect objects—shiny beetle carapaces, fragments of the soft copper metal, interesting stones—and incorporate them into their nests. Not randomly: arranged in patterns, sorted by color or size, displayed in ways that suggest aesthetic consideration. Males do this more than females, and females visit multiple nests before choosing, spending time examining the arrangements. Sexual selection based on artistic merit. But also: you observe a laceflight using a twig held in its wing-claws to probe a hole in wood, extracting grubs, then dropping the twig and forgetting it. Intelligence and instinct blur.

They have culture—regional dialects in their songs, local variations in nest-building techniques, specific flight patterns unique to different chambers.

A laceflight from the eastern galleries sounds different from one in the western chambers, and they seem to recognize this, responding more readily to familiar dialects. Juveniles learn their home dialect from parents and neighbors, but occasionally you hear a youngster incorporating phrases from other regions, creating novel combinations, innovating. And very rarely, a new phrase spreads: you hear it first from one individual, then a few more, then many, until it becomes common in that area's repertoire. Cultural evolution in observable time.

The **hollowcages** are wrong in ways that make you doubt your own perception.

They appear to be structures—walking structures—frameworks of living willow-branches woven into roughly cubic forms, twice the height of a human, moving on stilted roots that emerge from their bottom edges like spider legs. They walk slowly, deliberately, each step requiring time as the roots probe and grip and shift weight. The willow branches that form their walls and roofs are alive: leafing, flowering, growing continuously, and the creatures—if creatures they are—seem to prune themselves, biting off or breaking off excess growth to maintain their basic shape.

But they are not empty cages.

Inside each one, visible through the gaps in the willow-weave, are other creatures. Multiple species, living in the interior space as if the hollowcage were a mobile habitat, a walking territory. You see small mammals nesting in the upper corners. Birds—or bird-like things—perching on the internal branches. Insects crawling over every surface. Even plants: vines growing through the framework, fungi sprouting from accumulated debris on the floor.

The relationship is unclear and disturbing.

Sometimes the hollowcage seems unaware of its inhabitants, simply walking, and they simply live, existing in parallel. But other times: the cage pauses at a fruiting tree, positions itself optimally, and the inhabitants feed. The cage detours around danger, and the inhabitants benefit from the protection. A predator attacks an inhabitant, and the cage responds—contracting its walls, crushing the predator, expelling the corpse through gaps in its weave—then resuming its walk.

Are the inhabitants parasites? Symbionts? Organs?

You observe a hollowcage for hours, trying to determine if it's one organism or many, and the answer keeps shifting. The willow framework has root-legs that move in coordination—that suggests unity, central control. But the inhabitants move independently, feeding and breeding and dying without the framework responding—that suggests separation. Yet when an inhabitant births young, the framework seems to accommodate, growing new internal structures, creating appropriate nesting spaces. When an inhabitant dies, the framework helps remove the corpse, pushing it through the walls with deliberate movement.

They communicate, or something like communication happens.

When two hollowcages encounter each other, they stop, face each other, and their inhabitants emerge—climbing out through the willow walls, moving between the two cages, examining each other's frameworks and interiors. Some inhabitants transfer permanently, moving from one cage to the other. Others return to their original cage. The frameworks sway and creak during this process, branches touching and withdrawing, root-legs shifting position. It looks like negotiation, like trade, like social interaction between creatures that might not be creatures in any conventional sense.

They build, but only specific things.

The hollowcages construct bridges—simple spans of woven branches connecting elevated surfaces—but only in places where they themselves cannot pass. The bridges are clearly for the smaller fauna, for the woodbones and others who use them immediately and constantly. Why do the hollowcages build these? No one knows, no one can know, but they do, patiently weaving branches over days, creating infrastructure for others, then walking away.

They also create gardens—clearings in the moss where they plant things.

A hollowcage will stop, dig shallow pits with its root-legs, place seeds or cuttings in the pits (where did it get them? were they inside it? did inhabitants provide them?), then cover them carefully. It waters them with a liquid it exudes from its root-tips—not the not-water, something else, something that makes plants grow with visible speed. Then it leaves, and sometimes returns weeks later to tend the garden, pruning and watering again. Sometimes the garden is found and consumed by other fauna. Sometimes it thrives and becomes a permanent feature. The hollowcage seems unconcerned either way.

Inside the cages, society develops.

The inhabitants aren't random—certain species consistently coexist in specific hollowcages, suggesting preference or compatibility or selection. You observe a cage whose inhabitants include: a pair of small arboreal mammals that maintain the internal plant growth, eating some and pruning more; a colony of social insects that clean debris and carry away waste; several birds that hunt the insects but not enough to exterminate them, maintaining balance; and a single larger creature, cat-sized, that sleeps in the center and seems to serve no function except perhaps intimidation, perhaps protection. They coexist in obvious equilibrium.

And the inhabitants decorate.

They arrange objects inside their mobile home: colored stones, pretty flowers, shed feathers, beetle shells. They create defined spaces—sleeping areas separate from eating areas, specific perches for specific individuals. They maintain the framework too, clearing dead leaves, repairing damaged branches. The hollowcage provides structure and mobility; the inhabitants provide maintenance and something like purpose. Neither could explain this relationship, but both participate fully.

The hollowcages reproduce, and it's profoundly strange.

You watch one stop walking, root itself more deeply, and begin to thicken, expand. Over days it grows dramatically, doubling in size, and its inhabitants become agitated, more active, adding materials to specific areas. Then: the framework splits, not breaking but dividing deliberately, and where there was one cage there are now two, slightly smaller. And the inhabitants have divided too—half in one cage, half in the other, though how they chose is unclear. Both new cages immediately begin walking in different directions. Both already contain full ecosystems. Both are complete, functional, alive.

Sometimes a hollowcage dies, though death is a strange concept for something that might not be alive in the usual way.

The framework stops moving, roots deeply, stops pruning itself. Growth becomes wild, undirected—branches proliferate everywhere, leaves cover everything, and the cage disappears under its own excess vegetation. The inhabitants usually leave before this happens, sensing the change. The dying cage becomes a fixed structure, a permanent building, quickly colonized by other fauna. Within weeks it's indistinguishable from the other wooden architecture. The hollow canopy consumes it, incorporates it, and moves on.

The **borers**—you never see them clearly, only evidence, only glimpses, only the sound.

They are the size of small dogs, armored in chitin plates that overlap like roofing tiles, colored dark honey-brown with variations toward black at the extremities. Six legs, powerful and clawed, adapted for gripping wood. The head is dominated by mandibles: curved, serrated, wickedly sharp, in constant motion—opening, closing, grinding, never still. Behind the mandibles are smaller mouth-parts: palps and maxillae, tasting and sorting, determining what's edible (most things) and what's not (very few things).

They eat the hollow canopy.

This is their function, their purpose, their entire existence. They bore into the wooden walls and floors and ceiling-roots, excavating tunnels, processing the wood through their digestive systems, extracting nutrients, excreting processed material behind them. The sound they make is continuous: clicking, crunching, scraping, the percussion of industry. In quiet moments—if quiet moments exist here—you can hear hundreds of them working inside the walls, their tunnels honeycomb the entire structure. Sometimes you hear them in stereo: boring through the wall on both sides simultaneously, about to meet, and when they do they pause, mandibles touching, chemical communication happening, then one reverses or changes direction and they continue.

Their tunnels transform the architecture.

What was solid becomes porous, lacy, fragile. Walls thin until they're translucent. Floors develop unexpected holes. Ceiling sections suddenly collapse without warning, sometimes burying fauna below, sometimes creating new open areas that immediately fill with different species. The borers are agents of change, of entropy, constantly undermining stability. Yet the wood keeps growing, keep replacing what's lost, staying barely ahead of consumption. The hollow canopy exists in tension between growth and destruction, and the borers are half of that equation.

They are not malicious—they lack the awareness for malice.

When their boring causes a collapse that kills other creatures, they either ignore the corpses or eat them opportunistically, depending on hunger. When their tunnels intersect a nest or dwelling, they simply bore through it, oblivious to the disruption. They are force of nature, biological process given form, neither cruel nor kind, just relentlessly, mechanically hungry.

But they have some awareness, some ability to sense and respond.

They avoid boring through the hollowcages—you notice this after watching several near-encounters. A borer will be tunneling through a section of floor, approach a hollowcage's rooted position, then turn aside, bore around it, leave it intact. Chemical markers? Sound cues? Or do they recognize the cages as something special, something not-quite-wood? You cannot determine, but the pattern holds: hollowcages remain unbored, respected or at least avoided by the creatures that eat everything else.

They swarm sometimes, though rarely.

Normally borers work alone, each in its own tunnel, meeting only at intersections and mating convergences. But occasionally—triggered by what?—dozens or hundreds will emerge simultaneously and move en masse in one direction. These swarms are terrifying: a river of armored bodies, mandibles clacking, moving with collective purpose. They overwhelm anything in their path: eating, trampling, burying. Other fauna flee before them. Even the woodbone packs retreat, climbing to safety, watching from above as the swarm passes. Then, as suddenly as it formed, the swarm dissipates, individuals peeling off to bore into new sections of wood, resuming their solitary consumption.

They molt, and their cast shells litter the hollow.

The shed exoskeletons are perfect hollow duplicates: empty armor, transparent and brittle, accumulating in drifts in the lower areas. Other creatures use them: the woodbones crack them open to eat the protein-rich fragments; the laceflights collect pieces for nest decoration; the builders sometimes incorporate whole shells into their structures, creating walls that shimmer with translucent chitin. The shells become resource, material, treasure. The borers ignore them entirely, only interested in wood, only ever wood.

Their larvae are found in the wood itself.

The adults bore into fresh sections and deposit eggs deep in the tunnels, then wall off the chamber with processed sawdust, leaving the eggs isolated. The larvae hatch and begin eating immediately, expanding the chamber as they grow, molting multiple times in the dark, never seeing light until pupation completes and they emerge as adults, breaking through the wall, mandibles ready, hungry from the moment of emergence. Some larvae never emerge—their chambers become tombs when structural collapses seal them permanently. Others emerge into unexpected places: the interior of a hollowcage, a woodbone nest, the middle of one of the structures. They adapt instantly, boring away to find appropriate habitat.

The borers are ecosystem engineers, but unconscious ones.

Their tunnels become habitat for hundreds of species: smaller insects colonize the abandoned passages; amphibian-like creatures lay eggs in the tunnels that intersect the not-water pools; fungi fruit from the tunnel walls, digesting the wood the borers processed; the builders use borer tunnels as starting points for their own excavations, expanding chambers, connecting passages. The hollow canopy's complexity—its maze of chambers and corridors, its three-dimensional livability—exists primarily because the borers created it while simply trying to eat.

Sometimes you see a borer pause in its work, mandibles still, body motionless, and you wonder: Is it thinking? Sensing something? Experiencing something like contemplation? Then it resumes boring and the moment passes, probably meaningless, probably anthropomorphizing mechanical behavior. But the uncertainty remains.

The **cloudswarms** are insects but calling them insects feels insufficient, reductive, like calling a hurricane "wind."

Individually they're unremarkable: small flying insects, beetle-like, perhaps two centimeters long, black or very dark brown, with four wings that beat too fast to see. Each one alone is nothing, ignorable, barely worth notice. But they number in the tens of thousands, perhaps hundreds of thousands, and they move as one.

The swarm has shape, structure, intelligence the individual lacks.

You watch it pour through the space: a ribbon of bodies, a twisting column, a perfect sphere, a wall ten meters wide and only centimeters thick. The shapes are precise, mathematical, impossible for random aggregation to achieve. Something coordinates them—pheromones, sound, electrical signals, nobody knows—but the coordination is total. When the swarm changes direction, every individual turns simultaneously. When it splits into two swarms, the division happens along clean lines, no stragglers, no confusion.

The sound is overwhelming—a buzz that becomes roar that becomes physical sensation.

Your chest vibrates. Your teeth ache. Your vision blurs slightly from the sheer sonic pressure when a large swarm passes nearby. The sound contains information: shifts in pitch and rhythm that precede changes in swarm behavior. Other fauna respond to these acoustic cues—the woodbones become alert and defensive, the laceflights scatter to avoid interference, the borers burrow deeper to avoid being engulfed.

The swarms hunt, and the hunting is beautiful and terrible.

They identify prey—usually one of the larger flying creatures, sometimes a woodbone separated from its pack—and converge. The individual insects bite: tiny mandibles, each bite insignificant, but thousands of bites happening simultaneously. The prey is exsanguinated in seconds, literally drained by ten thousand mouths, and the corpse drops and the swarm moves on, leaving behind only dessicated remains. It's not cruelty, it's efficiency: the swarm needs protein, this is how it obtains protein, the prey's suffering is irrelevant because the swarm cannot conceive of suffering, only hunger satisfied or hunger remaining.

But they also tend the gardens.

You observe swarms visiting the cultivated plots the hollowcages maintain, and instead of destroying, they pollinate: moving between flowers in organized patterns, ensuring genetic exchange, facilitating reproduction. The same creatures that hunt so effectively also serve as ecosystem managers, maintaining plant diversity, enabling fruiting and seeding. The swarm is predator and partner both, death and life, depending on context and need.

They build nothing, but they modify everything.

The swarm strips leaves from overgrowing vines, pruning through consumption, maintaining passage through the dense foliage. They clean carcasses to bone in minutes, preventing disease, accelerating decomposition. They attack mold and fungal overgrowth, eating it back to sustainable levels. The hollow canopy would choke on its own excess without them—they are the janitorial service, the maintenance crew, keeping everything in balance through relentless consumption.

The swarm has memory—group memory, persistent across time.

They return to the same areas on predictable schedules. They avoid traps that killed members days or weeks earlier. They learn to associate certain structures with the woodbones, certain heights with the laceflights, and adjust their flight paths accordingly. The individual insects live only weeks, but the swarm persists for years, knowledge encoded in behavior patterns, chemical trails, collective habit. The swarm is immortal even as its components are ephemeral.

Sometimes swarms merge, and the merging creates visible confusion.

Two swarms meet, interpenetrate, circle each other, and for minutes the combined mass moves chaotically—different movement patterns conflicting, different objectives competing. Then: resolution. Either they separate again, each returning to previous behavior, or they fully merge into one larger swarm with blended characteristics. You watch a hunting swarm merge with a pollinating swarm, and the result hunts half-heartedly while occasionally visiting flowers, then gradually commits to hunting entirely, the pollinating behavior fading. Cultural drift in real-time.

The swarms rest—this is the revelation.

At certain times, coordinated across all swarms by unknown mechanisms, they descend to lower areas and land en masse on wooden surfaces. The buzzing stops. The movement stops. Tens of thousands of insects, motionless, covering every surface like dark carpet. The silence when this happens is shocking, disturbing, because the swarm-sound is so constant that its absence feels wrong, broken, like a heartbeat stopped. Other fauna treat this as opportunity: some feed on the resting swarm, picking off individuals; others simply move through areas usually denied them by swarm presence. After hours, the swarms wake—not gradually but suddenly, all at once—and resume their aerial existence, and the sound and motion return, and the hollow canopy feels normal again.

The **makers** are harder to describe because describing them requires accepting that you're seeing them at all, that they're not hallucination, not pattern-recognition error, not desperate anthropomorphizing of mechanical behavior.

They are bipedal. This alone makes them distinct—everything else here is four-legged or six-legged or legless or ambiguously legged, but the makers walk upright on two legs, bend forward to use two arms for manipulation, maintain clear separation between locomotion and tool-use.

They are small: perhaps a meter tall, proportioned like humans but compressed, child-sized. Covered in short fur that might be gray or brown—it's hard to tell in the perpetually shifting light. Their faces are flat and wide with forward-facing eyes that show white sclera, making their gaze trackable, making eye contact possible in ways that unnerve you. They have hands—not paws, not claws, hands—with four fingers and an opposable thumb, and the hands are constantly active: touching, grasping, manipulating, creating.

They live in the structures—the ones they build deliberately, not the abandoned husks.

You find a completed dwelling: a dome of woven branches, walls filled with packed moss for insulation, a low entrance covered with a hide flap (whose hide? better not to ask). Inside: a fire pit ringed with stones, ash still warm. Sleeping platforms raised off the ground, covered with dried plant material. Storage alcoves built into the walls containing sorted materials—stones by size, plant fiber in coiled bundles, bones cleaned and stacked. Tools hanging from pegs: choppers, scrapers, awls, hammers. The space is organized, maintained, inhabited with clear intention.

The makers are communal—groups of ten to twenty living together, working together, building together.

You watch a construction project: five makers working on a platform, each with a defined role. Two gather materials, shuttling between resources and construction site. One shapes the materials—cutting vines to length, stripping bark, sorting usable from unusable. Two assemble, weaving and lashing and testing structural integrity. They vocalize constantly: not words exactly, but phonemes that carry information, coordinating action. "Hup" when lifting together. "Tsk" for negative, don't use that. "Cree" when something is complete, come see. Language-adjacent, pre-language, or language for which you lack translation—impossible to determine.

They use fire and understand it in ways that suggest genuine comprehension.

Their fire pits are sophisticated: stone-lined to contain heat, positioned under ceiling vents in the root-canopy where smoke can escape, fed with wood they've deliberately dried and stored. They cook food before eating—roasting meat, softening tubers—showing awareness that heat changes properties, makes things better. They use fire for light when the bioluminescence is insufficient, carrying torches of resinous wood when moving through dark areas. They use fire as weapon: you watch a maker wave a burning brand at an approaching borer, driving it away, protecting the dwelling.

They make things that have no immediate survival function—this is the detail that haunts you.

A maker sits by the fire, carefully working a piece of the soft copper metal, hammering it with a stone, not creating a blade or tool but shaping it into a curve, a spiral, a form that serves no purpose except being a form. Another maker nearby works with plant fibers, weaving them into a complex pattern far more elaborate than necessary for simple rope or basket. They're creating, making, expressing something—aesthetics? identity? play?—that goes beyond utility.

They care for their injured and old.

You observe a maker with a badly damaged leg—broken and healed wrong, leaving it unable to walk normally. The others accommodate: building a crutch, modifying the dwelling's entrance to be more accessible, bringing food to the injured one rather than making it forage. An old maker, fur grayed and movements slow, sits in the dwelling while younger ones work, and they bring it food, include it in their vocalizations, touch it gently as they pass. No abandonment, no expedient killing. Care, provisioning, perhaps something like compassion.

But they also war with each other—territorial, violent, organized war.

Two maker groups clash at a border area, both claiming the same space, the same resources. They don't fight individually but in coordinated groups: formations, flanking maneuvers, strategic retreats. They use weapons—clubs, sharpened stakes—and they use them with intent to injure, to kill. The violence is focused, tactical. The victorious group pursues the losers, driving them far from the disputed territory, then returns and reinforces their claim by building new structures, making their presence undeniable.

They have art, and the art tells stories.

On the walls of their dwellings, they make marks: scratches in the wood, arrangements of colored stones pressed into moss, drawings made with plant-based pigments. The images are crude but representational: creatures recognizable as laceflights, woodbones, borers. Scenes: a hunt, a construction project, a fire. A particularly elaborate panel shows what might be the hollow canopy itself—the root-ceiling, the structures, the various fauna—a map or cosmology or creation myth rendered in images. One maker creates while others watch, contributing suggestions through vocalization and gesture.

They teach their young deliberately, systematically.

An adult maker sits with three juveniles, showing them how to knap stone into tools: where to strike, what angle to use, how to recognize good fracture patterns. The juveniles attempt it, fail, try again. The adult demonstrates repeatedly, patiently correcting, until one juvenile succeeds in creating a basic chopper. Celebration: vocalizations, physical affection, the successful juvenile allowed to keep and use its creation. This isn't instinct—it's cultural transmission, knowledge passed across generations through teaching, a hallmark of something approaching humanity.

They interact with other species in ways that suggest Theory of Mind—understanding that others have different perspectives, knowledge, intentions.

A maker encounters a hollowcage and doesn't flee or attack but approaches cautiously, vocalizing in soft, non-threatening tones. The hollowcage pauses. The maker offers food—a fruit it was carrying—placing it at the base of the cage and backing away. The cage's inhabitants investigate, consume the offering. The maker returns the next day with more food. Over time, the hollowcage begins to route past the makers' dwelling regularly, and the makers always provide food, and the relationship becomes routine: the hollowcage provides protection against larger predators, the makers provide supplemental food, and both benefit. The makers understood they could establish this relationship and took steps to do so—that requires sophisticated cognitive modeling.

Yet they're still bound by the hollow's strange rules.

They build obsessively but never complete everything. They use tools but also abandon perfectly good tools for no apparent reason. Their intelligence flickers—brilliant problem-solving followed immediately by baffling stupidity. They'll engineer a complex pulley system to lift heavy materials, then be unable to figure out that walking around a small obstacle is easier than trying to climb over it. They contain the same contradictions as every creature here: emerging consciousness trapped in partial instinct, intelligence haunted by the not-yet-thinking.

You watch a maker standing alone near the edge of its territory, looking up at the root-ceiling, at the filtered light, and it makes a sound—a vocalization you haven't heard before, long and descending and almost mournful. Other makers don't respond. The sound seems to have no function, no purpose. The maker stands there for long minutes, just looking up, making that sound occasionally, and you realize with vertiginous certainty that it might be wondering. Questioning. Experiencing something like awe or longing or the terrible burden of being aware that you're aware.

Then it returns to its dwelling and resumes weaving a basket, and the moment passes, and you're left uncertain what you witnessed—consciousness or its ghost, thinking or its shadow.

---

Below everything, beneath the moss layers and the fungal shelves and the accumulated plant matter, in the deepest accessible areas of the hollow, live the **feeders**.

They are amphibian-adjacent: moist skin, sprawling limbs, bodies low to the ground. Varied in size from rat-large to dog-large, with some specimens significantly bigger, perhaps even larger than the woodbones though their low posture makes comparison difficult. Coloration tends toward translucent pale—you can see organs and musculature through their skin, watch their hearts pulse, see digestion happening in real-time. Eyes are small or absent; this deep, sight provides little advantage. Instead they navigate through touch and chemoreception, their skin sensing vibrations, chemical gradients, the presence of food and threat.

They feed on death—this is their role, their niche, their gift to the ecosystem.

When anything dies in the hollow—and things die constantly—the body eventually sinks or is dragged to the lower levels, into the feeders' territory. Here decomposition happens with shocking speed. The feeders swarm the corpse: dozens of them, hundreds if the corpse is large, covering it entirely, their mouths working constantly. They have enzymes in their saliva that liquify tissue, and they lap up the resulting slurry, consuming everything—meat, bone, chitin, plant matter, it's all food to them. Within hours, significant corpses are reduced to stains. Within days, even the stains are gone, processed into feeder tissue or excreted as nutrient-rich waste that fertilizes the moss layers.

They are essentially blind but highly social.

They cluster together when not feeding: huge aggregations of bodies piled atop each other, forming living mounds a meter high and twice that in diameter. They're constantly in contact—touching, sliding over and under each other, maintaining group cohesion through physical connection. When one feeder finds food, it vocalizes—a wet croaking sound—and the entire aggregation responds, moving as a group toward the signal. They share food without competition, without hierarchy, every individual getting access, none dominating or being excluded.

They care for the hollow in ways that aren't obvious until you observe closely.

The waste they excrete isn't just fertilizer—it contains fungal spores, bacterial colonies, chemical compounds that regulate the entire subterranean ecosystem. The moss thrives where feeders are active. The fungal shelves grow larger and healthier. Even the wood itself seems to benefit—you notice that areas heavily trafficked by feeders show more vigorous growth, bark healing faster, resistance to borer damage increasing. The feeders are keystone species, their presence essential for system health, their waste products as important as their consumption.

They reproduce in the not-water pools, and their reproduction is a synchronized spectacle.

At certain times—triggered by what? lunar cycles? chemical signals? collective decision?—all feeders converge on the pools simultaneously. Thousands of them, arriving from throughout the hollow, filling the liquid until the surface roils with their bodies. They release eggs and sperm into the water, clouds of gametes that combine randomly, fertilization happening in solution. For hours the pools churn with reproductive activity, the feeders calling constantly, a chorus of croaking that drowns out all other sound. Then: departure. The adults leave, return to their territories, their mounds, their work. The pools are left full of eggs that develop rapidly, hatching within days.

The larvae are fully aquatic at first: gilled, tailed, feeding on microorganisms in the not-water.

They develop in huge numbers—millions of larvae in each pool—and their density creates its own ecosystem. Larger larvae eat smaller ones. The cloudswarms descend to feed on larvae at the surface. Laceflights swoop down to snatch individuals. The mortality is tremendous—perhaps 99% of larvae die before metamorphosis. But the 1% that survive are sufficient, and they transform: losing gills, developing lungs, absorbing their tails, crawling out onto land as miniature versions of the adults, instinctively migrating to the deep areas, joining existing mounds, beginning their work as decomposers and nutrient cyclers.

The feeders are unintelligent but not unconscious—there's a difference, subtle but important.

They don't solve problems or use tools or build anything. But they respond to novelty with what might be curiosity: approaching carefully, tasting with extended tongues, determining edibility or threat. They show preference: certain feeding sites are favored over others, certain mounds are more popular, some individuals consistently lead migrations while others follow. They play, in their way: young feeders chase each other, slide down moss slopes, tumble and wrestle without aggression. Play serves no survival function—it exists for itself, suggesting experience beyond pure mechanism, subjectivity however limited.

They sing, and nobody knows why.

Not the reproductive calls—those are obviously functional. But at other times, randomly, a feeder will produce complex vocalizations: patterns of croaks and trills and resonant hums that last minutes, that have structure and variation, that sound almost musical. Other feeders don't respond, don't join in, don't seem to notice. The singing feeder finishes and returns to whatever it was doing. No purpose, no outcome, just sound made for the making of sound. Is it experiencing something? Expressing something? Or is it just mechanical behavior that happens to resemble art? The question is unanswerable but lingers.

---

There are others—so many others.

The **gliders**: membrane-winged creatures that don't truly fly but leap from high places and glide in slow spirals, using jet-propulsion from air-bladders to adjust direction, navigating the vertical space with grace, feeding on fungi growing on the upper walls, nesting in cracks in the root-ceiling, breeding in mid-air copulations that end when both partners finally land dozens of meters from where they started.

The **stone-dwellers**: creatures that have somehow adapted to live inside the rocks themselves, dissolving microscopic tunnels through mineral, extracting nutrients from stone through chemistry you can't fathom, visible only when they emerge to reproduce—pale, eyeless, moving like inchworms across rock faces before re-entering the stone and disappearing.

The **moss-shepherds**: small, delicate creatures with eight legs arranged radially, that actively cultivate specific moss species, keeping them free of competing plants, defending them from herbivores, harvesting portions sustainably—genuine agriculture at insect scale, domestication running in both directions as the moss has clearly adapted to being shepherded, growing faster, producing more of whatever the shepherds value.

The **weavers**: spider-adjacent but social, working in colonies of thousands to create web structures that span entire chambers—not sticky traps but architectural elements, three-dimensional lattices that other creatures walk on, nest in, use as infrastructure, while the weavers maintain and repair and extract tolls in the form of shed fur and chitin and dead insects that accumulate in the webs.

The **burrowers**: existing entirely below the moss floor, creating tunnel networks through the soil layer, rarely surfacing, blind and pale, but vital—their tunnels aerate the soil, allow water and nutrients to circulate, prevent the moss from becoming waterlogged and dying, another keystone species whose work is invisible but essential.

The **mimics**: creatures that have evolved to resemble other species—a non-toxic insect mimicking the cloudswarm individuals, a harmless creature shaped exactly like the borers, something that looks like a maker from distance but reveals itself as entirely different when observed closely—evolutionary arms races playing out in real-time, predator and prey locked in morphological competition.

The **giants**: rare, seldom seen, but definitely present—you find tracks, damage, kill-sites suggesting creatures the size of bears or larger, massive fauna for which the hollow barely has space, their existence improbable but evidenced, apex predators or mega-herbivores or something else, something that only emerges at specific times, in specific conditions, and then vanishes into the deep passages, the unexplored areas, the parts of the hollow too dangerous or inaccessible to enter.

---

And threading through everything, connecting all species, all behaviors, all territories: the **substrate itself**.

Because the wood is alive—not just living in the sense that the tree is alive, but active, responsive, participant in the ecosystem rather than merely foundation for it.

The walls pulse with phloem transport—you can feel it if you press your hand flat against the surface, a rhythmic throbbing like a slow heartbeat, nutrients and water moving through vascular tissue, the entire hollow's wooden structure networked and communicating, roots in the ceiling connecting to pillars connecting to floor, one continuous organism or many organisms merged beyond distinguishing.

The wood responds to damage: when borers excavate too aggressively, new growth accelerates, filling in the weakened areas, maintaining structural integrity. When makers cut branches for construction, wound-wood forms over the cuts, preventing infection, healing happening visibly over days. When the feeders excrete near the walls, root-hairs emerge to absorb the nutrients, the wood literally reaching out to accept the gift.

The wood provides: sap flows from cracks and can be tapped, not-water accumulates in natural cisterns formed in trunk-hollows, fruits and flowers emerge from bark at irregular intervals, edible fungi bloom from inoculated wood, the entire structure producing food, supporting life, mothering the ecosystem it contains.

The wood remembers—this is the disturbing part, the detail that shifts everything.

Areas of heavy traffic develop harder surfaces, more resistant to wear. Areas rarely visited become softer, more penetrable, as if the wood allocates resources strategically. Chambers that house maker dwellings develop better ventilation—cracks appear in ceilings, allowing smoke to escape, positioned too perfectly to be coincidence. Areas where cloudswarms rest regularly develop surface textures that accommodate clinging feet better. The wood is learning, adapting, responding to its inhabitants not just chemically or mechanically but with something approaching intention.

You find carvings—not made by the makers or any other fauna, but growing from the wood itself: patterns of raised bark that resemble the creatures living in the hollow, images emerging from the substrate over years or decades, the tree depicting its own contents, creating art or record or worship of itself.

You find what might be writing: sequences of bark-knots arranged in linear patterns, repeating symbols, structured information, and you cannot determine if this is the tree's growth responding to some internal logic or if the tree is trying to communicate, encoding messages in its own tissue, leaving records for future discoverers or messages for current inhabitants that can't be read because no creature here has sufficient abstraction to recognize text.

The hollow is not a place—it's an organism, a super-organism, a distributed intelligence made of wood and fauna and fungus and bacteria and minerals and moisture and sound and light and the relationships between all of these. The tree contains the ecosystem, but the ecosystem defines the tree, and neither could exist without the other, and the boundary between them is arbitrary, a distinction made by observers who need categories but aren't present to insist on them.

---

And you—you realize this slowly, with growing discomfort—you are also being incorporated.

Your presence changes traffic patterns: creatures avoid you or approach you, altering their routes, their habits. Your breath adds carbon dioxide to the specific atmosphere of whatever chamber you occupy. Your shed skin cells feed the moss at your feet. The spores you inhale colonize your respiratory system, probably harmlessly, possibly not. The wood remembers where you've walked, been, touched, and responds with subtle modifications—surfaces slightly smoother where your hands rest frequently, the floor slightly more solid where you stand.

You are becoming part of it, integrated into its cycles, another species in its catalog, another creature in its census. If you stay long enough—weeks, months—you'll be accounted for in its growth patterns, its resource allocation, its mysterious planning. The hollow will make space for you as it's made space for everything else: not welcoming, not hostile, just accommodating, accepting, incorporating you into its endless, emerging, terrible, beautiful whole.

And maybe—this is the thought that follows you up through the sap-lined fissure, back toward the surface, toward air that doesn't taste of growth and decay—maybe you already have been incorporated. Maybe the act of entering made you part of it permanently. Maybe you'll carry it with you: spores in your lungs, seeds in your clothing, the memory of its sound in your ears, its dim green-gold light in your vision, recurring in dreams, calling you back.

Maybe the hollow canopy doesn't end at its physical boundaries. Maybe it's in everything that passes through it. Maybe it's growing out there too, in you, using you as vector, as spore-carrier, as mobile expansion.

Maybe you're one more creature now, added to its census, counted among its fauna, permanently cataloged in wood-memory and fungal network and the collective awareness of ten thousand species working together toward a purpose none of them understand but all of them serve.

Maybe you always were, before you even entered. Maybe it called you. Maybe it's been calling everything, pulling life toward itself, accumulating species and consciousness and complexity, growing not just physically but conceptually, becoming more than tree, more than ecosystem, more than anything with a name.

Becoming.

Still becoming.

Forever becoming.

Never arriving.
