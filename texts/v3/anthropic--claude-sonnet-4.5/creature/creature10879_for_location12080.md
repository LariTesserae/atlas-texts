# Who Lives There

They are **the Altitude People** now, though they weren't born as such.

## The Transformed

The children who've never descended have lungs like bellows and hearts that beat differently—slower, more efficient, pushing blood that's learned to hoard oxygen like misers hoard coin. Their fingers are long and their chests are barrels. They move through the thin air as if it were sea-level atmosphere, leaping between platforms with a confidence that borders on the supernatural. Some of them have a faint bluish tint to their lips and fingernails that never quite goes away—chronic hypoxia that would hospitalize lowlanders but that they wear as a badge of citizenship. They speak in the clipped, economical sentences of people who've learned that breathing and talking are competing priorities.

**Merra** is eleven and has never touched ground. She was born on Platform 47-C during a night shift her mother couldn't abandon. She navigates the scissor-stairs with her eyes closed, running errands for the engineers, carrying messages and small components. She can identify every bird-call and knows which flocks presage weather changes. She's teaching herself to read using discarded manifests and instruction manuals. She collects mercury in a small vial she wears around her neck—it was her father's, before he fell—and watches it slide back and forth, mesmerized by its weight and shine. She has a theory, which she tells to no one, that the birds are counting something.

**Old Sovic** has been here thirty-seven years. He arrived as a young engineer and stayed, watching the city grow around him, watching his joints stiffen with cold and his hearing fade from decades of generator-throb. He's one of the memory-keepers now, one of the few who remembers the early excavations, when there were half as many platforms and twice as much optimism. He can read the mountain's sounds—knows which groans are settling and which are warning. He's missing three fingers on his left hand from a cable accident in year twelve. He speaks four languages poorly and has trained himself not to dream, because dreams at altitude are vivid and disturbing, and he's seen what sleeplessness does to judgment around heavy machinery.

## The Industrial Clergy

The **Generator Priests**—that's what the workers call them, half-mocking, half-reverent—are the engineers who tend the three great engines. They're a separate caste, marked by their leather aprons black with coal dust and oil, their specialized tools hung on brass chains, their technical knowledge passed down through apprenticeships that last years.

**Korvin** is Master of Generator Two. He's forty-three and has the hearing of someone much older, damaged by the constant thunder of pistons. He sleeps in a hammock strung in the generator's housing, because the machine requires monitoring every four hours and he's learned to wake at its rhythm-changes, feeling the vibration patterns through his bones. He's teaching his daughter the maintenance sequences, though she's only sixteen and the Guild says seventeen is minimum. He knows he won't last much longer—the coal dust has gotten into his lungs, he coughs black—and the machine needs someone who loves it, who understands that it's not just a device but the city's heart, and hearts are kept beating through devotion as much as technical skill.

His crew are **the Stokers**, young men mostly, who shovel coal into the furnace mouths in shifts, their bodies lean and corded with muscle, their faces prematurely aged by heat and particulate. They speak their own jargon, timing their shoveling to work-songs that coordinate the rhythm, that keep the feed-rate steady. Some of them will move up to maintenance positions. Most will burn out after a few years and descend, their lungs ruined, taking their severance pay and the chronic cough that will follow them forever.

## The Textile Handlers

In the mill sectors live **the Thread People**, families who've specialized in the processing of raw wool into cloth. They're mostly women, though not exclusively, their hands callused in specific patterns from the work—thumb and forefinger thick from testing thread tension, palms crosshatched from the shuttle-work.

**Aunt Beshu**—no one knows her real name, and "aunt" is courtesy title, not relation—runs a household of nine across two stacked platforms. She's a master weaver, her looms producing the heavy insulating fabric that keeps the city from freezing. She's Deaf—whether from birth or industrial accident, accounts vary—and communicates in a sign language that's become semi-standardized among the textile workers, a pidgin mixing her native signs with gestures invented for technical terms that didn't exist in any formal language. She can diagnose loom problems by touch, feeling the vibration patterns through the frame. She's trained seven apprentices. She's ruthless about quality control because she knows that inferior insulation kills, and she's seen it kill.

The textile platforms have their own culture—women singing work-songs in overlapping harmonies that somehow cut through the mechanical noise, children playing between the looms, a communal cooking area where the precious water ration is pooled and managed collectively. They've maintained traditions from a dozen different lowland regions, syncretic festivals and foodways adapted to altitude scarcity. They share childcare, share grief when the altitude takes someone, share the chronic worry about the water ration.

## The Bird Hunters

**Greigan and his sons** work the dawn harvest, nets in hand, moving along the higher platforms where the birds cluster. They're silent workers—bird-catching requires patience and stealth—and they've developed an almost preternatural ability to predict the flocks' movements. Greigan is fifty-something, leathery, with a face like eroded cliff-stone and eyes that never quite focus on near objects, trained to track motion against the sky. His eldest son, **Petran**, is learning the business but has the dreamer's temperament—he watches the birds too much, studies their patterns, sketches them in a journal with stubbed pencil, and Greigan worries the boy won't have the hunter's necessary coldness.

They sell to **the Butchers**, a loose collective who've claimed platforms in the mid-levels, where they process the birds, where blood drips through metal-grate floors into collection buckets below (nothing is wasted; blood is valuable, full of iron, mixed into rations). The butchers are hard people, necessarily—you can't make your living from small deaths and stay soft. But they're also the city's informal medics, understanding anatomy from their work, setting bones and stitching wounds with the same knives they use for processing carcasses.

## The Waterkeepers

Most crucial and therefore most powerful are **the Cistern Guild**, who manage the water supply with bureaucratic precision and physical force. They're led by **Madame Osk**, a woman of indeterminate age—somewhere between forty and sixty, her face a mask of professional neutrality, her authority absolute within her domain. She carries a brass-bound ledger everywhere, tracking every liter collected, every ration distributed, every unauthorized usage prosecuted. She has three lieutenants, each managing a sector of the collection nets and cisterns, each commanding teams of **net-minders** who check tension, repair tears, measure drip rates with obsessive attention.

The net-minders are often former climbers, people who aren't afraid of heights and exposure, working on suspended platforms or hanging in harnesses to tend the vast canvas expanses that catch the clouds. It's dangerous work—the nets are slippery with condensation, the wind is unpredictable, and falling from the net-platforms means dropping through the central shaft until you hit something solid far below. They lose one or two people a year. The Guild pays death-benefits to families with the same ledger-bound precision that it tracks water, because the families' silence is essential to maintaining order around resource scarcity.

**Young Tomic** is a net-minder at seventeen, working to earn enough to bring his mother up from below. He's discovering he likes the work—the solitude, the exposure to sky, the way the clouds sometimes pass through the nets and through him, cold and damp and otherworldly. He's started staying out on the nets longer than his shifts require, lying on his back on the suspended canvas, watching the broken crown's ragged opening frame the sky. He's developing theories about the clouds, about their patterns, writing them in water-proof ink in a notebook he keeps inside his shirt. The other minders think he's odd but harmless. Madame Osk is watching him, trying to determine if he's a visionary or a liability.

## The Lowborn

At the shaft's bottom levels live **the Cleaners** and **the Maintenance Crews**—those who do the work no one else wants, who crawl through the narrow spaces, who chip away rockfall and unclog drainage channels and scrape the accumulated grime from places where grime shouldn't accumulate. They're the newest arrivals mostly, working off their passage-debt, or they're people who've fallen from higher stations—gamblers who lost everything, workers whose lungs failed them before they could adapt, those who drank away their positions.

**Salim** is twenty-three and drowning in debt. He came up seeking fortune and found only hard labor. He works twelve-hour shifts in spaces so tight he has to exhale to squeeze through, breathing air that's more coal smoke than oxygen, scraping compacted coal-dust from ventilation channels. His lungs are already starting to go—he can feel it, the tightness, the wheeze. He lies awake in his hammock in the dormitory-platform where forty men sleep in stacked rows, listening to the coughing chorus around him, and he calculates obsessively: how many more shifts until his debt is cleared, whether he can make it that long, whether he should cut his losses and descend even deeper in debt. He thinks about the stories—the sealed sections, the levels below—and whether there's anything down there worth finding that might buy his freedom.

**Mari the Cleaner** is forty and has been bottom-level for five years. She doesn't talk about what she did before. She's developed a reputation for going places others won't—the deepest shafts, the sections where the electromagnetic anomalies make compasses spin and electric lights flicker out. She carries no light, navigating by touch and memory. She finds things down there sometimes—tools, components, bones. She sells what has value and keeps what doesn't, building a collection in her tiny platform-space: strange mechanical fragments, rocks marked with symbols that might be natural or might not, a bird skull that has too many eye sockets. She's started talking to herself, or to something. The other cleaners avoid her, but the engineers pay her well because she's the only one who'll inspect certain sections, and someone has to verify the structural integrity, even in the places that aren't on the official maps.

## The Makers

The **Instrument Workshops** are filled with a different class of resident—precision craftspeople, their status somewhere between worker and engineer, respected for specialized skills. They're clockmakers and jewelers and gunsmiths who've adapted their trades to manufacturing navigational devices.

**Master Vey** is a gyroscope specialist, her hands scarred from years of fine metalwork, her vision going (she'll be blind within five years, she knows, from the close work and the bad light). She employs six apprentices in her workshop on Platform 23-A, teaching them the art of balancing rotating masses, of polishing brass to tolerances measured in thousandths of millimeters. She's exacting and patient and capable of startling violence when someone damages a piece through carelessness—she once broke an apprentice's finger with a hammer for dropping a completed gyroscope assembly. She pays well, though, and trains thoroughly, and her former students run half the precision workshops in the city.

Her counterpart is **Old Chen**, who specializes in barometric devices—aneroid capsules and mercury columns, instruments that measure the pressure of air and therefore altitude. He's seventy and should have descended years ago, but he claims the altitude keeps him alive, that returning to sea-level pressure would kill him. He might be right—his body has adapted so completely that he's become a kind of human barometer himself, feeling weather changes in his joints, pressure shifts in his sinuses. He employs no apprentices, works alone, and produces devices of such precision that engineers pay premium rates. He's also, quietly, building something else, a device whose purpose he discusses with no one, in a locked side-chamber of his workshop. Visitors hear it sometimes—a rhythmic clicking, like the scissor-stairs but more complex, more deliberate.

## The Questionable

There are those whose roles are unofficial, whose presence is tolerated rather than sanctioned.

**The Rememberers** are story-keepers who move between platforms, trading tales for food, for water-rations, for small kindnesses. They remember who came from where, who's related to whom, who died and how. They're oral historians and genealogists and gossips. **Old Petra** is chief among them, blind now but her memory perfect, able to recite the entire three-decade history of Platform 18-B's occupants, or trace the descent-line of any of the altitude-born children. She's become a repository of the city's unofficial knowledge—the romances and feuds, the debts and favors, the secrets people thought were hidden. People court her favor and fear her knowledge in equal measure.

**The Spark-Reader** is what they call **Miklos**, though he insists he's simply an electrician. He maintains the junction boxes and wiring in the upper sectors, but he's developed a reputation for predictions. He claims to read the patterns of electrical discharge during storm activity, to see warnings in the way the lights flicker. He predicted the great cable failure of last spring—warned the engineers three days before it snapped, was ignored, and was vindicated when the main support cable on Platform 31-C parted and killed six workers. Since then, people listen. He's starting to attract followers, workers who gather at his platform after shifts to hear his interpretations. The engineers are watching this development with concern, trying to determine if he's genuinely sensing something in the electromagnetic patterns or if he's a charlatan building a cult. Either way, he's becoming a problem, because belief is contagious and industrial efficiency requires rational minds.

**The Pharmacist**—no other name is ever used—operates from a platform in the mid-levels, dispensing compounds from a stock of chemicals that couldn't all be accounted for by legitimate supply chains. She treats altitude sickness, suppresses coughs, provides abortifacients (necessary in a place where pregnancy is dangerous and additional mouths strain resources), and sells other things to those who ask correctly: stimulants to help workers endure double shifts, sedatives to help people sleep despite the noise and altitude-dreams, analgesics for injuries that can't be officially reported. She's perhaps fifty, her accent from somewhere far south, her past a mystery no one has successfully penetrated. She's protected by the complex web of people who need her services and can't afford to see her removed. Even Madame Osk, who officially disapproves, has been seen visiting her platform after dark.

## The Descended

Some who live here aren't present physically but are remembered so intensely they remain part of the community.

**Tavik the Faller** died seven months ago, slipping from a bridge during a shift-change, falling the full height of the central shaft—witnesses said he never screamed, just fell silent and focused, perhaps trying to calculate some way to survive. He didn't. But his name is still invoked—"Tavik's luck" means bad fortune, and workers touch the bridge-rails where he fell for... protection? Acknowledgment? No one's quite sure, but the ritual has taken hold.

**Saint Corvin**—no relation to Korvin the engineer, though the name similarity causes confusion—was one of the original excavators, dead now for twenty years. He's become a figure of legend, credited with feats of engineering that may or may not be true, invoked in the platform-shrine with equal fervor by the religious and the secular. His supposed words are quoted in disputes, his methods referenced in technical debates. Whether the historical Corvin was actually exceptional or whether the city simply needed a founding father is impossible to determine now.

## The Visitors

The elevator brings them: engineers from below, sent to inspect or troubleshoot; supply coordinators; company officials doing audits; occasionally, tourists—wealthy lowlanders who pay for the experience of altitude, who spend a few days gasping and gaping before fleeing back to breathable air.

**Inspector Rohl** arrives quarterly, a company man with ledgers and checklists, verifying production targets and safety compliance. He's efficient and humourless, and the city collectively holds its breath during his visits, hiding violations, coaching workers on correct responses. He knows he's seeing a performance and doesn't care as long as the paperwork is correct. He files his reports and descends, and the city relaxes back into its actual functioning, which bears only tangential relationship to official procedures.

But there are other visitors, occasional and troubling. People who arrive unscheduled, who carry authority that supersedes the company officials, who inspect sections that aren't on the official maps, who interview workers about things they shouldn't know about. They wear no identifying insignia. They ask questions about the anomalies, about the strange findings in the deep sections, about whether anyone has experienced unusual dreams or visions. They take notes in books they never let out of their sight. Then they descend, and their visits are not discussed afterward, as if by collective agreement that acknowledging them would make them more real.

## The Night-Watchers

When the lights dim and the shifts change, a different population emerges.

**The Insomniacs** can't sleep at altitude—something about the thin air disrupts their circadian rhythms, or perhaps it's the constant noise, or the dreams. They wander the platforms in the hours when most are sleeping, a loose community of exhausted shambling figures. Some of them are productive—they take the night-watch shifts, do maintenance that's easier when there's less traffic. Others are deteriorating, their judgment going, their grasp on reality loosening. They're watched carefully, because sleep-deprived workers make fatal mistakes, and one mistake can kill people on connected platforms.

**Yussef the Watcher** hasn't slept properly in three years. He works as a night guard, patrolling the higher platforms, ostensibly checking for fires or structural problems. Really, he's watching the birds. He's become convinced their night behavior is significant, that they're responding to something. He fills notebooks with observations—times, positions, flight patterns, which platforms they avoid. His handwriting has become increasingly erratic. His supervisors are debating whether to send him down, but he's reliable about the actual guard duties, and night-guards are hard to find.

## The Teachers

There's no formal school, but education happens. **The Platform Schoolhouse** is a cleared section of Platform 39-B where **Miss Kovar** runs lessons for children whose parents can spare them from work. She's twenty-eight, trained as a teacher in some lowland city, came up for reasons she doesn't discuss. She teaches reading, mathematics, practical mechanics—things children need to survive here. She has no official sanction and no guaranteed income, surviving on payments from parents who value education, supplemented by doing technical drawing for the engineers in her spare time.

She's fighting a losing battle against the city's practical demands—children are valuable workers, their small hands useful for fine assembly work, their labor contributing to family income. She loses students constantly to the workshops. But she persists, believing that literacy and numeracy are worth fighting for, that these altitude-born children deserve more than to simply replicate their parents' lives. Some of her students will descend eventually, carrying their education to the lowlands. Some will become engineers, clerks, administrators. Some will die in industrial accidents before they're fifteen. She teaches them all the same.

## The Dying

**Resh** is thirty-four and knows he's not descending alive. The altitude sickness never loosened its grip, and now his heart is failing, struggling against the thin air and losing. He works half-shifts in the textile mill, moving slowly, resting frequently. He's saying goodbye gradually, distributing his possessions, sharing stories with his children about the lowland village he came from, which they'll never see. He's maintained dignity and humor despite everything, become beloved on his platform-cluster. When he dies—soon, everyone knows it's soon—the textile workers will give him to the birds, the traditional disposal method, placing his body on an exposed platform where the scavengers will take him. It's practical (burial is impossible, cremation would waste fuel) and it's become imbued with meaning: the birds will carry him skyward, toward the broken crown, toward whatever lies beyond. He finds comfort in this.

**Grandmother Sil** is ninety-something—she's lost count—and impossibly old for this place. She should have died years ago but persists through stubbornness and genetics. She remembers the lowlands, remembers weather and trees and horizons, things the altitude-born know only from stories. She's become a living repository of before-the-mountain knowledge. Children cluster around her to hear about rain that falls from sky to ground (not drips from collection nets), about birds that aren't cliff-swifts, about the concept of "flat" and "far" and "green." She's trying to write it down, dictating to Miss Kovar, who transcribes in neat handwriting, preserving lowland memory before it dies with Grandmother Sil's generation.

## The Strange

There are people whose presence is difficult to categorize, who don't fit the city's social schema.

**The Man in Platform 7-D** exists in rumors more than in reality. His platform is near the very top, accessible only by a single scissor-stair that's usually retracted. Supplies are delivered to a collection point, but he's rarely seen. He's described variously as hideously disfigured, or preternaturally beautiful, or just deeply shy. He's said to be conducting experiments, or building something, or simply hiding from something below. The engineers claim he's a legitimate researcher, authorized by the company, studying altitude effects on human physiology. The workers have more exotic theories. Occasionally, late at night, people hear sounds from his platform—mechanical clicking, or music, or screaming, accounts vary. Requests to visit are politely declined through written notes left with supply requisitions.

**The Twins**, **Sasha and Sash**, are platform 52-C's mystery. They're perhaps twenty, identical to a degree that's unsettling, always together, speaking in overlapping sentences that finish each other's thoughts. They work as precision assemblers, their coordination perfect, able to manage tasks that would normally require careful verbal communication without speaking at all. They sleep in the same hammock despite there being space for two. They're unfailingly polite and utterly opaque. Attempts to separate them for different work shifts have failed—their individual efficiency drops to near-zero when they're apart. Theories about their nature range from the mundane (just unusually close siblings) to the wild (they're a single consciousness in two bodies, a side effect of altitude exposure during their mother's pregnancy, something worse). They ignore all speculation with serene detachment.

---

The city is **fifteen thousand people** at current census, though the number fluctuates with the elevator's arrivals and departures. Fifteen thousand bodies adapting to altitude, to cold, to the vertical geometry and resource scarcity. Fifteen thousand hearts beating in modified rhythms. Fifteen thousand pairs of lungs learning to extract oxygen from air that barely has any to give. Fifteen thousand stories, intersecting and diverging on the platforms and bridges, in the workshops and dormitories, in the shaft's echoing vertical expanse.

They're forming something new here—not quite a culture yet, but the bones of one. A language of altitude, vocabularies of precision and scarcity. Songs that work at thin air. Stories that explain why anyone would build a city in a mountain's throat. Children who'll never understand the lowlands, whose dreams are of vertical space and bird-flight and the view through the broken crown.

They're the Scissor Nest's people, and they're becoming something the lowlands hasn't seen before: a population adapted not just physically but culturally to the impossible altitude, to the industrial rhythm, to the particular madness of building lives in a place that shouldn't support them.

And they're making it work, day by day, shift by shift, through labor and adaptation and the stubborn insistence that here, in this carved-out throat of stone beneath a broken sky, is home.
