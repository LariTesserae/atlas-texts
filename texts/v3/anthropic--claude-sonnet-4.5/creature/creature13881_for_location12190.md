# The Inhabitants

## The People of the Porcelain Ranges

They are human, or human-enough that the distinction has become philosophical rather than practical. Generations at extreme elevation have left their marks—their chests are broader, their fingers slightly longer for climbing, their eyes adapted to the particular quality of light that filters through mineral-laden air. But these are subtle gradients, not ruptures. A child born here and a child born in the distant lowlands would recognize each other as kin, though they might move differently, speak at different rhythms, orient themselves in space according to different logics.

The population across all surveyed settlements numbers perhaps fifty thousand, distributed across distances that make accurate census a matter of estimation rather than certainty. They are not a single culture but a network of related ones, connected by trade, cable-systems, and a shared adaptation to verticality and dryness.

### Physical Adaptation

Their lungs have learned the thin air. Newcomers gasp and tire quickly; those born here breathe easily at elevations that would incapacitate lowlanders within hours. This isn't genetic mutation operating on evolutionary timescales—it's developmental plasticity, the body learning its environment during childhood and fixing that knowledge into bone structure, blood chemistry, the size and efficiency of alveoli.

Their skin tends toward pale, though with an undertone that isn't quite pink—more like the color of certain rose quartz deposits, as though the mineral dust that permeates everything has stained them from the outside in. They don't tan; instead, prolonged sun exposure gives them a faint translucence, their skin thinning slightly and taking on an almost porcelain quality that mirrors the landscape. The elderly sometimes look as though they're beginning to mineralize.

Their hands are distinctive: long fingers with pronounced knuckles, palms calloused in patterns specific to rope-work and stone-climbing. Even the scholars and artisans who spend their days indoors bear these marks—climbing is not optional here, and the body remembers. Their fingernails grow slightly thicker than baseline human, ridged in ways that provide better purchase on stone.

Their hair is universally fine and dry, prone to accumulating the suspended mineral dust until it takes on a matte texture. Most keep it short or bound tightly. There's no tradition of elaborate hairstyles—the environment makes them impractical. The dust settles into hair and never quite comes out, so even young children have hair that looks aged, greyish regardless of its underlying color.

### Movement and Gesture

They move *deliberately*. Not slowly, exactly, but with a quality of intention that makes every gesture seem considered. This is partly the thin air—unnecessary movement wastes oxygen—and partly cultural, a collective adaptation to living on cliff edges where a careless step has permanent consequences.

Their gait is distinctive: they walk with their weight slightly forward, knees bent, ready to catch themselves or drop into a climbing stance. Even on flat ground, they move as though the floor might tilt. They rarely run. When speed is necessary, they climb—transitioning to vertical movement where their adaptation gives them advantage over horizontal urgency.

Their gestures are economical and occur mostly in a vertical plane rather than horizontal. Pointing is done with the whole arm, indicating direction and elevation simultaneously. There's an elaborate hand-language for communication across distances or during the silent hours, conducted at arm's length against the sky where it's most visible.

They touch stone constantly, habitually—running fingers across walls as they pass, testing surfaces, reading texture. This is partly practical assessment (is this surface stable? is this edge sharp?) and partly something else, a kind of ongoing conversation with the material world. They can identify dozens of stone varieties by touch alone and will debate the specific composition of a particular cliff face with the intensity others reserve for politics or religion.

### Speech and Language

Their language is full of words that have no equivalents elsewhere: specific terms for types of thermal columns, for the quality of stone at different temperatures, for the particular sound cables make under different tensions. They have seventeen words for types of emptiness—the empty space between peaks, the empty space in a fracture, the empty space inside a thermal column, each one distinct in meaning and usage.

They speak slowly, with long pauses between sentences, sometimes between words. Conversations that would take ten minutes elsewhere stretch to thirty here. This isn't inefficiency—the pauses carry meaning, and interrupting one is a significant rudeness. In the silence between sentences, both speakers are thinking, reassessing, allowing the previous statement to settle and reveal its full implications.

Their voices are soft. Shouting is rare and considered a kind of violence—not against people, but against the acoustic environment, an imposition of chaos onto the carefully maintained quiet. Even their industrial machinery is designed to minimize noise, and workers who operate loud equipment are considered to be performing a kind of necessary sacrifice, damaging their own peace and the peace of the community for the collective good.

They have poetry, but it's not about sound—it's about rhythm and breath-pauses. Poems are performed not by speaking in meter but by breathing in patterns, sometimes over the course of several minutes, the actual words scattered sparsely through long silences. The art is in the placement of breath and word against the background silence.

They can communicate in the hand-language across distances of several hundred meters if the light is right. During the silent hours, entire settlements conduct their business this way, hands moving against the pale stone, visible from rooftop to rooftop. Children learn it in parallel with verbal speech, and many people think in it, their hands twitching slightly as they contemplate, signing their thoughts into near-invisibility.

### Social Structure

The settlements are organized around vertical rather than horizontal hierarchies, but "hierarchy" is the wrong word—it implies power structures that don't quite apply here. Instead, there are *functional differentiations* based on elevation and expertise.

The highest-elevation residents are typically researchers, philosophers, and those who tend the glider roosts. They live in thin air and extreme temperature swings, closer to the strange physics of the peaks. They are respected for their endurance and their proximity to whatever the mountains *are*, but they don't govern. They often spend months at high elevation before descending to mid-level settlements to share observations, trade goods, and participate in collective decision-making.

Mid-level residents, in the main basins and plateaus, form the bulk of the population. They work the industries, raise children, maintain the infrastructure. They are the settlement's stability, its metabolic center. Authority—such as it exists—resides here, in councils that meet weekly to address practical questions: resource allocation, construction priorities, cable maintenance schedules.

There are low-level specialists too—those who work in the deepest fractures, studying the vertical swimmers, or who maintain the geothermal taps that power the industrial systems. They live partly underground, in conditions unlike anywhere else in the Ranges, and develop their own sub-culture, their own relationship with the Underneath.

But these divisions are porous. People move between elevations regularly, and identity is not fixed to location. A researcher might spend a decade at high elevation, then return to mid-level and take up ceramics. A child raised in the industrial basin might develop an aptitude for thermal-mapping and relocate upward. The culture values experience across multiple elevations—the ideal elder has lived at all levels and can speak to the full spectrum of existence here.

### Governance and Decision-Making

There are no singular leaders, no presidents or mayors or chiefs. Instead, there are councils composed of whoever chooses to attend. Attendance rotates naturally—people participate when they have something to contribute, step back when they don't. Some individuals become regular voices in council, respected for their judgment or experience, but they hold no formal power and can be overridden by consensus.

Decisions are made through a process that looks like conversation but operates as collective thinking. Someone raises an issue—a section of cable needs replacement, or a new building is proposed, or there's a question about how to respond to an unusual glider migration pattern. The council discusses it, often over multiple meetings spanning weeks. People offer perspectives, technical knowledge, historical precedent, intuition about what the landscape will tolerate.

Eventually, a consensus emerges—not because everyone agrees, but because the shape of the decision becomes clear through accumulated conversation. Dissent is acknowledged and incorporated; decisions are understood to be provisional, subject to revision if new information or consequences emerge.

This process is frustratingly slow by external standards, but it produces stable outcomes. Decisions made this way rarely need to be unmade. The culture has learned that patience at the decision stage prevents crisis later.

Disputes between individuals are handled similarly—through conversation, facilitated by whoever is willing to listen, until the underlying issue resolves or transforms into something manageable. Violence is almost unknown. In a place where survival depends on cooperation and where the environment itself is the primary challenge, interpersonal conflict reads as absurd, a failure of perspective.

### Family and Reproduction

They have children, but not many. The birth rate across the Ranges is low—perhaps one child per woman over a lifetime, sometimes two. This is partly the environment (the thin air and mineral-heavy diet affect fertility) and partly cultural choice. Resources are limited, space is constrained, and the culture values stability over growth.

Children are raised communally. Biological parents are known and significant, but child-rearing is distributed across extended networks of adults. Children move between households, learning different skills, forming attachments to multiple caregivers. By adolescence, most have lived in several different homes at different elevations, apprenticed to various craftspeople or industries, developed a broad base of knowledge and relationship.

This makes kinship complex and non-linear. Family is less about blood and more about accumulated care. An elder might count two dozen people as children—some biological, some raised, some simply mentored over the years—and feel no need to distinguish between categories.

Romantic partnerships exist but aren't the primary unit of social organization. People form deep bonds—sometimes lifelong, sometimes temporary—but these are understood as one type of relationship among many, not privileged above friendship or mentorship or the complex interdependencies of living in close quarters at extreme elevation.

There's no marriage ceremony, no formal recognition of partnership. People simply live together, or don't. The culture is more interested in the network of relationships than in any particular dyad. What matters is that everyone is embedded in multiple caring relationships, that no one is isolated, that the social fabric is dense enough to catch anyone who stumbles.

### Childhood and Education

Children here are simultaneously cherished and expected to be competent. From age four or five, they're learning to climb, to read stone, to navigate vertical space. By eight, they're often working—not full days, but contributing to household and community tasks. By twelve, many have apprenticed to a craft or industry and are performing meaningful labor.

This isn't exploitation—it's integration. Work here is not separated from life; it's how you participate in the collective existence. Children learn by doing, gradually taking on more responsibility as their skill develops. Adults teach patiently, aware that rushing creates danger. A child learning to climb is a child learning to survive, and the teaching is intense, careful, never cavalier.

Education is informal but thorough. There are schools in the larger settlements—structures where children gather to learn geology, atmospheric physics, the biology of local fauna, history, mathematics. But much learning happens through apprenticeship, through daily living, through the accumulated teaching of anyone willing to share knowledge.

Children are taught to read the landscape—to predict thermal columns from the shape of peaks, to identify stone types by color and texture, to understand the behavior of gliders and stone-walkers. They learn the hand-language, the technical vocabulary of cables and kilns, the etiquette of vertical space. They learn patience, deliberation, the value of slow thinking.

They're also taught philosophy, though it's not called that. They learn to think about the Underneath, about what the mountains are, about the relationship between human settlement and the landscape's tolerance. These aren't abstract questions—they're practical ones, about how to live here without exceeding what the place can sustain.

### Work and Industry

Labor is organized around necessity rather than profit. The settlements produce what they need—building materials, tools, the mysterious processed water, cables, ceramics—and trade with each other and with distant lowlands for what they can't produce locally.

Most people work in some aspect of mineral processing: the mills that grind stone, the refineries that separate compounds, the kilns that fire ceramics. The work is physical but not brutal—the machinery does the heavy labor, and humans operate, maintain, and monitor it. A shift in a processing facility involves long periods of watchful waiting punctuated by short bursts of adjustment and repair.

There are artisans: ceramicists who produce not just functional materials but objects of beauty—bowls with glazes that mimic the color of selenite bands, sculptures that play with translucence and light. Stone-carvers who shape the local minerals into building elements or decorative pieces. Rope-makers who weave the cables that connect settlements, work that requires precision and patience and produces objects that will carry lives across voids for decades.

There are the specialized professions: cable-walkers who inspect and maintain the transportation systems, working at height without fear, their bodies adapted to the exposure and the thin air at span-midpoints. Thermal-mappers who chart the atmospheric conditions, providing data for glider-watchers and industrial planners. Fracture-guides who lead expeditions into the deep cracks, navigating spaces where vertical swimmers congregate and the air itself behaves strangely.

There are caregivers—those who tend children, elders, the injured and ill. This work is valued equally with industrial labor. The culture recognizes that care-work is survival-work, that a community persists through the maintenance of bodies and relationships as much as through the production of material goods.

And there are the watchers—people who simply observe. They sit at the edges of settlements or on remote platforms and watch the landscape, the fauna, the weather patterns. They keep records, sometimes, or they don't. They report unusual phenomena to the councils, or they keep their observations private. The culture respects watching as a form of labor, understanding that perception is itself a kind of work, that someone needs to notice when things change.

### Technology and Tools

Their technology is mechanical and durable. Electronics fail in the dry air and the suspended mineral dust, so they've developed alternatives: clockwork timing mechanisms, pneumatic controls, mechanical computers built from gears and levers that can solve complex load calculations for cable systems.

Their tools are heirlooms. A good set of climbing gear—pitons, carabiners, rope—might pass through five generations, each user maintaining and refining it. Craft tools—the specialized hammers of stone-carvers, the precise measuring instruments of ceramicists—are individual and irreplaceable. When someone dies, their tools are offered to apprentices or donated to communal workshops, never discarded.

They make everything repairable. Design philosophy centers on accessibility of components, standardization of parts where possible, and documentation of how things work. The culture has no patience for deliberate obsolescence—objects are made to last, to be maintained, to serve indefinitely.

The cables that connect settlements are their highest technology: woven from mineral fibers and embedded with those mysterious self-repairing structures produced in the factory at Perseverance. The cables are alive in some sense—not conscious, but responsive, adjusting their tension in response to temperature changes, rerouting stress around damaged sections, lasting decades or centuries with minimal human intervention.

They have simple weapons—knives for cutting rope, tools that could serve as clubs—but no martial technology. There's nothing to fight. The fauna ignore humans; neighboring settlements cooperate rather than compete. The concept of warfare is historical, something from lowland cultures or ancient times, not applicable to current existence.

### Diet and Sustenance

They eat sparingly. The processed water is their primary sustenance—it contains dissolved minerals and synthesized nutrients that sustain basic metabolism. Beyond that, they consume small amounts of what they call *stone-bread*: cakes made from mineral flour, mixed with trace organics extracted from the fauna's excretions, baked in the ever-running kilns.

Stone-bread is dense and nearly flavorless, with a texture like compressed chalk. It provides calories and minerals but little pleasure. Eating is functional, not recreational. Meals are brief and usually solitary—you eat when you're hungry, in whatever location is convenient.

There are occasional luxuries: certain stone-walkers produce a secretion that can be fermented into something mildly intoxicating. Some crystalline colonies yield fragments that, when dissolved slowly in the mouth, produce subtle flavor—not sweet or savory but something else, a taste that exists at the edge of perception. These are rare and shared during festivals or significant gatherings.

The lack of diverse food creates different sensory priorities. They don't bond over shared meals the way other cultures do. Instead, they share *textures*—passing around interesting stones, pieces of fabric, fragments of crystal. They gift each other tactile experiences rather than flavors.

Hunger is managed, not eliminated. The culture accepts a baseline level of not-quite-satisfied as normal. This contributes to the deliberate pace of life—without the energy from rich food, you naturally move slowly, think carefully, conserve resources.

### Health and Medicine

They are generally healthy—the dry air prevents many waterborne diseases, the sparse diet avoids metabolic disorders, the constant climbing maintains physical fitness. But they have their own ailments.

*Dust-lung* is common in industrial workers and the elderly: a gradual accumulation of mineral particles in the lungs that produces a distinctive clicking sound when they breathe. It's progressive but slow, and most people live decades after onset. The culture treats it as inevitable rather than pathological.

*Bone-brittleness* affects some, especially those who live at extreme high elevations—the thin air and mineral-heavy diet alter bone chemistry, making fractures more common. These are treated with compounds extracted from stone-walker shells, which somehow promote bone density when consumed.

The *translucence* that affects elders is considered natural aging, not illness. As people grow old, their skin thins and takes on that porcelain quality, their movements become even more deliberate, and eventually they seem to fade—becoming less substantial, more mineral, until one day they simply don't wake up, and their bodies are already part-way to stone.

There are healers who understand the body's relationship to this environment. They work with mineral compounds, with extracts from the fauna, with controlled exposure to different atmospheric conditions. Their medicine is chemistry and geography intertwined—they might prescribe time at a particular elevation, or exposure to the air of a specific fracture, or consumption of dust from a certain peak.

Mental distress is acknowledged but not pathologized. When someone becomes overwhelmed—by grief, by the vastness, by the accumulated strangeness—they're encouraged to change elevations, to watch the vertical swimmers, to sit with the glider roosts. The landscape itself is considered therapeutic. The culture understands that human consciousness sometimes requires recalibration, and the mineral emptiness provides that.

### Death and the Afterward

Death is expected and ritualized. When someone is dying—from age, from dust-lung advanced past tolerance, from a climbing accident—they're moved to one of the designated platforms where the gliders come. Family and friends gather, share memories and tactile gifts, perform the breath-poems that honor a life's particular rhythm.

The dying person speaks if they can, offering their own assessment of their time, the knowledge they wish they'd shared, the observations they'll leave unfinished. Then, when they're ready, everyone departs except perhaps one companion, and the person dies in exposure to sky and stone.

By the next day, the body is gone, claimed by the gliders and carried upward. What happens at altitude is unknown. Perhaps the bodies are consumed, perhaps they're deposited in some unreachable location, perhaps they're taken somewhere else entirely. The culture doesn't pursue the question—the gliders take the dead, and that's the end that everyone accepts.

There's no belief in an afterlife, no expectation of continuation. Death is understood as transformation—the body returning to mineral substrate, the patterns of thought dispersing into the atmospheric chemistry. You came from stone and air and the slow metabolic processes of this place; you return to them. The cycle is complete.

Grief is experienced but not prolonged. The culture provides a framework: you acknowledge loss for a specific period (usually a week), you speak the person's name during that time whenever you think of them, and then you gradually integrate their absence into your ongoing life. Their tools pass to new users, their dwelling is reassigned, their position in the community is filled organically.

There are memory-keepers: individuals who maintain records of who has lived and died, what they contributed, particular moments or achievements worth preserving. These records are sometimes written, sometimes oral, sometimes encoded in the arrangements of stones in specific locations—archives that exist as landscape modification rather than text.

### Art and Aesthetic

Beauty here is mineral. They appreciate translucence, layering, the way light interacts with crystalline structures. Their art consists primarily of arrangement—taking stones of different types and organizing them into patterns that reveal relationships, create visual rhythms, suggest meanings without stating them.

There are stone-gardens: spaces where carefully selected rocks are placed in specific configurations, then left to weather. The art is in the initial arrangement, but also in the patient observation of how the display changes over years or decades, how the pieces settle and weather, how new relationships emerge.

Ceramics are functional but made beautiful—the glazes carefully formulated to catch light in particular ways, the shapes refined to be pleasing to the hand as well as useful. A bowl might take months to make, the ceramicist returning to it repeatedly, making tiny adjustments, firing it multiple times until it achieves the exact quality of translucence they're seeking.

The breath-poems are performance art—someone standing in a public space, breathing in patterns, occasionally speaking a word or phrase, allowing the rhythm to develop over an hour or more. Others gather to witness, to synchronize their own breathing with the performer's, to participate in the creation of patterned silence.

They make music rarely, but when they do, it's through stone-chimes—carefully tuned pieces of mineral suspended in arrangements that ring in the thermal winds. The music is involuntary, created by atmospheric conditions rather than human performance, but the art is in the tuning and arrangement, determining what the wind will say.

### Relationship with the Fauna

They coexist with the local creatures but don't domesticate them. The stone-walkers wander through settlements, tolerated and occasionally fed with mineral-rich dust, but never controlled. The thermal gliders are watched with reverence but not approached—except when they come for the dead.

Some people develop fascinations with particular species. Glider-watchers spend their lives observing the aerial creatures, learning to predict their movements, trying to understand their life cycle. Swimmer-researchers descend into fractures to watch the vertical swimmers, developing theories about their biology and behavior that remain mostly speculative.

The crystalline colonies are treated with particular respect. The culture is uncertain whether they're alive, and the uncertainty creates caution—better to assume consciousness and be wrong than to assume inertness and destroy something sentient. When colonies appear in inconvenient locations, people work around them rather than removing them.

There are stories about individuals who claimed to communicate with the fauna—particularly with the vertical swimmers, whose movements sometimes seem responsive to human presence. Whether these were genuine contact or apophenia is debated. The culture remains agnostic, willing to believe but requiring no proof.

### Religious and Philosophical Beliefs

There is no unified religion, but there are shared intuitions. Almost everyone believes the mountains are *something*—not just geology, but intentional, designed, maintained. The Underneath is acknowledged as real, whether it's vast machinery or some other kind of presence.

Some people pray, though prayer here doesn't petition—it's more like reporting. They speak to the Underneath, describing their observations, their uncertainties, their gratitude for continued tolerance. The Underneath never responds, or responds only through the continuation of stability—the mountains not collapsing, the gliders continuing to soar, the cable systems not failing.

There are philosophical schools, informal groups that meet to discuss the nature of the place. The *Depositionists* believe the Ranges were made by some ancient civilization and then abandoned, and that the Underneath is automated maintenance systems still running after their creators' departure. The *Emergentists* argue the mountains are alive and growing, that the Underneath is the organism's core metabolism. The *Temporalists* think the Ranges exist in folded time, that the ruins predate the mountains because causality operates differently here.

These debates are ongoing and friendly. The culture values good questions over definitive answers. The uncertainty is considered appropriate—they live in a place they don't fully understand, and acknowledging that seems more honest than fabricating certainty.

There's a common practice of *sitting with questions*: when something confuses or troubles you, you go to a quiet location—a plateau edge, a fracture overlook, somewhere with long views—and you sit with the question without trying to answer it. You let it exist in your awareness while you watch the landscape, and sometimes clarity emerges, or sometimes you become comfortable with not-knowing.

### Festivals and Celebration

When the selenite bands glow, settlements gather for viewing. Platforms are crowded with people standing in silence, watching the mountains illuminate themselves. The glow lasts for hours, sometimes through an entire night-cycle, and people remain watching, or they watch in shifts, or they fall asleep there and wake to find the light still continuing.

These gatherings are the closest they come to collective joy. There's no ceremony, no program—just shared witnessing. Occasionally someone performs a breath-poem, or stone-chimes are hung to sound with the thermal winds generated by the glowing stone, but mostly it's silence and attention.

There are anniversaries—the founding of settlements, the completion of major cable-spans, the lives of particularly significant individuals. These are marked with gift-exchanges: people create small objects—carved stones, woven fibers, ceramic pieces—and distribute them to others. The gifts accumulate; homes fill with these objects, each one carrying the memory of a specific moment or relationship.

When a cable system is completed—a new connection between settlements that previously required weeks of difficult climbing—there's a crossing-ceremony. The first people to use the new cable walk it slowly, sometimes taking hours to complete the transit, while others watch from both ends. It's acknowledgment of human capacity to bridge emptiness, to create connection across void.

### Cultural Variations

The settlements differ in emphasis and character. *Clarity*, with its ceramics industry, has developed an aesthetic culture—more artisans, more attention to beauty, more philosophical discussion. *Perseverance*, with its mysterious factory, tends toward technical obsession—its residents are engineers and tinkerers, constantly refining processes.

High-elevation settlements are more austere, their residents more likely to believe in direct communion with whatever the mountains are. Low-elevation communities, working near the geothermal systems and the Underneath, are more pragmatic, focused on maintaining the industrial processes that make all life here possible.

But these are gradients, not divisions. People move between settlements, share knowledge, intermarry. The cable systems prevent isolation—you can reach any settlement from any other within a few days' travel. The culture is unified by shared adaptation, shared uncertainty, shared existence in a place that remains fundamentally strange.

### The Newcomers

Occasionally, people arrive from the lowlands—traders, researchers, the curious. The settlements receive them politely but without enthusiasm. Newcomers struggle with the altitude, the thin air, the diet, the slow pace. Most don't stay long.

Those who do remain begin to change. Within months, their breathing adjusts, their movements slow, they start touching stone habitually. Within years, they're indistinguishable from the native-born except in subtle ways—a different distribution of calluses, slightly different hand-language dialect, references to a different life that become less frequent over time.

The culture doesn't proselytize or recruit. They're content with their population, their balance with the landscape. If someone wants to stay, they're integrated gradually, given time to adapt, taught what they need to know. If someone wants to leave, they're helped with the departure, no judgment implied.

There's an awareness that this life isn't for everyone, that it requires particular temperament—comfort with emptiness, patience with slowness, tolerance for uncertainty, willingness to live in a place that might be alive and might be watching and might have purposes that have nothing to do with human flourishing.

### The Unspoken Understanding

Beneath all social structure, beneath the councils and the care-networks and the philosophical debates, there's a fundamental understanding: they exist here by permission, not by right. The landscape tolerates them, the Underneath allows them to tap its heat, the gliders accept their presence, the stone-walkers wander through their streets without concern.

This could change. The silent hours could extend indefinitely. The gliders could turn aggressive. The Underneath could stop its rhythmic pulsing and go still, or could accelerate into something catastrophic. The mountains could shift in ways that collapse settlements, sever cable-spans, eliminate the thin margin on which human presence depends.

So they are careful. They build sparingly, extract minimally, maintain respectful relationships with the fauna. They teach each generation to watch, to listen, to notice when something changes. They accept that their tenure here is provisional, that they might need to leave, that this place has its own priorities and human life is, at most, a minor consideration in vast mineral thinking.

This awareness shapes everything—the patience, the quiet, the deliberate movements, the way they touch stone as though checking that it still accepts being touched. They live as guests in a house they don't own and can't fully understand, grateful for shelter but ready to depart if the hospitality ends.

And so far—for generations, for however long they've been here—the hospitality continues. The mountains stand. The gliders soar. The Underneath pulses with its steady, mysterious rhythm. And in the spaces between peaks, in the thin air and the warm, dry silence, fifty thousand people live their vertical lives, climbing and watching and breathing slow, participating in the strange ecology of stone and thermal columns and accumulated patience that is the Porcelain Ranges.
