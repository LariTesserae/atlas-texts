# What Lives There

The **attendants** are here, though that word carries implications they neither confirm nor refuse. They wear the pale uniforms—sometimes lab coats that end at odd lengths, sometimes coveralls with too many pockets, sometimes simple tunics in that institutional beige-gray that matches the weathered concrete. Their clothing is always clean in a way that suggests it has never been truly dirty, that dirt slides off it or fails to register against its surface.

They stand for hours. Not watching in the way you watch—tracking movement, seeking patterns, getting bored and looking away. They stand oriented toward specific pools, specific junctions of pipe, specific elevations, and their stillness is so complete that you begin to understand it as a form of labor. They are working when they stand like this, though the work is invisible. Monitoring, perhaps, but monitoring in a register you can't access. Reading something in the water's behavior, the mineral deposits, the angle of light through mist, that requires this depth of attention.

When they move, it's with deliberation so extreme it almost parses as slowness, but isn't quite—each motion is simply entirely itself, uncommented-on by the motions before or after. They'll crouch beside a basin and extend one hand into the water, hold it there, withdraw it. The hand drips for exactly seven drops. They stand again. They walk seventeen paces north. They stop. They've accomplished something.

Their faces are difficult to hold in memory. Not blurred exactly, not featureless, but somehow resistant to description. You see them clearly while looking—there are eyes, a nose, a mouth, the ordinary architecture of a face—but when you turn away the details scatter. You remember the impression of a face rather than the face itself. They might all be different people or they might be the same person in different configurations. They might be people at all, or they might be aspects of the place that have found it useful to wear a human shape for purposes of interfacing with visitors, with the birds, with the water itself.

They don't speak, or you've never heard them speak. The voice you sometimes hear calling numbers—that might be them, or it might be something else using their frequency. They acknowledge your presence only through absence: if you approach directly, they're no longer where they were. Not fled, not hidden—simply elsewhere, having relocated through whatever means they use. But if you maintain a respectful distance, move parallel to them rather than toward them, they tolerate your proximity. You've stood thirty feet from one for an entire afternoon, both of you watching the same pool fill through its inscrutable cycle, and there was something almost companionable in it.

Sometimes you find their objects: the tools, the clipboards, the measuring devices. These seem like gifts, or tests, or simply abandoned equipment that they don't need anymore, that has completed its function. You pick up the wrench with seven handles and it's warm, as if recently held, though you saw no one here. The forms on the clipboards are filled out in handwriting that's too regular, each letter identical to every other instance of that letter, the work of someone writing the same report hundreds of times until it achieved a kind of calligraphic perfection.

There are hierarchies among them, or specializations. Some attendants only appear at the highest elevations, where the platforms are skeletal and the air is thinnest. Others stay low, near the baseflows and primary reservoirs. Some are associated with particular sections—you begin to recognize the one who tends the cluster of pools in what you think of as the eastern quarter, who always wears a coat with rust-colored staining on the left sleeve. Except one day the staining is on the right sleeve, or there is no staining, or it's a different coat entirely, and you can't be sure if it's the same attendant or if you've been mistaken about their distinctness all along.

They seem to have been here for a very long time—longer than the stacks, perhaps, or they've been here for exactly as long as the stacks because they and the stacks are coterminous, each requiring the other. The infrastructure accommodates them perfectly: the platforms have spaces that are exactly the right size for standing vigils, the catwalks include small alcoves that serve no obvious purpose except as places for an attendant to wait, sheltered from the brightest light. The system was built with them in mind, or they have shaped themselves to fit the system so precisely that the distinction has dissolved.

---

The **basin-birds** are the most visible life, and the most numerous. They cluster in the thousands but never quite in flocks—each bird maintains a small zone of independence, a bubble of personal space that other birds respect with rigor. When they congregate on a platform, they distribute themselves with geometric precision, equidistant from each other, creating patterns that look deliberate: grids, spirals, concentric rings around a filling pool.

They're pale—gray and white and cream and that specific shade of colorlessness that wet concrete achieves. Their feathers have a peculiar texture, almost waxy, that sheds water instantly. They can stand in the spray of a filling basin and remain completely dry, each droplet rolling off without penetrating. Or they can wade belly-deep and emerge as if they'd only been dusted with mist.

Their bodies are compact, pigeon-sized mostly, though there are larger varieties at higher elevations—those have wingspans that seem too large for their bodies, sail-like wings that catch the updrafts and let them hang motionless in the air for minutes at a time, drifting slowly across the vertical cityscape of water. The smaller ones walk more than fly, picking their way along platform edges, stepping delicately over pipes, navigating the infrastructure with the confidence of creatures who know every centimeter of their territory.

The beaks are straight and slightly elongated, functional for dipping into water but not for fishing—there are no fish here, and the birds don't hunt. They drink, frequently, but you never see them eat. Possibly they subsist entirely on the water, which might be more than water, which might carry nutrients or energy in forms not strictly chemical. Or they might eat when you're not watching, consuming the mineral deposits, the algaes and mosses, in small sufficient amounts that leave no sign.

Their eyes are dark and large and unsettlingly attentive. They track you when you pass, heads turning with that characteristic bird jerk, but the tracking feels less like wariness and more like data collection. They're noting your position, your trajectory, your relationship to the water systems. They're adding you to their understanding of the current configuration.

The sounds they make are minimal—that soft clicking, produced how you can't determine, that seems to come from the throat or chest rather than the beak. Sometimes a low humming that you feel as vibration before you hear it as tone. Very rarely, a sharp chirp that sounds almost digital, like an error notification or a confirmation beep. When many birds produce the hum simultaneously, it builds into a resonance that makes the platforms vibrate sympathetically, that sets up standing waves in the pools, that does something to the air pressure or the local gravity because you feel momentarily lighter, unmoored.

They nest everywhere the infrastructure permits: in the hollow pipes, in the gaps between platform segments, in the scaffolding and rebar that forms the bones of the place. Their nests are minimal—a few gathered stalks of the rust-colored grass, some moss, arranged in simple cups. The eggs, when you see them, are pale blue, almost white, and perfectly smooth, as if machined. The chicks are born featherless and gray, and they remain silent in the nest, making no demand, waiting with eerie patience for the parents to return with... whatever they return with. You've never seen the feeding happen.

There's a developmental cycle: the chicks mature over what seems like weeks, going through stages that don't quite match normal avian development. They seem to skip adolescence, transforming directly from nestling to full-sized adult. Or they mature slowly in some regards and instantly in others—one day a helpless chick, the next day its wings are fully formed though it still has downy body feathers, and it's standing at the nest edge, staring at the water with that characteristic intensity.

The birds participate in the water management in ways that seem both incidental and essential. They drink from pools that are about to drain, as if their drinking is the trigger or the final stage of the process. They congregate on platforms that are about to receive water, their presence serving as indicator or invitation. They fly through the mist and their passage creates eddies, vortices, patterns in the suspended droplets that persist longer than they should, that might be directing moisture toward specific collection points.

Sometimes you see them engaged in behaviors that are clearly functional but whose function you can't parse: twenty birds standing in a perfect line across a dry basin, motionless for an hour, and then dispersing all at once. A single bird pecking at a particular spot on a particular pipe with metronomic regularity, three pecks per minute for an entire afternoon. Groups walking in slow circles around a pool, maintaining their spacing exactly, creating a living compass or clock.

They show no fear of the attendants, and the attendants show no interest in the birds, but the two groups coordinate. When an attendant takes up position by a pool, the birds rearrange themselves, clearing a space or filling in around the human figure in ways that seem purposeful. It's a choreography, or a protocol, everyone and everything slotting into proper relationship.

The birds at the highest levels are different—larger, yes, but also more abstracted somehow. Their plumage is even paler, almost translucent in certain lights, and they spend more time airborne, less time in contact with the structures. They might be a different species, or they might be basin-birds that have achieved some other state, graduated to a different role. They ride the thermals between platform levels, circling slowly, and from below they look like white crosses turning in the bright air, and you can't shake the feeling that they're reading the entire system from above, comprehending it as a totality that you experience only as fragments.

---

The **maintenance fauna** are smaller, stranger, less easily categorized. The hard-shelled creatures that emerge from the drains are the most common: each about the size of your thumb, segmented bodies, many legs, moving with a rapid scuttling that somehow doesn't read as frantic. They're arthropodal in structure but wrong in the details—too many segments, or segments of uneven size, or leg joints that bend in an extra direction.

They appear in groups, sometimes dozens, sometimes hundreds, emerging when certain pools fill or drain according to patterns you can't predict. They spread across the wet concrete in loose formations and they're doing something—scraping the surface with specialized appendages, or secreting something, or absorbing something. They're part of the maintenance, you become certain. They're managing the mineral deposits, or the algae, or the chemical balance of the water. They're part of the system's immune response, or its digestive process, or its information network.

Their shells are translucent enough that you can sometimes see internal structures: things that might be organs, or might be mechanisms, or might be stored fluids. They reflect light strangely, showing colors that don't seem to be inherent to their bodies—iridescent blues and greens that shift as they move, as if they're picking up and rebroadcasting the light from the pools.

They make sounds: tiny clicking, softer than the birds' clicks, and a faint scraping. When hundreds are active simultaneously, it creates a texture to the silence rather than breaking it—a subtle graininess to the quiet, like radio static played at very low volume.

They vanish back into the infrastructure as suddenly as they arrived, flowing back into drains and gaps and spaces between pipes, and you're left wondering if they live in those spaces all the time, moving through hidden networks, or if they exist only intermittently, called into being when their function is needed and dissolving afterward.

There are other small things, less frequently seen: pale slug-like creatures in the dampest, darkest corners, leaving trails that glow faintly for a few minutes before fading. Insect-analogs with too many wings in arrangements that seem architecturally improbable, flying in stuttering paths through the mist. Things that might be larvae, translucent and wriggling, in pools that are somehow separate from the main water flow, in stagnant pockets that develop in the joints between platforms.

None of these creatures seems to interact with the others except through the infrastructure itself—they all feed on it, clean it, measure it, inhabit its margins and interstices, but they don't feed on each other. There's no predation here, or if there is, it's so subtle you never witness it. The ecology is aligned entirely around the water and the structure, everything derivative of that primary relationship.

---

And there are the **water shapes**, which are not creatures exactly but are certainly alive in some sense.

You see them when the light is right, when a pool is at a particular depth and stillness: coherent forms in the water that aren't reflections, aren't shadows, aren't debris or algae or tricks of refraction. They're regions of water that have density, intention, boundary. They move through the pools with purpose, keeping to the depths even in shallow basins where there shouldn't be enough depth to contain them.

They're approximately cylindrical, or they suggest cylinders, rotating slowly as they move. They're visible primarily as distortions—looking through them, the tiles at the bottom of the pool waver and bend, light refracts wrong, and you get a sense of something present even though there's no color to it, no obvious substance distinguishing it from the surrounding water.

They don't surface. They don't leave the water. You've never seen one cross from pool to pool, which means either they're confined to individual basins or they travel through the pipes, through the underground connections you can't observe. Or they're not individuals but local manifestations of something larger that exists throughout the entire water system, appearing at different nodes simultaneously or sequentially.

The basin-birds pay attention to them. When a water shape is moving through a pool, birds will gather at the edges, more than usual, and they'll dip their beaks in repeatedly, and their clicking intensifies. The attendants, too—you've noticed that they're more likely to stand vigil by pools that currently contain a water shape, and their stillness becomes even more absolute, their attention even more focused.

You tried once to touch one, reaching into a shallow pool where a water shape was moving slowly along the basin floor. Your hand passed through it—there was no physical resistance, no temperature change, no texture. But there was something: a tingling, a sense of motion against your skin that wasn't quite tactile, and a feeling of having interrupted something, of minor transgression. The water shape continued on its path, unbothered or imperceptive, and you withdrew your hand, which felt normal, which showed no effect, though for the rest of the day you were aware of it in a way you're normally not aware of your body parts, as if it had been marked or measured.

The water shapes might be what the attendants are here to monitor. Or they might be what draws the water through its impossible routes, biological or pseudo-biological pumps that move fluid through willpower or whatever equivalent they possess. Or they're inspectors, traveling through the system, checking on its integrity, reporting back to something deeper, something that exists in reservoirs you haven't found and perhaps can't reach.

Sometimes at the deepest pools, the ones that drop into darkness even in the brightest light, you see suggestions of larger shapes: movements that displace too much water, shadows that are too extensive to be cast by anything you can see. These might be mature water shapes, or they might be something else entirely, something that only exists at certain pressures or depths, something the visible system is merely an interface for.

---

In the highest, most skeletal reaches, where platforms are sparse and the gaps between them yawn for hundreds of feet, there are the **air-resting things**.

You've only seen them a few times, and always at a distance. They hang in the updrafts, held aloft without visible means—not birds, much too large, the size of cars or small buildings. They're difficult to look at directly; your eyes slip off them, want to focus on something else. They might be balloons or bladders of some kind, organic gasbags riding the thermal currents. Or they might be more like the water shapes—coherent distortions, regions of air that have achieved localized identity.

They're pale like everything here, barely distinguishable from clouds, and they might sometimes be clouds, might shift between states. They drift slowly between the highest platforms, rising and falling in patterns that span hours, and the soaring basin-birds avoid them, giving them wide clearance, which tells you they're significant, they're powerful or dangerous or simply deserving of respect.

You've never heard them make sound, but when one passes over you—or rather, when you pass under one, since you're doing the moving and it's merely drifting—there's a change in pressure, a heaviness or lightness, and the background hum of the stacks shifts in pitch. They might be part of the atmospheric regulation, managing the moisture and temperature, ensuring the mist maintains its proper cycle. Or they might be storage themselves, reservoirs in a different medium, carrying water as vapor or as something more abstract.

---

And finally, though you resist this knowledge, though you try to categorize yourself separately: there are the **absorbed**.

People who came here and didn't leave, or haven't left yet, or left in some sense but their presence remains. You've seen them, or you think you have—figures that look too much like the attendants but aren't quite, who move with less purpose, who stand at pool edges not in vigil but in something more like communion or resignation.

They're becoming part of the place. You can see it in how their clothing has started to weather to match the concrete, how they've learned the stillness, how they've begun to space themselves at those same geometric intervals the birds prefer. They're losing their distinctness, or they're gaining a new kind of distinctness that's aligned with the stacks rather than opposed to it.

You might be one of them. You've been here for—how long? Days certainly. Weeks probably. Longer? You've developed routines, as noted. You know which pools to visit when. You understand which platforms are stable and which are in transition. You've stopped trying to map the place and instead navigate by feel, by the sense that builds in your body, by the rightness of certain paths and the wrongness of others.

Your clothing is paler than it was, you think. Your footsteps have become quieter. You find yourself standing sometimes, watching a pool fill, and you've lost track of time, and it doesn't bother you the way it would have initially. The water is still just water, but you're learning to see what the attendants see, or the beginning of it—the patterns, the flows, the meaning in the mineral deposits and the mist-currents and the birds' arrangements.

You drink the water and it tastes like clarity, like purpose, and you're thirsty constantly but it's not an unpleasant thirst, more like a reminder, a call to participate in the cycle. You've started noticing your own reflection in the pools and finding it difficult to recognize, not because you've changed physically but because the context is overwhelming the content—you see yourself but you see yourself as part of the larger pattern, one element in the vast system, and your individuality seems less relevant than your function.

The absorbed don't seem unhappy. They're quiet, attentive, integrated. They've found their place in the mechanism, their role in the ongoing maintenance of this vast patient infrastructure of water and light. They're being cared for, perhaps, in the way the stacks care for everything here—by providing a place, a purpose, by incorporating them into its processes so completely that questions of inside and outside, self and system, become meaningless.

You're not there yet. You're still observing, still maintaining the distinction between visitor and resident. But the boundary is porous, and the place is patient, and eventually—you sense this with certainty—everyone who stays long enough makes the same quiet transition, puts down the clipboard, finds a pool, begins the vigil that will last for however long vigils last here, which might be forever, which might be exactly as long as necessary, which might amount to the same thing.

---

This is what lives in the Reservoir Stacks: attendants and birds, maintenance fauna and water shapes, air-resting things and the slowly absorbed. All of them aligned to the water, the infrastructure, the cool bright quiet. All of them performing functions that are clear in outline but opaque in detail, maintaining a system whose purpose is either the storage and management of water or something much larger that uses water as metaphor, as medium, as the material through which it thinks its slow vast thoughts.

And you, for now, moving through it, observing it, becoming gradually observable yourself, becoming data in the system you came to witness, becoming part of what lives here, the newest and least transformed element of an ecology that is patient enough, vast enough, strange enough to accommodate anything that stays long enough to learn its rhythms, to hear its hum, to stand at the edge of a filling pool and watch the water rise with the kind of attention that might, eventually, be indistinguishable from love.
