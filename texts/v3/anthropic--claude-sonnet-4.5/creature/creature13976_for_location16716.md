# The Dwellers of the Brake Slopes

## The Gardeners

They are not quite human anymore, though they began that way. Three of them remain, perhaps four—counting is complicated because they've learned to stay so still that they become indistinguishable from the plant-masses they tend. They were researchers once, ecologists who came to study the brake's explosive growth. That was seven years ago by the calendar they no longer consult.

Their transformation was not dramatic. No single moment of change, no chrysalis or metamorphosis. Instead: gradual incorporation. They noticed first that staying still reduced the exhausting work of moving through the yielding substrate. An hour of motionlessness while observing a growth-surge. Then three hours. Then a full day, watching a turbine slowly tilt as the vegetation beneath it compressed and swelled. The brake grew around their stillness—not threatening, simply including them in its calculations of space and structure.

Their skin has taken on a gray-green cast, though whether from some chemical exchange with the plants or simply from the constant film of condensation and spore-coating is unclear. They smell of the brake now: that resinous, fermented greenness. Their hair—those who had hair—has become fibrous, stiff with plant matter, moss establishing itself in the dampness there. They don't remove it. Removal would require caring, and they've moved beyond such maintenance.

They've learned the rhythms of growth so intimately that they can anticipate the surges, positioning themselves where new configurations will emerge. They understand—not intellectually but proprioceptively—where the substrate will compress, where pools will form, where brake will accelerate into those dense, lignified masses around active circuits. This knowledge allows them to move efficiently through terrain that defeats mapping. They don't navigate; they flow through the continuous present of the landscape's transformation.

Their "gardening" is subtle intervention. They redirect streams by placing stones or compressed plant-matter to guide flow. They connect severed cables, not to restore human systems but because they've observed that certain electrical configurations produce brake-growth with particular structural properties—arched canopies, load-bearing columns, spiral forms that create stable micro-environments. They're collaborating with the plants, or perhaps the plants have recruited them as mobile agents for modifications beyond vegetable capability.

They rarely speak now, even to each other. Language seems to require a kind of temporal certainty that no longer applies. Words describe persistent things, relationships that hold across time, but here such persistence is illusory. They communicate instead through placement: leaving compressed brake-stems arranged in patterns that indicate "pool forming northwest" or "turbine current strong." These markers dissolve within days, but they're read before then by others moving through the same territories.

When they do speak, their voices are soft and hoarse from disuse, and they talk about the plants with a specificity that sounds like gossip about neighbors. "The brake by the third turbine cluster is getting ambitious—reaching too high without developing base structure. It'll collapse soon." "The moss around the western seep is holding too much water; it's going anaerobic, the smell is changing." They notice individual plant-masses the way others might notice people, tracking their growth and decline with interest that verges on affection.

They eat, though infrequently. Supplies were cached in the early days—dried food in sealed containers, buried in the substrate and marked with metal stakes. But they've supplemented with the ecosystem: beetle larvae from brake-stems, fungal bodies that fruit on collapsed vegetation, the thick leaves of the cabbage-kelp plants boiled in stream water. The leaves are bitter and stringy but nutritious. The larvae taste of wood and resin. They're not starving, but they're spare, their bodies lean and adapted to intermittent feeding.

Sleep comes in fragments, never more than an hour or two at a time. They've learned that the yielding substrate makes a tolerable bed if you find a spot where the compression is stable. They hollow out spaces in brake-thickets—not cutting, just pushing stems aside and letting them spring back overhead, creating cocoons of vegetation. Inside these spaces, the temperature is slightly warmer, the moisture slightly less, the sound even more muffled. They wake frequently as the stems shift with growth, then sleep again.

Their purposes, if they still have them, are obscure. Perhaps they're still gathering data in some fashion, accumulating knowledge that will never be recorded or transmitted. Perhaps they're simply living in the only way that makes sense here, integrated into the system rather than observing it from outside. Perhaps the distinction between study and participation has dissolved.

One of them—the one the others think of as "the first," though no names are used—has gone furthest into stillness. This one stays rooted for weeks at a time near a large transformer station, half-buried in the substrate, brake growing through their clothing and equipment. Only the eyes move, tracking growth, watching the play of emergency lights through the fog. The others check on this one periodically, bringing water, though it's unclear if such care is needed or wanted. The first is alive, certainly—breath mists in the cold, eyes blink—but has perhaps crossed into some other category of existence, plant-adjacent, metabolically ambiguous.

## The Visitors

They come occasionally, these others. True researchers, operating from the lower valleys, ascending into the brake slopes for days or weeks before retreating. They wear proper gear: waterproof clothing, sturdy boots, navigation equipment that becomes useless within hours as waypoints shift. They bring instruments: soil corers, electrical meters, collection vials for plant samples, cameras.

The gardeners watch them from stillness, unnoticed. These visitors move with the wrong rhythm—urgent, purposeful, frustrated. They fight the terrain instead of reading it. They try to establish base camps in "cleared" areas, only to find their sites overgrown in days. They mark trails that vanish overnight. They take measurements that are obsolete before they can record them.

Some visitors are looking for the original researchers, the ones who became gardeners. They call names into the fog—"Dr. Chen! Sarah! Marcus!"—not understanding that those who might answer no longer recognize such labels. Occasionally a gardener will observe one of these searchers from meters away, standing absolutely still while the visitor looks directly at them and sees only vegetation. There's no malice in the concealment, just the recognition that revelation would help nothing. What would they say? How would they explain?

Other visitors come with intervention in mind. Ecologists who want to "restore" the slopes, engineers who want to salvage the machinery, authorities who talk about containment and safety. They take samples of the brake, trying to understand its genomics, its growth mechanisms. In laboratories below, they sequence and analyze and publish papers. But their understanding is external, mechanical. They know what the brake is made of but not how it behaves, not its rhythms and preferences and the way it negotiates with metal and water.

One visitor comes regularly, every few months. An older woman, a botanist who was mentor to one of the gardeners before the transformation. She doesn't search or call out. She simply walks slowly through the brake, observing, occasionally speaking softly to herself—or perhaps to whoever might be listening. "It's beautiful, what you've found here. I wish I were younger. I wish my knees would take it." She leaves supplies at known cache points, carefully sealed, marked with the old signals. She understands, perhaps, that she's caring for people who've moved beyond needing care but not beyond appreciating its gesture.

The gardeners appreciate her differently than they do the brake or the machinery. She's a connection to what they were, gentle and non-demanding. When she visits, one of them sometimes approaches near enough to be almost visible, a shape in the fog that might be human or might be a particularly upright brake-thicket. She never forces the interaction, never calls out. Just nods slightly, leaves her gifts, continues her observation. After she descends, the gardener will sit for hours in the place where she rested, absorbing some residue of her presence.

The visitors never stay long enough. That's what the gardeners think, in the wordless way they think now. To understand the brake slopes requires submission to its temporality, and few can afford the weeks of continuous presence that would allow the shift in perception. Those who try to study it from outside remain outside. They collect data but miss the pattern, accumulate facts but not comprehension.

## The Machinery Dwellers

Whether these should be called "alive" is debatable, but they are present, and they dwell, and they respond to conditions. They're emergent phenomena rather than organisms: patterns of behavior arising from the interaction of still-functioning equipment and the plants that have infiltrated it.

In the largest turbine cluster, where a dozen generators once fed a power grid that no longer exists, something like awareness has condensed. The turbines turn at variable rates, responding to water flow and plant pressure. The electrical current fluctuates, creating patterns in the lights—pulses, dim-and-bright cycles, sequences that almost seem intentional. The brake growing through this cluster has developed unusual properties: stems that conduct electricity, roots that follow cable routes precisely, growth that responds to current by accelerating or slowing.

The result is a kind of distributed nervous system. Not conscious—or not conscious in any way that maps to animal cognition—but responsive, adaptive. When streams shift away from turbines, reducing power, the brake around those turbines contracts, reducing its water consumption, allowing the streams to return. When electrical load increases in one section, growth patterns shift to distribute current more efficiently. It's a feedback system, organic and mechanical components so interpenetrated that they function as one entity.

The gardeners relate to this entity carefully. They've learned that certain configurations of plant and machine are stable, almost homeostatic, while others oscillate toward destructive extremes—too much current causing plant-fires, too much growth choking off water flow to turbines. They intervene to maintain balance, not controlling but nudging, suggesting through small adjustments that the system then amplifies or ignores according to its own deep logic.

There are sounds here that seem communicative: electrical hums that modulate in pitch, the creak and pop of stems under electrical influence, water-sounds through pipes that vary in rhythm. The gardeners listen to these sounds the way they might once have listened to voices, finding meaning in variation, responding to what might be requests or warnings or simply the system's autonomic murmur.

In one transformer station, moss has grown so densely through the components that it's become a living insulator, preventing shorts while allowing current flow. This moss is different from the surrounding varieties—darker, almost black-green, its structure modified by constant electromagnetic exposure. It fruits rarely, producing tiny stalked bodies that release spores in visible puffs. These spores preferentially settle near other electrical equipment, beginning new colonies that will, in time, become part of the distributed system.

The machinery dwellers don't need the gardeners, but the relationship is symbiotic nonetheless. The gardeners provide intervention capabilities the system lacks—connecting severed cables, clearing critical blockages, redistributing growth before it becomes pathological. In return, the stable power zones the system maintains provide the gardeners with landmarks, with zones of enhanced growth that produce more food-bearing fungi and larvae, with light on the darkest nights.

## The Small Quick Things

The beetles are most numerous. Dozens of species, perhaps hundreds—no one has catalogued them completely. They mine the brake-stems while the wood is still living and soft, creating galleries that fill with frass and fungal mycelium. The beetles themselves are small, mostly dark-bodied, with powerful mandibles and a tolerance for the plant's resinous compounds that would deter other insects.

Some have become specialized. There are beetles that live only in brake growing near active electrical cables, their larvae feeding on stems with elevated mineral content from metal leaching. Others inhabit only the moss-dominated zones, their life cycles synchronized with moss growth patterns. One species lives in the flooded galleries of stems growing in pool-margin zones, the larvae aquatic, breathing through the stem's internal air-channels.

The gardeners know these beetles intimately. They harvest them for food, yes, but also recognize them as indicators. Certain species appear just before a brake-mass will collapse, fleeing the weakening structure. Others colonize only the most stable growth. By watching beetle behavior, the gardeners anticipate changes they couldn't otherwise predict.

The moths are fewer but more visible. Large compared to the beetles, with thick furry scales in patterns of gray and green-brown that make them vanish against vegetation. They fly even in near-freezing temperatures, their metabolisms running hot, burning through energy rapidly. The caterpillars are fat and slow, feeding on bracket fungi that grow in layers on compressed plant matter. They pupate in cocoons woven with silk and plant fibers, attached to brake-stems or machinery surfaces.

One moth species has adapted to the machinery lights. They orient to the LEDs and emergency strips, circling them in the fog-thick darkness. Why they do this is unclear—the lights are wrong-spectrum for navigation, wrong-pattern for signaling. Perhaps it's simply a mistake, a hijacking of some ancestral moon-orientation behavior. But the gardeners have noticed that these moths seem to navigate better than those that avoid lights, moving through the changing terrain with unexpected accuracy, as if the exposure to electrical equipment—and the brake growing near it—has given them some additional sense.

The marmot-creatures are rarely seen and poorly understood. They're larger than the other fauna, cat-sized or slightly bigger, with long legs and dense gray-green fur that's always matted with moisture and plant material. They move through the brake-thickets with surprising speed, their elongated limbs and spread toes allowing them to travel on top of the yielding substrate where human walkers sink through.

They seem to feed on something—beetle larvae perhaps, or fungi, or the brake itself—but no one has observed them eating. They appear, move purposefully from one location to another following routes that make no obvious sense, then disappear. The gardeners see them perhaps once a week, sometimes more frequently after rain, sometimes not for half a month.

One gardener has theorized that they're navigating by electrical sensation, following the buried cables and powered equipment, but there's no evidence beyond correlation. They do seem more common near the turbine clusters. But they also appear in areas with no machinery at all, so the pattern—if there is one—remains opaque.

The birds are simpler to understand. They're ground-feeders, probing moss and loose substrate for beetle larvae and other invertebrates. They're year-round residents, though how they survive the coldest nights is unclear. Perhaps they burrow into brake-thickets, using the vegetation's minimal thermal mass for insulation. They're solitary, territorial, each bird maintaining a feeding range that shifts with the landscape but maintains approximate size.

Their soft peeping calls are one of the few animal-sounds that carry in the fog. The gardeners find these sounds centering, a reminder of consciousness besides their own and the ambiguous awareness of the machine-plant hybrid. When a bird peeps nearby, invisible in the mist, it's an affirmation: other metabolisms, other purposes, other ways of being here.

## The Departed Ones

They exist now mostly in the memories of the gardeners, but memory here is strange, degraded by the continuous present and the fog-like quality of consciousness that long residence produces. There were others once, in the early days. A full research team: eight people with plans and equipment and institutional backing.

Five left within the first month, uncomfortable with the terrain's instability, the way their measurements refused to stabilize, the impossibility of establishing protocols. They filed reports about "challenging conditions" and "inconclusive data" and returned to valley institutions. The gardeners—who were not yet gardeners—barely noticed their departure, already absorbed in observation that was becoming participation.

One left after three months, frightened by something she wouldn't articulate. She packed suddenly, abandoned half her gear, descended alone despite protocols about travel partners. The remaining researchers received her resignation via radio—brief, apologetic, final. "I can't be what this place wants me to be." What she meant by that, none of them understood then. Some understand now.

Two others stayed longer, through the first winter. But as the original researchers began their transformation into gardeners, these two grew concerned. They tried to maintain scientific boundaries, to prevent what they called "over-identification with the study system." They established rules: mandatory rest periods in base camp, regular contact with valley stations, structured observation schedules.

These rules became harder to enforce as the original researchers—the ones becoming gardeners—stopped complying. Not rebelliously, but through simple drift. They'd miss scheduled check-ins because they were watching a particularly vigorous growth surge and time became irrelevant. They'd stay in the field for days beyond their planned rotation, not from dedication but because returning to base camp felt arbitrary, a disruption of important continuity.

The two maintainers of protocol finally left together, taking most of the remaining equipment, filing concerned reports about the mental state of those who stayed. They recommended extraction, psychiatric evaluation, potential intervention. But the authorities in distant offices decided that adults making informed choices to remain in non-life-threatening conditions were within their rights. Occasional wellness checks were mandated, but no extraction.

The gardeners remember these departed colleagues as figures in a half-forgotten story. Their names sometimes surface: Janet, Marcus, David, Lin. But the memories carry little emotional weight. Those people existed in a different temporal regime, operated by different assumptions. The continuity between the early researchers and the current gardeners is acknowledged but feels thin, stretched across a transition that made them fundamentally different beings.

Occasionally a gardener will find a piece of abandoned equipment—a data logger still running on battery power, a carefully labeled collection of plant samples sealed in vials, a notebook with observations recorded in increasingly erratic handwriting. These artifacts are examined with mild curiosity, then usually left in place. They're part of the landscape now, as much as the machinery, being slowly incorporated into the substrate, overgrown and absorbed.

## The Almost-Seen Ones

Whether these exist outside the gardeners' fog-addled perception is genuinely uncertain. But the gardeners see them, or think they do: figures in the mist that might be other people, might be particularly upright brake formations, might be projections of isolated minds seeking company.

They appear most often at dawn or dusk—the times of weakest light—as shapes in the fog at the limit of visibility. Human-proportioned, roughly vertical, still or moving slowly. When approached, they're never quite there: either the fog thickens and visibility drops to nothing, or the shape resolves into vegetation, or it simply isn't present when you reach where it seemed to be.

The gardeners have different relationships with these almost-seen ones. One gardener ignores them entirely, perhaps doesn't perceive them, or chooses not to acknowledge them. Another watches them with interest, tracking their appearances in mental notes, looking for patterns. The third—the one who is now first, who has gone deepest into stillness—seems to commune with them somehow, orienting toward them during the long periods of motionlessness, eyes tracking movement no one else can confirm.

Sometimes the almost-seen ones are in places where a person couldn't be: standing in the middle of a pool, rising from substrate too unstable to support weight, positioned where brake grew too thickly to permit passage. This suggests hallucination. But sometimes they leave traces: compressed vegetation where a body might have stood, markings in moss that might be footprints, though they could also be beetle galleries or water-damage patterns.

The most unsettling sightings are of figures that look like the departed colleagues. Marcus's distinctive silhouette, Lin's particular way of standing with weight on one leg. But these people are years gone, down in the valleys, moved on to other projects or other lives. The gardeners know this intellectually. Yet the figures appear, and they look so specifically like those particular people that dismissing them as random pattern-recognition feels inadequate.

One theory, held by the gardener who is most verbal: the brake slopes somehow record presence. The plants grow around and through everything, responding to pressure, moisture, electrical fields. Perhaps they retain some impression of where people have been, what shapes moved through them, and under certain conditions—fog density, light angle, observer exhaustion—these impressions become briefly perceptible. A kind of organic photograph, time-delayed and incomplete.

Another theory, unspoken but present in the way the gardeners sometimes reach toward these figures: they're real, or real enough. Other people who came here and transformed even more completely than the gardeners. Who learned to move through the brake without disturbing it, to become so incorporated that they're indistinguishable from it most of the time, visible only in moments of thin boundary between states.

A third possibility, the one that feels most true in the deep night when emergency lights glow through meters of growth: there is no distinction. The gardeners themselves might be the almost-seen ones, observed by visitors they don't notice, half-visible in fog, not-quite-present. Everything here exists in the margin between states. Why should consciousness, presence, personhood be different?

## The Relationship Between Dwellers

The gardeners interact little but remain aware of each other. They leave markers—compressed stems, stones arranged in patterns—that convey basic information. They occasionally converge at the same location, drawn by the same event: a particularly dramatic growth surge, a pool forming in an unusual configuration, a section of machinery activating after long dormancy.

When they meet, they stand together in silence, watching whatever drew them. Sometimes one will gesture—indicating a detail the other might miss, a crack forming in a brake-mass, a shift in water-sound. The communication is minimal but sufficient. They're colleagues still, in some sense, co-observers of the system they've joined.

The first one, the nearly-motionless one, receives care from the others. They bring water, check for signs of distress, clear brake-growth that threatens to completely engulf. This care is given matter-of-factly, without ceremony. It's maintenance, like their other interventions in the system's flows. The first one doesn't acknowledge the care overtly, but positions shift slightly, a hand moves in gesture that might be thanks or simply autonomic response.

The relationship between gardeners and machinery dwellers is harder to characterize. It's not symbiotic exactly—the system doesn't need them. But it's not parasitic either—they take little from it. Perhaps commensal: they benefit from the stable zones the system creates, and the system is neither helped nor harmed by their presence, except when they intervene to prevent destructive oscillations.

But there are moments that suggest something more. When a gardener approaches a turbine cluster, the lights sometimes pulse in what feels like acknowledgment. When they redirect a stream to restore flow to generators, the hum of machinery shifts in tone—satisfaction, perhaps, or simply the sound of restored function, but the gardeners hear it as response. They've begun to develop an intuition for what the system "wants," if want is applicable to a distributed feedback network of plant and machine.

The gardeners and the small fauna exist in mutual tolerance. The beetles are food and indicators. The moths are companions in the darkness, their circling of lights a kind of presence. The marmot-creatures are mysteries that the gardeners no longer try to solve, simply noting their appearances as they note weather changes or growth surges. The birds are reminders of baseline biology, creatures that haven't been transformed by this place, that remain recognizably animal in conventional ways.

The visitors are treated with patient invisibility. The gardeners hide not from fear but from the recognition that interaction would be difficult and pointless. What would they say to people who still operate in the world of plans and purposes, who expect questions to have answers, who treat the brake as object rather than context? Better to remain unseen, allow the visitors to complete their futile measurements and depart.

Only the older botanist receives different treatment. For her, a gardener might become briefly visible, a presence acknowledged if not fully revealed. She's the bridge between what they were and what they've become, and they honor that by allowing her partial access to their transformed existence.

The almost-seen ones, if they exist, are treated with a kind of careful respect. The gardeners don't chase them or call out to them. They simply note their presence, watch them as they watch everything else here, allow them to be whatever they are—hallucination or hidden dweller or echo of past presence. In a place where the ground itself is uncertain, where growth and decay happen on the same timescale, insisting on certainty about anything seems misguided.

## The Future Dwellers

They haven't arrived yet, but the gardeners sense their eventual presence. The brake is spreading down the drainages, a few hundred meters per year, toward populated valleys. In a decade, maybe two, it will reach elevations where people live year-round. What happens then?

The gardeners imagine—in the wordless, image-based way they think now—communities adapted to brake-time. Villages that move with the growth, buildings designed to be temporary, overgrown and rebuilt continuously. People who learn the rhythms, who navigate by pattern rather than map, who accept the turbulence as condition rather than problem.

Or perhaps resistance: crews with cutting equipment and herbicide, engineers who dig barrier trenches, authorities who declare the brake an invasive species requiring eradication. Battles fought against growth that outpaces destruction, that regrows overnight, that absorbs and incorporates even the machinery meant to control it.

Or stranger possibilities: the brake changing as it descends, adapting to warmer temperatures, different moisture regimes. Becoming something else, something that doesn't require the narrow band of conditions present on these slopes. Spreading faster, or slower, or fragmenting into distinct varieties each suited to different elevations and exposures.

The gardeners won't be there to see it. They know this with certainty. Their bodies are already marginal, sustained by the specific conditions here—the cold that slows decay, the moisture that prevents desiccation, the minimal food requirements their reduced activity demands. They're adapted to this narrow range, like the brake itself. Descending would be lethal, not immediately but inevitably.

So the future dwellers will be others. Perhaps people who approach the advancing brake with the openness the original researchers lacked, who recognize earlier that transformation is the only sustainable relationship with it. Perhaps a new generation who grow up knowing the brake, who never expect stability, who learn its patterns from childhood.

Or perhaps the future dwellers will be something not-quite-human: the almost-seen ones made solid, the machinery dwellers developing mobility, the plants themselves achieving some kind of motile consciousness. The brake slopes feel like a place where such transformations are possible, where the boundaries between categories are negotiable, where new forms of being can emerge from the interaction of growth and electricity and cold and time.

## What They've Become

In the end, categorizing the gardeners as human or not-human misses the point. They're something the brake slopes have made: organisms adapted to turbulence, to impermanence, to living in the continuous present of growth and decay. They've lost capacity for planning and gained capacity for responsiveness. They've lost individual identity's sharp edges and gained intimacy with pattern.

They're not unhappy. Happiness requires a conception of alternative states, preferences between conditions, and they've moved beyond such frameworks. They're not fulfilled in any aspirational sense. But they're engaged, continuously attentive, embedded in flows of change that give their existence texture and meaning.

They live in a place that should be uninhabitable—too cold, too unstable, too far from resources. But the impossibility was based on assumptions about what dwelling requires, about the need for shelter and stability and clear boundaries between self and environment. The gardeners have discovered—or been forced into discovering—that other modes of dwelling exist.

They're intermediaries now between plant and machine, between growth and structure, between the organic and synthetic. They maintain balances, suggest adjustments, observe patterns. Whether this is purpose or simply behavior that emerged from long immersion doesn't matter. It's what they do, and doing it, they continue, and continuing is enough.

When they die—and they will, bodies can only defer decay so long—they'll become substrate. The brake will grow through them, their matter incorporated into the next surge of growth, their chemical components redistributed through root and stem. Perhaps some trace of their presence will persist in the system, the way the machinery dwellers exist as patterns in plant-and-metal assemblages. Perhaps not. The brake doesn't memorialize. It transforms and moves on.

But for now, they persist. Three of them, perhaps four, moving through the fog and growth, tending the strange hybrid ecosystem that has become their world, their home, their purpose, themselves. They're the brake slopes' only human element, its marginal consciousness, its witnesses and participants and experiments in what forms life can take when freed from—or forced beyond—conventional constraints.

They watch the stems grow. They listen to the turbines turn. They feel the substrate shift beneath them. They exist in the spaces between categories, in the fog between visible and invisible, in the time between growth and collapse. And in existing there, they've become the answer to a question no one thought to ask: what lives in a place that won't stay still long enough to be mapped, that grows too fast for permanence, that dissolves the boundaries between observer and observed?

They do. They live there. Not despite the turbulence but because of it, as it, within it. They're what the brake slopes create when they work on human consciousness long enough, patiently, the way they work on everything else: growing around it, through it, incorporating it into the pattern, making it part of the endless cycle of emergence and dissolution that defines this place.
