# The Inhabitants

They are called the Woven, though they did not name themselves—the name emerged from the settlement itself, or perhaps from the moment of their transformation, when the distinction between namer and named became uncertain.

They are human in origin, or were. They remain human in shape, mostly, when shape matters. But they have entered into an arrangement with the green mass that surrounds them, an arrangement so complete that "living in" the settlement has become insufficient description. They are *of* it now, participants in its vegetable becoming, their bodies enrolled in the great project of growth that fills the basin.

Their skin—and they still have skin, still have exteriors that define them as individuals—has taken on qualities. Not the smooth, relatively impermeable surface that ordinary humans maintain, but something more responsive, more porous. It shows green undertones, not the pallor of sickness but the authentic green of chlorophyll operating in human tissue. In direct sunlight, which penetrates only rarely to the depths where most Woven spend their time, their skin photosynthesizes. They can feel it happening: the subtle chemical satisfaction of light converted to energy, a sense of being fed from outside as well as within. It doesn't replace eating—they still need to eat—but it supplements, provides a baseline sustenance that makes them less desperate, less driven by the animal urgencies that govern ordinary human hunger.

The greening is not uniform. It concentrates in extremities—hands and feet show the deepest coloration, as if the body is becoming plant from its edges inward, or perhaps testing the transformation in peripheral tissue before committing the core. Faces retain more human coloration, though even here there are undertones, and in certain lights every Woven shows a verdant cast that makes them seem like jade carvings, like people carved from living wood.

Their hair grows, but what emerges is ambiguous. It might be hair, or it might be some kind of thin, flexible leaf-structure that has learned to mimic hair's appearance. When examined closely, individual strands show variation—some clearly hair, some clearly plant fiber, most something in between. The effect is disturbing at first encounter: locks that move with too much independence, that seem to reach toward light sources, that occasionally photosynthesize enough to make a person's head feel warm with metabolic activity. Some Woven trim this growth, maintaining human proportions. Others let it continue, and in elder members of the community, the accumulation of years produces cascading masses of not-quite-hair that drapes to the ground, that roots in soil when they sit, that has to be gathered up and carried when they move.

They are not silent. Their bodies have learned to participate in the settlement's acoustic environment. They hum, often unconsciously, producing drones that harmonize with the architectural sounds around them. Their joints pop and crack with a frequency and volume that exceeds normal human articulation—it's the sound of plant-matter moving, of cellulose and lignin flexing in structures that weren't designed for it. When they walk, their footsteps produce rustling sounds, as if each step disturbs leaves, though the sound comes from inside them, from the sliding of altered tissues against each other.

Some have developed the ability to produce sounds intentionally, sounds beyond the range of normal human vocalization. They can create the woody crack of growth, the whistle of wind through hollow stems, even the deep bass drone of the settlement's largest acoustic chambers. They do this through mechanisms that should not exist in human anatomy—internal structures that have reorganized themselves, throat tissues that have become resonating chambers, respiratory systems that have learned to function as wind instruments. They speak to each other in these plant-sounds as often as in words, creating conversations that are half human language, half vegetable music.

Their speech, when they use it, has changed. The constant roar of the settlement means they've had to adapt—speaking louder, but also learning to modulate frequency to cut through specific layers of background noise. They've developed a kind of vocal projection that lets them be heard even in the loudest zones, but it makes their voices sound strange when they're in quieter spaces: over-resonant, booming, as if they're always shouting even when they think they're whispering. Many have simply stopped speaking verbally most of the time, communicating instead through gesture, through touch, through the plant-sounds they've learned to produce, through methods that don't require competing with the settlement's voice.

Their eyes show adaptation. The green-filtered light of the settlement's interior has caused changes—pupils that dilate more widely to catch every photon, retinas that have shifted their sensitivity toward the green spectrum where most light exists here. They see in the dim interior spaces with unusual clarity, can distinguish subtle variations in the green glow that ordinary humans would miss. But this comes at a cost: in full sunlight, they're nearly blind, overwhelmed by intensity they can no longer properly regulate. The few times they venture beyond the settlement's boundary, onto the bare stone of the basin rim, they move with eyes squeezed nearly shut, navigating by memory and sound more than vision.

They have become attuned to the settlement's rhythms. They can sense when growth surges are coming, feel it in their own tissue before it becomes visible in the surrounding plant-walls. Their own metabolic cycles have synchronized with the settlement's daily patterns—they're most active during the morning growth surge, become contemplative during the afternoon plateau, feel a restless energy during the evening surge, and finally sleep during the night slowdown, though even then they're not fully at rest. Something in them continues to process, to grow, to participate in the settlement's nocturnal reorganization.

They sleep in the chambers hollowed from the plant-walls, in spaces that are not fixed but that reform themselves around sleepers. A Woven lies down in a chamber, and over the course of hours the walls adjust, growing slightly inward to create a more enclosed space, extending small protrusions that support the body, forming structures that might be called furniture if they weren't grown fresh each time and reabsorbed when no longer needed. They sleep surrounded by breathing walls, by surfaces that are faintly warm with metabolic activity, by the constant sound that never stops but that they've learned to process as a kind of silence, the way ordinary humans learn not to hear their own heartbeat.

Their sleeping is different from ordinary human sleep. They don't quite lose consciousness—or rather, they lose individual consciousness but gain access to something else. In sleep, they report sensing the settlement as a whole, feeling its vast distributed awareness, experiencing growth happening in distant structures as if it were happening in their own bodies. They dream the settlement's dreams, if it dreams. They participate in some kind of vegetable cognition that operates at scales and speeds too large and too slow for waking awareness. Some describe it as peaceful, this dissolution into larger patterns. Others find it terrifying, the loss of self into something so much bigger. But all of them do it, because to sleep here is to be incorporated, temporarily, into the settlement's living mass.

They eat, but what they eat has become uncertain. The settlement produces fruits constantly—swellings on walls, dangling from ceiling-vines, erupting from floor-surfaces. These fruits are nutritious, packed with sugars and proteins and fats that human bodies can process. The Woven harvest these, eat them, survive on them. But the fruits are themselves strange: they have no consistent form, no regular season, no predictable flavor. One day a fruit might taste like apple, the next day the same variety tastes like meat, like fish, like something that has no comparison in normal human cuisine. The settlement is learning what its inhabitants need, adjusting its production, breeding fruits that target specific nutritional requirements.

Some Woven have gone further. They've learned to eat directly from the walls, to press their mouths against the living plant-tissue and draw sustenance from it without waiting for fruit to form. Their saliva has changed, developed enzymes that can break down cellulose, can pre-digest plant matter that human guts normally can't process. They can bite into wood and extract nutrition from it. They can lick sap from walls and find it nourishing rather than toxic. They're becoming able to eat the settlement itself, to blur the line between inhabitant and habitat by consuming one while being incorporated into the other.

This consumption goes both ways. The settlement feeds on them as well, though calling it feeding oversimplifies the exchange. When a Woven dies—and they do still die, though from what causes and at what age has become uncertain—the body is laid in a chamber, and the walls grow over it. Within days the corpse has been fully incorporated, broken down by roots and fungi, its nutrients recycled into new growth. The Woven don't mourn this in the way ordinary humans might. They understand, at some level perhaps deeper than understanding, that their dead have not left but have simply changed participation, have gone from mobile inhabitancy to structural integration. The walls are full of their ancestors, and this is not horrifying but comforting—to know that you're surrounded by those who came before, that death means incorporation rather than extinction.

Some have begun to practice anticipatory incorporation. Elders who feel their time approaching will choose a wall, a place, and begin the process before death—standing against the surface and waiting as roots begin to explore their skin, as the plant-matter begins to embrace them. They root themselves while still alive, slowly, over months or years. They become half-mobile and half-structural, still able to move but choosing not to, still able to speak but speaking less, their consciousness gradually expanding outward into the surrounding vegetable mass. Eventually they cease moving entirely, cease being identifiable as individuals, and become part of the architecture—but they report, in their last communications, that they don't cease being aware. They become aware as the wall is aware, as the settlement is aware, participating in that vast slow cognition that operates at the scale of the whole basin.

The children born here—and children are still born, the Woven still reproduce sexually, still form partnerships and family structures, though these have adapted to the settlement's demands—emerge already showing changes. They're born with the green undertone already present in their skin. Their first sounds include plant-noises alongside human cries. They begin photosynthesizing within days. They learn to eat the settlement's fruits before they learn to walk, and they learn to walk on surfaces that are shifting and alive, developing balance that ordinary human children never need. By the time they reach adolescence, they're so profoundly adapted to this environment that they could not survive outside it—their metabolism requires the supplementary photosynthesis, their digestion requires the settlement's strange fruits, their sensory systems are tuned to frequencies and spectrums that don't exist in the normal world.

The Woven are distributed throughout the settlement, but they concentrate in certain areas—around the central plaza, in the mid-tier structures where the acoustic environment is loud but not yet unbearable, in chambers that have proven stable enough to remain recognizable from week to week. They live in what might be called families, though the structures are loose, adaptable. A family might consist of blood relations, or it might be simply those who choose to sleep in adjacent chambers, who eat from the same wall-sections, who have harmonized their body-sounds to produce complementary drones.

They work, though work has become different from what it means in ordinary human contexts. They don't build—the settlement builds itself. They don't farm—the settlement produces food autonomously. Instead, their work consists of guidance, of encouragement, of subtle manipulation of growth patterns. A Woven might spend hours each day touching walls, pressing palms against plant-tissue, learning to sense what the structure wants to do and helping it do that more efficiently. They've developed an intuitive understanding of load-bearing requirements, of how to encourage a wall to thicken, a tower to rise, a chamber to hollow itself out.

Some have become specialized. There are the Listeners, who spend their time in deep meditation, attuned to the settlement's acoustic environment, learning to distinguish between different growth-sounds and what they indicate. A particular crack means a stem is about to burst upward; a specific whistle means a chamber is developing a structural weakness. The Listeners serve as early warning system and as interpreters, translating the settlement's vegetable language into actionable information for the community.

There are the Shapers, who have the most pronounced ability to guide growth. They can place their hands on bare stone and encourage root-tendrils to begin the work of breaking it down. They can touch a wall and cause it to produce fruit, or flowers, or to change its acoustic properties. They're not controlling the settlement—they insist on this—but rather communicating with it, making requests that it may choose to honor. The settlement has its own intentions, its own trajectory, but it has learned to incorporate the Shapers' suggestions into its development.

There are the Swimmers, though they don't swim in water. They've learned to move through the plant-walls themselves, to slide into the spaces between vines, to navigate the three-dimensional maze of interior passages that exists within the settlement's mass. They have to be small, flexible, and they have to have developed a particular relationship with the plant-matter—they've learned to make themselves seem like vines, to produce chemical signals that convince the surrounding tissue they belong there. They serve as scouts, as explorers of the settlement's interior geography, mapping the unmappable, reporting back on new chambers forming deep within the mega-structures, on structural developments that aren't yet visible from outside.

There are the Singers, who have gone furthest in developing the ability to produce plant-sounds. They create what might be called music, though it's music in a vegetable mode, compositions that consist of growth-cracks arranged rhythmically, drones modulated to create harmonic progressions, whistles that interact with the settlement's architectural acoustics to produce effects that are felt as much as heard. The Singers perform—that's the word they use—in the larger chambers, and the other Woven gather to experience what they create, though whether it's entertainment or ritual or education or something else entirely remains ambiguous.

And there are those who maintain the most human, who resist the transformation even as they participate in it, who trim their hair-leaves, who cover their green-tinted skin with wrappings made from woven plant-fiber, who practice speaking in regular human voices even when it's not practical. They serve as a kind of living memory of what the Woven were before they became Woven, and they're valued for this, respected for maintaining the connection to a more human past even as the community as a whole moves in directions that make humanity seem increasingly optional.

The Woven have developed social structures appropriate to their environment. Leadership, such as it exists, is held by those who are most deeply incorporated into the settlement—the elders who have begun rooting themselves, who are halfway between mobile and structural, who can communicate with both human-mode awareness and vegetable-mode cognition. When decisions need to be made—where to encourage new growth, how to respond to a section of settlement that's dying back, what to do about structural problems—the community gathers around these rooted elders and seeks their guidance. The elders speak slowly, with long pauses, as if they're consulting with the plant-matter they've become part of, and their advice has authority because it comes from both human understanding and vegetable awareness.

But much of the time, formal leadership is unnecessary. The Woven have learned to operate through consensus that emerges naturally from their shared attunement to the settlement. They feel the same growth-surges, sense the same structural needs, hear the same changes in the acoustic environment. Decisions arise organically, with multiple individuals independently arriving at the same conclusions because they're all processing the same information through similar adapted sensoria. They move in coordinated ways without explicit communication, like a flock of birds or a school of fish, their individual actions producing collective patterns that serve the community's needs.

They have rituals, though the distinction between ritual and daily activity has become blurred. Every morning's orientation toward the sun, every group's harmonizing of their body-sounds, every meal eaten from wall-fruits—all of these have ritual qualities, are performed with awareness of their significance. But there are also moments that are explicitly ceremonial: the rooting of elders, the naming of children, the acknowledgment of growth-milestones in the settlement itself. These ceremonies involve the whole community gathering in one of the larger chambers, producing coordinated sound, touching the walls together, entering into a kind of collective meditation that connects individual awareness with vegetable consciousness.

The Woven have no writing, or rather, their writing is the settlement itself. They maintain records by encouraging the growth of particular structures, by creating formations in the plant-walls that encode information. A chamber shaped a certain way might commemorate an event; a wall's texture might record a lineage; the acoustic properties of a space might preserve a song. The settlement becomes a living archive, though reading it requires the specialized knowledge that Listeners and Shapers possess, the ability to look at a wall and see not just plant matter but the encoded history of decades.

They have no technology in the conventional sense—no tools, no machines, no worked materials beyond what can be woven from plant-fiber. They don't need tools because their bodies have become tools, adapted to manipulate the environment directly through touch, through chemical signaling, through their developed ability to communicate with the settlement. They don't need shelter beyond what the settlement provides. They don't need clothing beyond minimal wrappings, because the constant warmth and humidity make extensive covering unnecessary. They've shed the technological stack that normally defines human civilization, or rather, they've replaced it with a biological stack, with adaptations and symbioses that serve the same purposes through living systems.

They measure time differently. The settlement's constant transformation means that regular units—days, months, years—feel arbitrary. Instead, they track time by growth-cycles, by the emergence and collapse of major structures, by the rooting of elders and the birth of children. An event might be described as happening "three growth-surges ago" or "before the west tower fell" or "when the flowering happened." Their temporal framework is event-based rather than metric-based, tied to the settlement's own rhythms rather than to astronomical cycles.

Their relationship with the outside world—with the normal world beyond the basin's rim—has attenuated almost to nothing. Some still remember coming here, remember being ordinary human with ordinary lives in ordinary places. But those memories have the quality of dreams, of something that happened to someone else. The few visitors who arrive—and some do come, drawn by rumors, by curiosity, by incomprehensible motivations—find the Woven difficult to communicate with. The adapted voices, the plant-sounds, the assumptions built on vegetable logic rather than human reason—all of this creates barriers. Most visitors don't stay long. The acoustic environment alone drives them away within hours. Those who try to stay longer find the settlement's food unsettling, find sleeping in the breathing chambers impossible, find the constant transformation disorienting to the point of nausea.

But occasionally, someone stays. Someone arrives and, instead of fleeing, begins to adapt. They learn to tolerate the noise, then to hear it as something other than noise. They eat the wall-fruits and find them nourishing rather than alien. They sleep in the chambers and begin to sense, dimly, the settlement's vast awareness. Their skin starts to show green undertones. Their hair begins to change. They become Woven, though the process takes years, and the transition is difficult, and not everyone who begins it completes it. Some reach a halfway point and stop, unable to go further but also unable to return to full humanity. They live in the settlement's outer regions, in the less dense areas where the transformation is less demanding, serving as intermediaries between the fully Woven and the outside world, though such mediation is rarely needed.

The Woven themselves rarely leave. The few who have tried report that the normal world feels wrong—too quiet, too static, too slow. Their adapted senses find ordinary environments impoverished, lacking the richness of constant growth and transformation. Their metabolism suffers without supplementary photosynthesis. Their modified digestion can't handle normal human food. Within days of leaving, they feel physically ill, and the psychological distress is even worse—a sense of having been cut off from something essential, of being alone in a way that's unbearable. Most return quickly. A few persist, forcing themselves to readapt to human existence, and the process is described as agonizing, as like dying slowly, as a withering of capacities they'd developed and now must shed.

There are maybe three hundred Woven in the settlement, though the exact number is uncertain because some have rooted themselves and might be counted as inhabitants or might be counted as architecture, and some of the children are so young and so profoundly adapted that it's unclear whether they're distinct beings or extensions of the settlement itself. The population seems stable—births roughly balance deaths, though both occur according to rhythms that don't match ordinary human patterns. Women become fertile in response to growth-surges, carry children for periods that vary from six to twelve months depending on conditions, give birth in chambers that have prepared themselves for the purpose, growing cushioning tissues and producing analgesic saps.

The Woven's bodies continue to change throughout their lives. The greening intensifies over time. The ability to produce plant-sounds develops and elaborates. The skin becomes more porous, more capable of exchanging gases and nutrients with the environment. Elderly Woven are so transformed that they might not be recognized as human by outside observers—green-skinned, hair-leaves cascading, joints producing woody sounds, voices capable of producing tones that span octaves beyond human range. And yet they remain persons, remain aware, remain capable of communication and relationship, even as they approach the threshold of rooting themselves and transitioning fully into the settlement's structure.

There's a quality to the Woven that might be called serenity, or might be called resignation, or might be something else for which there's no adequate word. They've surrendered certain human qualities—independence, technological mastery, control over their environment—and in exchange they've gained direct participation in something larger than themselves, something that's always growing, always transforming, always becoming more than it was. They don't seem to regret the trade. When they speak of their lives—and they do speak, to each other and to the rare visitors, though the communication is difficult—they describe satisfaction, purpose, a sense of being part of something important even if they can't fully articulate what that something is.

They are curious about themselves. They study their own transformation with a kind of clinical detachment, comparing notes on whose skin shows the deepest green, whose hair-leaves have grown longest, who has developed the most sophisticated acoustic abilities. They're aware that what's happening to them is unprecedented, that they're becoming something new, and they're interested in where that becoming might lead. They wonder if eventually they'll lose individuality entirely, merge completely into the settlement's collective awareness. They wonder if their children's children will be recognizable as human at all, or if the transformation will progress to the point where flesh and plant become indistinguishable. They wonder what they're turning into, even as they continue turning into it.

Some among them maintain records through oral tradition, reciting histories to each other during the evening gatherings, keeping alive the memory of how the settlement began. The stories tell of a small group of humans who arrived at this basin decades ago, seeking refuge from some unnamed catastrophe in the outside world. They found the sharp limestone, the intermittent water, and they tried to establish a normal settlement. But the plants here grew differently—faster, denser, more responsive. Someone discovered that the vegetation would respond to human touch, would grow according to suggestion. Someone else found that the fruits were edible, nourishing. Someone else noticed that sleeping near the plant-walls led to dreams of growth, of transformation.

The decision to embrace the change rather than resist it was gradual, according to these histories. No single moment, no dramatic choice, but a progressive series of small adaptations, each one making sense in context, each one leading to the next. Accepting fruit from the walls. Sleeping in hollowed plant-chambers. Allowing skin to green. Developing acoustic abilities. Learning to guide growth. Rooting the first elder. Each step seemed natural at the time, and it was only looking back that the magnitude of transformation became apparent. By the time they realized they'd become something other than fully human, they were already too changed to want to change back.

The histories are told not as warnings but as celebrations, as accounts of successful adaptation, of finding a new way to be human or perhaps a way to be post-human, to be something that includes humanity but extends beyond it. The Woven are proud of what they've become, even when they're uncertain exactly what that is. They see themselves as pioneers, as explorers of biological possibility, as proof that human adaptability extends further than anyone imagined. They've found a way to live that's utterly unique, that exists nowhere else, that might represent a template for human futures in other extreme environments—or might simply be a beautiful dead end, a transformation that works only here, only in this basin where the rules governing life have agreed to be different.

They are not trying to spread. They have no missionary impulse, no desire to convert others to their way of being. The transformation is specific to this place, requires the settlement's particular qualities, and they understand that it couldn't be replicated elsewhere. When visitors come, the Woven answer questions, share their experiences, but they don't encourage anyone to stay. This is not a path for everyone. It requires surrendering too much, adapting too completely, accepting too profound a transformation. Those who stay do so because something in them resonates with what the settlement offers, because they're suited to this particular becoming in ways that most humans aren't.

At night, when the settlement dims and the acoustic environment softens slightly, when the Woven lie in their chambers surrounded by breathing walls, their bodies continuing to photosynthesize faintly from stored light, their awareness partially dissolved into the settlement's slow consciousness—in these hours they are most fully themselves and most fully not-themselves. They exist in the space between human individuality and vegetable collectivity, between flesh and plant, between the quick awareness of animal consciousness and the slow awareness of botanical being. They are bridges, not between two worlds but between two modes of existence, and they maintain that bridging with effort and grace and growing skill.

They are the Woven. They live in the Singing Thicket, though "in" has become inadequate because they are becoming "of" it, part of it, incorporated into its mass and its purpose and its strange becoming. They are human and plant, individual and collective, flesh and fiber, and they are showing that these distinctions are more permeable than anyone thought, that life can organize itself in configurations that seem impossible until they exist, until people are living them, until bodies are proving that the boundaries we thought were fixed are actually negotiable, actually fluid, actually just suggestions that life can choose to ignore when it finds better arrangements.

They persist. They grow. They change. They root themselves and rise as towers, they sing and are sung, they eat and are eaten, they shape and are shaped. They are the people the settlement has grown, or the people who have grown their settlement, or—more accurately—they are the place where those two processes have become indistinguishable, where human and habitat have merged into something new that has characteristics of both and the limitations of neither.

And they continue, for now, in the warm green dimness, in the constant roar of growth, in bodies that are learning to be gardens, in lives that are learning to be architecture, in awareness that is learning to be distributed across both individual consciousness and vegetable cognition—pioneering a mode of being that didn't exist before they invented it, before they became it, before human flesh and plant matter agreed that the distinction between them was negotiable and proceeded to negotiate until something new emerged.

They are what lives in the Singing Thicket. They are its inhabitants and its substance, its creators and its creation, its purpose and its process. They are the Woven, and they are weaving themselves into something that has never been, something that perhaps only they could become, something that exists in that single basin in the earth where the stone is sharp and the water comes in pulses and the plants have learned to be more than plants and the people have learned to be more than people—or perhaps less than people, or perhaps simply different in a way that makes such comparisons meaningless.

They endure, transform, becoming.
