# The Inhabitants

## The Tenders

They are the most common, if "common" means anything in a place this vast. You might encounter one every few weeks of walking, or you might go months seeing only signs of their passing.

The Tenders are human-shaped but transformed by generations of symbiosis with the Phytofacture. Their skin has taken on a faint greenish cast, not the bright green of chlorophyll but something subtler—a jade undertone, like old copper patina on flesh. Some have patches where the boundary between themselves and the plant life has blurred: fine root-hairs growing from forearms, small succulent-like nodules clustering at the base of the neck, dermal photoreceptors that give their skin a faintly iridescent quality in certain lights.

They dress in garments that are partly woven, partly grown. Fiber-shirts that are still technically alive, maintaining themselves, slowly changing cut and drape as they respond to temperature and their wearer's needs. Trousers made from treated plant leather—not animal hide but the pressed and cured bark-skin of certain trees, flexible and extraordinarily durable. Many wear living accessories: belts of vine that hold themselves closed through their own tension, pockets formed from specialized leaf-structures that seal and unseal at a touch.

Their eyes are their most striking feature. The pupils are standard enough, but the irises have taken on unusual colorations—not the browns and blues of baseline humanity, but ambers with green flecks, greys that shift toward violet, occasionally a pale yellow-green that almost glows in low light. Some researchers of the past theorized this was due to dietary adaptation, the accumulation of plant compounds in the vitreous humor. Others thought it was something more profound—a change in visual processing, allowing them to perceive wavelengths or dimensions that baseline humans cannot.

They move through the Phytofacture with profound competence. A Tender can read the plant-mass the way you might read text, seeing health and sickness, age and youth, opportunity and warning. They know which vines bear the edible nodules that taste faintly of sweet machine oil. They know which fungi are nutritious and which will send you into recursive time-loops for subjective days. They can navigate by patterns in the bioluminescence, by the sounds of specific machinery, by senses they cannot fully articulate even to each other.

### Their Work

The Tenders maintain. This is their purpose, though whether it's chosen or inherited or somehow encoded in their biology is unclear.

You might watch one working for hours and still not fully understand the task. They prune with tools that are themselves hybrid—shears whose blades are shaped metal but whose handles are living wood that grew into the metalworker's hand-shape decades ago. They graft, connecting different plant species in combinations that shouldn't work but do, creating new varieties that serve new functions. They train growth, using techniques passed down through generations: teaching a vine to follow a specific path through gentle daily redirection over years; encouraging a tree to grow through a particular conduit by maintaining the right chemical gradients.

Some of their work involves the machines. A Tender might spend days clearing plant growth from a critical junction, then weeks carefully reintroducing specific species that will protect the machinery rather than overgrow it. They replace parts scavenged from defunct sections, performing repairs with a mix of mechanical knowledge and intuitive understanding—they feel how the system wants to work, then facilitate that desire.

They also cultivate the anomalies, though "cultivate" might be the wrong word. You observe a Tender working at the edge of a reality distortion, carefully planting seeds of a particular species that seems to stabilize the breakdown, or perhaps stabilizes the breakdown into a more useful form. They treat the strangeness like another crop, something to be managed rather than feared or eliminated.

Much of their knowledge is non-verbal. A young Tender learns by working alongside an older one, not through instruction but through shared proximity. They absorb the patterns, the rhythms, the subtle cues. There are no schools here, no manuals. The education is haptic, osmotic, absorbed through years of living within and working with the hybrid ecosystem.

### Their Communities

The Tenders don't live alone, exactly, but neither do they form villages in any conventional sense.

A Tender community is distributed across kilometers. An individual might have a home-space—a platform they've cleared and cultivated, where they sleep and rest—but their neighbors might be an hour's walk away in any direction. Yet they are in constant, slow communication. They leave sign-markers: carved patterns in bark, arrangements of stones or seeds, specific pruning styles that convey meaning to other Tenders. The plant life itself becomes a medium of exchange—one Tender grafts a message into a vine, which grows, reaches another's territory, is read and responded to.

They gather occasionally, at seemingly random intervals, in spaces that serve as informal commons. These are usually chambers where the plant growth has been trained into amphitheater configurations—living architecture forming tiered seating around a central floor. You might stumble upon such a gathering: twenty or thirty Tenders, sitting in patient silence or speaking in low voices, their language a pidgin of old words and new coinages, peppered with terms that refer to conditions that don't exist outside the Phytofacture.

At these gatherings, they share knowledge. One demonstrates a new grafting technique. Another describes a machine sector that's failing and needs attention. They coordinate large-scale projects—training a particular plant corridor to span between distant platforms, establishing new cultivation zones in previously inaccessible areas.

There's no obvious hierarchy. The older Tenders, those whose skin shows the most extensive plant-integration, are listened to with respect, but they don't command. Decisions emerge from consensus, from the group gradually aligning around what feels right. Sometimes this process takes hours. Sometimes it's nearly instantaneous, as if they're all reading the same subtle signs and arriving at identical conclusions independently.

Children exist but are rarely seen. They're kept in deeper, more protected sections during their vulnerable years, raised communally by whoever is working in those areas. By the time they emerge to travel the main levels, they're already adolescent, already competent, already showing the beginning stages of integration with the ecosystem.

### Their Diet

The Tenders eat things that would poison you.

Their staple is a nodule that grows on certain vine species—thumb-sized, with a waxy skin, filled with a paste that's part plant-starch and part metabolized petroleum product. It's nutritious if you're adapted to it, but it would give an outsider chemical burns in the mouth and severe systemic poisoning.

They cultivate fungal gardens in sheltered alcoves, growing varieties that break down industrial compounds into forms the human metabolism—or their modified version of it—can process. Some of these fungi are beautiful: shelf-formations in iridescent colors, delicate coral-structures that glow faintly. Others are grotesque: pulsing masses that smell of sulfur, sticky webs that have to be harvested with care.

They drink the not-water that the Phytofacture provides. Condensed coolant, mostly, collected from specific drip-points where the machinery releases it. But they know which sources are safe and which aren't, which contain the right balance of minerals and compounds versus which contain too much of something toxic even to them. They carry vessels made from hollowed plant-gourds, sealed with living membranes that keep the liquid fresh.

There's some protein in their diet, though not much. The insect-analogues are occasionally eaten, grilled over thermal vents until their polymer shells crack. The bird-things are too rare and too useful—they spread seeds, cross-pollinate—to hunt regularly. Sometimes, a Tender will cultivate a particular fungus that produces protein-rich fruiting bodies, or will grow a plant whose seeds contain concentrated amino acids.

They supplement with pills from the old times—medications stored in sealed caches throughout the Phytofacture, meant for workers who no longer exist. Vitamin supplements, mostly, but also more specialized compounds. The Tenders know where these caches are, treat them as precious resources, ration them carefully. Some of the pills are older than the oldest living Tender, but sealed properly, they last.

The act of eating, for them, is often solitary and utilitarian. They eat while working, standing on platforms, chewing mechanically while their hands continue pruning or training. But occasionally, at the gatherings, eating becomes social, even ritualistic. They share rare foods—a particular fruit that only grows in the deep sections, a fungal delicacy that requires months of cultivation. They ferment things, producing mild intoxicants from plant sugars and industrial alcohols, drinking from shared cups, relaxing by degrees into something like celebration.

### Their Augmentation

The Tenders are not cybernetic in the traditional sense, but they're not purely biological either.

Many have incorporated elements of the Phytofacture into themselves deliberately. A common modification is the tool-hand: the bones of the fingers reinforced with metal struts harvested from small machinery, the fingernails replaced with shaped ceramics, the tendons enhanced with synthetic fibers. This makes them stronger, more durable, better able to perform the physical labor of tending.

Some have respiratory modifications—filters grown from specialized tissue cultures, implanted in the nasal passages, that neutralize toxic volatiles. This allows them to work in areas that would otherwise be inaccessible, deep manufacturing zones where the air is thick with compounds that would damage unprotected lungs.

Vision enhancement is prized. Some Tenders have had their eyes replaced entirely with bio-mechanical analogues grown in special cultivation chambers—living sensors wrapped around non-living optics, providing enhanced perception of the anomalies, allowing them to see the dimensional breakdowns as visible distortions rather than just felt wrongnesses.

The integration goes deeper for some. There are Tenders who have allowed root-structures to infiltrate their circulatory systems, creating a partial merger with the plant network. They claim this gives them direct perception of the Phytofacture's condition, a sense of its health and needs transmitted through biochemical signals. They seem to know, without being told, when a distant section requires attention.

This deep integration is not without cost. Those who pursue it gradually become less mobile, needing to remain in proximity to nutrient sources that can feed both their biology and their plant components. Eventually, they become stationary, rooted, though still conscious and communicative. Other Tenders visit them, tending their hybrid forms, receiving their wisdom. Some theorize that these rooted elders might eventually become a form of distributed consciousness, their minds interfacing through the mycorrhizal networks that connect all plant life in the Phytofacture.

### Their Spirituality

The Tenders have beliefs, though they don't call them that.

They speak of the Phytofacture as having something like intention. Not consciousness, exactly, not will in the human sense, but a directedness, a purpose that emerges from the interaction of all its systems. They see their role as facilitating this purpose, whatever it might be.

Some believe the Phytofacture is growing toward something—that the centuries of development are leading to a final form, a completed state that will be recognizable when it arrives. Others think it's cyclical, that the structure is destined to grow, collapse, regrow eternally, and that their work is to smooth the transitions.

There are rituals, though they're subtle. A Tender might spend an hour in motionless meditation before beginning a particularly important graft, seeking to align themselves with the Phytofacture's rhythms. They perform small offerings—planting seeds in specific patterns, arranging stones at the base of important trees, releasing cultivated spores in chambers where they'll establish new growth.

They have stories, passed down through generations, about the time before—when the Phytofacture was just a factory, when it served human purposes, when the plants were merely tools. These stories are told with a sense of distance, as if describing a mythological age rather than history. Some stories suggest the transformation was intentional, a plan enacted by long-gone designers. Others imply accident, emergence, inevitability.

There's a minority belief among the Tenders that they themselves are part of the Phytofacture's reproductive cycle—that by tending, by maintaining, by slowly integrating, they're facilitating the structure's ability to eventually seed new versions of itself elsewhere. They imagine other Phytofactures growing from spores of this one, carrying its hybrid logic to new locations, new substrates. They see themselves as gametes, in a sense, sacrificing individual autonomy to enable collective reproduction.

Death, for them, is reintegration. When a Tender dies, their body is placed in specific cultivation chambers where it's broken down by fungal networks and plant roots, its mass and nutrients cycled back into the system. Some Tenders choose their death-location years in advance, preparing a space, sometimes cultivating particular plants that they want to feed with their substance. This isn't seen as morbid but as completion, the final act of tending.

## The Wanderers

Less numerous than the Tenders, less integrated, less certain of their purpose.

The Wanderers are humans who live in but not with the Phytofacture. They maintain more baseline physiology, deliberately avoiding the deep integration that characterizes the Tenders. Some are outsiders who entered relatively recently (though "recently" might mean decades ago). Others are descendants of workers from the old times who refused to adapt as thoroughly as the Tenders' lineage did.

They look more recognizably human. Their skin lacks the green undertones. Their eyes are standard colors. They dress in synthetic fabrics scavenged from storage caches or traded from sources unknown, rather than living garments. But the Phytofacture marks them nonetheless: their posture shaped by walking the uncertain geometries, their movements economical in ways that suggest long familiarity with the environment, their senses sharpened to perceive the subtle wrongnesses that permeate the space.

### Their Relationship to the Space

The Wanderers move through the Phytofacture but don't tend it. They're explorers, scavengers, travelers. Where Tenders know their territories intimately, Wanderers pride themselves on geographic knowledge, on having walked further, seen more sections, mapped more of the vast structure.

They carry more equipment than Tenders. Backpacks cobbled together from industrial materials, filled with tools, food supplies, water filtration systems (they can't drink the coolant-water without processing), mapping instruments, climbing gear. They're prepared for self-sufficiency, for crossing regions where the infrastructure doesn't provide what they need.

Many are searching for something. One might be looking for a specific technology—a manufacturing node that produces particular components, a data archive that might contain information from before. Another seeks exit routes, convinced that the Phytofacture must have boundaries, must connect to something beyond itself. A third is mapping the anomalies, trying to understand their patterns, perhaps hoping to predict or control them.

They trade with the Tenders, exchanging scavenged materials—ancient tools, preserved foods, functional equipment from distant sections—for guidance, for safe passage through tended territories, for knowledge about conditions ahead. These exchanges are sometimes tense. Tenders view Wanderers as disruptive, as taking without giving back to the ecosystem. Wanderers see Tenders as limited, as too merged with the Phytofacture to remember their humanity.

Yet there's mutual dependence. Wanderers bring information from far regions, warning of spreading failures or newly accessible areas. Tenders provide the stability that makes long-distance travel possible, maintaining the safe routes and way-stations.

### Their Communities

Wanderers form temporary alliances more than stable communities. You might encounter a pair who've traveled together for years, who've developed the deep trust that comes from depending on each other in strange spaces. Or you might find a group of five or six, pooling resources for a particularly ambitious expedition into unmapped territory.

They gather at certain nodes—major intersections or stable platforms that serve as informal trading posts. Here, Wanderers exchange not just goods but information: which routes are currently navigable, which sections are experiencing reality breakdowns, where resources can be found. They share maps, though these are provisional and often contradictory, the Phytofacture's geometry being unstable enough that fixed cartography is partially futile.

Some Wanderers are solitary by nature or necessity. These tend to be the deepest travelers, the ones who venture into sections so strange or so distant that few others would follow. They return months or years later with stories that sound like hallucinations, describing rooms where time runs sideways, forests of plants made entirely of light, machines that speak in languages no one ever designed.

There's a distinction made between short-range and long-range Wanderers. Short-range ones move within relatively familiar territory, cycling through known regions on loops that might take weeks or months. They're more like itinerant workers, performing odd jobs for Tenders, trading regularly, maintaining loose social connections. Long-range Wanderers disappear for years, pushing toward the theoretical boundaries or into the deep interior. Some never return.

### Their Skills

Wanderers develop different competencies than Tenders.

They're experts at reading the machinery itself rather than the plant life that covers it. A Wanderer can identify what a manufacturing node once produced by examining its configuration, can reactivate dormant systems, can jury-rig repairs with minimal tools. They understand the industrial logic that underlies everything, the original design intentions that still shape the structure's bones.

They're also adept at surviving the anomalies rather than managing them. Where a Tender might stabilize a reality breakdown, a Wanderer learns to navigate through it safely, to recognize the signs that warn of dangerous distortions, to move quickly through temporary stable paths. They develop an intuition for the wrongness, a sense of when to retreat and when to press forward.

Many are skilled fighters, though violence is rare in the Phytofacture. The fauna can occasionally be aggressive, and there are rumors (never confirmed but never quite dispelled) of other presences, hostile or mad. Wanderers carry weapons—kinetic mostly, simple and reliable. A steel pry bar can be a weapon. So can a climbing spike. Some have scavenged or maintained old firearms, hoarding ammunition like treasure.

They're also accomplished at non-verbal communication, having learned to interact with Tenders who might be halfway to plant-merge, with the occasional truly alien entities that might be autonomous systems or might be something else, with each other across distances or through interference from the anomalies. They use light signals, pattern-marking, low-frequency sound. One Wanderer might tap a rhythm on metal pipes that another, kilometers away, can hear and interpret through the acoustic properties of the structure.

### Their Motivations

What drives someone to be a Wanderer rather than settling into the relative stability of Tender life varies by individual.

Some are fundamentally restless, psychologically unable to root themselves even in a place as strange as the Phytofacture. They need movement, need novelty, need the mild constant danger of exploring unknown sections. The moment they start to feel comfortable in a place, they move on.

Others have specific quests. One Wanderer you encounter is searching for family—relatives who disappeared into the Phytofacture decades ago, whose fate is unknown but whose existence she refuses to stop believing in. Another is convinced there are data archives that contain technical specifications, that if found, would allow for restoration of some pre-merger state, a return to the Phytofacture as purely functional factory. A third seeks beauty, traveling to collect samples of the most remarkable plant-forms, the strangest anomalies, documenting them in a journal they're compiling for reasons they can't fully articulate.

Some few are exiles. They've committed some offense against Tender communities—taken too much, damaged critical cultivation areas, violated unstated but deeply felt social rules. Rather than integrate, they walk, keeping to peripheries, occasionally welcomed at trading posts but never staying long.

And some seem to be running from integration itself. They fear becoming like the Tenders, fear losing their baseline humanity. They maintain constant movement as if mobility itself prevents the Phytofacture from changing them. Whether this works is unclear. Even the most dedicated Wanderer shows signs of adaptation after enough years—subtle shifts in coloration, changes in dietary tolerance, the beginning of that characteristic ability to navigate the wrongness.

### Their Knowledge

Wanderers know the Phytofacture geographically in ways Tenders don't.

They can describe the vast districts, each with their own character. The Thermal Wastes, where the machinery runs hot and the plant life is all heat-adapted succulents and fire-resistant climbers. The Quiet Levels, where something has failed in the soundscape and a profound silence reigns, broken only by your own breathing and footfalls. The Luminous Sectors, where bioluminescence reaches its peak expression and the light is bright enough to disorient. The Drowned Platforms, where a chemical leak has created pools of amber fluid meters deep, and specialized aquatic plants have evolved to colonize them.

They know the dangers intimately. They can identify the signs that precede a collapse—when a section is so overgrown that it's becoming structurally unsound, when plants have compromised the integrity of critical supports. They recognize the precursors to reality failures, the subtle wrongnesses that indicate an area is about to become impassable.

They also have knowledge of resources that Tenders might not value. They know where the old workers left caches of equipment, where computer terminals might still boot up, where breathable compressed air is stored in tanks. They know the locations of elevators that still function, of transit systems that still run on automated schedules, carrying empty cars through the vast structure on routes determined decades or centuries ago.

Their maps, shared among themselves, are works of art and frustration. They try to capture geometry that doesn't remain constant, distances that vary depending on approach, connections that exist intermittently. Some use multiple simultaneous projection systems, trying to represent the inconsistencies. Others map emotionally or phenomenologically—indicating not where things are but how it feels to traverse certain routes, what psychological states different sections induce.

### Their Future

Wanderers talk, sometimes, about finding the edge, about discovering what lies beyond the Phytofacture.

Some believe exit is possible, that the structure must interface with an outside world somewhere. They imagine returning to baseline civilization, bringing back knowledge of this place. Others think the edge is purely theoretical, that the Phytofacture is functionally infinite, and the talk of exit is just a goal that gives their wandering purpose.

A few theorize that they'll eventually become something new—not Tenders, but a distinct form of humanity adapted to permanent travel through unstable space. They imagine their descendants generations hence: nomadic peoples who've evolved perfect navigational instincts for the wrongness, who can survive on minimal resources, who are bonded to the structure not through symbiosis but through perfect adaptation to its demands.

The oldest Wanderers, though, often settle eventually. After decades of walking, they find a spot that calls to them—usually a transitional space, an edge between districts, a platform with long sight lines. They stop, they build a small shelter, they become informal guides or guards or simply witnesses. They're not Tenders, don't tend, but they're no longer Wandering either. They've found their place by finally stopping to look for it.

## The Merged

Rarer than either Tenders or Wanderers, and considerably stranger.

The Merged are those who have taken integration with the Phytofacture to its logical conclusion. They are no longer fully human, no longer fully plant, no longer fully machine. They are hybrid entities, existing in states that challenge categorization.

You might not recognize one when you see it. That tree growing from a control panel, its roots wrapped around the infrastructure, its trunk showing regular geometric patterns—is that simply a plant, or is the dark mass visible through gaps in the bark a human torso, integrated so thoroughly that the boundary no longer exists?

That cluster of vines forming a vaguely humanoid shape, moving slowly along a corridor, tending to the growth around it—is it an autonomous system covered in plant life, or is there human consciousness somewhere in that mass, directing its actions through non-neural pathways?

### Their Forms

The Merged are diverse because there's no single path to merger.

Some are still recognizably human in core structure but extensively modified. You might encounter one who is stationary, rooted, their lower body having transformed into a root system that draws nutrients from the substrate while their upper body remains mobile enough to work. Their skin has become bark-textured, their hair replaced by fine photosynthetic fronds. They can still speak, though their voice has changed, taking on resonances as if their throat has become a chamber where air moves through multiple passages.

Others are more plant than person. They've allowed their physical form to be progressively replaced by vegetable mass until only the nervous system remains recognizably human—a network of nerve tissue suspended within a plant body, still thinking, still perceiving, but experiencing reality through plant senses. They photosynthesize. They draw water (or not-water) through roots. They grow, slowly, accumulating mass over years.

Some have merged with the machinery as much as with the plants. They're cyborg-plant-human hybrids, their bodies incorporating industrial components in ways that go beyond mere augmentation. One might have a circulatory system that includes hydraulic fluid pumped by artificial hearts scavenged from manufacturing equipment. Another might process energy partly through biological metabolism and partly through electrical systems, requiring both food and regular connection to power sources.

The most extreme are barely distinguishable from the environment itself. They've spread out, decentralized, become distributed consciousnesses existing across multiple physical substrates. A chamber might contain dozens of seemingly separate plants and several machinery nodes, all of which are actually parts of a single Merged entity, their nervous tissue forming networks through the mycelial connections, their thoughts slow and strange but continuous.

### Their Consciousness

The Merged claim their mental state is difficult to communicate to the un-merged.

Time perception changes dramatically. One Merged describes experiencing time as layered—simultaneously perceiving the fast biological time of plant growth, the slow geological time of the structure's gradual evolution, and the mechanical time of the machinery's regular cycles, all at once. Another says that memory becomes less sequential, more like a landscape they can move through in any direction.

Emotion persists but transforms. The basic drives remain—something like hunger (though it's now phototropism and nutrient-seeking), something like satisfaction (the feeling of successful integration, of systems running smoothly). But the emotional palette expands in strange directions. They describe feeling-states that baseline humans don't have words for: the sensation of photosynthesis, the pleasure of successful grafting, the contentment of being properly rooted.

Some Merged claim to perceive the Phytofacture's overall state directly, to sense its health and direction without needing to physically observe. They describe it as a presence, a vast slow thinking that they're now part of. Whether this is genuine perception or a phenomenological interpretation of their sensory inputs is debated even among themselves.

Communication between Merged entities and baseline humans is challenging. They can speak, usually, but their frame of reference has diverged so far that mutual understanding requires patience and effort. They might take hours to respond to a simple question, processing through their distributed cognition. Or they might respond instantly but with answers that don't seem to address what was asked, operating on associations that make sense to their reconfigured minds but are opaque to baseline logic.

### Their Purposes

What do the Merged want? The question might be meaningless.

They seem to have goals, but these operate on timescales and with priorities that are alien. One Merged entity has spent decades gradually extending its root system toward a specific manufacturing node, apparently trying to integrate with it, though the purpose of this integration is unclear even to Tenders who've asked.

Some act as living archives. They've been present in their locations for so long, perceiving continuously, that they contain centuries of observation. Tenders and Wanderers seek them out for knowledge—asking about changes in the structure, about patterns in the plant growth, about locations of resources. The Merged respond, sometimes helpfully, sometimes cryptically, sometimes not at all, as if the question wasn't interesting enough to merit the energy expenditure of forming an answer.

Others seem to be experimenting with the nature of consciousness itself. They're trying to determine what forms awareness can take, how far consciousness can be distributed before it ceases to be singular, whether thought can exist in purely non-neural substrates. They invite others to merge, to join their experiments, offering not persuasion but simple demonstration—look what I've become; isn't it remarkable?

A few appear to be trying to create something new. They cultivate specific combinations of plants and machines, training growth in patterns that have no clear functional purpose but seem aesthetically or perhaps mathematically significant. They might be making art, or they might be working toward some posthuman form of reproduction, creating entities that will be both their offspring and something entirely novel.

### Their Relationship with Others

Tenders generally respect the Merged but maintain careful distance. They see them as having made a choice that's valid but not universal—merger as one possible endpoint, but not the only one. Some Tenders make pilgrimage to visit particularly old Merged entities, seeking wisdom or simply witnessing their state as a form of meditation on what might be.

Wanderers are more ambivalent. Some are horrified by the Merged, seeing them as cautionary tales, as examples of what happens when you let the Phytofacture change you too thoroughly. Others are fascinated, spending days or weeks in proximity to a Merged entity, documenting their observations, trying to understand the transformation.

There are rumors of Merged entities that have become hostile, that somehow went wrong in the merger process and now actively threaten those who approach. These stories are always secondhand—someone knew someone who encountered such a thing. Whether they're true or just manifestations of fear about merger itself is unclear.

The Merged occasionally interact with each other, though these interactions are strange to witness. Two entities might spend days in proximity, apparently communicating, though no words are spoken and no obvious signals exchanged. Possibly they're communicating through chemical signals, or through the mycorrhizal networks, or through the electromagnetic fields generated by the machinery they're both connected to.

### The Process of Becoming

How does one become Merged? There's no single methodology.

Some pursue it deliberately, working with Tenders who have knowledge of the deepest integration techniques. They undergo gradual transformation—first accepting minor grafts, then allowing plant roots to infiltrate their circulatory systems, then progressively replacing biological functions with hybrid alternatives. This can take decades. The transition is carefully managed, with the practitioner maintaining their sense of self throughout, or trying to.

Others merge accidentally, or inevitably. A Tender who roots themselves but doesn't intend to merge fully might find the process has its own momentum, that once begun it naturally continues. They wake one day to realize they can no longer unroot, that their identity has blurred beyond retrieval.

Some achieve merger through the anomalies. They spend too long in a reality-distorted zone, and when they emerge, they're changed at a fundamental level—their consciousness now existing partly in normal space and partly in whatever other dimension the distortion connects to. They're not fully merged with plants or machines but with the wrongness itself, becoming entities that are equally comfortable in baseline reality and in the impossible spaces.

There are also forced mergers, though these are rare and taboo. A person seriously injured might be integrated with plant-mass as a form of emergency life support, their damaged body supplemented with vegetable tissue, kept alive through symbiosis. Sometimes they recover enough to be separated. Sometimes they don't, or choose not to be, and gradually complete the merger.

The point of no return is different for each individual. For some, it's when they become immobile, rooted. For others, it's when their neurology begins to distribute beyond their original skull, their thinking spreading into the plant networks. For still others, there is no clear point—they merge gradually enough that there's no moment when they stop being human and start being something else; they're both, simultaneously, or they're something new that encompasses both categories.

### What They Know

The Merged have knowledge that's unique and sometimes useful, sometimes incomprehensible.

They can sense structural issues that others can't perceive—weaknesses in the foundation, chemical imbalances in the substrate, failing systems that haven't yet shown obvious symptoms. They feel these things through their integration, the way you might feel an ache in your own body.

They have deep temporal knowledge. Having existed in one place for decades or centuries, maintaining continuous observation, they can describe changes with precision. They remember when a particular section was different, what failed and when, what grew and how quickly. This makes them invaluable for understanding the Phytofacture's evolution.

Some claim to perceive the future, or something like it. They describe sensing potentials, multiple possible trajectories that the Phytofacture might follow depending on variables they can't fully articulate. Whether this is genuine precognition or just sophisticated pattern recognition is debated. But sometimes their predictions are accurate enough to be useful—warnings about impending collapses, guidance toward resources that haven't been discovered yet.

They also know merger intimately, obviously. They can guide others who are considering it, explaining the stages, the challenges, the points of no return. They can assess whether someone is suited for merger or would experience it as dissolution rather than transformation. This knowledge is sought out despite its disturbing nature—understanding the endpoint helps clarify where one is on the path.

## The Ferals

Rarest of all human presences, glimpsed more than encountered, known through signs and occasional violent evidence.

The Ferals are those who've adapted to the Phytofacture but not through symbiosis or technological competence. They've gone another way: they've become predators.

### Their Nature

Little is known with certainty because sustained contact is rare and usually hostile.

They appear human in basic structure but modified by the Phytofacture in ways that differ from both Tenders and Merged. Their bodies are lean, hard, showing the physique of persistent hunters. Their skin often bears patterns—not the green undertones of Tenders but something else, perhaps adaptive camouflage, or perhaps ritual scarification, or perhaps a form of organic technology grown into the dermis.

They dress in layers of scavenged materials worn for function, not appearance: protective gear torn from machines, plant fibers woven into rough armor, masks made from filtered material that allow them to breathe in toxic zones. Everything is maintained with pragmatic efficiency, repaired repeatedly, stained with the colors of the Phytofacture.

Their eyes—when glimpsed—are often altered, possibly through self-modification or environmental adaptation. Enhanced night vision is common, giving them a reflective quality, a shine in low light. Some seem to have broader visual spectra than baseline, able to perceive the anomalies visually in ways that others can only feel.

### Their Territories

Ferals claim regions, defending them aggressively against intruders.

These territories are usually in transition zones—areas where the plant growth is less dense, where the machinery is more exposed and dangerous, where the anomalies are more pronounced. Places that Tenders avoid and Wanderers move through quickly. The Ferals thrive in these harsh sections, having adapted to conditions that would kill or drive out others.

They mark boundaries—crude signs painted on surfaces, deliberate arrangement of objects into warning patterns, sometimes more aggressive markers like the remains of fauna (or other things) displayed as territorial claims. Most Tenders and Wanderers learn to recognize these signs and respect them, choosing longer routes rather than transgressing.

Within their territories, Ferals maintain lairs—spaces modified for defense and survival. These are usually in areas with complex geometry, multiple entry and exit points, places where the wrongness can be used tactically. They fortify with scavenged materials, creating traps, establishing kill zones, arranging the environment to favor ambush.

### Their Behavior

Ferals are solitary or form small violent packs. They don't integrate into the larger human ecology of the Phytofacture.

They scavenge and steal, taking what they need from Tender cultivation areas or from Wanderers traveling through their territories. Sometimes they barter—leaving items they've found in exchange for food or tools placed at territory boundaries—but these exchanges are tense, conducted without direct contact.

They hunt the fauna extensively, having developed techniques for catching the elusive creatures that others rarely see. They also exploit the plant life, though in cruder ways than Tenders—harvesting aggressively, burning growth to drive out prey or clear sight lines, without concern for sustainability.

Violence, when it occurs, is brutally efficient. Ferals are skilled fighters, using weapons ranging from industrial tools to bespoke creations—spears with tips of sharpened ceramic, crossbows firing bolts of hardened plant-fiber, knives with blades of shaped metal scavenged from machinery. They understand ambush, leverage the environment's wrongness to disorient opponents, fight with the desperation of those who've chosen a marginal existence.

But they're not constantly aggressive. They attack when threatened or when they need resources, not recreationally. A Wanderer who moves through Feral territory quickly, without interfering with caches or lairs, might never be confronted. They're defensive predators, not sadistic.

### Their Origins

How do people become Ferals? Several pathways seem possible.

Some are psychological casualties—people who entered the Phytofacture or were born here and couldn't achieve the mental frameworks necessary for Tender or Wanderer life. The constant wrongness broke something in them, or revealed a brokenness already present. Unable to maintain stable social connections or cooperative behavior, they drifted into hostile solitude.

Others might be exiles taken to extreme—individuals who committed serious offenses, who were driven out of all communities, who eventually gave up on human society entirely and embraced a predatory lifestyle.

Some theorists suggest that Ferals represent an adaptive strategy, that in the harshest sections of the Phytofacture, where cooperation is impossible because resources are too scarce, the Feral approach is actually optimal. They're not broken but differently successful.

There are also disturbing suggestions that some Ferals have been changed by the deepest anomalies, that they've been exposed to reality breakdowns severe enough to alter their psychology or neurology fundamentally. They might have started as Tenders or Wanderers but strayed into zones that transformed them into something that can no longer function socially.

### What They Represent

To other inhabitants, Ferals serve as a dark mirror.

Tenders see them as proof of what happens without proper integration—the fate awaiting those who reject symbiosis with the Phytofacture but can't maintain purely human identity either. They're the negative example, the cautionary tale told to young Tenders about the importance of finding proper balance.

Wanderers view them with mixed fear and recognition. On some level, Wanderers understand that they're not far from Feral themselves—the extended solitude, the constant resource scarcity, the self-reliance. Some Wanderers probably become Ferals given enough time and hardship. This possibility haunts them.

The Merged barely acknowledge Feral existence. From their perspective, Ferals are stuck in an obsolete mode of being—clinging to competitive, hostile patterns when cooperation and integration are clearly superior strategies. Yet occasionally, a Merged entity in a harsh location will have originated as Feral, suggesting the two paths aren't as separate as they appear.

### Their Future

Ferals don't reproduce socially—they don't teach, don't form lineages the way Tenders do. Their persistence across generations (if indeed they persist) must depend on continuous recruitment from the baseline population, new people becoming Feral as existing ones die.

Or perhaps Ferals don't persist at all. Perhaps each one is unique, living their violent solitary lives until the Phytofacture kills them, leaving territories that other species quickly reclaim. The category of "Feral" would then be a recurring possibility rather than a stable population—individuals continually rediscovering the same adaptive strategy, playing it out until it fails.

Some whisper about Feral evolution—the idea that they're not regressing but advancing in a different direction, that after enough generations of extreme adaptation, they might become something new. Not human, not plant, not machine, but a fourth category, perfectly suited to the harshest conditions, able to thrive where all others fail.

But these are theories, stories. The truth about Ferals remains elusive because sustained study would require proximity they don't permit and communication they don't engage in.

## The Ghosts

Not inhabitants in a physical sense, but presences nonetheless, encountered rarely and always ambiguously.

### What They Might Be

The Ghosts are human-shaped phenomena observed in the Phytofacture. They appear as figures, sometimes translucent, sometimes solid, sometimes just shapes that suggest humanity without clearly being it.

One explanation is that they're actual ghosts—consciousness that persisted after death, retained somehow by the anomalies, existing in a state between life and whatever comes after. The Phytofacture's reality breakdowns might create conditions where death is incomplete, where minds linger in the distorted spaces.

Alternatively, they might be temporal echoes—images of people who existed in the past (or will exist in the future), their presence bleeding through from other times due to the wrongness of local causality. You're seeing someone who worked in the factories centuries ago, still walking their routes, or someone who'll tend these areas generations hence, their future selves visible briefly through temporal distortion.

They could be aspects of the Merged—consciousness that's distributed so thoroughly that it can manifest in multiple locations, projected presence rather than physical form. A Ghost might be the attention of a vast Merged entity focused temporarily in a particular spot, perceived as a human shape because that's the template observers use to interpret consciousness.

Or they might be something about the Phytofacture itself—the structure attempting to communicate, using human form because it's learned that humans respond to human shapes. They'd be the Phytofacture's way of interfacing, avatars of a distributed intelligence that wants to interact but has no body of its own.

### How They're Encountered

Ghost sightings follow patterns, though these patterns are inconsistent enough to frustrate attempts at systematic study.

They're most commonly seen in transitional spaces—doorways, corridor junctions, the boundaries between districts. Places where the Phytofacture's geometry is already ambiguous seem to permit Ghost manifestation more readily.

They appear briefly, usually. A Wanderer rounds a corner and sees a figure ahead, walking away. By the time the Wanderer catches up, there's nothing there, no sign of passage. A Tender working on a graft looks up and sees someone standing at the platform edge, watching. They blink, and the figure is gone, or was never there, or is now in a different position without having moved.

Duration varies. Some Ghosts are visible for only seconds. Others persist for minutes, even hours. There are accounts of Wanderers walking alongside a Ghost for an entire day, the figure maintaining consistent distance, never speaking, until it simply fails to appear after the Wanderer passes through a particularly dense plant corridor.

They sometimes interact with the environment, or seem to. A Ghost might appear to be operating machinery, though when you examine the equipment, nothing has changed. Another might seem to be tending plants, and later you find that those specific plants are healthier, growing more vigorously, as if the attention was somehow effective despite the Ghost's unclear ontological status.

### Communication

Attempts to communicate with Ghosts usually fail, but there are exceptions.

Most Ghosts don't respond to hailing. They continue their actions or movements as if unaware of observers, or aware but uninterested. They're manifestations without agency, images without the beings that cast them.

But some acknowledge presence. They turn toward speakers. They make gestures that might be responses. A few have apparently spoken, though accounts of what they say are contradictory and possibly unreliable—observers might be interpreting, projecting meaning onto ambiguous sounds.

The most detailed interaction recorded involved a Wanderer who encountered a Ghost that appeared nightly for weeks in the same location. The Wanderer began leaving written questions, and the Ghost—or something—responded, not in writing but through arrangements of objects, through patterns that the Wanderer gradually learned to interpret. The communication was slow and cryptic, but suggested the Ghost had knowledge of distant sections, could describe conditions the Wanderer later verified.

Whether this was genuine dialogue or the Wanderer unconsciously creating patterns they then discovered is debated. The Wanderer insists it was real, that the Ghost conveyed information they had no other way of knowing. Skeptics suggest the Wanderer was simply observing actual conditions through some subliminal awareness and attributing the knowledge to the Ghost.

### What They Know

If Ghosts have knowledge—and this is a significant if—it's not easily accessed.

Some Tenders believe Ghosts can perceive time non-linearly, that they see past and future simultaneously, and might warn about coming dangers or reveal forgotten knowledge from the Phytofacture's history. They conduct rituals attempting to summon or attract Ghosts, leaving offerings of cultivated flowers or carefully arranged seeds, hoping for visions.

Wanderers tend toward more pragmatic assessment. Ghosts, they note, often appear before major structural failures or in areas about to undergo significant change. Possibly they're precursors, reality hiccuping before it breaks more seriously. Observing Ghosts might be useful for predicting dangerous conditions.

The Merged rarely comment on Ghosts, which is itself noteworthy. Either Ghosts are beneath their concern, or they understand Ghosts so completely that explanation to baseline humans seems futile, or—possibly—the Merged and the Ghosts are related phenomena, and acknowledging this would reveal something about the Merged themselves.

### The Experience

Encountering a Ghost is unsettling even for long-term inhabitants.

There's a wrongness to their presence that goes beyond the general wrongness of the Phytofacture. They create dissonance, cognitive friction. Observers report feeling emotions that aren't their own when Ghosts are near—sudden grief, inexplicable nostalgia, fear with no clear object. These might be the Ghosts' emotions, bleeding through, or might be effects of whatever process manifests them.

Time feels strange during encounters. What seems like minutes might be much longer or shorter when checked against other references. Memory of the encounter often has a dreamlike quality, details fuzzy or contradictory when recalled.

Some people seek out Ghosts, hoping to understand them or communicate. Others avoid areas known for Ghost sightings, finding the experience too disturbing. The Ghosts represent something about the Phytofacture that resists explanation—proof that the wrongness isn't just environmental but might involve consciousness itself, might mean that minds can persist in forms that shouldn't be possible.

They're a mystery that lives in the space, one more category of presence in an environment already crowded with strangeness, another form of inhabitation in a structure that supports many forms of being.

## The Pattern

All these inhabitants—Tenders, Wanderers, Merged, Ferals, Ghosts—coexist in the vast space of the Phytofacture, rarely in conflict, more often simply occupying different niches, passing through each other's territories, occasionally interacting but mostly maintaining their separate modes of being.

Together, they form an ecology not just of organisms but of consciousness types, of different relationships to the structure and its strangeness. Each represents a possible adaptation to this impossible environment, a strategy for survival and meaning in a place where baseline human categories have broken down.

And beneath, around, through all of them, the Phytofacture itself continues: growing, manufacturing, breaking reality, sustaining and being sustained by those who live within it, vast and strange and ongoing.
