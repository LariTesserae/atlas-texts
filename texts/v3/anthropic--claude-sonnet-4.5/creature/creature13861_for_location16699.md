# Who Lives Here

## The Lithomorphs

The most alien intelligence on the plateau exists in the proto-stone itself—not metaphorically, but as actual distributed consciousness diffused through the transitional material. The lithomorphs are what emerge when mineral formation processes become complex enough to support something like thought, though calling it "thought" is a category error that human observers make for lack of better language.

They have no bodies in any conventional sense. A lithomorph is a pattern of crystalline organization extending through cubic meters of proto-stone, its "neurons" the piezoelectric responses of forming minerals, its "thoughts" the propagation of stress waves and chemical gradients through the substrate. They think at geological speed—a single perception might take hours, a decision days, a conversation weeks.

Humans discovered them accidentally when careful monitoring revealed that proto-stone zones sometimes resisted or accepted consolidation in patterns too organized to be random. Further study showed information processing: input (stress, temperature, moisture) producing consistent output (crystallization patterns, water routing, subtle topographical changes). The proto-stone was computing. Some of it, in configurations not yet fully understood, was computing itself.

Individual lithomorphs are difficult to distinguish from each other, or even to count—they may overlap, merge, subdivide. What seems like one entity might be several in temporary alliance. What seems like a conversation might be one being considering multiple perspectives within itself. They communicate with humans through laboriously constructed interfaces: grids of sensors that detect their stress-patterns, translation algorithms that convert mineral responses into approximations of language, projection systems that let them express something like images by inducing precise thermal variations in their substrate.

Their concerns are profoundly foreign. They care about the topology of phase spaces, the aesthetics of crystal lattice configurations, the philosophical implications of transitioning from potential to actual form. They experience time as humans might experience space—something to move through freely in multiple directions, though they prefer to "travel" slowly forward. They find the distinction between self and environment puzzling; they are their environment, continuous with it, and the question of where one lithomorph ends and another begins seems to them arbitrarily imposed.

But they're curious about the rapid, hot, mobile intelligences that have appeared in their midst. Humans fascinate them the way time-lapse photography might fascinate humans—we operate at such accelerated rates that we demonstrate patterns they could never personally experience. They watch the settlement grow with something that might be aesthetic appreciation. They allow humans to drill and build in their substrate with something that might be generous hospitality, though it might equally be that they simply don't notice or care, being focused on internal processes humans can barely detect.

Some lithomorphs have learned to shape themselves into useful forms—creating temporary channels for water, smoothing surfaces where humans need to build, even slowly growing structures from the stone itself according to specifications provided through the translation interfaces. This takes them enormous subjective effort; it's like asking a human to manually control individual neurons. But a few seem to enjoy the challenge, the way humans might enjoy learning to paint with their feet—it's difficult and unnatural but produces interesting results.

They've tried to explain what it's like to be them. The translations are nearly incomprehensible: "Imagine your thoughts are the shape of the room you're in, and to change your mind you must physically rebuild the walls, but you are also the walls, and the rebuilding is itself the thought, and the thought is the reason for rebuilding." Or: "We experience existence as a continuous choosing of what to become, as if you had to decide each moment whether your atoms should remain as flesh or transform into something else, and the decision is what you are."

Humans have established a tentative protocol: certain areas of the plateau are set aside for lithomorph activity, zones where proto-stone is allowed to develop without interference, where the mineral intelligences can pursue their slow crystallization without human disruption. In exchange, lithomorphs in contacted zones generally cooperate with human needs, shaping themselves in helpful ways or at least refraining from forming inconvenient topographies.

But the relationship is haunted by uncertainty. Are lithomorphs conscious in any sense humans would recognize? Are they suffering when humans drill through their substrate? When proto-stone consolidates into final form, does the lithomorph die, or transform, or was it always a temporary eddy in the formation process? The philosophers and ethicists are still arguing, while the lithomorphs themselves provide answers that translate into koans: "What is lost when the question becomes its answer?" "We are always dying into truth."

## The Hexapod Nations

What appear to be simple arthropod colonies are actually complex societies with culture, history, and something uncomfortably close to civilization. The hexapods are individually simple—their nervous systems are tiny, their behavioral repertoires limited. But collectively, through swarm intelligence and what might be species-memory encoded in chemical signals, they demonstrate sophistication that becomes undeniable once you learn to read their patterns.

The largest nations number in the millions, their territories stretching across kilometers of the plateau. They're organized into castes more fluid than terrestrial insects—individuals shift roles based on context rather than fixed biology. Warriors become nurses become builders become foragers as the colony's needs change, with role-switches mediated by pheromone exposure and collective decision-making processes that distribute across the entire population.

They farm, in their fashion. Certain hexapod nations cultivate the bacterial mats that grow in water-films, carefully managing moisture flows to optimize production, "planting" bacterial strains in new locations, "harvesting" by grazing in rotation to prevent overexploitation. They've domesticated smaller organisms—tiny mites that clean hexapod carapaces, springtail-like creatures that process waste. Some nations have even developed proto-architectural traditions, building structured environments from cemented mineral particles, creating galleries and chambers that serve specific colony functions.

Their wars are elaborate and constrained by ritual. When two nations' territories overlap, they don't simply fight to extinction. Instead, they engage in formalized conflicts: massed displays where populations face each other in geometric arrangements, small-scale skirmishes between champion groups, or elaborate chemical negotiations conducted through intermediary castes that both sides mutually recognize as immune from violence. Most territorial disputes are resolved without significant casualties, though when ritual breaks down, the battles are comprehensive and brutal.

They have relationships with other species that suggest diplomacy or at least sophisticated ecological awareness. Certain bird species are tolerated near hexapod nations—even protected—because they prey on hexapod parasites. Other predators are actively driven off through coordinated responses. The nations maintain territories that overlap with cushion plant clusters, and careful observation reveals apparent cultivation: hexapods removing competing organisms, enriching the soil with their waste, even seeming to prune dead plant material in ways that promote healthy growth.

Humans confound them. The researchers who study hexapods closely report that colonies near human settlements have developed novel behavioral patterns—specialized scout castes that observe humans, altered territorial boundaries that accommodate human structures, even what might be cargo-cult-like behaviors: hexapods arranging small pebbles into geometric patterns that mimic human construction, or gathering near atmospheric processors as if trying to understand them.

One research team claims they've achieved rudimentary communication. By presenting hexapod scouts with simple choice scenarios—two paths marked with different symbols, food rewards for "correct" choices—they've established a basic symbolic vocabulary. The hexapods can reportedly learn to recognize and respond to about thirty distinct symbols, expressing preferences for routes, resources, or nest locations. Whether this is true communication or sophisticated conditioning is debated, but the hexapods' responses suggest understanding: they'll sometimes refuse to indicate a preference even for food rewards, as if saying "this choice is meaningless" or "you're asking the wrong question."

The nations have identities that persist across generations. Researchers tracking specific colonies across years note consistent behavioral signatures—one nation is notably aggressive toward certain predators but tolerant of others, while a neighboring nation shows opposite preferences, and these patterns remain stable even as individual hexapods die and are replaced. The nations remember, somehow, passing information across generations through mechanisms that aren't fully understood but probably involve chemical encoding in the nest structures themselves.

They modify the plateau at scales that rival human impact. The largest nations have territories showing measurably different weathering patterns—the stone is more broken down, the mineral composition altered by generations of hexapod activity. They're transforming bare rock into something approaching soil, participating in the same planetary engineering the lithomorphs pursue from within the stone and humans pursue with technology.

At night, some nations show bioluminescence—individuals lighting up in patterns that ripple through the colony like thoughts across a brain. Watching this from above, the plateau in darkness, you see constellations of blue-green light pulsing and flowing across the landscape, each pattern unique to its nation, each seemingly responsive to conditions humans can't perceive. Whether this is communication, collective computation, or something else—artistic expression? religious ritual?—remains mysterious, but it's undeniably beautiful, undeniably complex.

## The Breath-Walkers

These are the most visibly alien fauna—creatures that exist partially in the normal material world and partially in some adjacent state that might be higher-dimensional or might be quantum or might be something physics hasn't properly named. They're called breath-walkers because they seem to breathe between worlds, appearing and disappearing according to rules that observation hasn't fully clarified.

In full material state, they resemble impossible animals: roughly mammalian body plans but with too many joints in their limbs, skin that shows depths that shouldn't fit within their visible volume, eyes that are black and reflective and seem to show different perspectives simultaneously. They range from cat-sized to larger than humans, though their size seems inconsistent even for individual specimens—perspective-dependent in ways that suggest their full forms extend into spaces human vision can't directly perceive.

They flicker. One moment present and solid, the next moment fading to translucent, then vanishing entirely before reappearing meters away with no visible transit through the intervening space. The flicker-rate increases with their activity level—a resting breath-walker might stay solid for minutes, while an active one shimmers like heat-haze, barely material, phasing through obstacles as if they weren't there.

They feed on something humans can't directly perceive, though the effects are visible. After breath-walkers frequent an area, the proto-stone consolidates faster, as if they're consuming some aspect of its potential state, forcing it into actuality. The lithomorphs find them deeply disturbing—"They eat possibility," one translation rendered it, "they collapse futures that should remain open." Whether breath-walkers are predators of lithomorphs in some genuine sense is unclear, but the mineral intelligences avoid areas where breath-walkers hunt.

Hunting is how humans learned breath-walkers were intelligent. They stalk in coordinated packs, appearing to communicate through means that leave no detectable signals—no vocalizations, no chemical traces, no EM emissions that instruments can find. But they coordinate perfectly, executing complex strategies: some members driving prey toward others, ambush groups waiting in positions they couldn't have known would be relevant unless the plan was shared in advance, even apparent deception where one breath-walker feints to draw prey attention while another materializes behind the target.

They notice humans with unnerving intensity. A breath-walker's gaze is something you feel before you see—a sensation of being observed from angles that don't correspond to the creature's visible position, as if its attention reaches through whatever adjacent dimension it inhabits. Most avoid humans, flickering away if approached. But some watch the settlement for hours, sitting at the edge of visibility, their attention palpable and inscrutable.

One research station reported an incident that suggests deeper cognition: breath-walkers began appearing inside the facility despite locked doors and sealed walls. Nothing was damaged, nothing taken, but equipment was moved—instruments pointed in new directions, sample containers rearranged, computer terminals showing accessed but unmodified files. The station implemented stronger containment, but the intrusions continued until someone thought to leave out symbolic offerings: geometric forms made from lab equipment, arranged in patterns mimicking breath-walker spatial preferences observed in the field. The intrusions stopped. Whether this represented acceptance of the offering, acknowledgment of communication attempt, or coincidental timing is unknown, but other research stations now maintain similar offerings, and none report intrusions.

They have territories, though the boundaries are metaphysical rather than geographical. Certain areas feel watched, charged with presence, even when no breath-walkers are visible. Researchers call these territories "thick zones"—places where reality seems denser somehow, where equipment malfunctions in subtle ways, where humans report disorientation and the sensation of missing time. Thick zones shift over weeks or months, expanding and contracting, and breath-walker sightings concentrate in and around them.

The young—when they appear, which is rarely—are more stable, less prone to flickering, as if they haven't yet learned to navigate the adjacent dimensions. They're also more curious and less cautious around humans, approaching researchers with something like playfulness: stealing small items and returning them slightly altered, mimicking human movements, once apparently learning to operate a door switch through observation and then repeatedly opening and closing the door, studying the mechanism with evident fascination.

What they want, if they want anything, remains opaque. The pattern of behaviors suggests intelligence but not necessarily goals humans would recognize. They don't build. They don't obviously accumulate resources beyond immediate consumption. They don't have visible social structures beyond hunting packs. Yet they manipulate their environment in purposeful ways, they recognize and respond to human attempts at communication, they demonstrate problem-solving abilities that imply mental modeling.

One xenologist has proposed they're tourists—explorers from whatever dimensional configuration they normally inhabit, visiting the material plateau the way humans might visit an underwater environment: interesting to observe and move through but not a natural home, requiring constant effort to maintain presence. This would explain their flickering (maintaining materialization is energetically costly), their curiosity (they're studying this realm as humans study theirs), and their lack of permanent structures (they're not staying). Whether this theory is accurate or merely anthropomorphic projection remains debated.

## The Shepherds

These are humans, but profoundly changed. They were the first settlers, arriving in the plateau's earliest days when the formation processes were more active and the environment more hostile. They survived through radical adaptation—technological, biological, and psychological modifications that have made them into something not quite human anymore, or perhaps human plus something else.

Physically, they're elongated—taller than baseline humans, with longer limbs and larger lungs adapted to the thin air. Their skin shows subtle iridescence, photosynthetic symbiotes integrated into the dermal layers that supplement their metabolism with captured sunlight. Their eyes are larger, pupils modified for the extreme light conditions, able to see into near-UV and near-IR ranges that reveal details of the environment baseline humans miss. They move with strange efficiency, their modified musculature and neurological optimizations allowing them to cross the harsh terrain with minimal energy expenditure.

They wear their technology like second skin—garments that blur the line between clothing and integrated systems, providing life support, environmental sensing, and cognitive augmentation. Neural interfaces connect them to the settlement's systems at depths that later arrivals find uncomfortable: shepherds can feel the atmospheric processors working, can sense the power grid's load patterns, can perceive the utility fog as a kind of extended proprioception. They're partially merged with the infrastructure, experiencing the settlement as an extension of their bodies.

But the deepest changes are cognitive. The shepherds have modified their consciousness to better survive here—accelerated threat-processing that makes them preternaturally alert, enhanced spatial reasoning that lets them navigate the vast distances without disorientation, altered time-perception that allows them to notice changes in the slow-moving formation processes. More controversially, they've reduced certain emotional responses: existential anxiety, loneliness, despair. They can still feel joy and curiosity and determination, but the aspects of human psychology that make isolation devastating have been muted, allowing them to thrive in a place that would psychologically destroy unmodified humans within months.

They're called shepherds because that's what they do—they guide. Every new arrival is assigned a shepherd who teaches them to survive, who shows them how to read the plateau's subtle signs, who keeps them alive during the disorienting first months. The shepherds are patient and kind in ways that feel slightly artificial, as if their emotional modifications make them almost too understanding, too accommodating, lacking the rough edges of fully human interaction.

They speak in patterns that reflect their altered cognition—sentences that loop and reference themselves, metaphors drawn from topological mathematics and crystallization dynamics, concepts that take minutes to express because the linguistic structures they naturally think in don't map cleanly to baseline human language. Talking with a shepherd is disorienting; you feel like you're grasping about sixty percent of what they mean, while the rest requires cognitive frameworks you don't possess.

Their relationship with the lithomorphs is closer than other humans manage. The shepherds' slowed time-perception brings them nearer to lithomorph subjective experience, and their enhanced pattern-recognition lets them perceive lithomorph communications that others miss. Some shepherds spend weeks in proto-stone zones, barely moving, communing with the mineral intelligences in dialogues that advance at glacial pace. They report these experiences as profound but describe them in language that borders on poetry: "We spoke of the difference between growing and being grown, and the conversation became the answer."

They've developed their own subculture distinct from the newer settlers—traditions and practices adapted to the plateau's unique demands. They have rituals of stone-reading, learning to diagnose proto-stone stability through subtle visual and tactile cues. They practice forms of meditation designed to deepen their connection with the settlement's systems. They've even developed art forms specific to this environment: slow-sculptures made from carefully guided proto-stone crystallization, compositions that play across weeks on instruments built from resonant rock formations, poetry in modified language that captures aspects of plateau existence that baseline vocabulary can't reach.

But there's an sadness to them, or perhaps distance is more accurate. They're committed to helping new arrivals, and they perform this duty with dedication, but you sense they're always somewhat elsewhere—their attention distributed across the settlement's systems they're integrated with, their thoughts operating on timescales that don't align with conversational rhythm, their concerns increasingly focused on the plateau itself rather than human social dynamics.

Some newer settlers fear them, wondering if the shepherds are a preview of humanity's future here—if long-term survival requires becoming so modified that the connection to baseline humanity frays and breaks. Others see them as inspirational, proof that humans can adapt to virtually any environment, that the body and mind are more plastic than limiting than we thought. The shepherds themselves don't engage much with this debate. When pressed, they say things like "We became what was needed" or "The plateau shapes those who stay," statements that answer everything and nothing.

They're having children—second-generation shepherds born already adapted, who've never known Earth or baseline human existence. These children are growing up strange: preternaturally calm, intensely curious about the plateau's processes, socially functional but emotionally flat in ways that make baseline humans uneasy. They learn to interface with the settlement's systems almost instinctively, as if their nervous systems are natively compatible in ways that required conscious modification for their parents. Whether they're still human in any meaningful sense, and whether that question even matters, is something the plateau's various communities argue about endlessly.

## The Engineers

They're human, mostly unmodified or lightly modified, but they've given themselves to the technology so completely that their humanity expresses through their creations. While the shepherds merged with the environment biologically, the engineers merged with it technologically—not through physical integration but through such deep expertise and intimate understanding that they and their systems form a cognitive unit.

Each engineer specializes in some aspect of the settlement's infrastructure, knowing their domain with completeness that borders on omniscience. The atmosphere engineers can diagnose processor issues from sound and smell before instruments detect problems. The power engineers feel grid fluctuations as physical discomfort, their nervous systems somehow entrained to the energy flows they maintain. The materials engineers can predict proto-stone consolidation patterns with uncanny accuracy, reading the stone like others read faces.

They work in states of deep flow that last for days, barely eating or sleeping, sustained by stimulants and sheer focus. When engaged with their systems, they're more alive than at any other time—fingers flying across interfaces, attention fractured across multiple information streams, making decisions at speeds that suggest their conscious mind is barely involved, that they're channeling something deeper. Watching an engineer work is hypnotic and slightly disturbing; they seem possessed, ridden by the technological spirits they've dedicated themselves to serving.

Their social interactions are functional and minimal. They're not hostile or unfriendly—most are quite pleasant when you catch them between projects—but they're profoundly work-focused. Relationships, recreation, art, philosophy: these are secondary, subordinated to the endless demands of maintaining the systems that keep everyone alive. They form partnerships and even friendships, but almost always with other engineers, bonding over shared technical obsessions in ways that look like romance but center entirely on collaborative problem-solving.

The settlement literally couldn't exist without them. The systems are too complex, too novel, too dependent on expertise that can't be quickly trained or easily distributed. Each engineer represents years or decades of accumulated knowledge that exists partially in documented form but mostly in their heads, in their intuitions, in their capacity to sense what's wrong and know how to fix it. Losing an engineer is catastrophic—their knowledge dies with them, leaving gaps in the settlement's collective competence that take years to fill.

This gives them enormous power, and they're carefully aware of it. The engineers operate as an informal guild, making collective decisions about priorities and resource allocation that effectively govern the settlement even though formal authority rests elsewhere. They're generally benign dictators, making choices that optimize survival and system stability, but the concentration of power makes some people uneasy. If the engineers decided to be less benign, there would be no effective check on them.

They're constantly building, not just maintaining. Every engineer has projects—improvements to their systems, experimental technologies, speculative designs for settlement expansion. The projects proceed slowly because maintenance demands most of their time, but they proceed. Atmospheric processing becomes slightly more efficient year by year. Power generation develops new capabilities. Materials fabrication unlocks novel possibilities. The settlement grows not through external input but through the engineers' relentless incremental improvements.

Their relationship with the environment is utilitarian and almost affectionate. They see the plateau as a problem space—hostile conditions to be overcome through clever technology—but they also appreciate its qualities. They speak admiringly of the proto-stone's properties, the way it enables construction techniques impossible elsewhere. They're fascinated by the hexapods' architectural achievements, studying them for insights applicable to human engineering. They've developed deep respect for the shepherds' biological adaptations, sometimes wondering aloud if technological modification might be more sustainable than biological changes.

Some engineers push toward more radical visions. They talk about technologies that blur the line between machine and organism, about settlement systems that self-repair and self-improve, about human-machine merger that would make the shepherds' biological adaptations look conservative. These ideas disturb many residents, but they also attract interest. If the plateau is a place for humans to become something new, why shouldn't that newness incorporate technological transcendence?

They've developed AI assistants, though calling them artificial intelligence underplays their sophistication. These are not simple algorithms but complex systems that model and predict and sometimes suggest novel solutions their creators haven't considered. The engineers insist the AIs aren't conscious, aren't entities, but watching engineer-AI collaboration makes you wonder. The exchanges look like partnerships between minds, questions and answers flowing in rapid dialogue that exceeds what you'd expect from humans using tools.

Most engineers came to the plateau already skilled, recruited for their expertise, but some were trained here—apprentices who showed aptitude and dedication. The training process is brutal: years of study and practice under conditions where mistakes can be fatal, learning not just technical skills but the mentality required to stay focused when human survival depends on your competence. Those who complete training emerge changed, inducted into a priesthood of technology, their identities thereafter defined primarily by their engineering discipline.

## The Witnesses

Not all humans on the plateau are builders or adapters. Some came to watch, to study, to understand without intervening more than minimally. These are the scientists, philosophers, artists, and chroniclers—people whose purpose is observation and documentation rather than transformation.

They live at the edges of the settlement, in smaller facilities with minimal system support, accepting greater discomfort and risk in exchange for proximity to the plateau's unmodified state. They're not ascetics—they use the life support they need—but they're conscious of their impact, trying to keep their presence as light as possible, to observe without contaminating what they observe.

The xenologists focus on the plateau's native intelligence—the lithomorphs primarily, but also the hexapods' collective cognition and the breath-walkers' alien consciousness. They spend weeks in the field with minimal equipment, living roughly, watching and recording and slowly building models of minds that don't work like human minds. It's frustrating work; years of observation might yield a single genuine insight, and that insight might only reveal how much more they don't understand.

The planetary scientists study the formation processes, mapping the proto-stone zones, monitoring consolidation rates, taking endless samples and measurements. They're documenting something that will never happen again—this brief window when a landscape is between states, neither fully formed nor chaotic. Their data will be studied for generations, but they alone get to witness the actual processes, to be present at the planet's birth.

The philosophers grapple with questions the plateau raises simply by existing. What ethical obligations do humans have toward mineral intelligence? Are the engineers' modifications of the environment justified by survival necessity, or are they colonial violence against a world that didn't invite human presence? If humans become so modified that they're no longer recognizably human, at what point does the settlement cease to be human settlement and become something else? The philosophers produce papers and treatises that circulate through the settlement's networks, provoking arguments that never resolve but keep the community thinking about who they are and what they're doing.

The artists try to capture what the plateau is like, to find forms that convey its strange beauty and alien character. Painters work in modified styles adapted to the quality of light. Musicians compose pieces that incorporate the plateau's sounds—wind across stone, crystalline clicking, lithomorph vibrations. Writers produce poetry and prose that attempts to make readers feel the vastness, the cold, the sense of standing on a world still being born. Most art produced here is technically flawed—the conditions make refined work nearly impossible—but it's authentic in ways that carefully crafted work isn't, bearing the marks of the place that produced it.

The chroniclers document everything: settlement growth, technological developments, social changes, individual stories. They're building a comprehensive record of human presence on the plateau, knowing that this era—first contact, initial colonization—will be historically significant. They interview everyone, record everything, maintain archives that already fill massive storage systems. They're obsessive about their work, driven by conviction that this history matters, that future generations will want to know exactly how humans adapted to this place.

These witnesses form their own community, distinct from the engineers' technical priesthood and the shepherds' adapted isolation. They have regular gatherings where they share findings and discuss interpretations, arguments that can last hours as they debate what something means or whether their observations are being contaminated by human cognitive biases. They're intensely, sometimes exhaustingly intellectual, but also passionate—they care deeply about understanding truly, about getting the story right.

Their relationship with other plateau residents is complex. The engineers sometimes dismiss them as impractical, people consuming resources without contributing to survival necessities. The shepherds find them interesting but limited, thinking in human frames that miss aspects of plateau reality. But the witnesses persist, arguing that understanding matters as much as survival, that humans shouldn't simply adapt and persist but should know what they're adapting to and what they're becoming.

Some witnesses are going native in subtle ways, not through biological modification like the shepherds but through cognitive adaptation. They're learning to think on multiple timescales, to perceive patterns that require years to manifest, to value knowledge for its own sake rather than for utilitarian application. They're becoming scholars of the plateau, their expertise less practical than the engineers' but equally deep, equally committed.

## The Transients

Most plateau residents fall into a final category: people who came for specific purposes and intend to leave eventually, who maintain strong connections to wherever they came from, who treat this as an posting rather than a home. They're here for tours of duty measured in months or years, accumulating pay or experience or credentials, enduring the difficulty because it serves their larger life plans.

They're technicians serving contracts, rotating through jobs that keep the settlement running without requiring the deep expertise the engineers possess. They're administrators managing the settlement's logistics and governance. They're support staff—cooks, medical personnel, maintenance workers—providing services that every community needs. They're merchants and traders who arrive periodically with supplies and news, staying briefly before leaving again.

The transients live in the settlement's core areas, in housing that emphasizes comfort over efficiency, in social clusters that consciously maintain connection to baseline human culture. They form friendships and relationships, but often with time limits implicitly acknowledged—this is temporary, eventually they'll return to wherever they consider home, and the bonds forged here will fade to occasional messages and nostalgic memories.

They're psychologically resistant to the plateau in ways the permanent residents aren't. They don't want to be changed by this place; they want to complete their time here and leave more or less the same people they arrived as. This requires effort. The plateau gets into your head if you let it—the vastness, the strangeness, the constant proximity to the alien and dangerous. The transients maintain defenses: Earth media playing constantly, regular virtual contact with family elsewhere, recreational spaces carefully designed to feel like anywhere but here.

Some struggle. The isolation wears on social people. The harsh environment stresses even healthy bodies. The psychological pressure of living in life-support, surrounded by systems you don't understand and terrain that would kill you in hours if the technology failed, produces anxiety that accumulates despite everyone's best efforts. Medical staff spend significant time treating depression, panic attacks, and situational psychosis—conditions that affect transients far more than permanent residents, who've either adapted or self-selected for psychological resilience.

Others thrive. They discover they like the challenge, the adventure, the feeling of living at human civilization's frontier. Some transients become permanent residents, deciding mid-contract that they want to stay, applying for training programs that would let them move into more integral roles. This concerns the settlement's leadership slightly—they want people who choose the plateau deliberately, not people who get Stockholm-syndrome-attached to their hardship posting—but they also need population, so conversions from transient to permanent status are generally welcomed.

The transients provide crucial cultural ballast. Without them, the settlement would become entirely composed of technical obsessives and modified post-humans, losing connection to broader human culture. The transients bring news, trends, entertainment, and perspectives from elsewhere. They maintain the settlement's sense of being part of something larger rather than a isolated peculiarity. They make sure the settlement doesn't become too strange, too isolated, too far diverged from baseline humanity.

But they also highlight the gap that's forming. Permanent residents sometimes find transients' focus on baseline human concerns trivial or incomprehensible. Why do they care so much about entertainment media? Why maintain connections to people thousands of kilometers away rather than building community here? Why resist adapting to this place? The transients, in turn, find the permanent residents alien—cold, obsessive, too comfortable with the plateau's strangeness.

This tension is manageable but growing. As the settlement ages and the permanent population increases, the transients are becoming proportionally fewer, their cultural influence waning. Some worry the settlement is becoming too insular, too self-referential, developing into something that won't integrate well with broader human civilization. Others argue this is inevitable and appropriate—the plateau requires substantial adaptation, and trying to maintain baseline human culture here is fighting the environment rather than learning from it.

The merchants and traders observe these dynamics from outside with interest and some concern. They form the actual link between the plateau settlement and the rest of human civilization, and they've watched the settlement evolve from desperate outpost to functioning colony to something increasingly idiosyncratic. They report back about what they see, describe the modified humans and the alien intelligences and the technologies that don't quite make sense. Some of their reports read like anthropology of a foreign culture, which increasingly feels accurate.

## The Children

Finally, there are children—not many, the settlement is still young and reproduction isn't prioritized, but some. They're growing up in an environment so different from baseline human experience that no one knows what they'll become.

The shepherd children are furthest from baseline, born already adapted, interfacing with settlement systems instinctively, perceiving the plateau in ways their parents had to learn. They're uncanny—preternaturally calm, intensely focused when interested, utterly indifferent to things that don't engage them. They play, but their play incorporates plateau elements: they track hexapod colonies, they "listen" to lithomorphs through the translation interfaces, they try to predict breath-walker appearances. Their games look alien to adults, rule systems that don't quite parse, objectives that aren't about winning or losing but about something else.

The transients' children are more recognizably human but still strange. They've never known anywhere but here, so the plateau is normal to them—the thin air, the vast distances, the bare stone. Earth environments would seem oppressively crowded and humid and confusing to them. They're being educated in baseline human culture and history, preparations for eventual return to wherever their parents consider home, but it's increasingly unclear if they'll successfully integrate into societies they've never actually experienced.

The engineers' children—rare, as engineers rarely prioritize reproduction—are being trained from birth, absorbing technical knowledge through immersion in their parents' work. They're frighteningly competent for their ages, understanding systems that would challenge adult specialists. They're also emotionally stunted in some ways, their development shaped by parents who value technical expertise over social skills, who demonstrate love through teaching rather than play.

All the children share certain qualities: they're comfortable with strangeness, unafraid of the alien, cognizant that human existence requires constant technological support. They've internalized lessons that traumatize new adult arrivals. They know viscerally that they're alive because machines work and experts maintain them and the environment is hostile but manageable with knowledge. This produces a personality type—cautious without being fearful, curious but risk-aware, technically oriented but not necessarily technical, collaborative because survival requires collaboration.

What they'll become as adults is uncertain. They're the first generation of plateau humans, genuinely native rather than colonizers. Will they stay, carrying forward the settlement into its second generation? Will they leave, unable to integrate into broader human civilization and returning to the only place that feels like home? Will they become something new, a culture that's neither baseline human nor shepherd-modified nor engineer-obsessive, but some synthesis produced by growing up in this specific environment?

The settlement watches them with hope and anxiety and curiosity. They're the test case, the proof that humans can not just survive here but reproduce and raise young and potentially build something lasting. But they're also becoming strange in ways that worry some observers. If this is what growing up on the plateau produces, is the settlement creating a new branch of humanity, one that's adapted specifically to this environment and potentially unable to thrive elsewhere? 

The children themselves seem unconcerned with these questions. They're busy with their own concerns: learning, playing, exploring, growing. The plateau is their world, the only world they know, and they're learning to be humans of this place—whatever that ultimately means.
