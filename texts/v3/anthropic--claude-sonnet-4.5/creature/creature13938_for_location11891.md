# What Lives There

The people who dwell in the Hanging Garden call themselves the **Hollowborn**, though they use this name only when speaking to the rare outsider who finds their way down the ancient paths. Among themselves, they have no collective name—they are simply *the people*, *we who live here*, distinguished from one another by personal names that often incorporate sounds from the sinkhole itself: Kessa (like water dripping), Tomul (like stone settling), Shen (like wind through leaves).

They are physically distinctive in ways that speak of isolation and adaptation. Generations in the dim, green-filtered light have given them large eyes—not grotesquely so, but noticeably, with irises that tend toward amber and gray-green, pupils that dilate wide in the perpetual twilight of the lower levels. Their skin ranges from olive to a deep golden-brown, and it has a quality—not quite a sheen, but a slight luminosity in lamplight, as if it reflects and holds illumination more efficiently than it should. Some of the elders claim this is from a lifetime of eating the sinkhole's food, of drinking its mist, of breathing its air. The youngest children sometimes seem to glow faintly in complete darkness, though whether this is true bioluminescence or trick of the eye is a question the Hollowborn don't ask.

They are small-statured on average, compact, with strong legs and fingers, bodies built for climbing. The children move through the vertical city like it has no verticality at all, fearless, their sense of balance developed from infancy. An adult can stand on a ledge barely wider than their foot, lean out over the void to prune a vine, and feel no vertigo—the drop is simply space, simply air, as natural as the ground. Their hands are notable: broad palms, long fingers, the kind of hands that can grip stone or rope for hours, that can feel the difference between safe rock and crumbling, that can weave and carve and climb without thinking about it.

There is a gentleness to them that might read as timidity to an outsider but is actually profound confidence. They move quietly because loud movement is inefficient in a space where sound multiplies. They speak in modulated tones because shouting echoes chaotically. They touch each other often—a hand on an arm, fingers brushing a shoulder—because in the dim light and the dense vegetation, touch confirms presence more reliably than sight. They smell faintly of the green, of plant sap and stone dust and smoke, of the particular mustiness of places where wet and dry cycle endlessly.

**The Eldest** is what they call her, though she has a name: Miroan. She is perhaps seventy, perhaps ninety—even she isn't certain, as they don't count years the way outsiders do. She lives in one of the lowest inhabited chambers, a room that was already ancient when she was born, its walls softened to the texture of soap, its window opening onto layers of green so dense they form a living wall. Her eyes are milky at the edges but still keen at the center. Her hands are maps of her life—scarred from tools, stained from dyes, strong still despite the prominent veins and enlarged knuckles.

Miroan is the keeper of stories, but not in any formal sense. She simply remembers. She can tell you about the great vine-fall of her grandmother's time, when a plant that had bridged the sinkhole for generations finally died and crashed down, taking three dwellings with it. She can tell you which families have intermarried and which bloodlines are getting thin, who should partner with whom to keep the people healthy. She knows which plants are which, not just by their current form but by their entire life cycle, can identify a seedling and tell you what it will become, what it will need, whether to encourage or remove it.

She rarely climbs anymore, but people come to her, bringing questions, bringing problems, bringing objects they've found or made. She examines these with fingers and sometimes with tongue—she'll taste a plant, a piece of pottery, even stone, reading information through senses the younger people have forgotten to develop. Her chamber is thick with objects: baskets of seeds, dried plants hung from the ceiling, stones with interesting markings, pieces of carved wood that are either very old or very new—the surface quality is too similar to tell.

When Miroan speaks, people listen not just out of respect but because the acoustics of her chamber are unusual—her voice carries with a clarity that other locations don't provide, as if the room itself wants her words to reach. She sometimes sings in a voice that has gone reedy with age but still finds the notes, songs in a language that might be older than the current tongue, or might be something she's invented herself from the sounds of water and birds and stone. No one asks. The songs are part of the place's music, like everything else.

**Kael** is the primary climber, the one who goes highest, who maintains the uppermost dwellings and harvests from the plants that grow closest to full sunlight. He is perhaps thirty, tall for the Hollowborn at five and a half feet, lean in the way of people who move constantly. His hands and feet are calloused to the point of being leathery, capable of gripping holds that look non-existent. He carries coils of rope woven from plant fiber, hooks carved from bone, small bags of the sticky sap they use as adhesive.

Kael knows the upper reaches more intimately than anyone else alive. He can read the stone for stability, knows which ancient carved handholds are solid and which have developed internal cracks that will crumble under weight. He understands the plants that grow up there—aggressive climbers that reach for the full sun, their stems woody and thick, capable of bearing weight or pulling down structures depending on how they're managed. He prunes them, trains them, sometimes harvests them for material, always talking to them softly as he works. Whether the plants hear or not is irrelevant; Kael hears himself, and the talking helps him think.

He has a relationship with the birds that nest in the high reaches—not communication exactly, but understanding. He knows their patterns, times his climbs for when they're out hunting, leaves certain areas alone during nesting season. The swifts and swallows tolerate him, barely, because he's been a constant presence their entire lives, because his movements are predictable, because he never takes eggs from the same nest twice in a season.

Kael discovered something last year that he hasn't told anyone except Miroan: in one of the highest chambers, one that hasn't been regularly inhabited in living memory, there's a carving that doesn't match the others. The style is different—more fluid, more deliberately artistic. It shows figures that might be human or might be something else, arranged in a pattern that might be decorative or might be writing. The chamber itself has strange acoustics; sounds made there don't echo but seem to be absorbed, deadened. Kael goes there sometimes when he needs to think without the sinkhole's constant commentary, sits in the sound-dead space and listens to his own breathing, and wonders who carved those figures and why they chose that particular room and what they were trying to say or do or be.

**Shen** is seven years old and has never been to the surface. She was born in a chamber on the eastern wall, has spent her entire life in the sinkhole's vertical embrace, and cannot quite conceive of a world that extends horizontally farther than two hundred feet. When adults talk about the "outside," she pictures another sinkhole, maybe one that's wider or shallower, but fundamentally similar—a contained world with walls, with up and down.

She is small even for her age, quick as a lizard, fearless in a way that occasionally worries the adults but more often fills them with pride. She can climb routes that grown adults struggle with, her small fingers finding holds in the weathered stone, her light weight an advantage. She has appointed herself the keeper of a family of swallows that nest above her family's dwelling, can identify each bird individually by the pattern of their wing feathers, brings them offerings of insects she catches by hand—a practice that's useless but harmless, and the birds do seem to recognize her, altering their flight patterns when she's on the ledge, coming closer than they do for others.

Shen has a collection: stones with fossils, interesting seed pods, the shed skin of a snake, a beetle carapace that shimmers green and purple, a piece of rope so old it's halfway to becoming stone. She keeps these in a basket woven by her grandmother, arranges and rearranges them, creates categories that make sense only to her. She's teaching herself to weave by taking apart old baskets and studying how they're made, then trying to replicate the patterns. Her early attempts are lumpy, uneven, but improving. Her mother saves them all, knowing that someday the good ones will be indistinguishable from masterworks, and the clumsy early ones will be precious for their evidence of learning.

She has an imaginary friend or possibly a real presence that she calls "the Green Voice." It speaks to her, she says, in the rustling of leaves, tells her where to find good climbing routes, warns her when a stone is loose. The adults half-believe her. The sinkhole does seem to speak, after all, in its way. Why shouldn't it speak more clearly to a child who listens? They don't discourage her, don't tell her it's imagination. They just make sure she's not taking dangerous risks based on advice from rustling leaves. Mostly, she's fine. Her instincts are good. The Green Voice, whether internal or external, seems to give sound guidance.

**Polla and Tamir** are partners, have been for fifteen years, live in a chamber they carved out and expanded themselves—one of the few new dwellings made in the last generation. They chose a location mid-level on the southern wall where a natural cave had begun forming from water erosion. They worked for two years with stone tools and patience, removing rock, smoothing surfaces, creating a space that's larger than most—large enough for the workshop they wanted.

They are the makers. Polla works primarily with fiber—weaving cloth, making rope, creating baskets of intricate design. Her fingers are never still; even in conversation, they're plaiting small cords, knotting, testing strength. She can identify plant fibers by touch alone, knows which are best for strength, for flexibility, for water resistance. She processes them through a series of steps that look like magic: soaking, pounding, combing, spinning, dyeing, weaving. The cloth she makes is tightly woven, nearly waterproof, dyed in patterns that use knots to resist the dye, creating designs of geometric complexity.

Tamir works with wood and stone. He carves the tools they use—handles for knives, hafts for axes, the shuttles and needles and hooks that Polla needs for weaving. He makes furniture: stools that are ergonomic despite their simplicity, shelves that fit into irregular stone niches as if grown there, a cradle for their nephew that rocks in a perfect arc. His tools are stone and bronze, the bronze carefully hoarded, used only for finish work. The stone tools he makes himself, can take a nodule of flint and reduce it to a blade in a process that looks violent but is actually precise, each strike calculated, the flakes falling away in a pattern he holds in his mind.

Together, they're experimenting with something new: laminating thin wood with plant-based glue to create curved forms that are stronger than solid wood of the same thickness. It's not working yet—the glue isn't strong enough, or the wood isn't thin enough, or the curve is too aggressive. But they keep trying, making small adjustments, testing. This is unusual behavior for the Hollowborn, this deliberate innovation, but Polla and Tamir are tolerated, even quietly encouraged. The community is stable, but stability shouldn't mean stagnation. New techniques, carefully tested, might be valuable. Or might be dangerous. Time will tell.

They want children but haven't had them. This is treated as unfortunate but not tragic—there are enough children in the community, and Polla and Tamir contribute in other ways. They take apprentices, teaching their skills to youngsters who show interest. They share their dwelling space with Tamir's younger brother and his family, helping raise those children. The community is small enough that everyone is uncle or aunt to everyone else's children anyway. Blood relationships matter, but not more than functional ones.

**Orim** is the outlier, the one who doesn't quite fit. He is perhaps forty, lives alone in a chamber on the western wall, and speaks rarely. When he does speak, his voice is hoarse, as if unpracticed, though this isn't quite true—he talks to himself constantly, muttering observations, questions, fragments of thought that aren't meant for others' ears.

Orim was injured as a young man—a fall that should have killed him but didn't, that left him with a limp and chronic pain in his left leg and head. Since then, he's been different. Not diminished exactly, but changed. He sees patterns others don't see, makes connections that seem nonsensical until they prove useful. He predicted the great vine-fall three days before it happened, based on subtle changes in how the plant was shedding leaves, how insects were avoiding it. No one believed him, but they remember now.

He has developed a practice of mapping the sinkhole—not with drawings (he can't draw in any representational way) but with knotted cords, different knots and colors representing different features. He has strings for the dwellings, for the major plants, for water flow during mist, for sound patterns, for where different birds nest. The cords fill his chamber, strung from wall to wall, creating a three-dimensional representation that makes sense to him and possibly to no one else. He modifies them constantly, tying new knots, removing old ones, muttering about patterns and cycles and changes.

People consult him cautiously, aware that his advice is sometimes brilliant and sometimes bizarre, not always able to tell which is which until after the fact. He told Kael where to find the strange chamber with the carvings—pointed to a specific location on his cord-map and said "there's a quiet place there, a place that eats sound." Kael investigated out of curiosity more than belief, and found he was right. 

Orim eats little, seems to subsist on tea made from plants he collects and combines in ratios he won't share. He sleeps irregularly, is sometimes seen climbing at night with no lamp, navigating by touch and memory and possibly something else. He has conversations with the sinkhole itself, or says he does, listening to the stone and the green and reporting what they tell him. Most of it is poetic nonsense. Some of it is eerily accurate prediction. The Hollowborn have learned to listen without quite believing, to file away his pronouncements and see what comes true.

He is cared for, in the way the community cares for all its members. People bring him food he doesn't ask for. They check on him during storms. When his dwelling needed repairs, a group simply showed up and did the work while he muttered at them about disturbing the pattern. He is frustrating and possibly mad and also precious—a different kind of sensing organ for the community, reporting data from sources the others can't access.

**The children** are not just small adults but genuinely different in their relationship to the place. There are about fifteen of them ranging from infants to adolescents. They form a loose collective, supervised casually by whatever adults are nearby but largely self-organizing. The older ones watch the younger ones, teaching climbing routes, showing where the best insects can be found, passing down the endless accumulated knowledge of safe versus dangerous: which spiders bite, which snakes to avoid, which plants cause rashes.

They play games that look reckless to outsiders—leaping between ledges, racing up and down the carved stairs, hanging by their hands over drops that would mean death. But they're not being reckless; they're being precise. They know their capabilities exactly, push right to the edge but not over. A child who misjudges doesn't die because the other children are always watching, always ready to grab an arm or shout a warning. The community has lost children—it happens, gravity is unforgiving—but rarely, and the children themselves are the primary safety system, more reliable than adult supervision because they're always there, always present in the vertical world.

They have a secret language of gestures and bird-calls, can communicate across the sinkhole without words, coordinating their games and explorations. They know the sinkhole's creatures individually: have names for specific lizards, can identify which bat colony is which by their flight patterns, know the large spider that lives above the granary and consider her a sort of mascot. They leave offerings sometimes—a dead moth placed in a web, a stick arranged just so for a bird to use in nesting—small rituals of relationship with the non-human inhabitants.

The children are also the primary finders of things. They explore obsessively, squeezing into spaces adults can't reach, climbing to ledges that haven't been visited in generations. They find: old tools, pieces of pottery, stones with strange marks, bones of animals long dead, seeds that have somehow remained viable, pockets of crystal formation in the limestone, fossils that make them ask questions about ancient seas and time. They bring these to Miroan usually, or to their parents, adding to the community's collection of objects and knowledge.

**Dren** is the midwife and healer, keeper of medical knowledge accumulated over generations. She is perhaps fifty, calm in a way that suggests deep wells of experience with crisis. Her chamber is organized with the precision of a laboratory: baskets of dried plants labeled with knotted strings, ceramic vessels of salves and tinctures, tools for her trade—blades for cutting, needles of bone for stitching, strings for binding.

She knows which plants reduce fever, which stop bleeding, which ease pain, which can be used to prevent or end pregnancy. This knowledge is survival for a community this small; without it, they would have died out generations ago. She also knows the limits of her knowledge—knows when an injury or illness is beyond her capability, when the kindest thing is to ease passing rather than prolong suffering. The Hollowborn bury their dead in chambers that are then sealed, returning the body to the stone. Dren oversees this, making it gentle, making it a return rather than an ending.

She has delivered almost every child currently alive, knows them literally from first breath. She carries the grief of the ones who didn't survive birth, the mothers lost to bleeding or fever, the children taken by accidents or illness. These losses mark her; she wears a necklace of small stones, one for each death she's witnessed. It's long now, heavy. She doesn't hide it or speak of it, just wears the weight as a kind of responsibility, a reminder that her knowledge is precious and insufficient.

Dren is teaching two apprentices: a young woman named Kessa who has the temperament for healing—calm, observant, comfortable with bodies and fluids and the messy reality of human physiology—and a boy named Mol who is clever with his hands, good at the precision work of stitching and splinting. One or both will take over when Dren can no longer do the work. The knowledge will continue.

She also tends the medicine garden, a series of terraces on the eastern wall where she cultivates the plants she needs, experimenting with growing conditions, selecting seeds from the strongest plants, slowly domesticating wild species into reliable medicines. Some of her plants exist nowhere else in the sinkhole or possibly nowhere else in the world—varieties that have been selected for generations for potency, for ease of growing, for reduced side effects. This garden is one of the community's most valuable resources, and its care is taken seriously. Children are taught early not to disturb it; even the goats that browse on other vegetation avoid it, either trained or instinctively wary of plants that taste of medicine.

**The dead** are present too, in their way. The sealed chambers hold generations—hundreds of bodies returned to stone, slowly becoming stone themselves through mineral deposition and time. The Hollowborn don't fear these chambers but don't disturb them either. They're simply part of the architecture, rooms that have changed function, storage spaces for memory and bones.

Sometimes a child will ask what it's like in the sealed chambers, whether the dead are aware, whether they dream. The adults give various answers depending on their own beliefs: that the dead sleep and dream the slow dreams of stone; that they become part of the sinkhole's green mind, dispersed into the network of roots and fungus; that they simply stop, consciousness ending like a lamp going out; that they travel upward with the rising mist, eventually escaping into the sky. All of these might be true. None might be. The Hollowborn don't insist on a single answer.

What's certain is that the dead contributed to the place's ecology. Their bodies, their nutrients, feed the plants that grow near the sealed chambers. Certain mosses grow especially thick on those walls, certain mushrooms fruit there seasonally, both harvested carefully, with thanks. The dead provide for the living, even in ending. This is considered right, circular, the way things should be.

Names of the recently dead are spoken often for the first year, then less frequently, then rarely, then only in the context of lineage ("my mother's father's mother was...") or accomplishment ("the one who wove the large basket that..."). Eventually most names are forgotten entirely, subsumed into the generic "ancestors," the collective dead who made the current life possible. Miroan remembers more names than most, keeps them alive in her stories, but even she admits that many are lost, gone back into silence like mist evaporating.

**The animals**, though not people, are participants in the community in ways that blur the boundary between human and non-human society.

The **swifts** return every spring in numbers that seem impossible—thousands of them, filling the air, their calls overwhelming every other sound. They nest in the carved chambers, especially the higher ones, packing in so densely that some rooms are more bird than space. The Hollowborn tolerate and even welcome this invasion. The birds eat insects in massive quantities, keeping the mosquito and fly populations manageable. Their droppings, collected and composted, provide crucial nutrients for the gardens. And they're beautiful—their aerial displays at dawn and dusk are a kind of living art, murmurations that fill the sinkhole's cylinder with motion and sound.

Individual birds return to the same nesting sites year after year. Some of the Hollowborn recognize them, claim relationships—"that one with the bent wing, she's nested above my door for five years now." Whether the birds recognize individual humans is unclear, but they definitely distinguish between the Hollowborn as a group and outsiders, becoming agitated at unfamiliar people but calm around those they know.

The **bats** are less visible but equally important. They roost in the deepest chambers, the ones too damp and dark for human habitation, emerging at dusk in streams that flow upward toward the sky. They eat the night-flying insects, the moths and beetles that would otherwise overwhelm the gardens. Their guano accumulates in the chambers they roost in, becoming a nitrogen-rich fertilizer that the Hollowborn harvest carefully, disturbing the bats as little as possible, taking only the oldest deposits.

There's a truce between bats and people, carefully maintained. The Hollowborn don't enter the bat chambers during the day when the bats are sleeping. They don't harvest guano during breeding season. In return, the bats tolerate human presence nearby, sometimes roosting in the rafters of inhabited chambers if the people stay quiet and dim their lamps. A few Hollowborn claim to have relationships with individual bats, recognizing them by size or flight pattern, though this is harder to verify than with the larger, more visible swifts.

The **spiders** are everywhere, an omnipresent network of predators that keep other insect populations in check. The large orb-weavers—some with leg spans wider than a human hand—build webs that can catch birds, though they usually don't; they're after the large moths, the dragonflies, the beetles. The Hollowborn navigate around these webs with practice, duck under them, sometimes use them as markers: "meet me at the big web on the third level."

Some spiders live in what amounts to commensalism with humans, building their webs near food storage areas, catching the moths and beetles that would otherwise infest the grain and dried food. These spiders are protected, even named sometimes. Children learn early which spiders bite and which don't, develop an instinct for safe versus dangerous that's remarkably accurate.

The communal spiders are particularly interesting—several species build collective webs that cover areas the size of rooms, housing hundreds of individuals in a cooperative network. These mega-webs catch enormous quantities of insects, essentially straining them from the air. The Hollowborn work around them, sometimes diverting them slightly by providing anchor points, essentially collaborating with the spiders to position the webs in mutually beneficial locations.

The **snakes** are treated with respectful caution. Most are non-venomous constrictors that eat the rats and mice and birds. They're left alone, even welcomed—a snake living in your wall means fewer rodents. But there are a couple of species with medically significant venom, and these are killed when found near dwellings, though not elsewhere. Kael has a particular role here; he's the one most likely to encounter snakes in the upper reaches, has learned to recognize them by sight and behavior, relocates the dangerous ones when possible.

There's a particularly large python—or something like a python—that lives in the mid-levels and has been known for at least two generations. The Hollowborn call it Old Rope and consider it almost a pet, though it's entirely wild and would happily eat a small child if given the opportunity. It's large enough to be obvious, patterned in a way that's distinctive, and people report sightings: "saw Old Rope on the western wall, digesting something large." The snake has become a sort of mascot, a personality, evidence that the sinkhole contains lives that parallel human ones—born before we were, will die after we do, pursuing their own purposes adjacent to ours.

**The birds of prey**—hawks and small eagles—nest in the highest reaches and are treated with particular reverence. They eat snakes and large rodents but will take the Hollowborn's chickens if given a chance. This creates a tension, a negotiation. The people protect their birds with netting, but not too aggressively, accepting some losses as the cost of sharing space. The raptors in turn provide warnings of danger—their alarm calls alert to approaching storms, to large snakes, to anything unusual.

There's a relationship of mutual observation. The raptors watch the humans and learn to predict their behavior, hunting the edges of gardens where rodents are disturbed by digging. The humans watch the raptors and learn about weather, about the presence of prey animals, about the health of the ecosystem. When a raptor takes up residence in a new nesting site, people pay attention, consider what that location offers, what's changing in the sinkhole's ecology to make that spot attractive now.

**The insects**, though individually insignificant, collectively shape daily life. The Hollowborn's relationship with them is complex—they're food source, pollinator, decomposer, pest, indicator species, all at once. Certain large beetles are harvested and roasted, tasting of nuts. Certain caterpillars are considered delicacies, eaten raw or dried. The Hollowborn have an encyclopedic knowledge of insect life cycles, know which ones to encourage, which to control, which to watch as signals of larger changes.

The **ants** in particular have almost negotiated status. Several species have formed symbiotic relationships with specific plants, defending them from herbivores in exchange for nectar or housing. The Hollowborn have learned to work with these relationships, planting ant-defended species in locations where they want aggressive protection, avoiding disturbing ant colonies when possible, using ant behavior as a prediction tool—when the ants start moving their eggs to higher ground, rain is coming.

There are **fireflies** in the summer months, emerging at dusk to float through the sinkhole's cylinder in clouds of soft green light, blinking in patterns that shift and flow. The Hollowborn stop work to watch them, consider the display a kind of blessing, a reminder that beauty is not separate from function—the fireflies are looking for mates, but their search creates wonder. Children chase them, try to catch them, release them again. The light they produce inspired the Hollowborn to search for bioluminescent fungi, to cultivate certain species for gentle illumination that doesn't require fire.

**The fungi** deserve special mention—they're neither plant nor animal but their own category, and the Hollowborn relate to them differently. The mushrooms that fruit from the forest floor and from rotting wood are food, medicine, sometimes poison. The Hollowborn know which is which with a precision born of necessity; mistakes are fatal. But beyond the fruiting bodies, they're aware of the mycelial network in the soil, the invisible web of fungal threads that connects plants, that breaks down the dead, that moves nutrients through the ecosystem.

Some of the Hollowborn—particularly Orim—believe the mycelial network is intelligent in some way, that the sinkhole's ecology has a kind of distributed consciousness coordinated through fungal connections. Others think this is poetic interpretation of chemical signals. Either way, they treat the fungal network with respect, avoid disturbing the soil more than necessary, make offerings sometimes—pouring water on barren spots, leaving pieces of wood arranged to encourage colonization.

There are **fish** in the small pools that form temporarily after rains, tiny transparent things that live in the water films on rock faces, species so specialized they exist nowhere else. The Hollowborn notice them, wonder about them—how did they get here? How do they survive when their pools dry up? Do they have drought-resistant eggs, or do they somehow aestivate in the moss? No one knows. The fish are too small to eat, too fragile to study. They're just there, one more mystery among many, evidence that life will find a way into every available niche.

**The community's structure** is informal but deeply understood. There are no chiefs or councils, no formal authority, but there are people whose voices carry more weight: Miroan for her knowledge, Dren for her skill and her burden of life-and-death decisions, Kael for his mastery of the heights, a few others respected for their judgment. Decisions are made by discussion, often long discussions, with consensus sought but not always required. If someone wants to do something that doesn't harm others, they generally do it, and the community watches to see how it turns out.

Disagreements happen but rarely escalate. The space is too confined, the population too small, interdependence too obvious for serious rifts to be sustainable. People who can't get along find ways to minimize contact—living on opposite walls, working different tasks. The children, who have no such discretion, are sometimes deployed as mediators, carrying messages, their innocence providing safe channels for communication.

**Marriage** is informal—people partner when they choose, unpartner if they must, with the community's approval mattering less than personal compatibility. Children are valued regardless of their parents' relationship status, raised by the community as much as by biological parents. This flexibility is practical; in a population this small, rigid rules about relationships could lead to genetic problems or social dysfunction. What matters is that children are cared for, that adults treat each other decently, that the community continues.

**Property** is loosely defined. Dwellings are personal but not owned—if you leave, someone else can claim the space. Tools are shared more than hoarded. Food stores are individual but with an understanding that in hardship, they'll be pooled. The most valuable possessions are knowledge, skill, and social relationships—things that can't be stolen, that grow rather than diminish with use.

**Time** is marked by natural cycles rather than abstract numbers. Days are simply light and dark. Longer periods are measured by plant cycles: "since the great vine flowered," "before the cold season," "when the swifts return." The Hollowborn live very much in the present, planning ahead for practical needs (storing food, maintaining structures) but not in the abstract sense of long-term goals or historical progress. The sinkhole doesn't encourage linear thinking. It's circular, cyclical, a closed system where the past and future feel less distinct than the ever-present now.

**The relationship with the outside world** is minimal and carefully controlled. Paths lead to the sinkhole's rim, rarely used, maintained only enough to remain passable. A few young adults leave sometimes, curious about the wider world or restless with the confined space. Most return within a few years, finding the outside too open, too bright, too loud with the wrong kinds of noise. A few don't return, and this is accepted—the outside has claimed them, or they've chosen it. Either way, the community doesn't try to retrieve them.

Occasionally outsiders come—traders seeking rare medicinal plants, scholars who've heard rumors of the ancient carvings, lost travelers. The Hollowborn greet them with careful hospitality, provide food and shelter for a night, answer questions vaguely, make it clear through a thousand subtle signals that the visit should be brief. Most outsiders are uncomfortable anyway; the sinkhole's strangeness, its dim light, its overwhelming presence of green and sound and verticality, creates a claustrophobia that builds quickly. They leave relieved, carrying stories that sound like exaggerations, that other outsiders half-believe at best.

The Hollowborn prefer this. They're not hostile to the outside world, but they're not seeking connection either. The sinkhole provides everything they need, and contact with outside brings risk—disease, cultural contamination, the possibility that someone will want what they have and come to take it. Better to be thought legend, rumor, possible but unverified.

**The relationship with the sinkhole itself** is the deepest, most complex relationship any of the Hollowborn have. It's home, provider, protector, and prison all at once. They love it with an intensity that outsiders find difficult to understand—love its green smell, its warm stone, its constant sound, its verticality, its containment. And they chafe against it sometimes, feel the walls pressing in, dream of horizons and open sky.

But they stay. Generation after generation, they stay. Because the sinkhole is not just where they live but what they are. Their eyes have adapted to its light, their hands to its stone, their ears to its acoustics, their sense of self to its circular boundaries. To leave would be to become something else, someone else, and most find that the price is too high.

So they continue: climbing, planting, weaving, singing, birthing, dying, returning to stone. They maintain the balance between human and green, between dwelling and wild, between individual and community. They are the Hollowborn, the people of the vertical garden, the ones who live inside the mountain's throat, and they are content—as much as any human ever is—to be exactly where they are, doing exactly what they do, in the warm green darkness that holds them like a mother's hand.
