# The Tenders

They are difficult to see at first—not because they hide, but because they exist at the threshold of scale and motion where human perception falters. You might watch a section of cylinder wall for ten minutes before realizing that what you took for surface texture is actually a figure, pressed flat against the bark-tiles, reading them with its entire body.

They are thin. Profoundly, impossibly thin when they choose to be—bodies that can compress to the width of a playing card, though they're typically closer to two or three centimeters in depth. This thinness is functional, adaptive. They slide into gaps between fused cylinders, slip through the spaces where platforms have grown together but not quite merged, navigate the interior passages of the vascular networks by flattening themselves into the conduit walls and riding the pulse of fluids as living insulation.

In their neutral state—if they have such a thing—they're roughly humanoid in proportion but not in detail. Two meters tall, perhaps, though measuring them is difficult because they're rarely still and never straight. Four limbs that can serve as arms or legs depending on orientation and need. A torso that's more of a connecting structure than a body cavity—you get the impression of distributed systems rather than centralized organs, everything networked and redundant. The head is the least human part: a wedge-shaped structure that's mostly sensory surface, covered in millions of fine cilia-like projections that wave continuously, tasting the air, feeling vibrations, processing the chemical and thermal signatures that saturate this place.

Their substance is ambiguous. The skin—if skin is the word—has the look of aged wood, that same amber-dark coloration that dominates the cylinders and platforms, marked with fine grain patterns that might be their own growth rings or might be mimicry, protective coloration in an environment where everything is substrate. But the texture is wrong for wood: too supple, too warm, slightly yielding when they press against surfaces. Touch one—if you could ever get close enough, if they permitted it—and you'd feel something between leather and living bark, dry but not desiccated, firm but not rigid.

They seem to be made of the same substances as the place itself, or they've become that way through such long association that the distinction has blurred. Their surfaces incorporate the same geometric patterns you see in the bark-tiles: hexagons, nested polygons, tessellations that serve some structural purpose in distributing stress or channeling growth. When they're motionless against a cylinder wall, these patterns align with the cylinder's own patterns, creating camouflage so perfect it approaches invisibility.

But they move, and the movement gives them away.

It's not human motion—no stride, no gait, no bilateral symmetry of effort. Instead they flow, they pour themselves through space like something between liquid and solid, finding the path of least resistance through the three-dimensional maze of structures. A limb extends, becomes longer and thinner, reaching for a handhold three meters away that shouldn't be reachable. The limb makes contact and immediately thickens, becomes load-bearing, while the rest of the body begins to stream toward the new anchor point. Another limb extends in a different direction, finds purchase, and now the body bifurcates, dividing its mass between two routes before reconverging beyond an obstacle.

They can split further than that. You watch one encounter a gap between platforms—too wide to reach across, too narrow to fall through, just an awkward space. The figure pauses, then divides itself: not just spreading limbs but actually separating into three distinct portions, each perhaps a third the mass of the original. The three portions flow through different passages around the obstacle, then meet on the far side and recombine, merging back into a single entity without apparent seam or difficulty.

This is when you realize they might not be individuals at all, or not in any sense you understand. They might be temporary configurations of some distributed substance, coalescences of the place's own material achieving motility and purpose for a time before dissolving back into the substrate. Or they might be stable entities with malleable forms, capable of division and recombination without loss of continuity or identity.

You never see them eat, but they must process energy somehow. They spend much of their time pressed against the cylinders or platforms, spread wide to maximize contact, and you realize they're absorbing—drinking in the heat, the complex hydrocarbons in the haze, the electrical potentials that flow through the system's vast networks. They're plugged in, tapping the place's metabolism directly. When they move away from these charging positions, they move with greater purpose, greater energy, as if freshly fueled.

Their work—and it is work, clearly purposeful, clearly necessary—is maintenance at a scale both intimate and vast.

You see one attend to a vascular conduit that's bulging with pressure, about to rupture. The figure flows to the site, presses itself around the conduit like a bandage, and goes very still. Its surface darkens, seems to harden. The bulge subsides over the course of minutes, the pressure redistributing, and when the figure finally peels away, the conduit has thickened in that spot, reinforced from outside. Did the figure add material from itself? Did it stimulate the conduit to grow? You can't tell. The repair is seamless either way.

Another one moves along a platform surface where one of the crystalline extrusions has grown too large, disrupting the flow-patterns you couldn't see but which apparently matter. The figure wraps itself around the crystal and begins to... not break it, exactly, but encourage it to separate. The crystal fragments along natural cleavage planes, and the figure carefully transports the pieces to the platform's edge, dropping them into spaces below where they fall with distant chiming sounds. The platform surface is left clean, ready for whatever process uses that space.

A third figure tends to one of the photosynthetic chambers, the barrel-sized black-green organs that pulse and breathe. This one has stopped pulsing, its surface dull and cracked. The tender presses against it, spreads thin across its entire surface, and you see—or think you see—something passing from the figure into the chamber: nutrients, chemical signals, repair instructions encoded in molecular form. The chamber doesn't revive, but the figure begins to break it down, secreting something that dissolves the chamber's structure into a thick dark fluid that drains away through the substrate. In a few minutes, nothing remains but a clear space where new growth can emerge.

They work individually but seem aware of each other, coordinating without obvious communication. You watch three of them converge on a cylinder that's growing off-axis, tilting in a way that will eventually cause structural problems. They position themselves around the cylinder's base, evenly spaced, and simultaneously begin to do something—apply pressure, inject growth hormones, stimulate specific cells—and over the course of an hour, the cylinder's growth direction corrects itself by several degrees. The three tenders then depart in different directions without acknowledgment, task completed.

Their awareness of the system is total. They navigate without hesitation through the maze of structures, always moving toward some site that needs attention. They seem to sense failures before they become catastrophic, arriving at stress points just as intervention becomes necessary. You realize you're watching something like an immune system, or a nervous system's maintenance response—distributed repair organisms that are part of the place itself, not separate from it but extensions of its capacity for self-regulation.

But they're not mindless. There's something that reads as intelligence, or at least sophisticated problem-solving. When one encounters a repair beyond its individual capacity—a major platform crack that needs more material than a single tender can provide—it does something. Vibrates, perhaps, or releases a chemical signal. Within minutes, two more arrive, then a fourth. They coordinate the repair, some holding the crack closed while others fill it with extruded material from their own substance—or material they've gathered, or material they've stimulated the platform itself to generate. The repair takes hours, requires clear sequencing of steps, adjustments when the first approach doesn't work. This is not automatic response. This is diagnosis and treatment.

Their sensory apparatus seems to encompass modalities you don't possess. Those millions of cilia on their head-wedges are obviously sampling the air's chemical composition, but they also respond to things you can't detect. A tender will suddenly pause mid-motion, cilia all orienting in one direction, then rapidly move toward something—a failing vascular valve twenty meters away and hidden behind structures, or a platform section that hasn't cracked yet but will in the next few hours. They perceive the system's state in ways that transcend simple observation: reading thermal signatures, electrical potentials, the subtle vibrations that propagate through interconnected structures, perhaps even the flow of fluids through pipes they can't see but can somehow sense.

You never see them sleep or rest beyond those periods of recharging through contact with the system. They're always in motion—somewhere, somehow, some of them are always working. You wonder if they do rest in shifts, or if the concept of rest is meaningless when you can merge with the substrate, become temporarily inert structure before re-differentiating into motile form.

They notice you, certainly. When you first arrived, one approached—not close, but closer than working distance—and regarded you with that wedge-head, cilia writhing in your direction, sampling your scent and heat and the disruption you cause in the air currents. After perhaps thirty seconds of this examination, it made a gesture you couldn't interpret—a ripple passing through its body, a momentary inflation of some internal chamber—and then departed. Since then, they've moved around you without concern, occasionally passing close enough that you could reach out and touch them if you dared. They don't avoid you, but they don't interact either. You are irrelevant to their work, a temporary anomaly that poses no threat to the system.

But you sense they could respond if you did threaten something. There's a capability held in reserve. The way they can harden their surfaces, compress their forms, move with sudden explosive speed when necessary—you've seen one drop from a high platform, falling thirty meters before catching itself on a conduit, absorbing the impact by becoming momentarily viscous, then solidifying again to continue moving. They're stronger than they look, more durable, more adaptable. If you damaged something, if you disrupted the system's work, you suspect the response would be swift and overwhelming.

Their numbers are difficult to assess. You might see a dozen in an hour of observation, or you might see three. But are they twelve individuals or three individuals appearing in multiple locations? That ability to split and merge makes counting meaningless. Perhaps there are hundreds of distinct entities. Perhaps there are three or four who divide and recombine as needed, creating temporary workers for specific tasks. Perhaps there's only one, a distributed being that can manifest in multiple places simultaneously, and what you think of as individual tenders are just its fingers, its tools, its localized instances.

They leave no traces beyond their repairs. No shelters, no tools, no artifacts. They are perfectly integrated into the system, using its structures as their infrastructure, requiring nothing beyond what the place provides. You never find anything like a dwelling or a workspace or a storage area—everything they need is already here, part of the system's substance, accessible by direct interface.

Time has marked them, though. The oldest ones—and you learn to recognize age through subtle signs: deeper grain patterns, surfaces that have compressed into something approaching the platform substrate's density, movements that are fractionally more efficient, more economical—show signs of approaching their own architectural state. The oldest tender you see is pressed against a cylinder wall in a section of heavy traffic, other tenders flowing past and occasionally over it. This one has become almost indistinguishable from the structure it rests against, its patterns perfectly aligned, its substance seemingly merged with the bark-tiles. But it still moves, very slowly, still performing some function—perhaps the slowest, longest tasks, the deep maintenance that takes years or decades to complete.

You wonder if they eventually become structure entirely. If a tender old enough, performing its function long enough, finally transitions fully into the substrate—becomes a platform section or part of a cylinder wall, its motility lost but its contribution permanently incorporated into the system. It would explain the sense of intentionality in the structures, the way some sections seem more responsive or better designed. They're not built; they're the remains of builders, workers who gave up motion to become foundation.

The youngest ones—smaller, paler, their patterns less defined—appear occasionally from nowhere. You never see them being born or made, but suddenly there's a new figure, half the size of the adults, moving with less certainty, learning the system's geography. An older tender will sometimes pause near a younger one, touch it briefly—surface to surface contact—and you wonder if information is being transferred: maps, techniques, chemical recognition patterns, the accumulated knowledge of maintenance. Then the older one moves on and the younger continues its education, testing its abilities to stretch and compress, practicing the repairs on minor problems while older tenders handle the critical work.

They have something like culture, or at least tradition. You notice patterns in how repairs are approached: certain techniques preferred, certain sequences followed, variations that suggest these aren't purely instinctive responses but learned behaviors, transmitted methods. When a major repair requires multiple tenders, they coordinate in ways that seem practiced, refined—not just cooperating but performing a kind of choreography, each knowing its role in a dance they've done before.

But they never stop working to gather, never cease their labor for something like socialization or rest or play. Their existence appears to be purely functional, defined entirely by the system's needs. If they have interiority—thoughts, feelings, desires beyond the drive to maintain and repair—they give no sign of it. They are perfect servants or perfect components, so adapted to their role that the role has become their entire being.

Or perhaps that's wrong. Perhaps their satisfaction is in the work itself, in the perfection of a repair completed, in the stability of a system maintained. Perhaps they experience something like joy when a cylinder grows true, when a platform remains level, when the vascular networks pulse with unimpeded flow. You can't know. They're as alien in their way as the place they tend, their consciousness—if they have consciousness—oriented toward dimensions of experience you can't access.

Sometimes, rarely, you see them pause in their work not for recharging but for something else. A tender will stop on a high platform, spread itself wide but not against any surface, and simply remain there for a minute or two, cilia waving slowly, head-wedge oriented toward the dimness above. Observing? Thinking? Communicating with something distant? The moment passes and work resumes, but the pause suggests some interior life, some need or awareness that transcends immediate function.

You watch one tend to a photosynthetic chamber with extraordinary care, spending far longer than the task seems to require, adjusting and readjusting, testing the chamber's pulse, refusing to move on until the rhythm is exactly right. Another supervises a young tender's first major repair, not intervening but present, observing, ready to assist if needed but allowing the learning process to unfold naturally. These moments hint at something beyond pure function: care, pedagogy, standards that go beyond adequate to insist on excellent.

Their relationship with the system is symbiotic but unequal. The system could exist without them, probably—it did its work before they emerged, will do its work after they've all transitioned into structure. But it would be diminished, more prone to catastrophic failures, less able to adapt to changing conditions. They're not the system's creators or masters but its gardeners, its careful attendants, ensuring that the ancient turbulent work continues with maximum efficiency.

And you realize, finally, that you're seeing something like devotion. These thin flowing figures that split and merge and press themselves into the hot surface of processing cylinders, that spend centuries or millennia in patient labor to maintain a system they didn't build and can't fully control—they're not slaves or automatons. They're acolytes, practitioners of a faith that has no doctrine except maintenance, no ritual except repair, no god except the system itself in its vast indifferent continuity.

They will be here long after you leave, doing their endless work in the amber dimness, tending to structures older than memory in service to processes they've made their purpose. They will thin with age, merge with the platforms, become the substrate for future generations of tenders who will continue the work without beginning or end, without question or complaint, until entropy itself finally claims the place they've dedicated themselves to sustaining.

And in their patient flowing motion, their casual miracles of form and function, their absolute commitment to the system's continuation, they are perhaps the only thing here that approaches beauty—not despite their strangeness but because of it, because they've achieved such perfect adaptation, such complete integration with their environment, that they've transcended the categories of alive or built or natural or made. They simply are: the Living Foundry's living maintenance, as much a part of the place as the cylinders they tend, as necessary as the heat that drives it all, as eternal as the ancient turbulent work itself.
