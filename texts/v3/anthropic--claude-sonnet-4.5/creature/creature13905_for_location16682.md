# The Inhabitants of the Constructed Canopy

The people here move like water through their own infrastructure—following channels worn by necessity and choice, pooling in the flat spaces, cascading down the inclines with practiced efficiency. They are not many, but they are sufficient: perhaps three hundred souls distributed across the terraces, though no one maintains an exact count. Numbers here matter less than density, balance, the sense of whether there are enough hands for the work and enough yield for the hands.

## The Core Dwellers

There are those who were born high. You can identify them sometimes by the way they breathe—unconsciously, easily, their bodies calibrated from infancy to extract what they need from the thin air. Their chests are often barrel-broad, their movements economical. They speak in the middle of exertion without pausing, carry loads up staircases without stopping to rest halfway. A woman in her forties tends a tomato terrace on the fifth level, moving between plants with her daughter, maybe thirteen, both of them talking steadily while they prune and stake, neither one breathing hard. The daughter has never been below the cloudline. She knows this place as the measure of all places.

But many—perhaps most—came here as adults, and their bodies remember lower elevations. They pause more often. They've learned to pace themselves, to read their own limitations, to recognize when the slight lightheadedness means *stop, rest, breathe*. A man repairing a walkway junction works in measured intervals: twenty minutes of effort, five minutes sitting with his back against a support beam, his chest rising and falling deliberately. He's been here fifteen years, he tells you. His body has adapted as much as it will. This is enough.

Some arrived intentionally, choosing the altitude and its challenges for reasons they articulate differently. A couple in their sixties speak of wanting to live where survival requires attention, where you can't take air or water or growing things for granted. They wanted to live *deliberately*, they say, and this place demands it. Others came for work—the district needed someone with specific skills, machining or electrical systems or structural engineering—and stayed because leaving felt like more effort than adapting, and then adaptation became belonging.

And some came because they were fleeing something below. The altitude offers a kind of filtering protection; not everyone can tolerate it, so not everyone will follow. The community asks few questions about what people left behind. What matters is what they contribute here, now, in the loud bright present tense of this place.

## The Children

There are perhaps forty children, though this number fluctuates. Some families move down to the valleys before their children reach school age, wanting easier access to education, to medicine, to the broader world. Others stay, teaching their children through a hybrid system—online classes when weather permits reliable connection, visiting teachers who come up monthly, mentorship from skilled adults in the community, and the constant practical education of maintaining this place.

The children born here are different from those who arrive. The altitude-born run between terraces without thinking about breath. They climb the frameworks like the children below climb trees—which is to say these children don't climb trees because there are no trees to climb, only metal bones and pipe-limbs and cable-sinews. A boy of seven navigates walkways thirty meters above the next level with the casual confidence of someone who has never fallen and can't quite imagine falling. His mother watches from a distance but doesn't call him back. He knows his geography.

The children understand the systems intuitively. They know which cisterns refill fastest after cloud-pass, which garden beds yield the best berries, which cats tolerate being handled and which should be admired from distance. A girl of nine can read weather in the morning sky as well as any adult—she knows when to bring in the laundry before noon, when afternoon clouds will be heavy or light, when the wind will shift from south to north and what that means for temperature.

But there's also a loneliness to being young here. With few other children, friendships are precious and sometimes complicated. Siblings are playmates by necessity. The community tries to compensate—communal meals where children congregate, projects designed to give them time together, visits to lower settlements where they can meet valley children—but the isolation is real. You see it in how the teenagers cluster whenever they're in the same space, talking rapidly, reluctant to separate. You see it in how the youngest ones shadow adults, learning to find companionship in work.

Some of the children will leave. Everyone knows this. They'll go down to study or work or just to see what else exists. Some will return; altitude gets in the blood if you're born to it. Others won't, and the community accepts this without bitterness. The place sustains a certain population, no more, and the young who leave make room for others who might arrive.

## The Skilled Hands

The district runs because people here know how to make things work and how to fix them when they don't.

There's a man in his fifties everyone calls Machinist though he has another name. He maintains the metal: repairs pumps, fabricates brackets and fittings, keeps the pulley systems running. His workshop occupies what was once a boiler room, still stained with soot in the high corners. The space is dense with tools—lathes and drill presses and grinders, some electric, some hand-cranked for when power is scarce. He works with the concentration of someone performing surgery, which in a sense he is; these systems keep the place alive. He's training two younger people, teaching them to read metal by sound—the pitch of a struck pipe revealing its integrity, the whine of a bearing announcing its approaching failure.

A woman with white hair bound in a permanent braid manages water distribution. She knows the system completely: which tanks feed which terraces, where the pressure drops, which pipes have pinhole leaks that aren't worth fixing versus which need immediate attention. She walks the district daily, listening to water move through infrastructure, adjusting valves, redirecting flow. In her head she carries a map of the entire hydraulic system, updated constantly. She's teaching a younger man, mid-twenties, but she says it takes years to learn the system's moods, its seasonal variations, the way a tank sounds when it's truly full versus when it's full but about to develop a crack.

There are the gardeners—not everyone is one, but everyone does some gardening, so the dedicated ones serve more as coordinators, advisers, seed-keepers. A broad-shouldered person named Kris, who uses they/them, manages the seed library and planting schedules. They know what grows at this altitude, which varieties have failed over the years and which have adapted. They maintain relationships with seed banks in the valleys, trading their altitude-selected seeds for new genetics to trial. In their quarters you see jars and envelopes organized by plant type and year, meticulous notes about germination rates and yields and disease resistance. Their knowledge is recorded but also carried in body-memory—their hands know the size and texture of every seed variety they work with.

Several people specialize in structural maintenance. They're the ones you see on the high walkways with tool belts, replacing corroded bolts, reinforcing joints, applying rust-preventive coatings. One of them, a woman with rope-scarred hands, specializes in rigging—she's the one who calls when something heavy needs to be lifted or moved, when a new cable needs to be strung between buildings. She understands load and angle and stress, understands wind and how it acts on suspended objects. She's teaching anyone who wants to learn, because she knows that if she falls or falters, the knowledge needs to have transferred.

There are electricians, plural, because the power system is hybrid and temperamental—solar panels, wind turbines, the main line from below, battery systems, the ancient backup generator that runs on biofuel and gets tested monthly. Two brothers manage this collectively, dividing territory but consulting on problems. They spend much time fault-finding, tracing mysterious drains on the system, replacing corroded connections, upgrading sections when materials can be sourced. They're lobbying for more solar capacity, but panels must be purchased or traded for, and the community discusses such expenses slowly, communally.

And there's the medic, a term that covers both their training and the limits of it. He's not a doctor—the district can't sustain a full medical practice—but he's a trained nurse with additional wilderness medicine certification. His clinic occupies three rooms on a mid-level terrace: consultation space, treatment area, and pharmacy (modest, mostly stocked with basics, some medications sourced from below, others made from garden plants according to old formulae that may or may not do much). He treats altitude sickness in newcomers, sets broken bones, stitches cuts, manages chronic conditions as best he can with limited resources. Serious cases go down to the valleys, which means someone has to be stable enough for the descent. He's frank about his limitations, about what he can't do. But he's here, and his presence means minor problems stay minor, that someone knowledgeable makes the call about whether something needs evacuation.

## The Long-Term Adjusted

Some people have been here so long they've become part of the infrastructure, almost—human elements as permanent as the girders, as essential as the water tanks.

An elderly woman, possibly eighty, lives in a corner apartment with windows on three sides and a garden on her roof. She's been here forty years, arrived when the transition from industrial to residential was still happening. She tells stories about when there were fewer than thirty people, when whole sections were still abandoned, when they weren't sure the gardens would work. She speaks of the ones who left, the ones who died here and are buried in the lower cemetery, the children she helped raise who are now raising children of their own. She moves slowly now, but she still tends her roof garden, still appears for communal meals, still offers advice when asked. The community watches over her gently, checking in, helping with tasks she can't manage, but letting her maintain her autonomy as long as she can. When she can't anymore, everyone knows, decisions will need to be made.

A man in his seventies maintains the district's archive—a room lined with shelves holding logbooks, photographs, technical manuals, records of modifications and repairs, maps showing the infrastructure's evolution. He's not a historian by training, but he's made himself one, understanding that the place has a memory worth preserving. He's digitizing what he can, when power and time permit, but he also trusts paper, trusts redundancy. He gives tours to newcomers, explaining how sections were built, pointing out load-bearing elements versus decorative ones, showing where the original industrial site ended and the residential additions began. His knowledge will be hard to replace. He's writing it down, training anyone who shows interest, but some knowledge resists transcription—the gestalt understanding of how the whole place works together.

There's a cook, though everyone cooks for themselves too, who manages the communal kitchen and organizes shared meals. She's in her sixties, worked in restaurant kitchens below before ascending, and brought that expertise to a place that needed it. She understands bulk preparation, food preservation, how to make limited ingredients interesting through technique. The communal meals—held three times a week—are social anchors, times when people stop work and gather and eat together and talk. She plans menus around what the gardens are yielding, what stores remain from previous harvests, what's been traded from below. She's training her replacement, a younger man who's interested, but she's not ready to stop yet. The work still satisfies her.

## The Temporaries

Not everyone stays forever. Some people come for a season or a year, contract workers hired for specific projects, or visitors testing whether they want to live here, or researchers studying the altitude ecology.

A biologist spent last summer here, cataloging the insect populations, trying to understand how species have adapted to this constructed environment at altitude. She lived in a small apartment, worked long hours with nets and collection jars, left with data and promises to return. The community liked having her, liked learning what she learned, seeing familiar insects explained scientifically. Whether she returns depends on funding, on academic calendars, on things outside the community's control.

A young man is here for six months, part of an engineering fellowship, studying structural adaptation and maintenance strategies. He's documenting repair techniques, interviewing the maintenance specialists, creating technical drawings. His work might benefit the community—recommendations for reinforcement, updated structural assessments—or it might just become a thesis chapter, useful primarily to him. The community tolerates his questions, cooperates with his documentation, but doesn't depend on his conclusions. They've kept the place standing this long; they'll continue regardless of what his models predict.

Occasionally people come and don't stay long—a week, a few days. The altitude defeats them, or the isolation does, or they simply realize this isn't their place. The community accepts this without judgment. Not everyone can live here. Not everyone should. Better to discover it quickly than to invest years in struggling.

## The Marginal Ones

In any community there are people who don't quite fit the standard patterns, who occupy edges and interstices.

There's a man who lives alone in a tower section, rarely descending, tending his own gardens, doing maintenance work in exchange for supplies left at his door. He's not hostile but profoundly asocial, and the community respects this, lets him be. He contributes enough to justify the space he occupies. His mental health is questionable—possibly he came here escaping something, possibly the altitude has affected him, possibly he's always been this way. As long as he's not dangerous to himself or others, the community allows him his isolation. Someone checks on him periodically, leaves food if he seems to be neglecting himself, but otherwise he's left to his solitary patterns.

A younger woman cycles through depression, struggles visibly some seasons, functions well others. She's open about it, asks for help when she needs it, takes medication managed through the medic. The community accommodates her variability—picks up tasks she can't complete during low periods, celebrates when she's functional and productive. There's no mental health infrastructure here beyond the medic's basic capacity and whatever can be accessed remotely, so the community becomes the support system, imperfectly but genuinely.

An older man drinks too much. Not constantly, not dangerously most of the time, but enough that it's noticed, occasionally enough that it impairs his work. The community has discussed it collectively, has offered help, has set boundaries about what tasks he can be trusted with. He's not expelled—where would he go?—but he's monitored, managed, kept away from critical systems. He's aware of the community's concern and sometimes resents it, sometimes appreciates it. The situation is chronic, unresolved, tolerated because the alternative—forcing him out—seems harsher than managing the problem.

## The Caretakers of Margins

Some people have taken responsibility for the non-human residents, the boundary between the human community and the rest of the ecology.

An older person, soft-spoken and observant, manages the chicken coops—four of them distributed across terraces, housing maybe fifty birds total. They understand chicken behavior, chicken health, the genetics of breeding for egg production and cold tolerance. They cull when necessary, process birds for meat with practical efficiency, collect eggs daily, clean coops, monitor for disease. The chickens are production animals but also treated with care; this person talks to them while working, handles them gently, gives them good deaths when their time comes.

A teenager has taken responsibility for the cats—which is complicated because the cats aren't owned, aren't fully domestic, aren't entirely cooperative. But this young person feeds them reliably, treats injuries when cats allow themselves to be caught, monitors the population and sometimes arranges for spaying/neutering when a cat can be trapped and transported down to a valley vet. They've named all the cats, knows their territories and relationships, can identify which cats are breeding and which are beyond that. They've made this their role without anyone assigning it, motivated by love of cats and a sense that someone needs to pay attention.

Several people maintain the bee hives—it's skilled work requiring knowledge and protective equipment and careful timing. They harvest honey sparingly, leaving most for the bees to overwinter on. They monitor hive health, watch for mites and disease, split hives when they get too large, capture swarms that occasionally establish themselves in inconvenient locations. The honey is precious, distributed carefully, used medicinally as well as for food. The bees themselves are valued for pollination, for their contribution to garden yields, for the way they indicate ecosystem health.

## The Boundary Walkers

A few people regularly move between the high district and the valleys below, serving as connectors, traders, information conduits.

A woman in her thirties makes the trip down every two weeks, carrying goods to trade—excess produce, honey, crafts made by residents, altitude-selected seeds—and returning with things the district can't produce: certain foods, medications, tools, materials for repairs, news. She's strong enough to handle the descent and ascent, adapted enough to tolerate the altitude shifts. She knows the valley people, has relationships there, negotiates trades. The district depends on her consistency, her reliability. She's talked about training a backup, someone who could share the work or take over if she can't continue, but so far no one has committed to the role.

A younger man works the cable system—there's a cargo line running from the district down to a mid-altitude station, and he manages the loading and unloading, maintains the cable and pulleys, coordinates with the operators below. It's physical work, sometimes dangerous when weather is bad, but essential for moving heavy items that can't be carried. He's learning the maintenance aspects from older workers who've done the job for decades, understanding that the cable is a lifeline and failure isn't acceptable.

Occasionally, people from below visit. Valley relatives coming to see family. Officials checking on infrastructure connected to lower systems. Traders bringing goods. Tourists, rarely, curious about the high district but usually defeated by the altitude before they see much. The community receives visitors with cautious hospitality—welcome but watched, helped but not necessarily trusted until they prove themselves. Too many people coming at once would strain resources, so visits are informally managed, spaced out, limited in duration.

## The Quiet Holders

Some people's contributions are subtle, less visible, but structurally important.

An older couple maintains the seed library with Kris, but also keeps the community's oral history, remembering who did what, who knows what, how problems have been solved before. They're not official historians like the archivist, but they're the ones people consult when trying to remember "didn't someone fix this same issue five years ago?" or "who was it who knew how to repair that kind of valve?" They're living memory, institutional knowledge embodied.

A middle-aged woman facilitates the community meetings where collective decisions are made—when to start major repairs, how to allocate resources, whether to accept new residents, how to handle interpersonal conflicts that affect the whole group. She doesn't hold formal authority; the community's governance is loose, consensus-seeking, resistant to hierarchy. But someone needs to organize the meetings, keep discussions productive, ensure voices are heard. She's done this for years, good at the work, trusted to be fair. When she's tired of it, someone else will need to step forward, and the community will have to learn whether that person can do it as well.

A quiet man, possibly on the autism spectrum, maintains detailed records of weather patterns—daily observations of temperature, wind, cloud movements, precipitation. He's done this for twelve years, has notebooks filled with data, has identified patterns the community uses for planning. When to expect dry spells. How wind patterns shift with seasons. The likely severity of winter storms. His records have become essential, though he doesn't seek recognition. The work itself satisfies him, the pattern-observation, the data accumulation.

## The Animals

But the question was who lives here, and the answer isn't only human.

The cats are perhaps fifteen in number, though they move between territories and sometimes disappear or die or new ones arrive. They're working animals—rodent control—but they've evolved into something more complicated, half-feral and half-communal. Some allow handling, even seek attention. Others are pure function, visible as movement in peripheral vision, only interacting with humans at feeding stations. They're all named by the teenager who cares for them: Smoke, Patch, Captain, Ginger, Little Gray, Old Tom, Princess, Shadow, Three-Legs (who gets around fine on three), White Feet, Bandit, Scrapper, Mouser, Shy, and Young Tom. They hunt, they mate, they fight, they sun themselves on warm metal in afternoon light, they produce kittens that survive or don't. The community sustains them with supplemental food and basic medical care, and they in turn keep the rodent population below the level where it would become a serious problem.

The rodents—mice certainly, rats probably—live in the walls and under floors and in the spaces between things. They're intelligent, adaptive, persistent. They eat spilled grain, raid improperly stored food, chew insulation, occasionally get into places they shouldn't. The community has achieved equilibrium: not elimination, which is impossible, but management. Traps in sensitive areas. Food stored in metal containers. Cats allowed access to hunting grounds. An acceptance that rodents are part of the ecology, but with boundaries on where they can proliferate. Sometimes you see mice darting across floors at dusk. Occasionally a rat in a compost area. They're not welcomed but they're expected.

The birds are abundant and diverse. Pigeons nest in the eaves, fifty or more, their cooing a constant background sound. They're tolerated because they're inevitable—the district provides everything pigeons want: shelter, ledges, spilled grain, human proximity without predation. They're harvested occasionally for food, which keeps the population somewhat in check. The colored leg bands on some are remnants of a study done years ago, before anyone current can quite remember, and new pigeons hatch without bands, but the marked ones persist, the bands outlasting their original purpose.

Sparrows and finches live here in flocks, nesting in vegetation, in vents, in any protected cavity. They eat seeds and insects, bathe in water collection puddles, fill morning and evening with song. House swallows nest under eaves and hunt insects in flight, their acrobatics mesmerizing to watch. Someone has put up nest boxes in a few locations, which several pairs use.

Occasionally, larger birds appear: a hawk perched on a high tower, scanning for pigeons or rodents. Crows passing through, investigating but not staying—they prefer lower altitudes. Once, someone swears, an eagle, but at this altitude that might have been a large hawk misidentified.

And insects, uncountable. Bees from the managed hives and wild bees that nest in wall cavities. Butterflies in season, several species, attracted by flowering plants. Flies, inevitable around compost and chicken coops, managed but not eliminated. Beetles in wood, in soil, in stored grains—each species occupying its niche, most harmless, some destructive enough to require monitoring. Ants in several varieties, finding their way into buildings, establishing colonies, following scent trails to any available food. Spiders everywhere, in corners and under railings and between plants, catching other insects, mostly tolerated because they're efficient predators. Moths at night, drawn to lights, their wings dusty against window screens.

The whole ecosystem is constructed—nothing here is original, everything has colonized or been introduced—but it's functional, balanced, alive. The humans are the keystone species, maintaining the infrastructure that supports everything else, but they're not alone. They live surrounded by other lives, dependent on some (bees, chickens), tolerant of others (cats, most birds), in constant negotiation with the rest (rodents, ants, pigeons). The community understands itself as part of this ecology, not separate from it. They manage, they adapt, they accept that absolute control is impossible and undesirable.

## The Arrival and Departure

People come and go, though slowly. A new family arrived last spring—parents and two children—renting space while they decide if this place is home. They're adjusting variably. One parent is thriving, loves the gardens and the community, has skills the district needs. The other struggles with the altitude, gets headaches, misses the valleys. The children are adapting better than either parent, making friends, learning the territory. Whether they stay depends on negotiations still happening, whether the struggling parent can acclimatize or whether the family decides the cost is too high.

An older couple is planning to leave, moving down permanently. They've lived here fifteen years but are aging, and the altitude becomes harder with age, the thin air more burdensome, the isolation more difficult. They're selling their apartment to a younger person who wants to move up, transferring knowledge about their section's maintenance needs, gradually disengaging from responsibilities. Their departure will be felt—they've been steady contributors, reliable community members—but everyone understands. The place isn't for everyone forever.

A young adult who grew up here left for university three years ago and is returning for the summer, maybe longer. She's testing whether she still belongs here, whether what she learned below has uses here, whether she can reconcile who she became in the valleys with who she was in the heights. The community welcomes her return but doesn't pressure her to stay. She's carrying herself differently—not quite a resident but not quite a visitor, navigating an in-between status, relearning how to breathe the thin air she once breathed without thinking.

## The Question of Community

What holds these people together isn't agreement on everything, isn't perfect harmony, isn't absence of conflict. They disagree about priorities, about resource allocation, about who should do what, about how children should be educated, about whether to invest in this upgrade or that repair. Conflicts happen—personal tensions, philosophical differences, practical disputes.

But they're bound by shared necessity, shared vulnerability, shared investment in this improbable place. The infrastructure won't maintain itself. The gardens won't tend themselves. The water won't distribute itself. The systems require human attention, daily and constant, and that requirement creates interdependence. You can't live here truly alone; the place demands cooperation.

There's also something about surviving together at altitude that creates bonds. The thin air is a shared condition. The clouds passing through affect everyone. The winter storms threaten all sections. When something fails—a pump breaks, a platform develops structural problems, a medical emergency occurs—the response is collective. Help arrives because everyone understands they might need help next time.

The community has rituals that reinforce connection: communal meals three times a week, monthly meetings to coordinate work and make decisions, seasonal celebrations marking planting and harvest, memorial gatherings when residents die. These aren't mandatory but they're well-attended because they're social anchors in a place where isolation is easy, where people sometimes go days working alone on their sections without seeing others.

And there's pride, collective if unspoken. They've maintained this unlikely place. They've made gardens grow at altitude. They've kept old infrastructure functional for decades past its intended life. They've raised children here, sustained community here, created something that shouldn't quite work but does. That pride doesn't make them superior—they don't generally claim to be better than valley people—but it gives them identity, cohesion, sense of purpose.

## The Unspoken Understanding

There are things everyone knows but no one needs to say. That safety depends on attention—a single careless moment near an edge, around machinery, on a wet walkway, can be fatal. That resources are finite and must be shared fairly. That skills must be transferred because everyone ages, everyone eventually can't do what they once could. That children are collective responsibility because there are too few for any to be neglected. That the place is always close to various failures—mechanical, structural, ecological—and constant maintenance is what holds those failures at bay.

There's an understanding about privacy and community, about when to help and when to leave someone alone. People live in close quarters, hear each other through walls, see each other daily. Privacy is created through collective agreement to not intrude, to pretend not to notice some things, to respect closed doors and withdrawn energy. But there's also understanding that isolation can become crisis, so people check on each other, gently, offering help while respecting refusal.

And there's understanding about the outside world. The valleys exist, the cities exist beyond them, and the global systems of economy and politics and culture. News arrives through internet connections when they work, through visitors, through trips down and back. The community isn't isolated from the world, but they're buffered from it, separated by altitude and choice. What happens below matters variably—supply chains affect them, weather patterns affect them, political decisions about infrastructure funding affect them—but day-to-day they're focused on immediate systems, local problems, the territory of this constructed ecology.

They know they're unusual. They know most people would find this life too hard, too isolated, too dependent on systems that could fail. They know the place probably can't last forever—eventually maintenance costs will exceed capacity, or weather will deliver a storm that destroys critical infrastructure, or the valley systems they depend on will change. But eventually isn't now, and now is where they live, breathing thin air, tending high gardens, maintaining the metal and concrete and pipe-work that holds them all aloft, here at the vertical edge of viability, where people have made a place that shouldn't quite be possible but undeniably is.
