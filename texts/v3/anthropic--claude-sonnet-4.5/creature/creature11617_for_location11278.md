# The Inhabitants of New Engines

The Engineers live here, though "live" requires qualification and "Engineers" might be a courtesy title they no longer deserve or perhaps never earned.

There are eleven of them currently, though the number has been as low as seven and as high as sixteen depending on factors they don't discuss with outsiders and possibly don't discuss with each other. They are not a community in any conventional sense—they maintain no central habitation, hold no regular meetings, operate no shared schedule. Instead they exist in a dispersed network across the valley's three-mile breadth, each maintaining a circuit of machines, each walking their routes on rotations that might overlap with others or might not, creating an occupancy pattern that resembles the firing of neurons: sparse, distributed, somehow adding up to something that functions.

You recognize them first by their clothing, which has achieved a kind of convergence through pure pragmatism. Heavy wool, almost universally—dark grays, browns, the occasional faded olive. Waxed canvas over that, jackets and overcoats that shed the constant moisture without pretending to be waterproof. Rubber boots, tall ones, reinforced at the seams because the acidity of the moss-water eats through cheaper materials within months. They wear their hoods up almost always, not against rain exactly but against the dense air itself, the omnipresent mist that works its way into ears and neck and seems to carry sound directly into your skull if you let it. Underneath all this, you catch glimpses of what might be company uniforms from different companies, different eras—a faded logo here, a name patch there, suggestions of employment that may have ended decades ago or may continue in some theoretical sense, paychecks depositing into accounts no one checks.

Their ages are uniformly difficult to assess. The moisture does something to skin—keeps it oddly preserved, pale, less weathered than you'd expect from people who work outside constantly. But their hands tell different stories, hands that are uniformly damaged: fingers slightly crooked from breaks that healed while still working, calluses in strange places from gripping specific tools, nails discolored from impacts and chemical exposures. One of them—Kiet, though you won't learn his name unless you encounter him three or four times—is missing the last joint of his left ring finger. Not cleanly, not surgically, but gone in a way that suggests an argument with machinery that the machinery won. He works a circuit of seven pump stations on the northern slope and seems unbothered by the loss, has adapted his grip patterns, uses the shortened finger as a kind of lever for tasks that require wedging into tight spaces.

They speak infrequently, even to each other during the rare encounters on overlapping routes. When they do speak, it's in technical languages specific to their machines—compression ratios, flow rates, bearing temperatures. But also they've developed a vocabulary for the moss, for the different species and their behaviors, names that might be scientific or might be private invention. "Shelf-maker" for one of the horizontal woody species. "Cathedral" for the dome formations. "Weeping" for a particular iron-gray lichen that grows on stone faces and seems to accelerate water sheeting. They discuss these plants with the same pragmatic attention they give to gasket degradation and valve timing, as components of a system that must be understood to be worked within.

Marit walks the western circuit, nine machines over a two-day round, sleeping in one of the three-walled shelters or sometimes just in the moss itself, rolled in a waxed tarp, body heat trapped by the plant mass below. She is tall, angular, moves with a deliberate economy that suggests joint pain managed rather than resolved. Her particular expertise is the waterwheel mechanisms—she can diagnose bearing problems by sound from a hundred yards away, can disassemble and rebuild the wooden bucket assemblies using materials she carries or harvests from dead machinery elsewhere. She talks to the wheels while working on them, a constant low murmur that isn't quite language, more like acknowledging another presence, confirming mutual existence. When the wheels start turning again after her interventions, she watches for several complete rotations, counting something under her breath, then moves on without ceremony.

There's a younger one—younger being relative, perhaps forty where the others might be fifty or sixty or seventy—named Chen or Shen, the name softens differently depending on who's saying it. He maintains the cable systems, the strung lines between installations, and this means he climbs. You see him sometimes at distance, fifty feet up on a stone face, body pressed to the wet rock, moving between anchor points with a confidence that suggests either extensive training or extensive lucky survival. He replaces the ceramic insulators when they crack from temperature stress, re-tensions cables that have stretched or sagged, occasionally runs entirely new lines when something has corroded through. He carries his tools in a leather bag that's gone stiff and dark with absorbed moisture, and he's developed a peculiar practice of marking his climbs—small cairns of stacked pebbles left in moss-patches near the top of each major face. Not graffiti, not names, just evidence of passage, small stone witnesses to work done.

The oldest visible one, though again age is uncertain, is called Vos or Voss or possibly First, the name seems to have variants. Deep vertical creases in the face, white hair kept brutally short, glasses that he cleans compulsively with a cloth that can never quite dry in this environment. He works the central valley, the densest machine concentration, and he alone seems to maintain documentation—a series of notebooks wrapped in oilcloth, pages logged with observations in handwriting so small and precise it approaches code. He records machine states, certainly, but also moss advancement rates, stone subsidence measurements, the timing of the steam releases from the northern structure. Every third day approximately, he walks to the metal platform, the vantage point, and spends an hour simply looking, sometimes sketching, sometimes just standing. If you encounter him there and are foolish enough to interrupt, he will answer questions with technical precision but also visible annoyance, making it clear that observation is a practice, not leisure, and your presence is contaminating the data.

Then there are the ones whose names you don't learn because they actively avoid encounter. Shapes seen at distance, figures moving between machines at the edge of visibility. They might be doing the same work as the others—maintenance, monitoring, adjustment—or they might be doing something else entirely. One of them seems to work only at night or in the thickest fog, identified only by the sound of work carried on wind, metal on metal, the specific rhythm of a wrench on a stuck bolt. Another leaves marks on the machines themselves, small scratches or painted symbols that might be inspection records or might be some private language of blessing or claim. Vos mentions them occasionally in neutral terms—"the night circuit," "the marker"—acknowledging their existence without suggesting they should be named or approached.

The Engineers do not maintain the machines in the sense of keeping them running smoothly. Rather, they maintain them in the sense of keeping them running at all, which is different. These are systems that want to fail, that are constantly besieged by moisture, by moss infiltration, by the simple material fatigue of running for decades beyond expected service life. The work is archaeological as much as mechanical—figuring out what a machine was meant to do, what parts have been previously replaced with improvised alternatives, what modifications represent genuine improvements versus desperate expedients. Marit once spent six hours on a single pump station, disassembling a valve assembly only to discover that three critical components were carved wood, replacements made by someone years or decades ago when the metal originals corroded through. The wood had swollen with moisture, achieving a seal that the original metal gaskets could never have managed. She documented this in her own way—a brief nod, a moment of what might have been appreciation—then reassembled everything unchanged.

They harvest their materials from a kind of mechanical ecosystem of their own creation. Scattered across the terrain, in addition to the functioning installations, are the corpses of earlier machines, failed systems, equipment that broke too badly to justify repair. These become donor sites. Chen scavenges cable from a collapsed tower on the southern ridge. Kiet maintains a personal cache of salvaged bolts, sorted by size and thread pitch, kept in a canvas roll he carries always. Someone, no one admits who, has established a parts depot in one of the brick generator sheds—shelves made from machine grating, loaded with salvaged components: valves, gaskets, bearings, electrical components in various states of function. The organization system is opaque but apparently effective; you can watch an Engineer enter, locate a specific widget among hundreds of possibilities within thirty seconds, and depart. There is no inventory log, no checkout system. Things are simply there until needed, then not there, then eventually replaced by salvage from the next failure.

Their relationship to the moss is complex and not entirely reconciled. On one hand, they fight it constantly—clearing it from intake screens, scraping it from electrical housings, preventing it from infiltrating mechanisms where its expansion could jam movements. On the other hand, they seem to recognize it as the reason anything works at all up here, the only reason there's enough water for the wheels, enough stable surface for the installations, enough erosion control to keep the young stone from simply washing away. Marit actively encourages certain moss species around her waterwheels, has learned which ones bind stone best, which create the smoothest channel linings. Vos records moss advancement with what might be approval, though his writing never quite becomes emotional enough to confirm.

Some of them have developed strange intimacies with specific locations. Kiet has a pump station—North-7 in whatever numbering system they use—that he visits daily regardless of need, a visit that seems more like attendance than maintenance. He touches specific components in specific sequence, checking temperatures and vibrations with his hands, listening with his damaged ear pressed directly to metal housings. The machine has become instrument for some kind of sensory practice, feeding him information about states or conditions that might extend beyond the mechanical. He never explains this, would probably deny it if asked, but you can watch him stand there for twenty minutes, hands on iron, absolutely still, breathing synchronized to the pump rhythm.

They eat, presumably, though their relationship to food is utilitarian to the point of concerning. They carry dense, compact things—bars made from oats and fat, dried meat in quantities that seem inadequate, nuts, extremely hard cheese. One of them, name unknown, has established caches in waterproof containers buried or hidden near certain machines, allowing lighter travel between supply points. The caches contain not just food but also spare clothing, basic tools, first aid supplies that are mostly focused on bleeding control and hypothermia prevention. Once every few weeks, maybe once a month, there's a resupply—someone hikes in from lower elevations with loads carried on external frame packs, military surplus style. The supplies get distributed to the various caches and personal packs through some invisible coordination system. You never see the meetings where this happens, but the evidence appears: new supplies in locations, refreshed stocks, occasionally new equipment that someone determined was needed.

They sleep in the landscape, mostly. The three-walled shelters see some use, but just as often you'll find evidence of bivouacking—compressed moss, tarps weighted with stones, cold fire sites from the rare occasions when something burnable and dry can be assembled. They seem to have developed an ability to rest that doesn't require extended unconsciousness, instead taking brief heavy sleeps at odd times, twenty minutes curled in a pump station corner, an hour in afternoon sun on a south-facing moss dome. Their circadian rhythms have presumably adapted to the environment where daylight is consistent but heavily filtered, where sunrise and sunset are more theoretical than observed, where the constant sound would prevent normal sleep if they hadn't learned to incorporate it.

Medical issues get handled with grim pragmatism. Kiet's finger was clearly dealt with on-site—the scarring suggests tourniquet, cautery, basic closure, all performed in field conditions. Someone has set their own broken bones; you can see it in the way Chen's left collarbone has a visible offset, healed but never properly aligned. They carry medical supplies that are extensive but primitive—suture kits, antiseptics, antibiotics in bottles labeled in languages suggesting international sourcing of questionable legitimacy. Splints. Bandages. Painkillers in various strengths. One of Vos's notebooks contains what appears to be a medical reference section—dosages, procedures, diagrams of human anatomy with annotations about field treatment. They have accepted that serious injury up here means either self-treatment or death, and they've prepared for the former while presumably making peace with the possibility of the latter.

But they are not suicidal, not nihilistic, not death-seeking. They are instead deeply, almost incomprehensibly committed to presence here, to continuing the work, to maintaining these machines in this landscape. When they talk about the valley—rare, brief—there's something in their voices that isn't quite love and isn't quite duty but exists in between, a recognition of being the right organism for this particular ecological niche, of having found or created a role that suits something in them that wouldn't suit anywhere else.

New Engineers are not recruited so much as self-selected through a process of exposure and failure-to-leave. Occasionally someone comes up from below—usually someone with mechanical background, someone who's heard rumors, someone looking for work or solitude or escape. They're not turned away, but neither are they welcomed. They're ignored, allowed to follow if they want, allowed to attempt assistance if they can figure out what assistance means here. Most leave within days, defeated by the cold moisture, the constant sound, the complete absence of social infrastructure. Some stay weeks, trying, but eventually recognize that they're not adapting, not developing the particular sensory attunements or physical tolerances required. Very rarely, someone doesn't leave. They're still there after a month, after two months, and at some point one of the existing Engineers will hand them a tool during a repair, a simple gesture that constitutes acknowledgment, and if they use it correctly, if they prove they can learn the machines and tolerate the landscape, they've become part of the dispersed network. Naming might come later or might not.

There's a woman—call her Senna, though she's never confirmed this—who's perhaps three years into her tenure, still learning her circuit, still occasionally making errors that more experienced Engineers have to correct. She maintains a cluster of installations on the eastern slope, six machines in a pattern that requires significant climbing between them. She's developing Chen's comfort on stone faces but hasn't achieved it yet; you can see the hesitation in her movements, the moments where fear has to be consciously overridden. The older Engineers watch her progress with what might be approval or might be simple assessment of whether she'll survive long enough to become useful. She's begun her own notebook, mimicking Vos's practice, though her entries are longer, more descriptive, not yet pared down to the essential data. Give her another five years and she'll either be indistinguishable from the others or she'll be gone.

They have no visible hierarchy, no leader, no decision-making structure beyond individual judgment applied to specific situations. When two Engineers encounter a problem that requires coordination—a repair needing two sets of hands, a cable that must be tensioned from both ends simultaneously—they work together with minimal communication, moving through the task with practiced efficiency, then separating again without discussion. There are no arguments, but this seems less because of harmony and more because of isolation; you cannot argue with someone you see once every two weeks for thirty minutes. Disagreements, when they occur, are apparently resolved through individual action. If Kiet thinks a machine should be run differently than Marit does, they each implement their approach on their respective equipment, and whichever approach succeeds becomes informally adopted by others. Natural selection applied to maintenance philosophy.

Their relationship to the outside world—to whatever society exists in lower elevations, to the possibility of employers or authorities or families—is obscure. They receive supplies, so someone is funding this, someone is manufacturing the soap and oats and replacement gaskets that arrive in those monthly packs. But whether the Engineers are employees in good standing, abandoned workers still showing up to a forgotten posting, or something else entirely, they don't clarify. Vos might know; his notebooks might contain contact information, organizational charts, budget documents. But he's not sharing, and the others don't seem to need that information, content to exist in the continuous present tense of maintenance work and moss observation.

Once—witnessed but unexplained—two of them met at the bridge, the old brick-and-steel structure crossing the main drainage. Marit from the west, Chen from the east, their routes intersecting at sunset or what passes for sunset under cloud cover, a gradual dimming of the omnidirectional light. They stopped on opposite ends of the bridge, perhaps forty feet apart, and for maybe five minutes they just stood there, looking at each other or past each other or at the valley itself. No words exchanged, no gestures. Then Marit reached into her coat and withdrew something small—it glinted, metal, might have been a harmonica—and played a single note, held it for several seconds, let it release into the sound of water and wind. Chen responded not with sound but by raising one hand, palm out, acknowledgment or greeting or goodbye. Then both continued their routes, crossing the bridge in opposite directions, not slowing as they passed. Ritual, communication, shared moment—whatever it was, it was sufficient, and then it was over.

They know things about this landscape that aren't written anywhere, aren't transferable through language. Which moss species indicate stone stability. How the sound changes twelve hours before a significant storm. Where the water table sits relative to the moss surface, and how that relationship shifts seasonally. Which machines can be left unattended for weeks and which require daily attention. The safe routes through areas where the moss conceals deep voids. The particular feel of a machine that's about to fail catastrophically versus one that's just complaining. This knowledge exists in their bodies, in their walking patterns, in the unconscious adjustments they make while moving through terrain that would be impassible to outsiders.

And they know, though they don't discuss it, that they are becoming part of the place in a way that might not be reversible. That the moisture is working into them, that their breathing has adapted to the thin air and constant dampness, that their hearing has shifted range to accommodate the sound environment. That leaving might now be physiologically difficult, might require a decompression period their bodies wouldn't tolerate. Whether this is actually true or just a story they tell themselves to justify staying, they act as if it's true, which makes it functionally true.

There are suggestions—half-glimpsed, never confirmed—that some who came before are still here but no longer recognizable as Engineers or people. A figure seen at extreme distance on the northern peak, moving wrong, too fluid or too angular. A presence felt near certain machines at certain times, a sensation of being watched by something that understands machinery from the inside. Stories Vos might tell late on the rare occasions when more than two Engineers share a shelter, stories about the earliest maintainers who stayed too long, who learned too much about what the moss could do, who made treaties with the mountain itself and remained to honor them in forms that gradually stopped being human. Whether these stories are literal truth, metaphor for psychological dissolution, or just entertainment passed around to fill the rare social moments, they clearly hold meaning for the Engineers. They affect behavior—certain machines are approached with more caution, certain areas are avoided during specific conditions, small offerings are occasionally left: a clean bearing, a good wrench, things a ghost-engineer might appreciate.

When they die—and they must die, eventually, from injury or exhaustion or simple time—there's no evidence of burial, no markers beyond perhaps those small cairns Chen leaves, which might be for the living or might be for someone else. Bodies probably return to the moss, the quickest and most practical solution. Or they're carried down to lower elevations by the supply hikers, returned to whatever society exists below for whatever protocols that society maintains. Or—the possibility hangs unspoken—they don't die exactly, but transition into something else, becoming part of the system they've maintained, their awareness diffusing into the network of machines and moss and water, continuing their work in forms that no longer require food or sleep or intact fingers.

The Engineers don't think about this, or claim they don't. They think about flow rates and bearing temperatures and moss advancement. They think about whether the northern steam release is early today and what that might indicate about pressure below. They think about their next shelter point, their next machine, the specific sound they heard from a pump station yesterday that suggests investigation today. They think in mechanical time, problem-solution cycles, the endless iterative loop of maintenance that has no completion, only continuation.

But sometimes, on the platform, standing at the vantage point overlooking the central valley where machines dot the green like dark punctuation, breathing the dense air while water whispers and engines chug and the mountain imperceptibly rises beneath it all—sometimes they stop. They stand there, these Engineers, these maintainers, these inhabitants of New Engines. They stand in their worn coats, their damaged hands loose at their sides, and they look at what they tend. At the moss doing its work of binding and building. At the machines doing their work of moving and measuring. At the whole bright, loud, wet, alive system doing whatever work it's doing, creating whatever it's creating, becoming whatever it will become.

And if in those moments they feel something beyond duty, beyond function, beyond the pragmatic satisfaction of keeping broken things running—if they feel something like participation in a process larger than machinery or ecology, something like being cells in an organism that's learning to think through moss and iron and the patient attention of people who've given up everything else to be here—they don't speak it. They just check their watches, if they still carry watches that can survive the moisture. They note the time in their internal schedules. They turn from the platform and walk back into the green, toward the next machine on the circuit, toward the work that continues, that must continue, that they are now part of in ways that might extend beyond their individual lives into something stranger and more permanent.

The sound swallows them as they descend. The moss accepts their weight. The machines mark time with their steady rhythms. And the Engineers continue, dispersed and patient, these eleven or seven or sixteen people who live in the way that moss lives, that water lives, that mountains live—constantly, essentially, constituting the place through their presence, through their work, through the simple fact of being exactly what this landscape requires to maintain its loud, luminous becoming.
