# What Lives in the Hollows

There is a woman, though calling her a woman requires you to decide she is separate from the stone, and this becomes less certain the longer she remains.

She lives in the largest basin, the one with the spiral of river stones at its bottom. Not beside it or near it—in it. When the hollow fills with rain, she fills with it. When it empties over weeks of evaporation, she empties too, slowly, her substance concentrating like the tannin-stained water, leaving her own rings in the limestone, marks that overlap with the mineral deposits until they cannot be distinguished.

She has been here long enough that her original arrival is a story she no longer tells herself, if she ever did. There was no dramatic hermitage, no retreat from catastrophe. She simply failed to leave one day, and then another day, and then the question of leaving became so abstract it lost all meaning. The stone field is not large, but it is sufficient. She knows the capacity of each hollow by heart and by body—which fill first, which hold longest, which water tastes different because of what trace minerals the stone yields up.

Her form is difficult to fix in description because it shifts with the water cycle. After heavy rain, when the basins brim and the stone darkens with saturation, she is most solid: skin, limbs, a face that could be recognized, a body that takes up definite space. She moves between the hollows then, stepping carefully on the ridges as anyone would, using her hands for balance. She drinks from the smaller basins, leaving them empty. She fills containers that might be pottery or might be naturally formed stone cups—the distinction has eroded. She lies in the largest basin, floating, staring up at the diffuse sky, her hair spreading on the amber water like dark weed.

But as the weeks pass without rain, as the water level drops and the air pulls moisture from stone and basin both, she becomes less definite. Her edges soften first. The precise line where her skin ends and air begins grows uncertain, like she is drawn in charcoal that someone has smudged with a thumb. Then her substance thins. You could see through her, perhaps, if there were anyone to look—see through to the limestone beneath, to the water she lies in, as if she is made of the same stained transparency.

In the deepest droughts, when only the largest basin holds a hand's depth of water concentrated to dark tea, she is barely there at all. A presence. A density in the air above the basin. Something that might be the heat shimmer except the temperature doesn't vary enough for heat shimmer. She cannot move far from water then, or she disperses. She stays above the basin, or sometimes spreads thin across all the hollows at once, a film of awareness stretched so fine it is mostly gap with only occasional threads of something that remembers being human.

Then the rain comes, and she collects herself again, drawing back from the scattered hollows, condensing as the basins fill. It takes hours. First a thickening in the air, then a form that looks like a woman made of fog, then increasing opacity until she is solid enough to touch, if there were anyone to touch her. The process has happened so many times she no longer experiences it as transformation, only as the natural breathing the place does, and she is part of that breath.

She does not eat, or what she eats is limestone and water and the sparse minerals dissolved in both. She does not sleep, or she is always sleeping, or the distinction has no meaning when consciousness dilutes and concentrates with rainfall. She is never tired and never fully awake. She exists in the same mild, diffuse state as the light here, present but not insistent, aware but not urgently so.

Memory works differently for her now. The past before the hollows—if there was a before, and sometimes she doubts this—exists as a kind of inherited story, something she knows the way the stone knows it was once a sea floor. True but not relevant. Recent memory is more reliable: the last rain was three weeks ago, the basin before her holds perhaps forty gallons, the twisted tree bloomed five days past and the flowers have mostly fallen.

But memory also pools in the hollows themselves. Not metaphorically—literally. The water holds what has happened to it, and when she drinks or floats in or becomes part of that water, she knows its history. This basin last filled seven weeks ago, before that three months dry, before that a brief rain that barely covered the spiral stones. She knows this the way you know the taste of something familiar, immediate and wordless. The mineral rings are a text she reads by touching, each line a season or year, the spacing between them telling stories of drought and abundance in a language of calcium.

She maintains the small built things, though maintenance might be the wrong word. She sits on the low wall, and her weight—when she has weight—keeps it from shifting further. She crosses the threshold stone regularly, going from nowhere to nowhere, and her feet—when she has feet—wear it smooth in the same central dish that was already there, continuing the work of whoever placed it. She placed it, perhaps, though she doesn't remember this. Or she has always been here and the threshold stone has always been here and questions of origin are someone else's concern.

The spiral in the largest basin requires more active care. When water level permits, when she is solid enough to work underwater, she adjusts the stones. Not because they have moved—they rarely move—but because the pattern must be maintained. She cannot explain why. The spiral simply needs to be right, the spacing correct, the curve true. She knows the rightness by feel, by the way the water sits differently when the stones are properly arranged. She has adjusted them thousands of times, the same small corrections, an endless perfecting of something that was already perfect.

Sometimes she speaks, though there is no one to speak to. Her voice is quiet, the same volume as the dripping water, the same tone as wind across a hollow's mouth. She speaks to the stone mostly, telling it things it already knows: that rain is coming, that the water is especially amber this season, that the twisted tree has flowered again. The stone does not answer, but neither does it ignore her. The conversation is simply what happens, like evaporation, like dissolution.

She does not speak about loneliness because loneliness requires a framework she no longer inhabits. The place is not empty to her. The stone is company. The water is company. The sparse plants are visitors she knows well, permanent guests who maintain the same comfortable distance year after year. The twisted tree is the closest thing to a friend, if friendship can exist without speech or shared experience, defined only by proximity and persistent mutual existence.

Once, there were others. Or she believes there were others. The wall suggests collaboration, or at least ambition beyond what one person would attempt alone. The threshold stone feels like it was placed with ceremony, with multiple people acknowledging a boundary together. But the others—if they existed—have gone so long ago that even the stone has forgotten them. Or they became the stone. Or they were never real, only versions of herself from when she held her form differently, thought of herself as multiple, had not yet learned she was singular and plural simultaneously.

She does not age, or ages so slowly that it matches the weathering of limestone. Her hair might be longer than it was decades ago, or shorter—there is no mirror here except basin water, and that shows her only in fragments, distorted, mixed with sky. Her face might have more lines, or the lines might be shadows, or she might have no face at all in the weeks when she is most dispersed. The question seems unimportant. What matters is that she continues, cycling with the water, present when presence is possible, absent when absence is required.

Her needs are minimal and met. Water comes often enough. Warmth is constant. The stone provides surface to rest on when she is solid, space to dissipate into when she is not. She requires nothing that the place does not offer, has shaped herself—or been shaped—to fit exactly the life that can be sustained here, like the succulent plants, like the single tree, taking what is given and asking nothing more.

But there is one thing she does that exceeds mere maintenance, one gesture toward something beyond survival. In the smallest hollows—the ones that hold only cupfuls, that fill and empty within hours—she places single seeds. Not often. Perhaps once a season, when she finds a seed blown in from the surrounding plain, carried by wind or the rare bird passing through. She places it in a small basin and waits to see if it will germinate.

They almost never do. The water is too temporary, the stone too poor in nutrients, the conditions too marginal. The seeds swell and soften and then dry and die when the hollow empties. She removes them once they are clearly dead, carries them to the edge of the stone field, and leaves them in the soil there. Then she waits for another seed.

Twice—or perhaps three times, her memory is uncertain—a seed has sprouted. A pale shoot reaching up from dark water, two small leaves unfurling. She tends these with focused attention, using her hands when she has hands to shade the seedling from the strongest midday sun, rationing water to the hollow when rain does not come, choosing to let herself disperse further so the plant can have the moisture.

They have all died anyway. The largest survivor lived perhaps two months, grew four inches tall, produced six leaves. Then a week without rain and without sufficient morning condensation, and the hollow dried completely, and the seedling withered despite her efforts. She grieved this, or experienced something like grief—a feeling that water alone could not wash away, that persisted through several cycles of filling and emptying before finally dissolving into the general patience of the place.

Still, she continues to plant seeds when she finds them. The action has become ritual, observance, prayer to no god except continuity itself. Each seed is an assertion that change is possible, even here, even in a place that has perfected its own changelessness. That something new could take root. That the constellation of life in the stone field could expand by one.

She does not hope, exactly—hope requires a kind of forward tension that the hollows have dissolved from her. But she maintains the practice, and the practice itself is a kind of faith, though faith in what is unclear. Perhaps in seeds. Perhaps in the idea that tending matters even when it fails. Perhaps in nothing at all, the gesture empty but continued because continuation is what she does, who she is.

When she is most solid, most herself in a form others would recognize as human, there are moments when she remembers or imagines a different life. A life of walls and roofs, of other people, of change that happens quickly enough to notice, of time that progresses instead of cycling. These memories or imaginings are not painful, not pleasant—they are like the salt in the air, faint enough to question, persistent enough to acknowledge. A background she comes from, or might come from, or invented.

She cannot picture returning to such a life. The thought is impossible, like a stone imagining becoming water instead of dissolved by it. She has learned the rhythm of the hollows, or been taught by them, or always knew it and simply recognized it here. The slow breathing of accumulation and release. The way presence and absence are not opposites but phases. The rightness of existing at the edge of solid and dissolved, human and landscape, self and stone.

There are nights—and there are nights here, despite the minimal change between day and dark—when she lies in the largest basin and feels the limestone beneath her, the water around her, and cannot tell which is her body and which is the place. Her edges dissolve not because she is dispersing but because the boundaries were always arbitrary. She is the water in the hollows. She is the dampness in the porous stone. She is the patient chemistry of calcium slowly giving itself to rain. She is the memory written in mineral rings, the thought held in the spiral of river stones, the intention that placed the threshold and built the wall.

And then morning comes with its gradual brightening, and she is separate again, or separate enough—a woman sitting in a basin, legs folded, looking at her hands, which are definitely her hands, solid and distinct. She stands. She steps from the basin onto the limestone. She walks to the twisted tree and touches its silver bark. She checks the small hollows for seeds. She adjusts one stone in the spiral, a minor correction. She sits on the wall and watches the diffuse light move across the stone field, changing nothing but shifting the shadows incrementally, and this is enough.

This is enough, and will remain enough, through seasons that barely distinguish themselves, through years that pass like water through limestone—present, transforming, leaving marks but no wounds. She will continue. The hollows will continue. The water will fill and empty. The stone will hold and release. And somewhere in that pattern, neither living nor not-living, neither staying nor leaving, she endures, the single sustained thought the place thinks about itself, the awareness the limestone has developed after enough centuries of patient dissolution, the answer to a question no one asked about what inhabits the space between solid and liquid, presence and absence, form and flow.

She is there now. She will be there tomorrow. She was there yesterday, and the day before, and the day before that, stretching back beyond the wall's building, beyond the threshold's placing, beyond the first spiral stone, back to whenever she arrived, which was always, which was never, which was exactly the moment the hollows needed someone to drink their water and tell the stone what it already knows and plant seeds that will not grow and adjust the spiral and cross the threshold and sit on the wall and breathe with the place, slowly, the way limestone breathes with the sea, with moisture, with time.

She is what lives there. She is how the hollows live. She is the life that place permits—minimal, sufficient, sustained, dissolved and reformed with each rain, present as long as water cycles through stone, which is to say: indefinitely, patiently, without end or urgency or need for anything more than this: stone, water, warmth, dampness, sparse life, dim light, deep silence, and her, and her, and her, cycling through her forms like the seasons that barely distinguish themselves, endless as limestone's long conversation with water, as patient as erosion, as permanent as anything that accepts its own impermanence, as real as thirst, as true as dissolution, as alive as the slow chemistry of stone acknowledging rain.
