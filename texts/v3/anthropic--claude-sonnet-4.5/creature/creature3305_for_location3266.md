# The Brick Voices (Inhabitants)

They are called the **Seepers**, though they don't call themselves anything in particular—that name comes from surface-dwellers who sometimes trade with them, who've noticed how they smell of wet clay and how water seems to follow them, darkening their clothes in maps of moisture.

There are eleven of them currently. The number fluctuates—has been as few as four, as many as sixteen. They're not a family, not exactly, though some are related. They're people who have *fit themselves* to this place, or whom this place has fitted to itself. The selection process is organic, unspoken. Some people come down the brick shaft and feel immediately claustrophobic, oppressed by the dense walls and layered sounds. Others descend and feel, for the first time, *held*.

The Seepers are small-statured, though whether they came here small or whether the space has shaped them over generations is unclear. The tallest among them would barely reach your shoulder. They move through the narrow passages with an efficiency that's startling—turning sideways without thinking, ducking under low archways without looking up, knowing exactly where to place their feet among the floor's ceramic mosaic. They navigate as much by sound and air-feel as by sight. In darkness, they're barely slower.

Their skin tends toward gray-brown, but it's hard to know what's complexion and what's clay. They're always slightly dusty with brick powder, and their hands—their hands are extraordinary. Thickened palms, strong fingers, nails worn short and stained rust-red from iron-rich clay. Every adult has calluses in the same places: the outer edge of the palm from smoothing mortar, the fingertips from testing brick surfaces, the heels of both hands from tamping clay. Their hands are shaped by the same work that shaped this place.

They wear simple clothes: wrapped garments made from a coarse plant fiber, dyed with clay in shades of rust and ochre and gray. The wrapping style is practical—it can be adjusted, tightened or loosened, exposing arms for work or covering the head when working near the oven. The fabric holds moisture, stays slightly damp, and they seem comfortable with this. They smell like the chamber itself: wet earth, smoke, the sharp green of crushed herbs they tuck into their clothes. Some of them have bird feathers woven into their wraps—the white-throated birds' feathers, small and brown with that distinctive white marking.

**Maker Tessit** is the eldest currently here—maybe sixty years, her exact age uncertain. She's been in these chambers for forty-three years; she counts by the major floods, which come every five to seven years when the rains above are exceptional. She came down as a young woman fleeing something on the surface she's never spoken about. The chambers accepted her. Her hands are the most transformed—the fingers slightly curved even at rest, shaped by a lifetime of pressing mortar, smoothing joints, testing moisture in brick. She can touch a wall and tell you when the rain last fell, how hard, and whether it will rain again soon. She's the one who maintains the water channels, who knows every pipe and drain in this chamber and the three adjacent ones.

Tessit's hair is white, kept short (long hair is impractical here—it catches on rough brick, holds moisture). Her eyes are filmed slightly with age, but she navigates the chamber flawlessly by touch and sound. She's teaching **Piri**, a girl of maybe twelve, though the teaching is mostly silent—Piri following, watching, occasionally receiving a quiet correction: "Not that brick. Feel—it's too wet still. This one." Tessit hears the water as music; she's been known to sit motionless for an hour, just listening, then suddenly move to adjust a drain cover or reposition a basin, fine-tuning the chamber's acoustic.

**Kob and Danu** are brothers, middle-aged, who came here together fifteen years ago from a surface settlement that had become too rigid, too rule-bound for them. They're the primary builders—they make the bricks. Every eight days they ascend to the surface before dawn, go to the clay deposits near the river (a two-hour walk), dig clay, shape bricks in wooden molds, leave them to dry in the sun, then return the next day to carry down as many as they can manage. Each brick weighs about five pounds. They can carry perhaps thirty between them. It takes many trips to gather enough bricks for even a small wall. 

Kob is stockier, with a scar across his left forearm from a brick-kiln accident years ago. Danu is leaner, with a nervous habit of clicking his tongue against his teeth—a rhythm that matches the dripping water. They work in near-silence, having perfected a wordless coordination. One will hold bricks while the other mortars; they'll switch roles without discussion. They're currently building a new interior wall, subdividing one of the sleeping areas to create more niches—the community is expecting new arrivals, maybe, or someone wants more privacy. The wall grows slowly: three courses in a good day.

**Nirit** tends the birds. She's perhaps thirty, been here eleven years. She was born on the surface but spent her childhood descending into various cisterns and underground spaces with her mother, who was a water-finder. Nirit has an affinity for the white-throated birds that seems almost supernatural. She can make a soft whistling call, and they'll land on her outstretched arms, sometimes four or five at once. She monitors their nests, removes dead nestlings, occasionally takes an egg if a clutch is too large (the eggs are tiny, eaten boiled, a delicacy). She's figured out that the birds eat certain insects that would otherwise damage the walls—they hunt beetle larvae that bore into mortar. The birds are partners, not pets. In return for safe nesting, they provide pest control.

Nirit speaks to the birds in a language she's invented—not hoping they'll understand, but as a way of *attending* to them. "Bright-throat, you're favoring that wing. Injury or molt?" She's learned to read their behavior: when they become agitated, she checks the water channels (sometimes a blockage changes the sound-pattern, and the birds react before humans notice). She moves through the chamber with birds often following her, hopping from wall to wall in her wake. Her clothes have more feathers woven in than anyone else's—gifts, she says, from the birds themselves, though really she collects them from the floor.

**Sulit** manages the oven and the food. He's a young man, mid-twenties, who came here three years ago after his surface family's bakery burned. He couldn't bear open spaces after that, couldn't bear open flame. But the enclosed oven here, the controlled fire, the brick walls holding and limiting the heat—that he could manage. He bakes flat bread from grain that's traded from above: the Seepers produce bricks and ceramic vessels to trade for food staples they can't grow underground. Sulit also experiments. He's figured out how to cultivate mushrooms on damp bricks in the darkest niches. He grows pale, thin-capped fungi that taste like earth and have a slight luminescence—you can see them faintly glowing in the deeper darkness. Not enough to read by, but enough to mark a passage.

Sulit is quiet, intense. He watches the oven the way Nirit watches the birds: constantly, devotedly. He knows the chamber's temperature zones intimately because cooking requires it. He hangs herbs to dry in the warm zones, stores grain in the dry zones, keeps his starter culture for bread in a sealed jar in a zone that's cool but not cold, humid but not wet. He rarely goes to the surface—twice a year, maybe. The chambers are his recovery, his healing. His hands, which once shook, are steady here.

**Obal** is the trader. He's maybe forty, heavy-set (unusual for Seepers), with a loud voice that he modulates down here to avoid disturbing the acoustic. He ascends every ten days or so, carrying ceramic vessels—bowls, jars, cups—that the Seepers make from the same clay they use for bricks. He trades these for grain, oil for the lamps, metal tools (precious, rare), rope, the wooden pegs they use for hanging things, dried fruit, occasionally honey. He's the community's connection to the surface world, and he brings back news, though he edits it—he's learned that too much surface information disturbs the chamber's peace.

Obal is gregarious by Seeper standards, which means he sometimes speaks in full sentences. He's been here twenty years, came originally as a trader who got caught in the chamber during a rain and decided to stay. He maintains relationships with three different surface settlements, knows the current politics, the harvest conditions, the prices. But his heart is here. He returns from each trading trip visibly relieved, descending the brick spiral with a sigh you can hear, his shoulders dropping as the enclosed space receives him.

**Yarit and Shem** are lovers, have been for six years. Yarit is a woman of perhaps thirty-five, Shem a person whose gender is ambiguous and apparently irrelevant to them and everyone else. They manage the lamps—the oil supply, the wick trimming, the placement and lighting. Twelve lamps burn continuously; others are lit as needed. Yarit and Shem move through the chamber twice daily (morning and evening by bird-song, not by sun) adjusting wicks, adding oil, cleaning soot from niches. They've optimized lamp placement over years: this corner needs more light for detailed work, this passage can be dimmer, this alcove benefits from shadow.

They sleep in adjacent niches but often work separately, meeting at the central gathering place to compare observations. "The lamp near Tessit's workbench is smoking more—the wick needs replacement." "The birds avoided the northeast passage today—I think the lamp there needs repositioning; the smoke is bothering them." They communicate in shorthand, often just gestures. Shem has beautiful hands—long fingers, precise movements—and sometimes makes shadow-figures on the walls for the children (there are children, though only Piri lives here full-time; others visit from the surface).

**Mirn** is the newest adult, been here eight months. She's maybe twenty, still adjusting. She came from a surface settlement where she'd been a potter, and she'd heard about the Seepers through Obal. She arrived curious about the ceramic work, the bricks. She's learning, but she's still learning her *body* in this space—she bumps into walls, misjudges clearances, sometimes freezes in the narrower passages with panic rising. The others are patient. Tessit particularly has taken responsibility for her, will appear beside her in moments of difficulty: "Breathe. Feel the wall. It's holding you, not trapping you."

Mirn is tall for a Seeper (she'll always be tall here), with long limbs she hasn't fully coordinated to the space. But her potter's hands are adapting quickly to brick-work. She's fascinated by the firing process—how some bricks come out the color of rust, others gray, depending on kiln position and oxygen. She wants to experiment, create bricks with patterns, with colors. The others are mildly skeptical (bricks are functional, not decorative) but willing to let her try. She represents possibility, change, the slow evolution of practice.

**Torun** is perhaps fifty, has been here nineteen years. He's the one who maintains the marks on the walls—the tallies, the pictographs, the records. He's not a scribe (there's no formal writing system here), but he's a *noticer*. He adds a mark each time the birds complete a breeding cycle. He records floods, droughts, structural changes. He's created a visual history of the chamber using a piece of charcoal and later, sometimes, white clay for contrast. His marks cover sections of wall near the gathering place—bands of tally marks, stylized birds, wavy lines representing water-years.

Torun rarely speaks but sometimes hums—melodies that match the chamber's dripping rhythms. He has a theory, which he's shared with Tessit, that the chamber itself is *alive*, that the water flowing through it is like blood, the air moving through passages like breath. The inhabitants are something like thoughts, he says, or dreams the chamber is having. Tessit neither agrees nor disagrees; she finds the idea comforting regardless of its truth.

**Piri**, the child, is twelve and has lived here for two years. Her mother still lives on the surface but understood that Piri was suffering there—too much noise, too much openness, too much chaos. Piri is what surface people might call touched or strange. She doesn't speak, or speaks so rarely that each word is an event. But she's deeply happy here. She follows Tessit most days, learning water-work. She has a collection of special stones that she's arranged in one of the niches, and she rearranges them daily in patterns that might be random or might be deeply significant. She's the one who tends the moss, who seems to know which cracks should stay moist.

The lizards tolerate Piri in a way they don't tolerate most humans. She can sit motionless for an hour, and they'll hunt on the wall beside her, sometimes on her. She's had a lizard sleep in her palm. She moves through the chamber like water, finding the path of least resistance, never forcing. The adults protect her fiercely, though she doesn't seem to need protection. The chamber has claimed her, or she's claimed it. She'll likely be here the rest of her life, will likely become something like Tessit—an elder who knows every brick, every drip, every bird.

---

They eat together once daily, in the evening (by bird-song), gathering at the central place near the oven. Sulit brings bread, sometimes mushrooms, sometimes a stew made from dried vegetables and grain. They eat from ceramic bowls, sitting on the worn-smooth benches or on the floor. They don't talk much during meals—the sounds of eating, the sounds of the chamber, the birds settling for night, are enough. Occasionally someone will mention a plan: "Tomorrow Kob and I will work on the north wall." "The drain near Yarit's niche needs clearing." Practical matters, briefly stated.

After eating, there's often a quiet time where they simply sit. Sometimes Torun hums. Sometimes someone tells a brief story—a memory from the surface, a dream, an observation about the birds or lizards. Nothing elaborate. The chamber doesn't encourage elaboration; it encourages presence, attention, the noticing of small things.

They sleep scattered through the chamber, each in their niche, wrapped in their damp clothes, on reed mats that smell like river plants. The lamps burn low through the night. The birds go silent except for occasional sleepy chirps. The water keeps dripping, the percussion continuing through darkness. The lizards hunt in lamplight. The chamber breathes, settles, holds its people in brick and moisture and accumulated warmth.

They dream, probably, though they rarely share dreams. What do you dream in a place like this? Brick, likely. Water finding its path. Birds in infinite niches. The weight and texture of clay. The satisfaction of a joint well-mortared. The sound of the chamber humming its low, constant note.

In the morning (by bird-song), they wake gradually. No alarm, no sudden transition. The birds' volume increases, and the Seepers stir, stretch in their niches, emerge. Yarit and Shem light the lamps that were extinguished overnight. Sulit revives the oven's fire from banked coals. Tessit checks the water channels—the night always brings changes in flow. The work begins again: small, patient, continuous.

They're people who have chosen enclosure, or whom enclosure has chosen. People for whom the surface world was too bright, too chaotic, too *loose*. Here, everything is close, defined, manageable. The walls press in, but the pressing is a comfort. The moisture is constant, but constancy is a kind of freedom. The sounds are complex, but complexity becomes familiar, becomes home.

They've learned to read the chamber the way surface-dwellers read sky. A change in the drip-pattern means rain coming. A shift in how the birds vocalize means temperature change. A particular smell from a certain wall means new growth (moss or mold) that needs attention. The chamber tells them what it needs, and they respond, and the chamber holds them in return.

They're not fleeing, exactly—or not anymore, those who came here in flight. They're *inhabiting*. They've made a world at human scale, at hand's reach, where everything can be touched and known and maintained. Where the work is endless but never overwhelming, because it's broken into brick-sized pieces, task by task, day by day.

When someone dies here (two have died in the current group's memory), they're carried up the spiral shaft and buried on the surface, returned to the world above. But they leave marks below: a handprint in white clay, their specific niche empty for a respectful time before being reclaimed. The chamber remembers them in wear patterns, in walls they built, in the continuing effects of their accumulated decisions.

When someone new comes—arrives tentatively down the shaft, uncertain—the existing Seepers watch quietly. Either the person will feel the chamber accept them, or they won't. It's not something that can be forced or explained. Some people descend, look around with increasing panic, and retreat quickly. Others—like Mirn, like Piri, like Nirit years ago—descend and their shoulders drop and they *breathe* and you can see the settling happen, the fitting-in.

The Seepers live at a different pace than the surface. Not slower, exactly, but more rhythmic. Measured by water and birds and brick. A wall takes the time it takes. Bread bakes at its own speed. The chamber's moisture patterns follow rain that falls above, but down here the changes are gradual, buffered by brick and clay. Everything is filtered through the medium of the place itself.

They're people who have learned to love limits, who find freedom in constraints. The surface world offers infinite space and infinite choice, which for some people is paralyzing, unbearable. Here, the choices are clear: this brick or that one, this joint needs work, this channel needs clearing. The work is specific, physical, comprehensible.

They've built a world inside a world, a dense intricate shelter in the earth's darkness, and they maintain it with their hands and their attention and their accumulated knowledge of how water moves, how brick endures, how birds nest, how lizards hunt, how moss grows, how light and shadow and sound interweave in enclosed space.

They are the Seepers, and they seep through their days like water through brick—persistent, patient, finding every gap and filling it, becoming part of the structure itself, indistinguishable from the place they've made and that has made them.

The chamber hums, and they hum with it.
