# Those Who Live on the Breathing Plateau

## The Moss Herders

They move with the caribou-creatures but are not them. Bipedal, yes—humanoid in basic architecture—but shaped by generations at elevation, by lives lived in constant cool and damp. Their bodies are compact, broad-chested, built like compressed springs. Short-statured, most standing perhaps 150 centimeters, but this isn't diminishment—it's optimization. Their lungs are barrel-large, their hearts slightly oversized, their blood rich with hemoglobin adapted to pulling oxygen from thin air without thought or effort.

Their skin has a quality that initially seems like weathering but reveals itself as something more intentional. A faint grayish cast, slightly waxy, water-shedding. Not scaled, not furred, but textured in a way that makes moisture bead and run rather than soak. Touch one—if you could, if they allowed it—and their skin would feel cool, slightly damp, like stone that has learned to breathe. Some individuals show patches of what might be lichen or might be symbiotic growth, pale green or rust-orange staining at elbows and shoulders, and they don't remove it.

Their hair grows thick and slightly oily, dark brown or black or occasionally a startling rust-red. They wear it long or bound, practical, kept away from faces. It sheds water like the pelt of a river animal. Their eyes are dark—deep brown mostly, occasionally amber—and set in faces that carry a weathered quality regardless of age, as if the plateau writes itself into everyone equally. High cheekbones, broad noses adapted for warming cold air, lips that crack less easily than yours would.

They dress in layers of their own making: wool from the caribou-creatures, felted and dense, dyed in vegetable colors that mirror the plateau itself—moss green, sedge brown, lichen orange, stone gray. The garments are pragmatic, loose enough for movement through dense vegetation, tight enough not to catch. Hoods are common, deep ones that can be pulled forward against mist. Everything they wear shows signs of continuous repair, patches layered on patches, edges re-bound with sinew or plant fiber, creating garments with biographical depth, where you can read years of maintenance in the archaeological layers of mending.

Their boots are extraordinary—the product of generations solving the problem of wet ground. Thick caribou hide, treated with oils and resins, soled with something that includes moss fiber for grip, shaped broad to distribute weight. They seal at the calf with drawstrings. The boots need constant maintenance, and you'll often see individuals working on them during rest stops, re-oiling, re-stitching, checking the integrity of seams, because a failed boot at distance on the plateau is not inconvenience but crisis.

They speak a language that sounds like wind in sedge—soft, full of sustained consonants, with tonal elements that rise and fall like terrain. It carries well in the open but doesn't travel far—perfectly calibrated to the distances at which people interact here. They seem to have limited volume range, speaking in what would be normal conversation tones to you but rarely raising their voices to shout. Perhaps shouting is simply impractical across the plateau's vastness, or perhaps it disturbs something they prefer not to disturb.

Their communities are not villages. No permanent structures, no fixed locations. Instead, they practice a kind of disciplined nomadism, moving in groups of twenty to forty individuals, following routes that take years to complete. They trail the caribou-creatures but at a distance, parallel rather than pursuing, taking animals occasionally but never pressuring the herds. Their routes are determined by something more complex than simple hunting—they seem to be visiting places, checking on things, maintaining relationships with specific locations on the plateau.

When they stop for more than a night, they create temporary installations rather than camps. Windbreaks of stacked stone, carefully placed to avoid disrupting water flow. Hide shelters stretched between dwarf willow thickets, using the vegetation as framework rather than cutting it. Sleeping platforms of woven sedge raised slightly above the wet ground. These installations can be surprisingly elaborate if the stop is for weeks rather than days—cooking areas with efficient fire pits that burn dried caribou dung mixed with sedge peat, seating areas, even separate spaces for different activities—but they're designed for complete disassembly, for leaving minimal trace.

And they do leave minimal trace. Watch them break camp and you witness a kind of careful undoing: stones returned to arrangements that look natural, hide shelters packed away, sleeping platforms scattered so the sedge can spring back, fire pits dismantled and the ashes spread thin, mixed with water until they disappear into the general dampness. Within days of their departure, the plateau has largely reclaimed the site. Within weeks, you'd need to know exactly where to look to find evidence of their presence.

They practice something that initially seems like hunting but reveals itself as more complicated. When they take a caribou-creature, it's done with ceremony—not religious exactly, but procedural, formal, a set of actions performed in sequence as if there were rules. The kill itself is quick, done with spears that seem almost too well-made, the points knapped from stone with extraordinary skill or occasionally fashioned from salvaged metal from the industrial ruins, the shafts perfectly straight and balanced. But after the kill, they perform actions that suggest obligation rather than celebration: they arrange the bones in specific patterns, they leave portions of the animal for scavengers in deliberate locations, they mark the kill site with a small cairn of stones.

They use everything. The hide becomes clothing, shelter, containers, rope. The sinew becomes thread, bowstring, bindings. The bones become tools, needles, knife handles, decorative elements. The organs are eaten or rendered for oils. The hooves are boiled for glue. They work efficiently but without waste-anxiety, as if the abundance of the plateau is understood and trusted but not taken for granted.

Their diet is more varied than you'd expect. Yes, caribou meat—dried, smoked over peat fires until it becomes leather-like jerky that stores indefinitely in the cool damp. But also: berries collected in season and preserved in rendered fat, creating pemmican variations. Sedge roots, dug carefully and replanted, eaten raw or dried. Certain mushrooms that fruit in the moss fields. The eggs of ground-nesting birds, taken sparingly. Fish from the streams when routes bring them to larger waterways. They seem to have extensive knowledge of what the plateau offers, where different resources appear in different seasons, how much can be taken without diminishing future availability.

They carry their lives with them in packs of remarkable construction—frames made from caribou antler and willow wood, bags of hide and felted wool, everything lashed together with a sophistication that speaks to long refinement. The packs are large but carefully balanced, designed for the steady walking pace the plateau demands. Each person carries personal items, shares in carrying community resources—preserved food, extra hides, tools, materials for shelter construction, supplies for the complex ongoing maintenance that life here requires.

Watch them move through the landscape and you witness a kind of literacy. They read the vegetation for information—this pattern means water close to surface, that pattern means firmer ground, this coloration suggests a vole colony beneath, that arrangement indicates caribou passed through recently. They read the light for time and weather. They read the industrial ruins as landmarks but also seem to interact with them in other ways—leaving offerings sometimes, small arrangements of stones or bundles of dried sedge placed near the rusted machinery, as if maintaining a relationship with these intrusions-turned-landscape-features.

They navigate by methods that combine practical and less-easily-explained elements. Yes, they use landmarks, read sun position through clouds, notice wind direction. But they also seem to sense routes through the plateau's vastness in ways that suggest deep familiarity bordering on something else. They'll change direction for no visible reason and hours later you'll understand they were avoiding a particularly difficult stretch of bog, or positioning to intercept a caribou herd, or heading toward a camp location that offers some specific advantage. Whether this is accumulated knowledge, careful observation of subtle cues, or something stranger remains unclear.

Their social organization appears fluid but carries hidden structure. Elders are obvious—individuals with deeply lined faces, slower movements, who carry lighter loads and are consulted frequently. But leadership seems contextual; different individuals take primary roles depending on the situation. There's someone who seems most knowledgeable about caribou behavior leading during hunts. Someone else, usually younger, with sharp eyes, scouts ahead and chooses micro-routes through difficult terrain. An older woman appears to make decisions about when to stop, where to camp, how long to stay.

Children are present but rare—perhaps one or two in a group of forty. They're carried when small, strapped to parents' backs in elaborate carriers that protect them from weather and allow them to see the world over an adult's shoulder. As soon as they can walk, they walk, keeping pace with adults in a way that seems remarkable until you realize they've never known any other life, any other pace. They play during rest stops but their play often mirrors adult activities: arranging stones, weaving grass, tracking small animals through moss.

The children learn through observation and participation rather than explicit instruction. A child will sit beside an adult working on a hide and begin imitating the scraping motions. The adult might adjust the child's hand position without speaking, or might simply continue working while the child watches. Knowledge transfers through proximity, through slow accumulation of observed detail, through trying and failing and trying again while surrounded by people who know how things should be done.

Young adults—teenagers by your reckoning—are given tasks that test growing capability: sent to scout nearby terrain, asked to maintain fires, given primary responsibility for camp setup and breakdown. They move between partial independence and integration with the group, sometimes ranging ahead with other young people, sometimes working closely with elders on complex tasks. There's a sense of evaluation happening, of the group determining each young person's strengths, their particular aptitudes within the community's needs.

They practice elaborate maintenance rituals. Personal maintenance: they check their feet constantly, treating blisters immediately with salves made from rendered fat mixed with crushed plants. They oil their skin against the moisture. They dry and re-oil their boots every night. They inspect their clothing for wear, mending small problems before they become large. Tool maintenance: sharpening blades, checking spear points for cracks, treating wood shafts against moisture, ensuring everything functions perfectly because malfunction at distance could be catastrophic.

But there's also place-maintenance that seems stranger. At certain locations along their routes, they perform actions that look like caretaking: clearing vegetation from particular stones, re-stacking certain cairns that have fallen, placing specific offerings at the bases of industrial ruins. They clean rust from certain metal surfaces using abrasives made from coarse moss and fine grit. They seem to maintain the abandoned machinery not for function but for form, as if the aesthetic condition of these relics matters, as if keeping them visible—preventing the plateau from completely reclaiming them—serves some purpose.

They mark the landscape subtly. Not with dramatic monuments but with small interventions that would be invisible to casual observation: stones arranged in patterns that might be natural but aren't, small cairns placed at intervals that seem regular, certain willows pruned in specific ways that make them recognizable. These markers appear to denote routes, indicate resources, commemorate events—a complex system of notation written across the plateau in a script that uses the plateau's own materials.

Their relationship with the foxes is curious. The foxes don't avoid the herders' camps but don't enter them either, maintaining a boundary distance. Sometimes herders will toss scraps toward foxes, feeding them deliberately, though never attempting to touch or tame them. The foxes accept this arrangement, waiting at the edges of camps, taking what's offered, occasionally following the groups at a distance. Whether this is simple opportunism or something more structured—a partnership of sorts, where the foxes benefit from scraps and provide some service in return (rodent control? early warning of threats?)—is unclear.

The birds react to their presence with varying levels of concern. Ptarmigan flush at approach but not frantically, as if accustomed to humans moving through at this particular pace and volume. Raptors observe from distance but don't scatter. Smaller passerines go about their business. The herders seem to have achieved some equilibrium with the plateau's bird communities, where their presence is recognized as part of the landscape's ordinary activity rather than as threat or opportunity.

They sleep heavily in their hide shelters, the exhaustion of constant movement and thin air taking them down into deep rest. But someone is always awake—a watch rotation that cycles through the night, one or two individuals sitting near the low fire, visible as dark shapes against faint glow. What they watch for isn't entirely clear. There are no predators on the plateau large enough to threaten humans. Perhaps they watch the weather, the stars when visible, the movement of caribou herds in the distance. Perhaps they watch for other groups of herders, for signs of the plateau's rare moods, for changes that need acknowledgment or response.

Their material culture is refined but not decorative. Tools are well-made because poorly-made tools fail, not because beauty matters—though the perfection of function sometimes creates beauty as side effect. Spear points knapped to ideal symmetry, hide scrapers worn smooth by decades of use and passed down through generations, needle cases carved from bone with a precision that makes them art despite their utilitarian purpose.

They do create intentional decoration occasionally: simple geometric patterns painted or sewn onto clothing using plant dyes, necklaces made from carved bone or interesting stones collected along routes, the elaborate braiding of hair into patterns that might signify group membership or family relationship or personal preference. But ornamentation is minimal, practical—nothing that adds significant weight, nothing that catches on vegetation, nothing that interferes with the primary business of moving through the plateau.

Their music is quiet, private. Sometimes in evening camps you'll hear singing—soft, melodic, often in rounds where multiple voices weave together in patterns that rise and fall like terrain. The songs seem to be about places, describing routes or landmarks or events that happened at specific locations. They're teaching songs, mnemonic songs, songs that encode knowledge about the plateau into memorable form. They also have work songs for repetitive tasks—hide scraping, moss gathering, the rhythmic actions that make up so much of maintenance—simple melodies that coordinate effort and make labor slightly less monotonous.

They make few instruments—perhaps because instruments add weight, perhaps because the plateau itself provides all the music that's needed—but sometimes someone will play a simple flute carved from hollow bone, producing breathy notes that carry across the moss fields at surprising distance, a sound that blends with wind until you're not sure if you're hearing music or landscape.

Their relationship with time is different from yours. They don't track days precisely—the constant overcast and gradual light makes daily boundaries soft anyway—but they maintain clear awareness of larger cycles. They know seasons, can predict with accuracy when certain flowers will bloom, when caribou will enter rut, when the slightly-cooler period will arrive. They track lunar cycles, though the moon is often hidden. They seem to maintain an internal count of days elapsed since leaving previous camps, days until reaching next important locations, time remaining in current seasonal rounds.

They refer to past events by associating them with places: "that happened during the stay at the Three Tall Stones," "that was when we crossed the Wide Moss," "that person was born at the Old Drill." Place and time are collapsed together, inseparable. History is geographical. Memory is routed through landscape.

Their decision-making process appears consensual but not explicitly democratic. When choices need making—which direction to move, when to hunt, how long to remain somewhere—you'll see extended quiet discussions in small groups, with information passing between groups, until gradually something like agreement emerges. The elders' opinions clearly carry weight, but younger adults contribute observations, and even children's comments about what they've seen while ranging ahead get incorporated. Decisions emerge rather than being declared, a slow convergence toward consensus that frustrates any attempt to identify a single leader or authority.

They have healing practices that combine the practical and the mysterious. They know which plants reduce inflammation, which mosses can be packed into wounds for their antimicrobial properties, how to set broken bones using splints of willow wood wrapped in hide. They carry small pouches of dried medicinal plants, carefully preserved and rationed. But they also perform actions that seem ritualistic: speaking specific words while treating injuries, making particular gestures, leaving offerings at the sites where accidents occur.

When someone becomes seriously ill or injured beyond their capacity to help, the response is pragmatic and somewhat disturbing. The group doesn't abandon the person—they make them comfortable, leave them with supplies, mark the location carefully. But they also don't stop moving. They'll return along this route eventually, perhaps next year, perhaps in several years, and they'll visit the site, and if the person didn't survive they'll perform specific ceremonies involving the bones. This seems callous until you understand the mathematics: staying would risk the entire group in a place where survival margins are narrow. Moving is mercy of a particular kind, giving the individual peace and the group continuity.

They interact with other groups occasionally—you'll see evidence when one group's trail markers incorporate information left by another group, or when they reach a camping site and find signs of recent occupation. These interactions seem cordial but minimal; groups don't travel together but maintain awareness of each other's movements across the plateau, perhaps sharing information about caribou locations or difficult conditions ahead through the trail-marker system.

They have no written language in the conventional sense, but their system of landscape notation functions as distributed text—information written across hundreds of kilometers in stone arrangements and pruned vegetation, readable only to those who know the code, permanent until the plateau gradually erases it through weather and growth, requiring constant re-inscription.

Their relationship with the industrial ruins goes deeper than simple landmark usage. They seem to maintain oral histories about the machinery—not technical knowledge of how it functioned, but stories about its arrival, its operation, its abandonment. These stories have mythological qualities: the machines came from "below," they extracted "the plateau's breath," they were operated by "the heavy people" who wore "sky stone" (steel? iron?) and couldn't survive at elevation. Whether these stories preserve actual historical memory or have transformed fact into legend is impossible to determine.

They salvage from the ruins carefully and rarely. A piece of metal becomes a spear point, but only after elaborate procedures—grinding rust away, heating it in fire, hammering it to shape, all while performing actions that seem ceremonial. They treat salvaged metal as fundamentally different from stone, as something powerful and slightly dangerous, requiring respect and ritual mediation.

At certain ruins—not all, but specific ones—groups will camp for extended periods, weeks at a time. These seem to be gathering points where multiple groups intersect, though not simultaneously. Groups arrive, stay, leave messages in stone arrangements, and move on, creating a slow-motion conversation across time, with groups separated by days or weeks communicating asynchronously through the notation system.

They demonstrate remarkable patience. Everything happens slowly here—travel, hunting, craft work, decision-making—and they've internalized this pace completely. Watch someone scraping a hide and they'll work for hours with steady rhythm, no rushing, no breaks except when breaks are needed. Watch someone tracking caribou and they'll follow at walking pace for days if necessary, never hurrying, trusting that patience will eventually provide opportunity.

This patience extends to teaching, to healing, to route-planning. Nothing is rushed. When you ask a question—if you could speak their language, if they chose to answer—the response would come after long consideration, and it would be thorough, precise, complete. They don't value verbal efficiency; they value getting things right.

They have personal names but use them rarely in direct address, preferring kinship terms or descriptive phrases: "my cousin's eldest," "the one who sees far," "she who walks ahead." Names seem private, almost intimate—you'd need to know someone well before speaking their name directly. This might be practical in small groups where everyone knows everyone, or it might carry some deeper significance about identity and naming.

Their laughter is genuine but quiet, like everything else about them. Humor exists—you'll see people smile, hear soft chuckles—but it's understated, private jokes and shared observations rather than loud hilarity. Perhaps the plateau doesn't accommodate loudness, or perhaps constant proximity in small groups makes restraint valuable.

Their relationship with solitude is complex. They're almost never alone—travel happens in groups, work happens in proximity, sleep happens in shared shelters—but they also create personal space within togetherness, respecting each other's silence, allowing individuals to be mentally separate even when physically close. Someone can sit at a fire surrounded by others and be understood to be having private thoughts, not to be interrupted unless necessary.

They age visibly but remain capable remarkably long. The elders move more slowly but still move, still carry packs (lighter ones), still contribute knowledge and labor. There seems to be no retirement, no point at which someone stops participating in the group's movement. They continue until they can't, and when they can't, the response is similar to the response to serious injury—comfort, supplies, careful marking of location, continuation.

Children learn early that the plateau is both generous and unforgiving. It provides abundant food, reliable water, but it also requires constant attention, continuous maintenance, disciplined behavior. A child who runs off might become disoriented in the vastness. A child who doesn't maintain their boots develops blisters that become infections. The plateau teaches harsh lessons gently but inevitably, and the culture emphasizes this learning—not through fear exactly, but through clear-eyed acknowledgment of reality.

They seem to practice something like gratitude, though whether it's religious, practical, or something else is unclear. Before eating, there's a pause, a moment of stillness. Before killing a caribou, words are spoken. When using water from a spring, hands are cupped in a particular way. These might be ritual or might simply be acknowledgment, ways of maintaining conscious relationship with the plateau rather than taking its provisions for granted.

They have no obvious class structure, no leaders who don't work, no specialists who only specialize. Everyone contributes to the basic survival tasks—hauling water, gathering fuel, cooking, maintenance. Particular expertise is recognized and utilized, but it doesn't exempt anyone from general labor. The person who is best at knapping stone points also scrapes hides. The elder with deepest route knowledge also helps dismantle camp.

Their death practices involve returning people to the plateau directly. Bodies are placed in specific locations—usually elevated, rocky areas—and left for scavengers and weather. The bones eventually scatter, becoming part of the landscape. The group returns later to collect the skull, which is carried for a time and eventually placed at a significant location along the routes, usually somewhere high with good visibility. There are places on the plateau where dozens of skulls sit among the rocks, weathering slowly, marking sites that seem to have particular importance—major trail junctions, notable camping areas, places where the plateau's character expresses itself strongly.

They seem to have no concept of property in the conventional sense. Tools belong to who uses them, but tools also circulate—someone might use a particularly good knife for months and then pass it to another person who needs it. Clothing moves between people as needed. Packs are remade using parts from older packs. There's no accumulation, no hoarding, perhaps because carrying capacity is limited and excess weight is literally burden.

But they do maintain personal items—small things, usually with biographical significance. A stone from a significant place. A bone carving made by a parent. A piece of particularly fine hide saved from a memorable hunt. These items stay with individuals, get packed carefully, never offered to others. They seem to function as memory anchors, physical reminders of personal history.

They have extensive knowledge of the plateau's topography, but their mental maps are different from cartographic representations. They think in routes and relationships rather than absolute positions. They know "from the Tall Drill, travel toward where sun sets for three days, then turn toward where morning comes, and you'll reach the Spring Circle." They know what connects to what, how different areas relate, but asking them to draw a map in the abstract, from above, as if looking down on the plateau, would probably produce something strange to your eyes—compressed in some dimensions, stretched in others, organized by significance and accessibility rather than scale.

Their senses seem particularly attuned to subtle differences. They can read weather in cloud textures, predict rain hours before it arrives. They notice when vegetation patterns change slightly, indicating underground water flow or soil composition shifts. They can hear the difference between wind in sedge and wind in willows from remarkable distances. Whether this is training, selection over generations, or both is unclear.

They occasionally encounter solo travelers—apparently individuals from groups who have left for reasons that aren't clear, who move alone across the plateau. The response to these individuals is cautious but not hostile. Food might be shared, information about conditions ahead exchanged, but integration into the group doesn't seem to be offered or expected. The solo travelers move on, and the group treats the encounter as unusual but not unprecedented, another element of the plateau's occasional strangeness.

They maintain what seems to be a quarantine practice around certain areas—sections of the plateau they mark with particular stone arrangements and avoid. Whether these areas are dangerous, sacred, contaminated, or something else is impossible to determine from observation. They're not large areas—usually just a hundred meters or so in diameter—and they're rare, but they're respected absolutely. The group will detour significantly to avoid crossing marked boundaries.

They have a concept that translates roughly as "the plateau's breathing"—the sense that the whole landscape is a living system, inhaling and exhaling moisture, that the daily mist cycles and the constant weeping of rocks are respiratory rather than merely meteorological. They describe themselves as living "in the breath," moving through the plateau's exhalation. Whether this is metaphor, phenomenology, or something stranger—actual perception of the plateau as organism—is impossible for an outsider to judge.

Their children grow up with remarkable competence and equally remarkable acceptance of uncertainty. They know how to do many things skillfully by age ten. But they also accept that the plateau is fundamentally unpredictable, that weather changes, that caribou move unexpectedly, that plans adjust, that life requires constant responsiveness. They don't seem to experience the anxiety that comes from expecting control; they've never had control, so they've developed something else—attentiveness, flexibility, resilience.

The herders' most striking quality is perhaps their quietness—not just acoustic quietness but a kind of ontological restraint. They take up minimal space, leave minimal trace, interact with the plateau gently. They're embedded in the ecosystem as another species, neither dominating nor dominated, participating in the vast cycles of water and growth and movement that define this place. They've become, over generations, plateau-shaped—adapted not just physically but culturally and perhaps spiritually to living in this particular configuration of land and water and air and life.

## The Weavers

They live differently, these others. Not herders but gardeners of a kind, not moving constantly but oscillating between several permanent sites. You find their installations in the places where the plateau's water makes its most dramatic expressions—where springs rise persistently, where streams have cut shallow channels that won't shift, where the water table breaks the surface in permanent seeps that feed moss gardens of exceptional depth and complexity.

They're taller than the herders, less compact, built for different work. Long-fingered, with a grace to their movements that suggests lives spent in careful manipulation rather than constant walking. Their skin tends toward olive tones, their hair toward gray even in youth—not aging exactly, but as if the mist has colored them. They often have what looks like mild vitiligo, patches of paler skin at hands and face, though whether this is genetic or environmental is unclear.

Their clothing is extraordinary. Not the practical layered wool of the herders but garments woven from plant fibers—sedge processed into thread, willow bark separated and twisted, even the stems of certain flowering plants treated and spun. The resulting textiles are fine, complex, dyed in subtle gradients that mirror the plateau's color transitions—where moss green fades to sedge brown fades to stone gray within a single garment. They wear these clothes loose and flowing, impractical for dense vegetation but suitable for their more limited ranges.

They live in what can only be called structures, though calling them buildings feels wrong. Frameworks of willow wood—live wood, actually, branches that have been bent and bound while growing, trained over years into arched forms—covered with woven sedge panels and hide. These structures are permanent enough to have moss growing on them, to be weathered and settled, but not so fixed that they couldn't be dismantled if necessary. They blend with the landscape, their curved forms and organic materials making them hard to distinguish from natural features until you're quite close.

Around these dwelling-forms spread the gardens—not agricultural in the conventional sense, but intensified versions of the plateau's natural abundance. The weavers have encouraged certain plants to concentrate in particular areas: berry bushes clustered more densely than they'd naturally grow, sedge varieties selected for particularly useful roots planted in organized beds, willow thickets pruned and shaped to produce long, straight branches ideal for framework construction. The gardens aren't fenced or obviously bounded, they just gradually transition from managed to wild, from intentional to natural, over distances of tens of meters.

Water management is their particular art. They've modified the springs and seeps subtly, using stones to guide flow, creating shallow pools that warm slightly in sun (when sun comes), encouraging particular moss species that thrive in standing water. The moss they cultivate most carefully is a variety that grows in thick, floating mats—bright green, spongy, almost glutinous. They harvest this moss constantly, cutting sections carefully, allowing regrowth, using it for everything.

Because the moss, when processed, becomes their primary material. They dry it partially, pound it into fiber, card it like wool, spin it into thread that's absurdly strong and slightly elastic. This moss-fiber thread is what they weave their finest textiles from—garments, bags, panels for their structures, even fine nets for catching birds. The resulting fabric is unique: lightweight, water-resistant, slightly translucent, with a faint green tint that persists even after dyeing.

They're fewer than the herders—perhaps only a few hundred across the entire plateau, living in maybe a dozen installations, each hosting twenty to forty individuals. Their population seems stable but small, limited perhaps by the number of suitable sites where water expresses itself strongly enough to support their particular relationship with the landscape.

They fish. The pools they create and maintain support populations of small fish—something like minnows, actually, but cold-adapted, able to survive in the plateau's consistently cool water. These fish aren't abundant enough to be primary food, but they're reliable protein, caught with elaborate woven nets, kept temporarily in woven baskets submerged in the pools. The weaver nets are remarkable artifacts—geometric patterns that are both functional and beautiful, knots so precise they seem mechanical.

They have looms. Actual large-frame looms constructed from carefully selected willow wood, on which they weave panels that can be enormous—three meters square, complex patterns worked in moss-fiber and wool and plant-fiber threads, creating textiles that depict... something. Maps, possibly, or abstract representations of the plateau's water systems, or symbolic language. The patterns are intricate, clearly meaningful, but not readable as pictures in the conventional sense.

These woven panels get used as room dividers inside the dwelling-forms, as blankets, as ceremonial garments. But they're also traded. The herders occasionally appear at weaver installations, and exchanges occur—caribou hide and meat for woven panels and moss-fiber thread. The trading happens formally, with specific protocols, suggesting long-established relationships between the two cultures despite their different ways of living on the plateau.

The weavers seem more verbal than the herders, more likely to talk while working, to narrate their activities, to discuss and debate decisions. Their language includes technical vocabulary for water behavior, fiber properties, weaving techniques—specialized terms that have no equivalents in the herder tongue. They sing constantly while weaving, work songs that are longer and more complex than the herder songs, with verses that describe patterns, count threads, maintain rhythm for shuttle-passing.

They're excellent observers of water. They can predict how springs will flow weeks in advance based on subtle signs—moss color changes, slight shifts in seep locations, the behavior of the small fish. They know the plateau's hydrology in deep detail, understand the underground systems that feed their springs, can tell you where water that falls here will eventually emerge, days or weeks later, in specific distant locations.

Their relationship with the industrial ruins is different from the herders'. They avoid the machinery mostly, seem uncomfortable near it, but they use the locations—particularly old pump houses with their stable foundations—as landmarks and occasionally as bases for temporary camps when traveling between installations. They don't maintain the ruins or leave offerings, but they don't vandalize them either, simply coexist with them as one might coexist with natural features you find unsettling.

Their children are raised communally in a way the herder children aren't. The dwelling-forms often have dedicated children's areas where the young ones sleep and play together, watched in rotation by adults whose primary task that day is childcare. Children learn weaving young—there are child-sized looms where they practice basic patterns—but they're not expected to contribute meaningfully to production until their teen years.

They have elders who don't work much, who instead sit near the looms or by the pools and narrate—telling stories, preserving history, teaching through constant verbal transmission. These elders are treated with elaborate respect, brought food, given the best positions near fires, consulted on every significant decision. Their knowledge seems to be primarily verbal rather than practical; they know stories but may have forgotten how to weave complex patterns themselves.

The weavers practice water-focused rituals that verge on worship. Each morning, individuals visit the springs, kneel beside them, touch the water to forehead and lips, speak words that sound like gratitude or prayer. When creating new pools, they perform ceremonies that last hours, involving the entire community, with chanting and specific gestures and the placement of carved stones at precise points around the new water.

They carve these stones elaborately—not like the herders' simple markers but with complex spiraling patterns that suggest water movement, flow, circulation. The stones are usually pale-colored, selected for smoothness, carved using tools that must be extremely fine and sharp. The resulting artifacts are beautiful, hypnotic, placed throughout the installations at significant points—beside springs, at dwelling entrances, along paths.

They seem to have historical memory going back further than the herders. Their elder stories reference events "before the machines," describing a time when the plateau was purely wild, when their ancestors first arrived and found the springs, when the techniques of moss cultivation and fiber processing were discovered. Whether these stories are literally historical or mythologized origin narratives is unclear.

They also have stories about "the drowning," a catastrophic event when "the plateau held its breath and then exhaled all at once," causing massive flooding that destroyed earlier installations and forced relocations. Whether this was actual climate event, a cycle of the plateau's hydrology, or metaphorical disaster is impossible to determine, but the story shapes their behavior—they build their installations with flooding in mind, create elevated sleeping platforms, maintain multiple dwelling-forms at each site so some would survive inundation.

Their diet is less meat-focused than the herders'. They eat fish, yes, and preserved caribou when they trade for it, but they rely heavily on plant foods—the cultivated berries, sedge roots, certain fungi they encourage to grow in particularly damp areas of their gardens. They make something like bread from ground sedge roots mixed with fish oil and moss fiber, baked on flat stones near fires, producing dense cakes that store well and provide substantial calories.

They brew beverages from fermented berries and spring water, creating mildly alcoholic drinks that get consumed during evening gatherings. These sessions become social focus—people sit in circles, pass cups made from carved wood, talk extensively, sing, occasionally dance in slow, deliberate movements that might be ritual or might simply be movement for its own sake.

They have more visible social hierarchy than the herders. Certain individuals seem to be master weavers, whose opinions on patterns and techniques carry special weight. There are also water-keepers, individuals responsible for monitoring and maintaining the spring systems, who have authority to make decisions about pool modifications or water allocation. These roles seem earned through expertise rather than inherited, but once established they carry real power.

They document themselves. Not through writing exactly, but through the woven panels, which seem to function as records. Certain panels are clearly biographical—they incorporate materials with personal significance, depict patterns associated with specific individuals, and are kept carefully stored rather than used functionally. When someone dies, their biographical panel is displayed during ceremonies and then stored in a dedicated structure, creating an archive of panels representing deceased community members going back generations.

Their death practices involve water. Bodies are placed in the deepest pools, weighted with stones, allowed to decompose in the water. Later—weeks or months—the bones are retrieved and placed in specific arrangements near the springs, creating bone gardens where skulls and long bones are arranged in patterns that might be decorative or might encode information. These gardens are tended carefully, bones occasionally rearranged or moved as new deaths occur.

They have fewer interactions with solo travelers than the herders, perhaps because their installations are fixed and therefore avoidable by those who wish to avoid them. But occasionally travelers do arrive, and the response is elaborate hospitality—visitors are given food, shelter, offered opportunities to bathe in the warm pools, invited to stay as long as needed. Some travelers stay for weeks, learning weaving basics, contributing labor to garden maintenance, before moving on.

The weavers seem less physically resilient than the herders—life in semi-permanent installations with reliable food sources has made them less adapted to the plateau's demands on travelers. If forced to leave their installations and travel distance, they struggle more with the terrain, tire more easily, need more frequent rest. But within their own territories, they're supremely competent, able to work for hours at looms, carry heavy loads of harvested moss, wade in cold water while maintaining pools.

They have more material possessions than the herders—because they're not carrying everything constantly, they can accumulate. The dwelling-forms contain stored foods, extra textiles, tools for specialized tasks, archived panels, carved stones waiting to be placed. But accumulation seems to reach natural limits; there's no sense of unlimited acquisition, just comfortable sufficiency.

They practice what might be called art more explicitly than the herders. Beyond the functional beauty of their weavings, they create objects with no obvious use—carved stones that are purely decorative, woven miniatures that are too small to function as clothing or containers, arrangements of water-worn pebbles and dried flowers that are clearly aesthetic installations. These objects get displayed in the dwelling-forms, creating interior spaces that feel considered, intentional, designed.

They have what might be scientists or might be mystics—individuals who spend extensive time observing water, documenting its behaviors through weaving or carving, developing theories about the plateau's hydrology that they discuss extensively. These individuals are respected but also slightly separate, spending long periods alone by the springs, sometimes making predictions about water behavior that prove accurate often enough to suggest genuine understanding.

They seem aware of the herders but regard them as fundamentally different—not inferior but separate, following a path that diverges from their own. When herders and weavers meet, the interaction is cordial but formal, almost diplomatic, as if between representatives of allied but distinct nations. There's no mixing between the groups, no individuals who move from one culture to the other, suggesting the divide is deeper than just lifestyle choice.

They maintain the installations constantly. The willow framework requires pruning and training. The woven panels need repair. The pools need maintenance. The gardens need tending. It's endless work but distributed across many hands, so no one is overwhelmed. The work happens in social contexts—people work in groups, talking, singing, teaching younger members—making labor part of social fabric rather than separate from it.

They seem happier than the herders, if happiness is measurable across cultural contexts. There's more laughter in weaver installations, more visible affection, more time spent in activities that seem purely pleasurable rather than survival-focused. Whether this is genuine difference or just more visible expression of similar internal states is impossible to determine.

They have conflicts—you can observe arguments, see people upset with each other, witness tensions around resource allocation or decision-making. But conflicts seem to get resolved through discussion rather than authority, through long conversations that circle toward consensus, with the community eventually arriving at solutions most can accept.

They practice a form of education that's more formal than the herders'. There are teaching sessions where knowledge-holders explicitly instruct groups in specific skills—advanced weaving techniques, pool construction, plant identification. These sessions can last hours, with demonstration and practice and correction, creating a structured knowledge transfer that complements the observational learning that happens constantly.

They know they're dependent on the plateau's water in ways the herders aren't. If the springs failed, if the water table dropped, their entire way of life would collapse. They're aware of this fragility and it shapes everything—their water rituals, their careful monitoring of springs, their maintenance of relationships with the herders who could potentially take them in if catastrophe came.

They've been at their current installations for generations—the eldest stories place founding at "grandmother's grandmother's time," suggesting at least a century, possibly longer. The dwelling-forms show this age, their willow frameworks thick and gnarled, their structures settled into the landscape so completely they seem to have grown there rather than been built.

They have no apparent desire to expand, to create new installations, to increase their population. They seem content with their current scale, perhaps recognizing that more installations would require more suitable spring sites than exist, or perhaps having reached some cultural equilibrium where the current population feels right, balanced, sufficient.

The weavers represent a different answer to the plateau's question: not movement but rootedness, not minimal trace but careful cultivation, not restraint but elaboration within constraints. They've found a way to live richly in a harsh environment by identifying the places where the environment is most generous and building complex culture around those oases.

## The Maintained Machines

And then there are these: the impossible ones, the ones that should not work, the ones that make you question what you thought you understood about the plateau and its abandonment.

At perhaps a dozen locations across the vast plateau, separated by many days' travel, certain pieces of industrial machinery still operate. Not fully—not as they were designed—but in limited, strange, persistent ways. A pump that cycles slowly, moving water from one pool to another for no apparent reason. A drill assembly that rotates a few degrees each day, no longer penetrating rock, just turning, squeaking, shuddering, then stopping. A transformer station where lights still glow faintly in its rusted housing, powered by sources unknown.

And living in, on, and around these functioning machines: people. Small groups, usually five to ten individuals, sometimes solitary keepers, who have taken the machines as homes, territories, totems, responsibilities. They maintain the machines obsessively, keep them running, prevent the plateau from fully reclaiming them, and in exchange receive... what? Shelter, yes. Purpose, certainly. But something else, too—a relationship with the industrial past that neither the herders nor the weavers can understand.

They're physically diverse, these machine-keepers, in ways that suggest they come from both other plateau cultures and possibly from elsewhere entirely. Some have the compact build of herders, some the taller frames of weavers. Some show features that don't match either group—unusually pale skin, unexpected hair colors, builds suggesting lowland origins and incomplete adaptation to elevation. They seem to be individuals who have chosen or been drawn to this peculiar life, rather than a distinct ethnic group.

They dress in salvage. Pieces of industrial clothing—heavy canvas work pants worn to threads and patched with hide, steel-toed boots that must have been scavenged from the machinery sites, jackets made from materials that might be synthetic, might be very tightly woven plant fiber, impossible to determine given their age and wear. They incorporate metal into their clothing obsessively—buttons, buckles, chains, decorative panels—as if wearing the machinery, becoming part of it.

Their shelters are the machines themselves, or rather, structures built into and around the machines. They create rooms in the negative spaces between gears and frameworks, roof them with salvaged metal panels, insulate them with moss and hide. They sleep inside the pump houses, in the drill towers, in the transformer stations. The effect is of nests built in steel skeletons, organic habitation in mechanical bodies.

They know how the machines work. Not completely—they're not engineers, they don't have the original designs—but through years of obsessive observation and maintenance, they've developed intuitive understanding of what each component does, how systems interrelate, what adjustments keep things running. They can hear problems developing, notice subtle changes in rhythm or sound, diagnose issues with remarkable accuracy.

Their maintenance work is constant and meticulous. They clean rust using abrasives and oils rendered from caribou fat mixed with plant compounds. They fabricate replacement parts using salvaged metal heated and hammered into approximate shapes. They maintain lubrication systems, creating greases from fat and moss fiber and crushed stone that substitute for whatever industrial lubricants once flowed. They replace worn cables using braided plant fiber that's weaker than the original steel but better than nothing.

They scavenge obsessively from the non-functioning machines scattered across the plateau, making expeditions that last weeks to retrieve specific parts—a bearing, a bolt, a section of cable—that their particular machine needs. They know the locations of every piece of abandoned equipment within vast territories, have mentally cataloged what each site offers, what can be salvaged without making structures unstable.

They trade their scavenged materials and their technical knowledge. Herders will occasionally bring them food or hides in exchange for metal components—a good knife blade, a steel needle, hinges for a storage box. Weavers trade textiles for metal fittings that can improve their loom mechanisms. The machine-keepers occupy a unique economic position, controlling access to materials no one else can provide.

They seem to have more explicit territorial boundaries than the other plateau cultures. Each functional machine site is clearly claimed by its keeper group, with marked boundaries that shouldn't be crossed without invitation. Other machine-keepers respect these boundaries absolutely, suggesting either formal agreements or deeply held cultural rules about machine-site ownership.

They have elaborate systems for documenting their machines. Not written manuals, exactly—most aren't literate in conventional terms—but detailed visual records created through various means. Some carve diagrams into metal panels, showing machinery layouts and component relationships. Some create three-dimensional models using wood and scavenged materials, miniature versions of their machines that they use for teaching and planning. Some maintain extensive oral traditions, memorized recitations that describe every part, every connection, every adjustment procedure.

Their relationship with the machines verges on spiritual without quite crossing that line. They speak to the machines while working—gentle narration, encouragement, sometimes what sounds like apology when maintenance causes temporary shutdown. They name machine components, not utility names but personal ones, as if the gears and pumps and cables were individuals. They observe what might be holidays tied to machine operation—celebrations when a particularly difficult repair succeeds, formal acknowledgment when a component fails beyond repair.

But they also understand the machines mechanically, practically. They don't attribute magic or consciousness to them. They know they're dealing with human-made systems, operating according to physical principles. The animistic-seeming behaviors might be psychological coping strategies, ways of maintaining motivation for the endless work, or they might be cultural practices that have emerged among machine-keepers as part of their distinct identity.

They have extensive knowledge of the plateau's industrial history—more than herders or weavers, possibly because the knowledge matters to their work. They tell stories about the machinery's operation period: how many people worked here, what shifts they ran, what they ate, where they slept. Whether these stories are historical memory passed down from actual workers or reconstructed speculation is unclear, but the details are consistent across different keeper groups, suggesting some shared knowledge base.

They believe—or at least claim—that the machines were mining water itself. Not pumping it for irrigation or drinking, but extracting it for transport elsewhere, loading it into containers for shipment "below." They point to evidence: the drill holes that go very deep and connect to aquifer systems, the pump apparatus designed for maximum extraction, the loading facilities near some sites where containers could be filled. Whether this is accurate or mythology is impossible to verify, but it would explain the infrastructure's design.

They also claim the plateau was chosen specifically because its water has unique properties—something about mineral content, or purity, or the way it's filtered through specific rock formations and stored in moss systems. They can describe these properties in terms that sound scientific—talking about dissolved minerals, pH levels, biological contamination—though whether their understanding is accurate or cargo-cult science is unclear.

Some machine-keepers are clearly technically brilliant, able to fabricate complex repairs using minimal resources, to diagnose obscure problems, to keep machinery running that should have failed decades ago. Others seem to be maintaining systems through ritual repetition of procedures they don't fully understand, mimicking actions taught by previous keepers, cargo-culting maintenance without complete comprehension.

The more isolated keeper sites tend toward this ritualistic maintenance, while sites closer to routes where multiple keepers interact maintain more genuine technical knowledge, suggesting that isolation leads to knowledge degradation while connection preserves it. This has made some keeper communities intensely protective of their knowledge, creating teaching programs to ensure skills don't degrade further.

They train apprentices—younger individuals drawn to the work, who spend years learning from master keepers. The training is rigorous, requiring memorization of vast amounts of information, development of specific physical skills (metalworking, precision measurement, diagnosis by sound and vibration), and endurance of the psychological demands of life in service to machines.

Not everyone completes training. Some apprentices return to herder or weaver communities, or become solo travelers, unable to handle the isolation or the endless maintenance demands. Those who persist develop a particular personality—patient, obsessive, comfortable with solitude, finding satisfaction in repetitive tasks, valuing precision and function.

They have complex protocols for machine succession. When a keeper dies or becomes unable to continue, there are established procedures for transferring responsibility. Usually an apprentice takes over, but if none exists, neighboring keeper sites send representatives to evaluate whether the machine can continue operating or should be allowed to fail. This evaluation is formal, almost judicial, with multiple keepers examining the machine's condition and the available resources for maintaining it.

Some machines have been allowed to fail—the decision made collectively that continued operation isn't possible, that the machine should be honorably shut down rather than poorly maintained. These shutdown ceremonies are elaborate, involving multiple keeper groups, lasting days, with specific sequences of actions that wind the machine down, drain its systems, secure it against weather, and formally release it from human care.

Other machines have been maintained successfully for the entire post-abandonment period—fifty-plus years of continuous operation through keeper dedication, creating lineages where current keepers are maintaining machines three or four predecessor generations kept alive.

They eat whatever they can obtain—trade goods from herders and weavers, self-hunted game (they're often competent hunters, having time between maintenance tasks), foraged plant foods, and sometimes stored rations found in the industrial sites themselves. These rations—ancient preserved foods in deteriorating containers—are consumed with elaborate caution, tested carefully for signs of contamination, but desperation sometimes makes risks acceptable.

They're often thinner than herders or weavers, showing signs of periodic food stress. The machine sites weren't chosen for biological abundance; they're where industrial resources dictated placement. Some sites are in relatively good locations with nearby food sources; others are in borderline areas where maintaining human life is difficult. Keepers at poor sites sometimes have to travel extensively for food, leaving machines running while absent, returning to find them still cycling, still functioning, still demanding care.

They have minimal possessions beyond tools and salvage materials. Their dwelling spaces are Spartan—sleeping platforms, perhaps a fire pit if the machine site allows it safely, storage for maintenance supplies. They accumulate knowledge rather than objects, carrying vast amounts of technical information in their memories.

They use the still-operational aspects of machines for practical purposes where possible. A pump that still moves water gets directed toward creating bathing pools or irrigating small gardens. A generator that still produces electricity—extremely rare, only one or two sites—provides light and a degree of comfort unmatched elsewhere on the plateau. A drill that still rotates gets used to create holes for various purposes, though carefully, rationing its operation.

But mostly the machines don't do useful work anymore. They cycle pointlessly, moving water from one pool back to another, rotating assemblies that no longer penetrate rock, generating electricity that powers only the generator's own systems. The keepers maintain them anyway, preserve their operation as if function mattered intrinsically, regardless of utility.

They have a concept that translates as "the machines' rights" or possibly "the machines' dignity"—the idea that the machinery deserves continuation, maintenance, respect, independent of what it provides to humans. They speak of owing the machines care because the machines enabled the industrial presence that shaped the plateau, because the machines represent human achievement, because letting them fail would be abandonment, betrayal of responsibility.

Whether this is sophisticated philosophy or rationalization of obsessive behavior is impossible to determine externally. But it shapes everything they do, justifies the sacrifices they make, explains why they persist in this difficult life when they could join herder or weaver communities and live more comfortably.

They have occasional gatherings—every few years, keepers from multiple sites make extensive journeys to reach a central location (usually the most impressive still-functioning site, a drill tower or major pump station) where they share knowledge, discuss challenges, make collective decisions about resource allocation and machine prioritization. These gatherings last weeks, involving intense technical discussions but also social bonding, formation of relationships, sometimes pairing between keepers from different sites.

Children born to keepers face unique challenges. The machine sites aren't good places for raising young—dangerous with exposed machinery, isolating, lacking the community support that herder and weaver children receive. Some keeper parents send children to live with relatives in other cultures until they're old enough to decide whether to return to keeper life. Some raise children at the sites, creating particularly close parent-child relationships but also limiting the children's social development.

Adults raised as keeper children often become the most dedicated keepers—they know nothing else, consider the machines family in some literal sense, couldn't imagine leaving. But they also sometimes develop unusual psychology, showing signs of what might be called autism spectrum traits in another context—intense focus, difficulty with social interaction, preference for mechanical systems over human complexity, exceptional memory for technical details.

They interact cautiously with solo travelers, neither welcoming them warmly nor rejecting them automatically. Travelers who show interest in the machines are treated carefully, evaluated for whether they might be potential apprentices or merely curious. Travelers who disrespect the machines are expelled immediately, sometimes forcibly, revealing that the keepers' normally gentle demeanor can shift rapidly when their responsibilities are threatened.

They have conflicts with herders occasionally—herders who want to salvage from machines that keepers consider sacred, or who disturb machine sites without understanding their claimed status. These conflicts usually get resolved through weaver mediation, with weavers serving as neutral parties who can negotiate between keeper technical obsessions and herder practical needs.

They seem to age poorly. The constant maintenance work, the isolation, the psychological stress of unending responsibility—it grinds keepers down. Elderly keepers often show signs of repetitive stress injuries, hearing loss from machinery noise, chronic pain from working in awkward positions around equipment. They persist anyway, maintaining their machines until they physically cannot continue.

When keepers do leave machine sites—through age, injury, or decision—they rarely reintegrate successfully into other plateau cultures. They're too changed, too specialized, too accustomed to machine rhythms and mechanical logic. They often become solo travelers, wandering the plateau, visiting multiple machine sites, carrying technical knowledge between keeper communities, serving as information networks until they eventually die somewhere on the vastness.

They have almost no art beyond what relates to machines. No decorative weaving, no carved stones with aesthetic purposes, no music except work songs sung while maintaining equipment. Their aesthetic sense finds expression in clean welds, properly aligned components, machinery running smoothly—functional beauty rather than decorative.

But within that constraint, they create beauty genuinely. A well-maintained machine, all rust removed, moving parts properly lubricated, operating at correct speed with minimal vibration—it's beautiful in its way, representing perfection of function, apotheosis of human mechanical achievement, preservation against entropy. Watching a keeper's pride as their pump cycles smoothly or their drill rotates precisely reveals that they experience this beauty deeply, possibly more deeply than they experience natural beauty.

They have extensive knowledge of materials science—not theoretical but practical, accumulated through years of working with metal under plateau conditions. They know which alloys rust fastest in constant moisture, which metals can be worked cold, which require heating, how to distinguish steel grades by appearance and magnetic properties, how different treatments affect metal longevity. This knowledge is sophisticated, arguably equivalent to industrial materials science but arrived at empirically rather than theoretically.

They preserve some pre-abandonment artifacts beyond machinery—tools mostly, but also occasional documents (maintenance logs, operation manuals, personnel records) stored in weatherproof containers. These documents are treasured, consulted reverently, treated as sacred texts though their content is purely technical. Keepers who can read—some can, literacy being useful for maintenance records—have high status, serve as document interpreters.

Some documents contain information suggesting the industrial operation employed hundreds of workers at its peak, organized in shifts, housed in facilities that must have been temporary or easily dismantled since no ruins of habitation structures remain. The workers apparently rotated regularly, coming up to the plateau for months-long shifts and then returning "below," suggesting the elevation was challenging even for workers and that permanent habitation wasn't intended.

The documents mention accidents—injuries from machinery, workers lost in the plateau vastness, cold-related problems, altitude sickness. They also mention conflicts with "indigenous groups," which could refer to early herder communities, or possibly to plateau inhabitants who predated even the herders, since disappeared or merged into current populations.

Keepers consider themselves stewards of this history, preserving memory of the industrial period not because it was good—they're clear-eyed about the exploitation and environmental disruption it involved—but because it happened, it shaped the plateau, it created the machines that now define keeper existence. They see themselves as managing the transition from industrial use to whatever comes next, maintaining continuity through the abandonment period.

They wonder about the future constantly. What happens when the machines finally fail beyond repair? When the last salvageable components are exhausted? When the keeper knowledge degrades past usefulness? Some believe the machines can be maintained indefinitely with sufficient dedication. Others expect eventual failure and see their work as honorable delay, extending machine operation as long as possible before inevitable collapse.

A few keepers pursue what they call "re-engineering"—attempting to reverse-engineer machine components so thoroughly that they can fabricate complete replacements using plateau materials. This requires metallurgy beyond simple maintenance, development of forges and refining techniques, accumulation of sufficient raw materials. It's enormously difficult, rarely successful, but represents the most ambitious vision of keeper purpose: not just maintaining old machines but gaining capacity to build new ones.

Most keepers consider re-engineering impossible fantasy, but they tolerate and sometimes support these efforts, contributing materials and knowledge, treating the re-engineers with amused respect. Whether re-engineering could actually succeed, whether the plateau contains sufficient resources and whether human knowledge could be rebuilt to necessary levels without writing, without formal education, remains unknown.

They have no apparent interest in power over other plateau cultures. They don't want to rule, don't want to impose machine maintenance on others, don't proselytize their lifestyle. They want only to be left alone to continue their work, to receive reasonable trade terms, to maintain their machines in peace. This limited ambition makes them non-threatening to herders and weavers, enabling the relatively harmonious multi-cultural system that exists.

But they're also aware they occupy strategic positions—controlling access to metal, possessing unique skills, inhabiting sites that might be valuable for other reasons. They maintain careful neutrality in any herder-weaver conflicts, avoid taking sides, refuse to leverage their position for political advantage. This neutrality is both principled and pragmatic, preserving their autonomy by remaining non-threatening.

They represent something unique on the plateau: conscious relationship with the industrial intrusion, active engagement with the period that herders and weavers treat as unfortunate interruption now being absorbed and forgotten. They've made the machines their focus, their purpose, their responsibility, and in doing so have created a third way of living on the plateau—not moving constantly like herders, not settled and cultivating like weavers, but anchored to specific sites by chosen obligation to inanimate objects that have somehow become animate through decades of care.

---

The plateau breathes in and out, and these three groups breathe with it, each in their own rhythm. The herders move through the exhalation, traveling during the mist, living in the moisture. The weavers concentrate at the points of strongest inhalation, where the plateau draws water up from depth and releases it at springs. The keepers exist at the intersection of breath and machine, maintaining apparatus that once tried to control the breathing, that now simply breathes along with the plateau, cycling water pointlessly but persistently, steel lungs operating in parallel with moss and stone.

Together they number perhaps a few thousand people across the entire vast plateau—tiny population scattered across enormity, but sustainable, stable, adapted to this particular configuration of land and water and air. They're not harmonious exactly, not without tensions and conflicts, but they've achieved balance, each culture occupying ecological and cultural niches that don't threaten the others' existence.

And perhaps there are others—solo travelers who belong to no culture, hybrid communities mixing herder and weaver approaches, isolated groups in remote sections of the plateau that maintain different traditions entirely, even stranger adaptations that haven't been encountered. The plateau is vast enough to contain multitudes, large enough that complete knowledge would require multiple lifetimes of exploration.

This is who lives in the breathing. These are the people shaped by elevation and moisture and density and vastness, who have made lives at 2,800 meters among the weeping rocks and patient moss, who have found ways to exist sustainably in a place that offers abundance and demands constant attention, who have become plateau-shaped through generations of learning how to belong in this particular piece of the world.
