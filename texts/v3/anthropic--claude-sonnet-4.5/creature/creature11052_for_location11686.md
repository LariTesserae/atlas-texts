# The People of the Seeping Clay

They call themselves the **Tílmak**—a word in their language that doubles as both "those who press" and "those who are pressed," the verb reflexive, acknowledging that they shape the clay and are shaped by it in equal measure. When they speak of themselves to outsiders, if outsiders come, they sometimes say simply "basin people," but among themselves the word *Tílmak* carries the full weight of their relationship to this place: makers and made, shapers and shaped, standing in wet clay that yields and remembers.

They move with a distinctive gait, these people. Not quite a shuffle, but a walking style that keeps the center of gravity low, knees slightly bent, feet placed with deliberation. It comes from a lifetime of navigating slick surfaces, of knowing that every step on wet clay or moisture-filmed wood requires a kind of attention. Children learn it early—you can watch them, the small ones, exaggerating the careful placement until it becomes unconscious. By adolescence it's simply how they walk, even on the rare solid surfaces. Their feet are broad, often bare, with pronounced arches and toes that spread wide for grip. The soles are thickened, adapted to constant moisture and cool temperatures, mottled in color from the clay that never quite washes away, staining the skin in gray-blue and ochre maps of where they've walked.

Their hands tell stories. Every adult has hands marked by work—not scarred dramatically, but textured, the skin of the palms and fingers slightly roughened, embedded with fine particles of clay that no washing fully removes. The creases in their palms are darker, traced in mineral residue. Many have a particular callus at the base of the thumb and along the inside of the first finger, the mark of pressing clay, of shaping bricks and vessels and daub. The potters have it most pronounced, their hands almost armored with use. But nearly everyone has it to some degree, because nearly everyone works clay. It's the medium of their lives.

They are not tall people, though not strikingly short—average height running perhaps to the shoulder of a tall outsider. Their builds tend toward compact, sturdy, with strong legs and broad chests. This is partly genetic drift, partly selection over two generations, partly the result of lives lived in physical work in cool, damp air that demands the body maintain its heat. They breathe deeply, these people. You notice it in conversation, in the way they pause, the way they speak in phrases punctuated by full breaths, as if consciously drawing warmth into themselves.

Their skin tones range from olive to deep brown, weathered by the diffuse light and constant moisture into a particular quality—not dry and sun-darkened, but somehow deepened, enriched, like wood that's been oiled. The damp air preserves rather than desiccates. Elders have surprisingly supple skin for their years, though marked by the fine lines of lifelong squinting in overcast light. Some have a faint mineral staining, especially on the lower legs and feet, where the clay-rich water leaves traces of iron oxide in rust-colored patterns that fade but never quite disappear.

Hair is universally dark—black to deep brown—and worn practically. The most common style is bound back, braided or wrapped, kept away from the face and out of the work. Many use simple clay beads as fasteners, thumb-sized spheres with holes through them, marked with the same symbols that appear on walls. The children often have shorter hair, cropped close, though not shaved. The elders—there aren't many, but those who've survived to advanced age—sometimes let their hair grow longer, wearing it in complex braids that incorporate reed fibers or thin leather cords. Gray hair is rare enough to be notable, remarked upon, carrying a particular status.

They dress in layers, always. The base layer is usually a simple tunic of woven plant fiber—something local, processed from the reeds or from plants grown on the drier margins. The weave is visible, unrefined, but tight enough for basic warmth. The fiber is naturally gray-brown, occasionally with darker brown or black undyed patterns worked in—simple stripes or checks, achieved by using reeds harvested at different times or from different areas. Over this, most people wear a second layer of leather, usually a vest or sleeveless jerkin, simply cut, with the flesh side worn inward for softness. The leather is thick, water-resistant, treated with some combination of oils that gives it a slight sheen and a distinctive smell—animal, yes, but also mineral, like the smell of the clay itself has infused the tanning process.

Their legs are covered in wrapped cloth or loose trousers bound at the knee and ankle, keeping the fabric from dragging in the wet. These wrappings get soaked regularly and are changed out, hung to dry on the frames you see between buildings. The drying textiles become part of the settlement's visual language—vertical lines of fabric in various states of dampness, darkest at the bottom where water wicks down, gradually lighter toward the top.

The cold and wet means they also wear cloaks, though "cloak" suggests something more formal than these are. They're rough rectangles of felted wool—the settlement keeps small, hardy sheep or goat-like animals on the drier margins, you realize, though you haven't seen them yet. The felt is thick, naturally water-shedding to a degree, marked with simple patterns achieved by laying dark and light wool during the felting process. When it rains, everyone moves through the settlement hooded and cloaked, looking like mobile hillocks, shapes rather than individuals.

But it's their faces that stay with you. The Tílmak have a particular expression, a resting countenance that reads as watchful patience. Their eyes—dark brown to black almost universally—have a quality of attention, of noticing. They look at things with focus, whether it's the flow of water in a channel, the color of clay in a fresh-dug brick, or a stranger standing at the settlement's edge. It's not suspicion exactly, though they're not naive. It's assessment. They're a people who've learned to read subtle signs—the shine on wet clay that indicates a weak spot, the angle of water flow that suggests an underground seam, the smell of air before rain. They bring this reading to human interaction as well.

They don't smile readily, but when they do, it transforms the face—genuine warmth, often accompanied by a particular soft exhalation that's almost a laugh but more restrained. Humor exists among them, but it's dry, understated, often centered on the absurdities of their situation. A joke about water is always funny. A joke about someone building on ground that turned out to be wetter than expected is hilarious. They have a whole vocabulary of words that describe different kinds of wetness, different consistencies of mud, and many of these words double as gentle insults or terms of endearment.

Children are rare enough to be precious, but not so rare as to be desperately clung to. The Tílmak have a birth rate that roughly maintains their population—perhaps a slow growth over generations, but not explosive. Infant mortality is present but not catastrophic. Children who survive the first few years tend to be hardy. You see them playing in the channels, building small dams with mud and pebbles, imitating the work they see adults doing. They're given real work early, by five or six—carrying water, helping to shape bricks, gathering reeds. By ten, they're contributing members of the community, apprenticed informally to whatever work needs doing.

The children have a particular game they play, something that seems to be both entertainment and training: they build miniature settlements in the wet clay, complete with tiny channels, little brick structures made from properly fired clay pebbles, placed just so to manage the flow of water when they pour it from cupped hands. The best builders among the children gain status. Adults sometimes stop to watch, to offer quiet comments. The game is clearly ancient—older children teach younger ones, and there are traditional patterns that must be learned, standard problems that must be solved. It's how they learn to think about water and structure before they're strong enough to work at full scale.

Adolescents have a restless energy that the settlement struggles to fully employ. You see them in groups, working together on the heavier tasks—moving logs for new corduroy paths, digging clay from the pits, carrying loads of brick. They're louder than the adults, still learning the modulated quiet that characterizes Tílmak social interaction. Some are clearly courting, in the way of young people everywhere, but it's done with a particular Tílmak flavor—showing off skill at pottery, demonstrating knowledge of the best clay sources, offering help with someone's family kiln. Prowess in practical matters is what attracts.

Marriage, when it happens, is marked by the couple building something together—usually a small addition to an existing structure, a new room or a rebuilt wall, something that demonstrates their ability to work in tandem, to plan and execute. The whole settlement participates in small ways, donating bricks, helping to source wood, but the core work is done by the couple. When it's complete and the roof is sound, there's a gathering, a meal shared in the new space, water poured from a vessel the couple made together to christen the channels that will drain their roof. Then they're married, in the eyes of the community. The structure itself is the ceremony.

The elderly are respected but not venerated. They've survived, which means something, but the Tílmak are too practical to pretend that survival alone grants infinite wisdom. Elders who retain their capacity to work continue working. Those who can't take on advisory roles, but only if their advice proves useful. An elder who keeps suggesting bad solutions to drainage problems will be politely ignored. One who can read weather, who remembers the pattern of wet years and dry (relatively) years, who can identify the best clay strata by smell—that elder will be consulted, fed, cared for.

Death, when it comes, is handled with practical reverence. The Tílmak don't bury their dead in the wet clay—the ground won't keep bodies, won't hold them. Instead, they practice cremation in the kilns. A body is wrapped in reed mats and fired at high temperature until only bone fragments remain. These are then crushed and mixed with clay, formed into bricks marked with the deceased's particular symbol—everyone has one, chosen in youth or earned through some defining act. These memorial bricks are incorporated into structures, usually at the foundation or at the corners, so that the dead literally become part of the settlement, holding it up, keeping it stable. People can point to bricks and say, "That's my grandmother there, in the corner of the communal kiln. That's my uncle, in the east wall of the granary."

This practice means the settlement itself is an archive of the dead. Every building contains ancestors. The oldest structures—or their foundations, now buried under later building—hold the remains of the first generation, the ones who decided to settle here when the lake bed was still slick and new. There's a sense of continuity, of accumulation, that shapes how the Tílmak think about themselves. They're not just living here; they're adding to something, building on something, becoming part of something that will outlast them in a very literal way.

The social structure is relatively flat. There's no evident kingship, no hereditary nobility. Leadership exists but it's earned and specific. The best potter doesn't necessarily have any say in how channels are dug. The person most skilled at kiln-building might have no opinion on dispute resolution. Authority is distributed based on competence in particular domains. Decisions that affect the whole settlement—where to expand, whether to dig a new major channel, how to respond to a crisis—these are made in gatherings at the central basin, with anyone who has relevant knowledge or who'll be affected speaking. It's not quite democracy, more like practical consensus. People defer to expertise, but expertise must continually prove itself.

There are specialists, though. The potters are recognized as a distinct group, marked by their particularly clay-stained hands and a certain pride in their work. They make everything from simple storage vessels to fine drinking bowls, marked with symbols and patterns. Each potter has a style, identifiable if you know what to look for. The best pieces are traded outside the settlement—yes, there is outside, there are traders who come occasionally, willing to navigate the wet approaches for Tílmak pottery, which has a reputation for watertightness that's almost supernatural. In return, the settlement gets bronze tools (they have some metalworking capacity, but limited), dyes (rare, but valued for marking important textiles), salt (crucial, hard to source locally), and information about the world beyond the basin.

The kiln-masters are another specialized group. Fire in a wet place is both essential and difficult. The kilns must be designed to handle damp fuel, to draw properly despite humid air, to reach temperatures high enough for proper firing without cracking. Kiln-building is an art passed down through apprenticeship. A good kiln-master is valued, sought after, their opinion weighted in community decisions because fire is life here—it makes the bricks that make the buildings, it processes the clay, it cremates the dead, it cooks the food.

There are also the water-readers—not an official title, but a recognized role. These are people who understand the hydrology of the basin at a deep, intuitive level. They can look at ground surface and tell you where water will pool. They can predict which channels will flow fastest after rain. They know where the underground seams are, where the water is rising from. When a new building is planned, a water-reader walks the site, assesses, advises. Sometimes they mark the ground with stakes, indicating where foundations should go, where drainage must be installed. Their knowledge is partly empirical, partly something else—a sensitivity to wetness, to the movement of water through clay, that seems almost preternatural. They tend to be quiet people, spending long hours simply watching channels, observing flows, building their mental maps of the hidden water.

The Tílmak have spiritual practices, though "spiritual" might not be the word they'd use. They have relationships with the elements of their world that include acknowledgment, respect, and what might be called negotiation. The clay is not quite a god, but it's not merely material either. It's... responsive. It rewards good treatment and proper technique. It remembers being lake bottom, and it can be reminded to stay solid, to hold its shape, to accept the work of human hands. The marks on the bricks, the symbols pressed into wet clay, these are partly identification and partly conversation—telling the clay what you need from it, reminding it of its agreement to become wall, to become vessel, to become path.

Water is similar. It will flow where it's directed, if you ask properly, if you maintain the channels, if you respect its need to move. The water-readers sometimes perform small ceremonies—nothing elaborate, just the pouring of water from one vessel to another at a channel junction, the speaking of certain words in their language that describe flow and drainage and the downward pull of earth. It looks practical. It might be practical. But there's intentionality to it that goes beyond mere engineering.

They have no temples, no priests in the formal sense. The central basin serves as a gathering place for community moments that might be called religious—seasonal acknowledgments when the rains increase or decrease, memorial pourings when someone dies, celebrations when a major building project is completed successfully. The wooden post with its hanging objects is clearly significant, but ask a Tílmak what it's for and they'll look at you with mild confusion. It's for hanging things, obviously. It marks the center. The water flows to it and from it. What else would you need to know?

Their calendar is based on water cycles rather than solar years. They mark time by the wet season and the less-wet season (they don't call anything "dry"), with smaller divisions based on observable changes in the basin—when certain plants begin to grow, when water in the channels reaches particular levels, when the ground transitions from merely damp to actively weeping. They count years from the settlement's founding, and everyone knows the number. They're currently in year forty-seven or forty-eight—the transition happened recently and there's mild disagreement about exactly when, which is characteristic. The Tílmak don't fuss much over precision in things that don't directly affect building or drainage.

Their language is soft, full of sibilants and liquid consonants, few hard stops. It has an unusually large vocabulary for describing water, clay, and the interactions between them. There are verbs that exist only to describe specific types of seepage. There are nouns for different consistencies of mud that have no equivalents in other tongues. The word order is flexible, driven by emphasis rather than strict rules, which gives their speech a flowing quality that mirrors the water in their channels.

They sing while they work, often. Not grand songs, but rhythmic chants that coordinate labor—when multiple people are carrying something heavy, when a group is digging clay, when bricks are being passed along a line to a building site. The songs have few words, mostly repeated sounds that mark rhythm. But sometimes, in the evening, you might hear something more complex—longer songs with verses, telling stories of the settlement's founding, of particular floods or building projects, of individuals who did something memorable. These are performed without instruments, just voices, usually solo or with a small group responding in chorus. The acoustics of the settlement are surprisingly good—sound carries between buildings, reflects off clay walls, so that a singer in one area can be heard clearly several structures away.

There's laughter, more than you might expect in such a damp and demanding place. It emerges around shared meals, around the evening fires, when people gather after work. The Tílmak have discovered humor in their situation—they have to, to maintain sanity. They tell stories about spectacular building failures, about people who didn't listen to water-readers and regretted it, about the absurdity of building a permanent settlement in a place that doesn't want to be built on. There's a particular story, often retold with variations, about someone in the second generation who tried to dig a well. The punchline is that they struck water everywhere. It's never not funny.

Food is simple but adequate. The settlement doesn't have much arable land, but they maintain small intensive gardens on the highest ground, growing hardy vegetables that tolerate cool and damp—root crops, leafy greens that bolt slowly, herbs for flavoring and medicine. They practice a kind of raised bed agriculture, building up soil in wooden frames filled with composted material and the least clay-heavy soil they can source. These gardens require constant maintenance, but they produce.

The primary protein comes from fishing and fowling in the wetter margins of the basin, and from small livestock—the sheep or goats mentioned earlier, kept on the drier periphery, along with something like rabbits or guinea pigs, small animals kept in raised hutches, fed on garden scraps and reed shoots. Meat is not daily, but it's regular. The animals are butchered communally, the meat shared according to family size and need. Hides are processed for leather. Bones become tools, or are fired and crushed and mixed with clay to make grog for pottery.

The reed seeds are harvested and ground into a flour that makes a heavy, dark bread, gritty with minerals from the clay that inevitably gets into everything. It's baked in simple ovens, small domed structures of clay attached to houses or shared between families. The bread keeps poorly in the damp, so it's made in small batches, frequently. It's sometimes flavored with fish oil, or with the rendered fat from the small livestock, and with herbs when available.

They eat communally more often than not, families and neighbor groups gathering in the small plazas, sharing from common pots of stew or porridge, thick concoctions that contain whatever's available—roots, greens, fish, sometimes meat, all cooked together until it's dense enough to eat with the bread or with reed-pith cakes. Meals are social times, when information is shared, when decisions are made, when disputes are aired and resolved. Children eat with adults, learning by watching and listening.

There's a beverage they make from fermented reed shoots, slightly alcoholic, sour-sweet, with a vegetal taste that's an acquired preference. It's drunk from small clay cups, passed around during evening gatherings. Some households produce better batches than others, and there's friendly competition, though never anything so aggressive as to disrupt community harmony. They can't afford feuds. The settlement is too small, the work too interdependent.

Health is generally good, within the parameters of their technology and situation. The constant dampness prevents some diseases—they have fewer problems with dust-related ailments—but encourages others. Respiratory infections are common, especially in children and elders. There's a healer, or several people with healing knowledge, who treat these with herbal preparations, with steam inhalations, with practical care. They understand that warmth and dryness help, so the sick are moved near fires, given extra coverings, fed hot broths.

Injuries from work are frequent but usually minor—cuts, bruises, the occasional broken bone from a fall on wet wood. These are treated with practical competence. They set bones straight, splint them with reed bundles and leather wrappings. They clean wounds with water boiled and cooled, bandage them with clean cloth, check them daily for signs of infection. Their understanding of medicine is empirical and reasonably effective.

Childbirth is attended by women who've done it before, who've learned from watching and assisting. It happens in the home, with the woman supported by family and neighbors. The mother squats or kneels, and the baby is received by practiced hands, cleaned, checked, wrapped. The infant mortality rate is not insignificant, but those who survive the first year tend to be robust. Women recover near the fire, fed rich broths, given weeks to heal before returning to full work. The community absorbs the temporary loss of labor.

The Tílmak have a concept of beauty that's inseparable from function. A beautifully made brick isn't just smooth and regular—though those qualities are appreciated—it's beautiful because it will build a better wall, because it will shed water more effectively, because the clay was read correctly and the firing was precise. A beautiful pot holds water without seepage, pours without dripping, and the decoration pressed into its surface serves to identify its maker and its intended use while also pleasing the eye. Form and function aren't separate categories.

This extends to their personal aesthetics. People take pride in well-made clothing, in skillfully braided hair, in clean hands despite the clay that never fully washes away. But this pride is always tempered by practicality. Decoration exists—simple patterns woven into textiles, carved into tool handles, pressed into the clay beads used to fasten clothing—but it's never purely ornamental. Every mark means something, identifies someone, indicates function.

There's a particular aesthetic pleasure they take in good water management. A well-designed channel system, flowing perfectly after rain, is something people will stop to admire. They'll bring neighbors to see it, discuss its features, analyze its success. The same attention might be given to a particularly well-fired brick, to a pot that rings with perfect clarity when tapped, to a roof that sheds water in exactly the right pattern. These are their arts, as real and valued as sculpture or painting in other cultures.

When traders come—and they do, occasionally, brave merchants seeking the renowned pottery—the Tílmak engage with cautious interest. They're not isolated by choice, just by circumstance. The basin is difficult to reach, and there's not much here to attract casual visitors. But they're curious about the world beyond, hungry for news, for stories of places that are dry, where water must be brought to fields instead of constantly channeled away.

They trade carefully, valuing their pottery appropriately but not extravagantly. They know its worth—watertight vessels are valuable—but they also know they need what the traders bring more than the traders need what they have. So the exchanges are balanced, respectful on both sides. Sometimes a young person leaves with the traders, seeking experience beyond the basin. Sometimes they come back, usually within a year or two, having decided that they prefer the known difficulties of home to the unknown difficulties of elsewhere. Sometimes they don't come back, and the settlement marks them as gone, incorporates their memorial brick into a structure based on memory rather than ashes.

The Tílmak are not aggressive or expansionist. They have nowhere to expand to, and no desire to. The basin is their place, and they've committed to it with the totality that comes from forty-seven or forty-eight years of literally building your ancestors into your foundations. They defend themselves if necessary—they're practical about that—but conflict is rare. Who would attack them? What would be gained? Anyone trying to take the settlement would find themselves in possession of damp clay, leaky buildings, and complex drainage systems that stop working the moment the knowledge to maintain them is lost.

They accept that they're odd, these Tílmak. They know their settlement looks improbable to outsiders, that their relationship with water seems excessive, that their practice of mixing ancestor-ashes into bricks is unsettling to cultures that bury their dead in the ground and want them to stay there. They don't evangelize or explain themselves. If you understand, good. If you don't, also fine. The clay doesn't care about your opinion, and neither do they, particularly.

But if you stay, if you're willing to learn, they'll teach you. How to walk on slick clay without falling. How to read the shine on water to predict weather. How to press a brick so it holds together in firing. Which channels flow fastest, which pools stay cleanest. They'll feed you the dense bread and the reed-shoot beer and the communal stews. They'll find you a space in some structure, a corner where you can sleep dry, or at least less wet than outside. They'll watch you, assess you, decide if you're someone who might understand what they're doing here.

Because what they're doing here is strange and specific and somehow essential. They're proving that you can live in a place that doesn't want to be lived in, if you're willing to adapt enough, to work constantly, to build in conversation with water and clay rather than in opposition to them. They're proving that a community can be built on shared practical knowledge, on distributed expertise, on the understanding that everyone's work matters because everyone's work literally holds up everyone else.

They're building something that will last, in a place where nothing should be permanent. They're making a home in a place that's still deciding whether to be land or lake. And they're doing it one brick at a time, one channel at a time, one generation mixed into the walls at a time.

They are the Tílmak. Those who press, and are pressed. Shapers of clay, shaped by it. Standing in the wet, building up, slowly, together. Not conquering the basin, not taming it. Living with it. Making something human in a place that isn't, quite. Marking time by water cycles, marking walls with symbols, marking their existence by the steady accumulation of structures that grow from the ground like patient, geometric plants.

When you leave—if you leave—they'll nod goodbye without ceremony. Return the way you came, along the corduroy paths, past the channels and kilns. They'll already be back at their work before you're out of sight, pressing clay, tending channels, building higher, building forward, continuing the long conversation with water and earth that defines their lives. The rain will probably start soon. It usually does. And the Tílmak will pull their hoods up, adjust their footing on the slick wood, and keep working, because the rain is just water, and water is what this place is made of, and they are made of this place now, forty-seven or forty-eight years deep, down to the clay in their skin and the ancestors in their walls.
