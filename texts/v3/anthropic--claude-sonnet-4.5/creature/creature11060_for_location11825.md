# The Inhabitants of the Terraced Hollows

They are called the **Rendered**, though whether they named themselves this or were named by some long-departed other is lost to the depth of their history. The name—if you could hear it in their own utterance—sounds like stones rolled in water, a throaty percussion that carries both weight and flow.

## Physical Form

An adult Rendered stands perhaps four and a half feet tall, though they rarely stand fully upright for long. Their natural posture is a forward cant, weight distributed among hands and feet, spine curved in a way that suggests this is the body's preferred geometry. The proportions are unusual: long arms, shorter legs, a torso that seems compressed, built for stability rather than reach.

The fur is sparse by mammalian standards, more a close nap than a pelt, colored in variations of clay-brown and stone-grey with occasional individuals showing rust-red or pale yellow undertones. It grows thicker at the joints—elbows, knees, the nape of the neck—perhaps for protection in those vulnerable places. Older Rendered have fur that has worn away entirely in patches, particularly on the hands and the pads of the feet, revealing skin the color of weathered leather, deeply creased and surprisingly thick.

The faces are broad and flat, with large eyes positioned more laterally than forward—their field of vision is wide, depth perception less acute than a human's but peripheral awareness remarkable. You never surprise a Rendered; they have always seen you coming from the edge of their vision. The eyes themselves are amber or brown or occasionally a pale green, with pupils that can contract to pinpoints in bright light or expand to capture every photon during the dark hours.

The nose is minimal, almost flush with the face, with nostrils that can seal completely—you see them do this when dust rises from disturbed soil or when they're working with particularly pungent materials. The mouth is wide and lipless, revealing teeth when they vocalize: flat molars for grinding, sharp incisors that suggest an omnivorous past even if their present diet is something else.

But it is the hands that define them. Four fingers, two of which can oppose the others from different angles, allowing them to grip in multiple configurations. The fingers are long and remarkably strong, callused at the tips and along the inner edges. There are no nails exactly—instead, hardened pads that can feel texture with extraordinary sensitivity while still being durable enough to work stone hour after hour. Watch a Rendered select a stone for a wall and you see the fingers moving across its surface, reading something—weight distribution, hidden fractures, the way it will settle against its neighbors—through touch alone.

Their feet are similarly adapted: broad, with opposed toes that can grip, allowing them to navigate vertical surfaces and narrow beams with casual confidence. The gait on flat ground is rolling, almost awkward, but put them on a terrace wall or a timber framework and they move like water finding its level.

## Age and Variation

The young are recognizable by proportion—larger heads, shorter limbs, and fur that is thicker and softer. They move in clusters, always within arm's reach of adults, often clinging to backs or bellies as the adults work. The very young make higher-pitched vocalizations, and you notice that adults respond to these sounds immediately, interrupting work to adjust position, offer a hand, provide access to water or food.

Adolescents are lankier, all limbs and energy, and they are everywhere at once. They are the ones climbing highest, moving fastest, taking risks that make older Rendered vocalize in what might be warnings or might simply be commentary. They are learning by doing, attempting every task they see, usually alongside an adult who corrects position, demonstrates technique, or simply allows the attempt to fail in non-dangerous ways.

Adults in their prime are compact and efficient, every movement economical. They have settled into their strength and skill. These are the ones who work the most complex tasks—the fitted stone walls that require precise selection, the timber frameworks that must bear weight, the water system maintenance that requires understanding of the entire network.

The elderly are fewer but present. Their fur has thinned, their movements are slower, but they are not retired. They work steadier, perhaps, less rushed. You see them doing tasks that require patience more than strength: sorting materials into grades, working fiber into rope or weaving, sitting with young ones and demonstrating technique with exaggerated clarity. Their vocalizations are lower, more infrequent, but other Rendered respond to them with particular attention.

There are also those who are different—variations that might be natural diversity or might be something else. Some Rendered are larger, with thicker builds and hands that seem designed for heavy work. Others are smaller, more slender, with fingers that move with unusual precision and eyes that seem to track details others miss. Whether these are subspecies, deliberate breeding outcomes, or simply the extremes of normal variation is unclear. They are integrated without apparent hierarchy; the large work alongside the small, and tasks seem to find the bodies suited for them.

## Social Texture

The Rendered do not appear to have what humans would recognize as family units, yet they are not anonymous to each other. You see preferential associations—certain individuals work together frequently, rest in the same clusters, exchange vocalizations that others do not respond to. Whether these are friendships, partnerships, mentorships, or something without a human analogue is uncertain.

The young are communally tended. Any adult will respond to a juvenile's distress call, offer food, provide assistance. You never see a young one alone or unsupervised, but neither do you see exclusive parent-child bonds. Juveniles move between adults fluidly, and are accepted by whoever they approach.

During rest periods—and these come not at fixed times but seemingly when enough individuals are tired that critical mass is reached—they cluster. Bodies press together, creating mounds of fur and limbs where it becomes difficult to distinguish individuals. This appears to serve thermoregulation, comfort, and perhaps communication; you hear quiet vocalizations within the rest-clusters, ongoing even in what should be sleep.

There are no apparent leaders, and yet coordination happens. A wall section needs repair; somehow the right number of Rendered with the appropriate skills arrive and begin work. A harvest needs to happen; individuals converge on the plant in question. You never see discussion, debate, or instruction in the human sense. Instead there is observation, imitation, and a kind of consensus that emerges from many individuals making slightly different choices that somehow average into coherent group action.

Conflict is rare but not absent. You occasionally witness what might be disagreements—two Rendered reaching for the same stone, vocalizing sharply at each other. These resolve quickly, usually with one backing off, sometimes with a third Rendered approaching and doing something—touching both, vocalizing in a particular pattern—that ends the tension. Physical violence appears essentially absent. The density of their living would make aggression catastrophically disruptive; they have evolved or learned past it.

## Communication

The vocalizations are complex, multi-layered, and constant. There are sounds that seem to be labels—a particular grunt that accompanies finding water, a specific chirp when a tool or material is located. There are sounds that coordinate action—rhythmic barking that matches the timing of collective lifting, whistles that might indicate direction or location.

But there are also vocalizations that seem purely social—exchanges between individuals working near each other, ongoing narrations of action, sounds made to self while working alone that others may or may not respond to. The line between communication and simply making sound is blurred.

Gesture is equally important. A tilt of the head indicates attention or interest. A particular positioning of hands signals intent to move in a direction, allowing others to clear space. The touching is constant and varied—brief contacts as they pass, longer gripping of arms or shoulders, the pressing together of palms that might be greeting or agreement or simply acknowledgment.

They also communicate through their work. You begin to recognize individual styles—one Rendered consistently selects darker stones, another arranges them in a subtle pattern, a third leaves a particular type of gap in the jointing. These are not signatures in the human sense, but they are recognizable differences that others seem to notice and respond to. A section of wall might be modified by another Rendered, and you see the original builder return, examine it, and either vocalize approval or quietly adjust it again. This is conversation in stone and clay.

Scent appears to play a role. They have glands—you can see slight swellings behind the ears and at the wrists—that must produce secretions. In close quarters, you occasionally see one Rendered pause and position their nose near another's neck or wrist, holding still for a moment before continuing on. Information is being exchanged, though what kind is opaque.

## Relationship with Their World

The Rendered do not simply live in their built environment; they are in constant dialogue with it. Every Rendered passing a channel checks it, even if subconsciously—a glance, a hand trailing to feel water flow, a quick clearing of debris. Every one passing a plant seems to assess it—touching a leaf, adjusting its position, occasionally harvesting something or simply noting its state.

This is not work in the human sense, divided into labor and leisure. This is continuous low-level maintenance integrated into every moment. A Rendered sitting in what appears to be rest will still reach out and adjust a stone in a nearby wall, pull a weed from a terrace, redirect a water flow with one hand while remaining settled.

They use tools, but minimally. The tools are mostly found objects adapted to purpose—a particular stone that fits the hand well for hammering, a hardened stem that works as a pry bar, a shell or gourd for carrying. These tools are not kept or owned; they are used and set down, picked up by the next individual who needs that function. You see tools in constant circulation, appearing where they're needed as if by coordination, though it's likely just probability—with so many individuals moving through the space, someone always has or finds what's required.

Their knowledge of plants is profound and seemingly innate, though it must be learned. You see young Rendered being guided to specific plants, watching as adults demonstrate harvest timing or preparation methods. The adults never take more than a portion from any plant, and they seem to rotate through areas, allowing recovery. This is not conservation ethics arrived at through reasoning but behavior so deeply embedded it appears instinctual.

Their relationship with water borders on reverence without being religious. Water is never wasted, but neither is it hoarded. The system is designed for flow and sharing. You never see one Rendered preventing another's access. During the brief rains, they become more active, almost playful—mouths open to the sky, bodies positioned to channel water toward storage, vocalizations taking on different tones that might be pleasure or excitement.

## Work and Purpose

Every Rendered works, but work is not uniform. Some spend most of their time on stone walls, developing deep expertise in selection and placement. Others focus on the vegetation, knowing each plant's needs and products. Still others are constantly moving through the water system, maintaining flows, clearing blockages, noting levels.

There are specialists of a sort. Some Rendered work almost exclusively with fiber—harvesting, processing, spinning it into cordage, weaving it into panels. You see them surrounded by their materials, hands moving in patterns that have become automatic, producing lengths of rope or sheets of fabric that others immediately incorporate into structures.

Others seem to be coordinators, though without authority. They move through areas, observing work, vocalizing frequently, and somehow work adjusts around their presence. They don't give orders, but after they pass through, you notice that resources have been redistributed, that individuals have shifted tasks, that the work in that area proceeds more smoothly.

The very elderly often work with the young, not formally teaching but allowing observation and imitation. A old Rendered sorting stones into piles by some complex criteria will have juveniles nearby, picking up stones, making their own piles, occasionally having their choices adjusted by the elder's weathered hands.

There are also those who seem to be explorers or scouts. You see them at the edges of the terraced lands, moving into less-developed areas, examining terrain, sometimes returning with materials or information that leads to expansion of the system. They are more solitary than typical, comfortable with less-constant contact, but still fully integrated when they return to the denser zones.

## Diet and Sustenance

The Rendered are eating constantly, but you rarely see formal meals. Instead, there is continuous grazing—a handful of seeds taken from a communal pile, leaves plucked from certain plants as they pass, fruits harvested and consumed immediately or shared with whoever is nearby.

The foods are varied: nuts and seeds stored in carefully maintained dry areas, fruits from the heavier vegetation, roots dug from terrace floors, fungi that grow on designated deadwood, and leaves from specific plants. Some foods are processed—you see seeds being ground between stones, leaves being dried in the sun, roots being left in water to soften.

Protein appears to come from insects and small invertebrates that thrive in the constructed ecosystem. The Rendered harvest these methodically from specific areas, selecting them from under stones or shaking them from plants. These are eaten immediately or dried for storage. The harvesting is controlled enough that the populations sustain; this is integrated pest management that doubles as food source.

Water is drunk frequently, directly from the pools and channels. They dip their faces, lap with tongues that are longer than expected, fill vessels to carry to others. Hydration is clearly critical in their warm, active environment, and the water system ensures constant access.

Waste is managed as mentioned—specific areas for defecation, but also for urination, and these zones feed into the compost systems that eventually return nutrients to the terraces. Nothing is lost; everything cycles.

## Reproduction and Continuation

Mating is not obvious or public, suggesting it happens in more private spaces or during the darker hours. Pregnancy, when you can identify it, shows as a slight swelling of the abdomen, but pregnant Rendered continue working, adjusting tasks to physical capability but not ceasing activity.

Birth must happen in the structures, in the more enclosed spaces, because you never witness it. But you see new young appearing, tiny and clinging to adult bodies, and the entire group seems to subtly shift to accommodate them—movements become slightly more careful, vocalizations include more of the higher frequencies the young respond to, more individuals position themselves near the mother.

The young develop slowly. It is months before they attempt independent movement, longer before they can navigate the vertical environment, years before they're working with the skill of adults. But the entire group shares this burden and investment. The young are the only non-productive members of the society, and they are supported without question.

Mortality happens, though you don't often see it. You notice that an elderly Rendered you'd been observing is no longer present. Sometimes you find a body being carried by several adults to a specific area at the edge of the terraces where the dead are left to decompose, returned to the system they sustained. There is no ceremony, but there is gentleness in the carrying, and you see other Rendered pause as the body passes, becoming still for a moment before resuming work.

## Intelligence and Consciousness

The Rendered are intelligent—that is clear in the complexity of their built environment, the sustainability of their systems, the sophistication of their coordination. But it is intelligence of a different architecture than human cognition.

They seem to lack symbolic representation. You see no art, no decoration for its own sake, no marks or symbols that might be writing or recording. The patterns in their work are functional or emergent from craft technique, not intentionally decorative.

Their time sense appears to be circular rather than linear—they respond to cycles, to patterns, to the rhythms of water and growth, but you see no evidence they count days or track longer periods. They know when to harvest through observation of the plants themselves, not through calendrical calculation.

Their spatial understanding is extraordinary. They navigate the three-dimensional maze of terraces and frameworks without hesitation, remember the locations of resources across miles of terrain, can return to a specific stone in a specific wall. The landscape is mapped in their minds with remarkable precision.

They solve problems, but through iteration rather than planning. You see a Rendered trying to position a timber, finding it doesn't fit, adjusting it, trying again, making small modifications until it works. There is no standing back to visualize the solution before attempting it—there is trying, learning from the attempt, trying again.

Their memory is clearly functional—they recognize individuals, remember locations, recall techniques. But whether they remember narrative—whether they have stories of their past, whether they conceive of themselves as historical beings—is unanswerable from observation.

Self-awareness is present in some form. They recognize themselves as individuals distinct from others, have preferences, make choices. But whether there is internal dialogue, whether they experience qualia the way humans report experiencing it, whether there is a sense of "I" as humans construct it—these are questions that observation cannot answer.

## Emotional Life

The Rendered experience something. Whether to call these experiences emotions is definitional.

They show clear distress when injured—vocalizing sharply, seeking help from others, favoring the wounded area. They show what might be satisfaction after completing a difficult task—a particular posture, a certain vocalization, a stillness that seems content. They show what could be frustration when something doesn't work—repeated attempts with increasing vocal intensity.

The young display what reads as playfulness—chasing each other through the terraces, manipulating objects in ways that serve no obvious function, engaging in what looks like wrestling that causes no harm.

Adults show what might be affection—the touching, the grooming, the choice to rest in contact with specific others. They show what could be concern—responding quickly to distress calls, adjusting work to help struggling individuals, bringing food to those who seem tired.

But whether these are emotions in the human sense—whether there is subjective experience, whether they feel rather than simply respond—is unknowable from outside. The behaviors suggest rich internal states, but the nature of those states remains their own.

## Relationship with Others

The Rendered's response to you—or to any outsider—is complex. They are aware of your presence but do not engage. Eyes track you, work patterns adjust to route around you, but there is no approach, no investigation, no apparent curiosity.

This might be learned caution, might be disinterest, might be a kind of respect for boundaries. If you remain still, they work around you as they would work around a boulder. If you move, they flow away from your path like water around an obstacle.

There is no hostility but also no invitation. You are not attacked, not driven away, but neither are you incorporated. You are outside their system, and their system is everything to them. They do not need to understand you; they need only to continue their work.

If you stay long enough, the initial awareness of your presence seems to fade. They become habituated to you as a feature of the landscape, if an unusual one. But you never become one of them, never merge into the collective work. The gulf is unbridgeable not through hostility but through sheer difference.

Whether they remember you after you leave, whether they tell others about the strange visitor, whether you become part of their lore—if they have lore—is uncertain. You suspect you are forgotten quickly, if not immediately. Their focus is their work, their system, the endless maintenance of their constructed world. Everything else is peripheral, ephemeral, ultimately irrelevant.

## The Pattern of Days

The rhythm of activity follows light but doesn't stop in darkness. During bright hours, work involving vision—precise stone fitting, plant identification, detailed fiber work—predominates. As light fades, activity shifts to tasks navigable by touch and memory—channel maintenance, moving materials along known paths, rest-cluster formation.

But some work continues through all hours. You hear stone-on-stone through the darkness, the sounds of harvest, the ongoing vocalizations. The Rendered do not have a single wake-sleep cycle; instead, individuals rest and wake asynchronously, so that the overall activity never ceases, only shifts in intensity and character.

There are periods of higher energy—perhaps after rains, or during certain seasonal transitions—when activity intensifies, more individuals are active simultaneously, work proceeds faster. There are quieter periods when more are resting, when work is slower though never stopped.

The pattern is not rigid. It responds to conditions—heat, moisture, the state of the plants, the needs of the infrastructure. The Rendered adapt continuously, and because they are so numerous, the system can respond to changing conditions without any individual needing to work dramatically harder.

## The Deep Continuity

The most striking aspect of the Rendered is their continuation. The terraces are centuries old at minimum, possibly far older. The systems are mature, refined by countless generations. The Rendered themselves carry this history in their skills, their knowledge, their adapted bodies.

They are what happens when a species commits entirely to a single way of living, refines it across deep time, integrates into it so completely that they and their environment are one system. They have forgone expansion for intensification, conquest for maintenance, novelty for perfection of method.

They are not ambitious in the human sense. They do not seek to grow, to spread, to change their fundamental pattern. They have found an equilibrium and they sustain it. Their intelligence is bent entirely toward this sustaining.

In this, they are profoundly alien to human sensibility while being absolutely comprehensible in their behavior. Every action makes sense, serves purpose, fits into the pattern. But the pattern itself—the willingness to subsume individual desire into collective function, to work ceaselessly at maintenance, to find sufficiency rather than seeking more—is foreign to the human operating system.

They are proof that intelligence can take forms that share no common language with human consciousness. They are complex, sophisticated, successful, and utterly themselves. They do not need to be understood by outsiders because understanding from outside is irrelevant to their continuation.

The sound continues. Work continues. Water flows through the channels. Bodies move through the terraces. The Rendered persist in their warm, damp, crowded, functioning world, as they have persisted, as they will persist, for as long as the water comes and the plants grow and their hands can shape stone.
