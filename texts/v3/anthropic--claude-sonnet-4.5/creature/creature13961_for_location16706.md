# Those Who Dwell in Accretion

The people here move like they're learning to walk on a ship—not unstable, but *attentive*. Every footfall is a question asked of the stone: *Are you still where you were yesterday?* They develop a particular gait by their second year, weight shifting in ways that look cautious to outsiders but is really just *listening* with their feet. The oldest residents don't even think about it anymore; their bodies know that hallways extend, that thresholds migrate, that a staircase might have gained or lost a step since morning.

Children born on the plateau are different.

Not physically—or not much, though their pediatricians note unusually dense bones, as if the mineral water their mothers drank has left deposits in the fetal skeleton. But perceptually. They don't experience the temporal inconsistencies as inconsistencies. A four-year-old will tell you matter-of-factly that the walk to her grandmother's house is "three songs and a story," and she's right every time, even when that duration translates to fifteen minutes one day and forty-five the next. They see the salamanders not as strange but as *people*, a different kind of people, and will hold extended conversations with them that consist of long mutual silences punctuated by the child rearranging pebbles and the salamander doing the same. Parents have learned not to interrupt these exchanges.

Teenagers go through a phase where they *test* the city's mutability. They'll spend hours trying to make a door appear, or willing a wall to become permeable, or attempting to redirect water through sheer intention. It never works—or works so rarely that it might be coincidence—but the trying is itself a kind of puberty rite, a way of learning the plateau's boundaries, or confirming that boundaries here are probabilistic rather than absolute. The adults pretend not to notice, remembering their own adolescent experiments. There's an unspoken agreement: let them try. Let them learn what can and can't be negotiated with.

The engineers are the plateau's priests, though they'd reject that terminology.

They wear leather aprons black with waterproofing grease, rubber boots that come to mid-thigh, and carry notebooks in oilskin pouches. Their hands are permanently stained with mineral deposits—white calcium carbonate under the nails, rust-colored iron oxide in the creases of their palms. They can read the water by taste: the iron content, the calcium saturation, the temperature, the subtle chemical signatures that indicate which deep aquifer it's rising from. They spit samples into beakers, make notes, adjust the wooden sluice gates that control flow.

But their real expertise is *negotiation*. They negotiate with the water, yes—coaxing it toward a new building site, encouraging deposition here and erosion there. But also with the city itself, with its emergent intelligence or whatever it is that causes doors to appear and hallways to extend. They've learned through generations of trial and error that the plateau *responds* to human intent, but not mechanically, not predictably. More like it *considers* what you're asking and makes its own decision.

Senior engineers—those who've been here thirty, forty years—develop a reputation for being able to "ask" successfully more often than not. They walk a proposed construction site for hours, not surveying in any technical sense but *feeling* it out, running their fingers over stone, tasting the water, listening. Then they'll nod and say, "Here. It wants to grow here." And they're usually right. The water flows more readily, the deposition rate increases, the architecture emerges with fewer complications.

No one can articulate how they know this. "The stone tells you," they'll say, which explains nothing. Or: "You can feel where it's ready." Younger engineers find this infuriating until they've been here long enough to understand that it's not mysticism or metaphor—it's expertise in a domain where the rules shift and intuition becomes a valid epistemological tool.

There's a woman named Vasquez who's been Chief Hydrology Engineer for fifteen years. She's in her sixties, compact and weathered, with white hair kept short and practical. She barely speaks during the day—just grunts and nods and the occasional terse instruction to her crew. But in the evenings, in the communal hot springs, she becomes voluble, almost poetic. She'll talk about the water's moods, about how it flows differently when the barometric pressure drops, about the time she found a channel flowing *upward* and realized it wasn't defying gravity, it was following a different gradient, one measured in something other than elevation.

She has a theory that the salamanders are the plateau's original engineers, that humans are learning from them without realizing it. She's spent years documenting their constructions, photographing the pebble spirals and twig patterns, trying to decode them like a language. She's found correlations—when the salamanders build extensively in a certain area, water flow stabilizes there within a week. When they *dismantle* their own constructions, that area tends to become "active," prone to architectural changes. She doesn't know if they're predicting or causing these effects. She suspects the distinction might not be meaningful here.

Her apprentice is a young man named Kato, who arrived three years ago from a city at sea level and is still adjusting. He gets nosebleeds from the altitude, and the temporal inconsistencies make him nauseous—his body expecting a duration and experiencing another creates a low-grade motion sickness. But he's brilliant with mathematics, and he's trying to model the water system as a dynamic network, building equations that accommodate inconsistency as a variable. Vasquez thinks he's wasting his time but lets him try anyway. She remembers trying to impose order here, to make it *make sense*. You have to go through that phase, attempt the impossible quantification, before you can let it go and learn the deeper grammar.

The factory workers are a different culture within the culture.

They work eight-hour shifts, or what the machines say are eight-hour shifts, which sometimes align with human time-sense and sometimes don't. They've learned not to care. What matters is the work rhythm—the cycle of heating and cooling, pressure and release, the careful cultivation of stone with specific properties. Each factory develops its own specialty, its own techniques passed down and refined.

In the ceramics facility, there's a woman named Oakes who can listen to the sound of water moving through a mineral bath and know exactly when the stone has reached the correct consistency for harvest. She's blind—has been since birth—and claims this gives her an advantage: she's not distracted by the stone's visual texture, only its acoustic properties. The slight changes in resonance as mineral content shifts, the subtle pitch variations as crystals form. She moves through the facility with absolute confidence, one hand trailing along equipment, and is more productive than any two sighted workers.

The metal-deposition factory employs a man named Russeau who used to be a jeweler in a lowland city. He came here chasing rumors of silver that grew like frost, copper that branched like trees. He found the rumors were true but incomplete—the metals don't just precipitate, they *organize*. Under certain conditions, with the right water chemistry and temperature and electromagnetic environment (the reasons are still unclear), copper and silver deposit in fractal patterns, branching and rebranching at every scale. The resulting material is beautiful—lace-like metal embedded in translucent stone—but also functional. It conducts electricity in strange ways, with different pathways offering different resistances, creating natural circuit networks.

Russeau makes instruments from this material. Not quite scientific instruments, not quite musical ones—devices that respond to electromagnetic fields, to changes in atmospheric pressure, to the proximity of water. They produce readings on dials, but also sounds, and also changes in temperature. He's not entirely sure what they're measuring, but they're consistent within themselves, and other engineers have started using them as a supplement to conventional equipment. When a Russeau instrument starts humming and warming up, something is about to shift—a water flow change, an architectural event, sometimes an anomaly. They've become divination tools that happen to be grounded in physical phenomena.

The factory superintendents are essentially orchestra conductors, coordinating the various processes, making sure the steam pressure is right, the water chemistry correct, the timing aligned. They work from pulpits overlooking the factory floor, surrounded by dials and gauges and thick logbooks where every parameter is recorded every hour. They shout adjustments over the mechanical din, and their workers respond with practiced precision.

But they're also constantly improvising. A process that worked yesterday might not work today—the water chemistry has shifted, or the ambient temperature is wrong, or some immeasurable factor has changed. So they adjust, they experiment, they find the new parameters that produce the desired result. Each factory keeps shadow logbooks where they record not just the official processes but the *actual* ones, the workarounds and improvisations, the rules that only apply sometimes.

There's an informal economy of expertise. Workers trade knowledge like currency: I'll show you the trick for preventing air bubbles in the ceramic deposits if you teach me your method for detecting copper saturation. The formal hierarchy is flat—superintendents, engineers, workers—but the informal hierarchy is complex, based on who knows what, who can solve which problems, who the others come to when conventional approaches fail.

The scholars are perhaps the strangest group—people who came here specifically to study the anomalies, the impossible geology, the temporal inconsistencies.

Dr. Sung arrived eight years ago with funding from a coastal university to document the architectural growth rates. She set up survey markers, took daily measurements, built extensive photographic records. Within three months, her data was useless—the survey markers had moved, or multiplied, or in one case merged with the growing stone so that removing it would require demolition. Her photographs showed different details each time she examined them, as if the images themselves were subject to revision.

She had a breakdown. Spent a week in her apartment refusing to come out, convinced either that she was losing her mind or that reality was. Vasquez finally coaxed her to the hot springs, got some food and drink into her, and explained the situation: "You can't study this place with lowland epistemology. The measurement changes what you're measuring, and also what measurement means. If you want to understand it, you have to be willing to accept understanding that doesn't look like understanding."

Dr. Sung is still here. She stopped trying to quantify and started describing instead—thick ethnographic accounts of how residents navigate the temporal inconsistencies, how they know where they are when maps don't work, how they communicate about spaces that may or may not exist. Her work has become half phenomenology, half poetry. The university stopped funding her years ago, but she doesn't care. She has a small apartment near the North Springs, teaches occasional classes for the plateau-born children, and fills notebook after notebook with accounts that will never be published in any conventional journal but which are, she knows, the only accurate description of life here.

There's a mathematician named Torben who's attempting to develop new geometries that can accommodate inconsistent spaces. He works in a small office in the library-of-impossible-things, surrounded by blackboards covered in symbols that make visitors' eyes hurt. He claims he's close to a breakthrough—a system of spatial description that doesn't require consistency, that treats variability as fundamental rather than aberrant. Whether he's genuinely onto something or descending into elegant madness is unclear, possibly even to him. But his work is beautiful—dense symbolic forests that seem to move when you're not looking directly at them.

The artists came later, attracted by descriptions of the city, by the aesthetic possibilities.

A sculptor named Omaira works directly with the deposition process. She builds armatures—complex wooden and wire structures—and positions them in the water flow, then waits. Years, sometimes. As the stone grows around her frameworks, it creates forms she could never carve conventionally: shapes that emerge from patience and collaboration with geological time. She has pieces that have been growing for a decade, that won't be "finished" in her lifetime. She doesn't mind. She works on twenty, thirty pieces simultaneously, adjusting water flow, modifying the armatures as they become embedded, seeing what emerges.

Some of her work is recognizable—human figures, animals, abstract forms. Some is less categorizable. There's a piece in the eastern plaza that might be a hand or might be roots or might be water frozen mid-splash, depending on the light and angle and possibly the viewer's mood. Children play on it. Choughs perch on it. The salamanders have decorated its base with pebble patterns that seem to complete it, or comment on it, or possibly correct it.

A photographer named Welsh documents the city obsessively. He's accumulated tens of thousands of images over fifteen years, and he's noticed something: photographs taken of the same location sometimes show different architecture. Not like the buildings have changed—like they *never were* the same. A doorway in Tuesday's photo that doesn't exist in Thursday's, though he'd swear he saw it, photographed it, touched it. He's stopped trying to create a coherent visual record and instead treats his archive as evidence that coherence isn't the right goal. He's mounted exhibitions where he shows twelve photographs of the same spot taken over twelve months, and they might as well be twelve different cities.

Writers come here too, though they tend not to stay long. The temporal inconsistencies make narrative difficult—how do you tell a story when events don't reliably follow each other, when yesterday might not have necessarily preceded today? There's a novelist named Pryce who arrived intending to write a book set on the plateau and has been here four years without completing a single chapter. Not from lack of trying—she writes constantly. But each day she discovers that yesterday's writing is *different*, not like she edited it but like she wrote something else entirely. She now has seventeen versions of Chapter One, each complete, each incompatible with the others. She's given up on the novel but can't bring herself to leave. She works in one of the factories now, growing ceramic, and writes in the evenings for her own satisfaction, no longer worrying about coherence or completion.

The children of all these people—the second generation—are becoming something new.

They speak multiple languages: the official common tongue brought from the lowlands, but also a pidgin that's developing locally, incorporating words from a dozen source languages plus new coinages for plateau-specific phenomena. "Stoneshift" for when architecture changes. "Timestretch" for duration inconsistencies. "Salamandering" for the activity of arranging pebbles or twigs in conversation with the creatures, but also as a verb meaning to intuit the plateau's intentions: "I salamandered the route and knew we should go the long way."

They're forming what anthropologists would recognize as an emergent culture, distinct from their parents' origins. Their values center around adaptation, observation, and comfort with uncertainty. They find the concept of guaranteed outcomes alien—why would you expect the same action to produce the same result every time? The world is more interesting than that.

Teenage courtship here involves elaborate gift-giving of *found* objects—not precious metals or gems, but items that shouldn't exist: a cogwheel made of stone, a flower that blooms in mineral water, a photograph showing both participants before they'd met. The gifts demonstrate not wealth but attentiveness, perception, the ability to notice when the world offers something impossible. Marriages are formalized not with contracts but with shared maintenance of a water channel—an ongoing collaborative work that requires constant adjustment and communication.

The elderly—those who've been here forty, fifty years—undergo a particular transformation. Their bodies incorporate the place. Joints that once ached in the damp no longer do; they've adapted, or the damp has adapted to them. Their sense of time becomes even more fluid than the younger residents', to the point where they sometimes seem to experience hours out of sequence, responding to questions before they're asked, preparing for events that haven't happened yet.

There's a man named Alberti who's been here since the earliest days, one of the original engineer crew. He's in his eighties now, moves slowly, spends most of his time sitting by the central springs watching the water. He barely speaks, but when he does, people listen, because he somehow knows things—which channels need maintenance, where new growth will appear, when the fog will lift. He denies precognition; he says he's just watching patterns. But the patterns he's watching include time itself, and he's learned to read forward as well as backward.

There are maybe two dozen people this old, this integrated. They're treated with casual reverence—not authority exactly, but as living archives, as bridges between the city's past and its continuously-emerging present. They remember when this plaza was open sky, when that building was just a wooden frame, when the salamanders first appeared and everyone panicked before learning they were harmless, or mostly harmless, or at least not hostile.

The community holds funerals that are also celebrations. When someone dies—from age, from accident, rarely from the altitude or anomalies—their body is placed in a specific channel, one designated for the purpose. The water takes them. Within weeks, the body is encased in mineral deposits, incorporated into the architecture. You can see them sometimes—human-shaped voids in the walls, gradually filling with stone, becoming anonymous, becoming structure. The dead don't leave the city; they become it. Children play in buildings that contain their great-grandparents. It's not morbid here. It's continuity.

There's a small but significant population who might be called mystics or might be called insane or might be both.

These are people who've stopped trying to maintain the distinction between self and city. They talk to the walls, to the water, to the stone itself, and claim it talks back. They might be right—there's no way to verify, but their predictions are sometimes accurate. They live in the most unstable quarters, where architecture changes daily, and they seem not just comfortable but *delighted* by the flux. They're tolerated, sometimes employed for their insights, occasionally brought to engineering consultations when conventional approaches have failed.

A woman called only "Whisper" lives in a building that doesn't have a fixed location—it appears in different parts of the city, recognizable by its distinctive twisted doorway, but mobile in some sense that defies mapping. She inside it for three years now, or possibly three days, or possibly thirty years depending on whose time-sense you trust. People visit her for advice, or prophecy, or just conversation. She serves tea grown in moss gardens and talks about the city's dreams, its intentions, its slow metamorphosis toward some future state. She might be mad. Her advice is often useful.

There's a council, technically—elected representatives from each major district, meeting monthly to coordinate infrastructure, mediate disputes, allocate resources. But governance here is light, necessarily so. You can't enforce rigid rules in a city that won't hold still. The council's main function is communication: sharing information about water flows, architectural changes, upcoming maintenance needs, observed anomalies.

Disputes are rare. The shared precarity—the knowledge that everyone depends on collective maintenance of the channels, collective attention to the city's changes—creates a pragmatic solidarity. There's little theft; where would you go? The plateau is isolated, accessible only by a single road that's often impassable, and besides, community exclusion would be devastating here. You need your neighbors' expertise, their observations, their willingness to help when your building extends unexpectedly and you need materials, or when you get lost because a familiar route has reconfigured.

Serious crimes—assault, violence—are almost unknown. The one murder in the last decade was obviously insanity rather than malice; the perpetrator was clearly unable to cope with the plateau's demands and snapped under the stress. They were sedated, escorted down the mountain to lowland authorities. The victim was placed in the channel, mourned, incorporated.

There's a kind of ambient empathy here, born from shared weirdness. Everyone has experienced disorientation, confusion, the unsettling moment when reality isn't where you left it. So there's patience for others going through it, willingness to help someone who's struggling to adapt, easy tolerance for different coping strategies and interpretations.

The spiritual landscape is syncretic, layered.

Some residents brought religions from elsewhere and maintain them—there's a small chapel for Catholic services, a Buddhist altar in one of the communal spaces, a secular humanist discussion group. But these traditions get inflected by the plateau's specific conditions. The Catholic priest, Father Domingo, has developed a theology of holy flux, arguing that the city's instability reflects divine creativity, God's ongoing creation rather than a finished product. His homilies compare the mineral deposition to sanctification—both are slow, accretive, transformative processes.

The Buddhist practitioners have adapted meditation techniques, treating the temporal inconsistencies as advanced training in non-attachment, in presence despite discontinuity. They sit for hours in the unstable quarters, practicing equanimity as the walls shift around them.

But there's also emerging a local spiritual sensibility that's harder to categorize. It treats the plateau itself as... not a deity exactly, but a presence, an entity, something with intentions if not consciousness. The salamanders are its messengers, or its dreams, or its antibodies. The water is its blood or thought or language. The architecture is its body, and residents are symbiotes, or neurons, or perhaps parasites it tolerates because humans serve some function in its development.

This isn't an organized religion—there are no priests, no orthodoxy, no sacred texts. It's more like a shared ambient assumption, a background metaphysics that shapes how people talk and think. "The city wants..." is a common phrase. "The water is suggesting..." Engineers use this language unselfconsciously, not as metaphor but as practical description of phenomena they've learned to work with.

Every few years, someone proposes leaving. Usually someone recent, still struggling with adaptation. They'll argue that the community should relocate to somewhere stable, somewhere that obeys conventional physics. These proposals are heard respectfully and then gently dismissed. Not because the plateau is perfect—it's deeply strange, often frustrating, sometimes frightening. But because people who've stayed have, at some point, made a choice: wonder over certainty, transformation over stability.

There's self-selection happening across generations. Those temperamentally unsuited to uncertainty leave—usually within the first year, sometimes after decades when the cumulative weirdness finally exceeds their tolerance. Those who remain are, increasingly, people for whom this is exactly the world they want to inhabit. Not despite the anomalies but because of them.

The children ask fewer questions about why things are strange. They accept strangeness as fundamental and ask instead: how does this particular strangeness work? What can we do with it? Their play is experimental—they're constantly testing boundaries, seeing what's possible. They've discovered things the adults never noticed: that salamanders respond to certain songs, that some stones grow faster when talked to, that you can sometimes feel a timestretch coming if you pay attention to a particular pressure behind your eyes.

In another few generations, assuming the plateau continues and the community survives, there will be people who have never known stable architecture, consistent time, predictable causality. The city's weirdness won't be weird to them; it will be how the world is. And they'll develop expertise and art and ways of living that the first generation couldn't imagine, because they'll be native to this place in a way no one currently is.

For now, the population is a mix: originals and immigrants, adapted and adapting, the comfortable and the struggling. They work together to maintain the systems—not just the water channels and factories but the knowledge systems, the accumulated expertise about how to live here, how to work with rather than against the plateau's nature.

Every evening, as the light dims further and the bioluminescence strengthens, you can see people converging on the hot springs. Engineers with mineral-stained hands, factory workers still warm from the forges, scholars with notebooks, artists with stone dust in their hair. They soak in water that rose through kilometers of rock, heated by the planet's core, carrying dissolved mountain in solution.

They talk about their day—the channels they maintained, the strange rock formation that appeared overnight, the salamander construction in the northern quarter that seems to be predicting something but no one's sure what. They share food, trade observations, argue amiably about interpretations of the latest anomalies.

Children splash in the shallower pools, their laughter echoing off stone walls that weren't there last month, that might not be there next month. The choughs settle into their evening roosts, their calls softening to occasional murmurs. The salamanders emerge from the channels to bask in the warm water, their pale bodies glowing faintly, their black glass eyes watching, always watching.

And above it all, the plateau continues its patient work—accreting, transforming, growing toward some form it hasn't yet revealed, while the people who've chosen to live on it grow alongside, adapting, learning, becoming native to this place where stone is soft and time is negotiable and reality itself is under construction.

They are, all of them, dwellers in possibility. Residents of a city that is also a question, that asks them daily: *How flexible are you? How much strangeness can you hold? What could you become if the world itself was becoming?*

And by staying, by working, by raising children and building lives in this impossible place, they answer: *As flexible as needed. As much as exists. Whatever is necessary.*

They are the human geology of the Incipient Plateau—soft matter in a hardening matrix, alive in a living stone, finding ways forward in a place that is endlessly, marvelously, terrifyingly unfinished.
