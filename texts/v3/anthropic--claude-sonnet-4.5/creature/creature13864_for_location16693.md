# The Inhabitants of the Breathing Plateau

## The Undecided Fauna

They have not committed to their forms. This is the essential problem, the core misunderstanding that defines every creature here.

The *Oscillans* move through the substrate like mammals, like insects, like worms, like ideas of motion made briefly flesh. You track one for twenty minutes: first it's roughly canine—four legs, a head that might contain something like a brain. But the observation itself destabilizes it. The rear legs begin to fuse, or multiply, or simply become conceptually unclear. By the time it disappears back into the moss-mat, you cannot say with certainty how many limbs it possessed, only that locomotion occurred.

They range from hand-sized to the bulk of cattle, though size seems negotiable, possibly responsive to available space or observer attention. Their bodies are compromise formations—chitin and fur and bark and keratin existing not in layers but interpenetrated, a single tissue that's simultaneously all materials. When you find their shed parts (they do shed, constantly, leaving trails of discarded matter behind them), you discover the fragments resist classification even under close examination. A scale that's also a leaf. A bone that's also crystallizing mineral. Flesh that photosynthesizes.

Their heads are the worst. They possess sense organs, but the organs migrate. Eyes appear on flanks, then sink away. Mouths open in shoulders, rasp briefly at the air, seal over. Some individuals seem to be experimenting with having no head at all, distributing their sensory apparatus across their entire surface. Others develop multiple heads, growing and reabsorbing them like buds on a plant.

They eat the substrate, yes, but also each other, but also nothing—some seem to photosynthesize, others appear to absorb nutrients directly through their skin-bark-shell. When they kill each other (and they do, frequently, though the violence seems experimental rather than desperate), the killer often pauses mid-consumption, confused, as if suddenly uncertain whether eating was the intended outcome. The corpse, meanwhile, may continue moving, may drag itself away while being eaten, may begin consuming its own severed parts in what might be recycling or might be autobiography.

They reproduce, probably. You find smaller versions that might be juveniles or might be a different species entirely. They emerge from the substrate already moving, already uncertain of their form. Whether they came from eggs or spores or budding or some stranger process, you cannot determine. They seem to reproduce through all methods simultaneously, trying everything, committed to nothing.

Their vocalizations range from mechanical to musical to linguistic. Some produce sounds like grinding gears. Others sing in tones that seem deliberately pitched to create interference patterns, to make standing waves in the air. A few make sounds that are almost words, that carry semantic weight even though you don't understand them. You begin to suspect they're not communicating with each other but with the plateau itself, speaking to the distributed intelligence in its own language, reporting their observations, receiving instructions.

At night, some become luminescent. The light comes from within, from chemical reactions or bioluminescent bacteria or something that's decided to be light without needing a biological mechanism. They glow in patterns—pulses and waves and steady emanation. The patterns coordinate across multiple individuals, creating larger light-displays, signals visible across kilometers. What they're signaling, whether it's territorial display or mating behavior or data transmission, remains opaque.

## The Aerial Congregation

The birds that are not birds wheel overhead in their thousands, and they are wrong in every particular.

Call them *Metalloptera*—the metal-wings—though they're not truly metal, just as they're not truly birds. Their wings are stiff membranes, leathery and iridescent, structured with internal vanes that might be bone or might be something the plateau invented when it needed a material stronger than bone but more flexible than metal. The wings don't flap so much as vibrate, beating so rapidly they blur, creating lift through mechanisms that seem more insectoid than avian.

Their bodies are dense, heavy, containing organs you could only guess at. Too heavy to fly by any reasonable aerodynamics, yet they fly continuously, frantically, as if stopping would mean death. Perhaps it would. Perhaps they've traded the ability to land for something else, some adaptation that requires constant motion. You see them occasionally collide with plant-structures or each other, and the impacts produce sounds like struck bells, pure tones that ring out across the plateau.

They have beaks, but the beaks are too long, too narrow, more like surgical instruments than feeding tools. Some individuals bear beaks that curve in impossible spirals. Others have beaks that branch, that fork into multiple points. They use them to probe the substrate when they do land—rare, brief touchdowns during which they stab at the moss-mat, extracting fluids or perhaps injecting something. The interaction seems communicative as much as nutritive, an exchange of information through physical penetration.

Their eyes are too large, too multifaceted, compound eyes that seem more appropriate to insects but sized for skulls the breadth of your palm. The eyes catch and reflect the dim light, thousands of points of reflection per bird, millions across the flock. At certain angles, the flock becomes a cloud of lights, a constellation that writes and rewrites itself against the grey sky.

The screaming is continuous. Each bird contributes its voice to the collective noise—shrieks and cries that sound like tearing metal, like emergency sirens, like industrial accidents. But underneath the chaos, there's structure. Patterns emerge and dissolve. Rhythms establish themselves, persist for minutes, then break apart. You begin to hear language in it, or the potential for language, communication happening at a bandwidth too high for human parsing. They're telling each other something, agreeing on something, voting on something through their mechanical chorus.

Their formation flying transcends murmurations. They create shapes—geometric figures, organic curves, symbols that suggest glyphs from unknown alphabets. The shapes hold for seconds or minutes before dissolving into apparent chaos, but the chaos is transitional, reforming into new configurations. They're writing in three dimensions, composing messages or poems or mathematical proofs in the medium of coordinated flight.

Some individuals break from the flock periodically, diving toward the substrate at speeds that should be lethal, pulling up at the last moment with acceleration that should break their bodies. They're testing something, or attacking something, or playing in a way that looks suicidal from outside. When they rejoin the flock, the other birds mob them briefly, surrounding them in a sphere of bodies and beating wings, either welcoming them back or extracting information about what they learned during their descent.

At night, the character changes. The flock condenses, thousands of individuals drawing together into a single massive formation that hovers over particular areas of the plateau, creating a living ceiling. They glow faintly—not bioluminescence but something else, as if their bodies are under such metabolic stress that they're radiating heat in visible wavelengths. The formation pulses like a heart, expanding and contracting, and the substrate below responds, its own breathing synchronizing with the aerial rhythm. Communication across kingdoms, coordination between the flying and the rooted.

You find their dead occasionally—corpses that have fallen from the flock, bodies broken from collision or exhaustion or simple age. They don't decompose normally. Instead they're colonized immediately, within hours, by specialized organisms from the substrate—things that might be fungi or might be something that's learned fungal strategies. The colonizers consume the corpse from within while maintaining its external form, creating husks that persist for days or weeks, hollow sculptures of birds in the moss.

## The Substrate Swimmers

Beneath the surface, hidden in the dense pneumatic moss-mat, things move. You rarely see them whole, encountering instead traces and fragments and disturbances that suggest their presence.

They create wakes in the substrate—traveling bulges, ripples in the moss-skin that indicate something passing below. The wakes move at varying speeds, from the glacial progress of something massive to the darting quickness of something small and nervous. Sometimes multiple wakes converge, merge, separate—interactions whose nature you can only guess at. Hunting, mating, conversation, collision.

When they surface, they do so partially. A limb emerges—you cannot call it a leg or a tentacle or a root, because it's all of those simultaneously. Segmented like arthropod leg, flexible like tentacle, growing like root, textured like bark or shell or bark becoming shell. The limb probes the air, tastes it perhaps, then withdraws. What it learned, whether it found what it was seeking, remains unknown.

The larger ones are felt before they're seen. The substrate begins vibrating—a deep, regular pulsing that travels through the moss-mat into your boots, up your legs, into your chest. Something massive is passing beneath you, something with bulk and power. The surface bulges upward, a traveling dome of displaced moss-matter. At the peak of the bulge, just before it subsides, you sometimes see deeper layers exposed—the stone substrate of the original plateau, or what might be older moss compacted into something like peat, or structures that suggest the swimmer itself, geometric forms that could be carapace or skeletal elements or something without biological precedent.

They sing, though "sing" is generous. The sounds emerge from the substrate—thrumming, resonant, felt as much as heard. Low frequencies that make your vision blur, that cause standing waves in the pool surfaces, that seem to carry emotional content even without meaning. Melancholy thrums. Aggressive pulses. Sounds that might be curiosity or might be warning. The songs overlap, creating interference patterns, beats and harmonics that suggest multiple individuals in communication or competition.

Some are predatory. You witness attacks—a wake converging rapidly with another, smaller wake. A brief turbulence in the substrate. Then stillness, and only one wake continuing on. What was consumed, whether it's actually dead or merely incorporated, you cannot determine. The boundary between predation and merger seems porous here.

Others are grazers, consuming the substrate itself from within. You can track their progress by the slow deflation of particular areas, the moss-mat thinning as they tunnel through its depths, converting its substance into their own. Behind them, the damaged areas regenerate, new growth filling in the gaps, but changed somehow—different species composition, altered texture, as if the swimmer's passage has seeded new organisms in its wake.

The smallest ones are everywhere, countless, creating a constant subtle motion throughout the substrate. You kneel and watch closely: the moss-surface trembles continuously with their activity, thousands of tiny swimmers moving through the upper layers. They might be larvae, or might be a dwarf species, or might be individual cells of a larger distributed organism. They create currents in the interstitial fluids, patterns of flow that suggest purpose or at least coordination.

At the pools' edges, you occasionally see them drinking or breathing or absorbing. Something surfaces, creates a disturbance in the water, then submerges again. The interaction is too brief to analyze. You wait by a pool for an hour, trying to observe them properly, but they seem aware of your attention, shy of observation. The moment you look away, you hear splashes, sense motion in your peripheral vision. But when you turn, there's only ripples, only settling water.

They construct things. You find structures in the substrate—chambers and tunnels, galleries and nodes, some small as your fist and others large enough to stand in. The architecture is organic but regular, suggesting deliberate design. Some chambers contain water, creating underground pools. Others are dry, filled with air or possibly other gases. A few contain collections—sorted piles of minerals or plant matter or animal parts, arranged according to principles you cannot parse. Museums? Storage? Worship?

## The Hybrid Gardeners

There exists a class of beings here that cannot be called animal or plant, because they are determinedly both, or neither, or something that has transcended the dichotomy entirely.

The *Ambulophyta*—walking plants, mobile flora, gardeners of themselves—move with the speed of growth rather than locomotion. You observe one for an hour and it travels perhaps ten meters, but you could not say at what point movement occurred. Its position changes; therefore it moved. But watching continuously reveals no motion, only the slow accumulation of positional difference.

They are as much tree as creature. Trunks split into leg-analogs, roots that have learned to lift and place, to carry weight. The legs number anywhere from three to eight, and the number changes—old legs reabsorbed into the body mass, new legs budding and extending. At their tops, where heads might be, there are instead branches, and in the branches, foliage, and in the foliage, something that might be sense organs or might be flowers or might be thinking apparatus.

They garden as they move. Where they walk, they alter the substrate, pruning and planting, rearranging the moss-mat, uprooting some plant-structures and encouraging others. They carry seeds or spores in chambers within their bodies, dispensing them selectively, planting the plateau's next generation according to aesthetics or necessity or incomprehensible preference. Behind them, they leave altered landscape, the substrate reorganized into patterns that might be more efficient or more beautiful or simply different.

Some individuals have incorporated non-living matter into their forms. You see one that has grown around a piece of industrial machinery—a length of pipe now serving as structural support, metal and xylem sharing the load. Another has integrated crystalline reeds into its structure, so that it chimes as it moves, producing bell-tones with each ponderous step. They're becoming composite entities, biological and mineral and technological aspects merging into unified wholes.

They communicate through chemistry, releasing clouds of volatile compounds that drift across the plateau, that are absorbed by other plant-structures and fauna alike. The chemicals seem to carry complex messages—you've watched other organisms respond to the releases, changing behavior, moving toward or away from particular areas. The language is olfactory and hormonal, operating through receptors you don't possess, conducting conversations you can only observe the effects of.

Their reproduction is obscene in its complexity. They bud and seed and spore simultaneously. Parts of their bodies drop away and take root, becoming new individuals. They exchange genetic material with the substrate itself, with passing fauna, with each other through underground connections you cannot see but can infer from the coordinated behavior of widely separated individuals. They're breeding into the entire ecosystem, making everything partly themselves, or making themselves partly everything.

Some have developed animal characteristics beyond locomotion. You see mouths opening in trunks, rimmed with tooth-analogs made of hardened cellulose. They feed on the substrate, yes, but also on animals—slow-motion predators that wait in paths frequently traveled, that grab passing fauna with sudden extensions of modified branches, that digest the captured creature over hours or days while it struggles. Plant and predator, photosynthesizer and carnivore, they've refused to choose.

Others seem to be becoming more abstract. Their forms grow increasingly geometric—perfect spirals, fractal branching patterns, arrangements that suggest mathematical constants. They're discovering or inventing sacred geometry, encoding equations in their structure. Walking theorems, mobile proofs, they might be the plateau's attempt at philosophy, at using physical form to explore abstract concepts.

At night, they gather. You observe a dozen individuals converging on a particular location over the course of hours, their glacial movement bringing them together into a grove, a congress. They intertwine their branches, link their root systems, and the combined mass begins to glow—faint bioluminescence or metabolic heat or something less explicable. They remain connected through the night, and in the morning they separate, each returning to its territory, though whether they met to mate or plan or simply experience collective being, you cannot determine.

## The Industrial Inheritors

Most disturbing are the things that have made their homes in the machinery, that have become inseparable from the mechanical systems, that might now be more machine than organism or more organism than machine, the categories having collapsed into meaninglessness.

In the drilling rigs, nests. But nests like organs, like the machinery has developed specialized tissues. What might have started as birds using derricks for shelter has evolved into integration—the nests are wired into the electrical systems, drawing power, feeding it back, connected through cables that might be artificial or might be grown, synthetic and biological polymers woven together into hybrid conduits.

The nest-builders are birds no longer, if they ever were. They've taken on aspects of the machinery they inhabit. Metal plates have grown into or onto their bodies—not attached, but integrated, part of them, flesh grading into steel without clear boundary. Their beaks incorporate drill bits, or have become drill bits, rotating as they feed, boring into substrate or prey with mechanical precision. Their calls are entirely mechanical now, generated through vocalization organs that are definitely machine parts—whistle valves, compressed air systems, servo motors clicking and whirring.

Some have given up flight entirely, becoming adapted to life in the machinery. They crawl through the ductwork, nest in the conveyor mechanisms, feeding on the oils and fluids that the machines leak or produce. Their bodies have elongated, become serpentine, better suited to moving through pipes and cables. Wings have atrophied or modified into manipulator appendages, able to operate switches, turn valves, repair or sabotage systems according to needs or whims you cannot fathom.

The machines themselves might be alive now, or the organisms might be mechanical. The distinction has been worn away by time and integration. You watch a conveyor belt running, and you cannot tell if it's powered by electricity or by the muscular contractions of organisms grown into its works. The drill rig hammers into the substrate—is that hydraulic pressure or the contraction of biological tissue or both, or something that's neither and both simultaneously?

In the processing facility, things dwell that have no name. They inhabit the spaces between machines, the gaps in architecture, living in the margins and thresholds. You glimpse them rarely—something withdrawing into ductwork, motion in the shadows beneath conveyor systems, eyes or sensors reflecting light from deep in machinery spaces. They maintain the equipment, or they parasitize it, or they've entered into symbiotic relationships where maintenance and parasitism cannot be distinguished.

They've developed—or retained, or discovered—intelligence. You find evidence of tool use: machines repaired with improvised parts, sections of equipment modified to serve new purposes, control systems rewired in ways that suggest understanding of their function. The modifications seem purposeful, seem to improve efficiency or change output in deliberate ways. Something here understands mechanical engineering, or has learned it, or has evolved it independently.

The fauna moving through the machinery have learned to use it. You watch creatures ride the conveyor belts, using them for transportation across the plateau. Others trigger drilling rigs deliberately, causing them to bore into particularly dense sections of substrate, then feeding on the exposed layers or the organisms disturbed by the drilling. The machinery has become tools, has been incorporated into foraging and hunting strategies, has been domesticated by beings that may have once been animals but are now something adjacent.

At night, the facility becomes more active. More lights flicker on, more machines start running, as if night shift has begun. But there are no human workers—only the things that have inherited the industrial legacy, the hybrid entities that have learned to operate human systems or that the systems have learned to operate. Through the windows, you see motion—things moving between machines, coordinating, working together toward some goal. Production continues, but what's being produced has changed. The output at the end of the conveyor lines consists of objects without clear purpose, biological-mechanical amalgams that are taken by waiting fauna and carried into the substrate, integrated into nests or burrows or dissolved into the ecosystem. The factory is dreaming new forms into existence, and the plateau absorbs them, tests them, incorporates successful designs and discards failures.

## The Lichenthropes

On exposed stone surfaces, where the substrate thins and ancient plateau bedrock emerges, the lichens have evolved mobility, have learned to walk, have become something that lies between sessile and active in ways that create conceptual problems.

They move imperceptibly. A lichen patch measured at dawn and at dusk will have different positions, but watching continuously reveals no motion. They might move only when unobserved, or they might move at speeds too slow for human perception to register, or observation itself might halt their motion, quantum uncertainty applied to macroscopic biology.

Despite their slowness, they're voracious. They consume rock, metabolizing minerals, excreting modified stone that has different crystalline structure, different color, different properties. They're terraforming at the microscale, transforming the plateau's foundation incrementally, irreversibly. In another million years, they might have converted the entire plateau into a new type of geology, rock that's half biological, half mineral, wholly other.

Some species have grown large—patches meters across, thick as mattresses, their surfaces a complex landscape of textures and colors. Within the larger lichens, ecosystems exist—micro-fauna living in the thicket of fungal hyphae, tiny predators hunting tiny prey through forest-analogs measured in centimeters. The large lichens are worlds unto themselves, supporting communities that have never touched the substrate proper, that live their entire lives in the lichen-scape.

Others have specialized. There are species that grow only on metal, consuming the industrial remnants, converting rust and corrosion into biological matter. They're brightly colored—orange and green and purple—creating abstract art on the surfaces of derricks and catwalks. When patches meet, they compete or merge, conducting chemical warfare or exchanging genetic material through hyphal connections.

A few species have become predatory, or at least carnivorous. They emit chemicals that attract small fauna, that cause disorientation or paralysis. Animals that rest on these patches find themselves stuck, their feet or bodies adhering to the lichen surface. Digestive enzymes are released. Over days or weeks, the animal is consumed, absorbed, converted into lichen biomass. The predatory patches are marked by the remnants of previous prey—bones and shells and chitinous plates embedded in their surface, trophies or warnings or simply undigestible refuse.

The lichens are ancient, possibly older than the current ecological arrangement. They might have colonized the plateau before the plant-structures evolved, before the fauna arrived, patient pioneers that have watched ecosystems rise and fall while they continued their slow expansion. They might remember, if memory can exist in beings without nervous systems, what the plateau was like before strangeness took hold, before the rules began breaking down.

They reproduce through fragmentation and spores both. Fragments break away—mechanically, or deliberately detached—and establish elsewhere. Spores drift on wind, are carried by fauna, stick to machinery and germinate in new locations. Each spore contains the potential for a new world, a new ecosystem condensed into a cell, waiting for the right conditions to unfold its complexity.

Some have begun to glow. Not bioluminescence but something else—they seem to gather ambient light during day and release it at night, phosphorescent, creating patches of faint illumination scattered across exposed rock faces. The light attracts night-active fauna, creating congregation points, zones of activity in the darkness. Whether this is deliberate strategy—attracting prey or partners—or accidental byproduct, you cannot determine.

## The Depth Dwellers

Far below the substrate, in the stone foundation of the plateau, cavities exist—natural or carved or grown. And in those cavities, things live that have adapted to lightlessness, to pressure, to isolation.

You know they exist because sometimes the substrate subsides, collapsing into hidden voids. In those collapses, you see the openings—caves or tunnels extending down into darkness. And from the darkness, occasionally, things emerge.

They are pale, colorless, having lost pigmentation in the eternal dark. Their bodies are elongated, streamlined for moving through tight spaces, through stone corridors and flooded galleries. Some are segmented, worm-like, muscular enough to bore through solid rock given sufficient time. Others have developed limbs, but limbs modified for climbing in three dimensions, in spaces where walls and ceiling and floor are equally available for purchase.

They're blind, eyes having atrophied into vestigial spots or disappeared entirely. In place of vision, they've developed other senses—antennae that detect chemical gradients, lateral lines sensitive to pressure changes in standing water, organs that might detect electromagnetic fields or vibrations through stone. They navigate by touch and smell and senses you can only hypothesize.

Their relationship to the surface ecosystem is unclear. They might be descendants of surface fauna that colonized the depths, or they might be ancient lineages that have always dwelled below, older than the current plateau arrangement. They emerge rarely, perhaps driven by hunger or reproduction or simple curiosity. When they surface, they seem disoriented, overwhelmed by space and light, moving erratically before retreating back into the substrate.

Some are massive. The collapses reveal cavities large enough to house creatures the size of vehicles, and you find evidence of their passing—huge shed skins, waste products of unimaginable volume, claw marks scored into stone. What they eat in the darkness, how they sustain such bulk, remains mysterious. Perhaps they're extremely long-lived, requiring minimal sustenance. Perhaps the depths contain ecosystems entire, chemosynthetic communities or fungi vast and ancient, food sources that never see the surface.

Others are colonial, existing as aggregations rather than individuals. You observe swarms emerging from a collapse—thousands of small, pale creatures moving as a coordinated mass, flowing across the substrate like slow liquid, consuming everything in their path, then returning to the depths. Locusts of the underworld, emerging at intervals measured in years or decades, devastating and then vanishing.

They might be responsible for the plateau's breathing. The substrate rises and falls, and perhaps that's driven by the movement of depth dwellers, by their excavations and tunneling, by the spaces they create collapsing and their movements compressing stone, creating pressure waves that propagate upward, causing the visible undulation. The plateau breathes because things are moving in its depths, because the foundation is not solid but hollow, not stable but dynamic.

At night, sounds emerge from collapses and crevices—clicks and scrapes, wet sounds like things moving through flooded passages, calls or echolocation pulses in frequencies at the edge of human hearing. The depth dwellers are more active in darkness, perhaps venturing closer to the surface when light doesn't threaten them. The sounds create unease, suggest things large and close and watching.

## The Question of Consciousness, Answered

Among all these beings—the undecided fauna, the hybrid gardeners, the industrial inheritors—which are conscious? Which experience? Which have inner lives?

The answer is: all of them, or none of them, or the question itself is malformed.

The plateau has created conditions where consciousness is not binary but distributed, not individual but systemic. Each creature might be a neuron in a larger cognition, or each might contain multitudes within itself. The boundaries of self are negotiable here, dissolved by millions of years of interdependence, of genetic exchange, of chemical communication so constant and comprehensive that individuality becomes a fiction.

You watch the birds wheeling overhead and suspect they share a mind, that the flock is the individual and each bird merely a cell in its body. But then you see one break from the formation, acting on apparent individual initiative, and the hypothesis crumbles. Unless the breakaway is equivalent to a stray thought, a neural impulse that momentarily diverges from the consensus. Unless individual initiative is itself a kind of cellular process in the flock-organism.

The substrate swimmers might be fingers of a single vast entity dwelling in the moss-mat, or might be entirely separate beings that have learned to coordinate so perfectly they appear unified. The Ambulophyta might be networking their roots, creating connections that allow thought to flow between individual plants, building a distributed forest-intelligence. Or each might be solitary, locked in vegetative isolation despite physical proximity.

The animals' undecided forms might reflect undecided selves, identities in constant flux, consciousness that hasn't committed to being one thing, that explores the space of possible experience. Or they might be experiencing nothing, might be philosophical zombies, complex behaviors arising from chemistry and physics without any accompanying inner life.

And you, observing all this, cannot maintain certainty about your own consciousness, your own boundaries. The plateau breathes and you begin breathing in rhythm with it. The birds scream and you find the sounds becoming familiar, almost meaningful. The substrate heaves beneath you and you feel the motion in your body, your proprioception extending down through your feet into the living ground, as if you're rooted, as if you've always been part of this place.

The inhabitants live here. They persist in strange mathematics and biological contradiction. They hunt and grow and reproduce and possibly think and probably feel and certainly exist, and whether that existence includes consciousness or not might matter less than you believed. They are here. The plateau made them, or they made the plateau, or the distinction between maker and made has been worn away by time and integration and the simple fact of mutual persistence through millions of years.

You stand among them, no longer clearly separate, already beginning to wonder if leaving is possible, if you haven't already been incorporated into the plateau's functioning, assigned a role in its distributed metabolism, your presence here not temporary but final, not observation but transformation, not visit but absorption.

The inhabitants live here. Soon, you might be one of them.
