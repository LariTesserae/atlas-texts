# Who Lives in the Thermal Hollows

The people here are *smooth* in ways that take time to notice.

Not physically smooth, though their skin does have a quality — perpetually damp, yes, but also slightly lustrous, as if the moisture has changed its texture over generations, made it less absorbent, more like the surface of something adapted to permanent wetness. But the smoothness is more in how they move, how they interact, how they negotiate the density. They flow through spaces that should be too crowded, should generate constant collision and friction. Instead there's a liquid quality to human movement here, people sliding past each other with millimeters of clearance, adjusting trajectory without apparent thought, maintaining motion through impossibly packed environments.

You see it most clearly in the market-states, when a district surges into exchange mode and the density of human presence spikes to what should be unmanageable. Thousands of people in spaces designed for hundreds, and yet they circulate, they browse, they negotiate and transact and move on, all without the crushing pressure you'd expect. They've learned something about occupying space collectively, about moving as fluid rather than as individuals. Or perhaps they're being moved, coordinated by systems you can't perceive, guided by the substrate itself through mechanisms that operate below consciousness.

They speak to each other constantly, but not always in words.

Verbal language happens, of course — dozens of languages, hundreds of dialects, all mixing and evolving in the acoustic chaos. But much communication occurs through gesture, through touch, through expressions that might be facial or might involve the whole body. Someone wants to pass — they don't say "excuse me," they shift their weight in a particular way, angle their shoulder, and the crowd opens without anyone consciously moving aside. Someone is selling something — they don't shout their wares, they adopt a particular stance, hands positioned just so, and people who want to buy orient toward them as if drawn by magnetism.

There's a layer of communication happening through the augmentation, the interfaces most people carry in their bodies. You catch glimpses of it — someone will pause mid-conversation, eyes unfocused for a moment, then nod and continue as if information just arrived through channels you can't access. Groups will coordinate complex activities with minimal verbal planning, as if they're sharing intention directly. Children especially seem to operate this way, moving in packs that swirl through the city with an eerie coherence, playing games that have rules you can't deduce from watching, responding to cues you can't perceive.

The augmentation is ubiquitous but understated — you wouldn't necessarily know someone was modified unless you looked for the signs.

Small ports behind ears or at the base of the skull, usually covered by hair when present. Slight differences in eye color or pupil response, the sheen of corneal enhancement. Patterns visible under the skin when light hits at certain angles — circuitry or something like it, integrated into dermal layers. Hands that move with too much precision, fingers that can grip wet surfaces with impossible security. People who can stand for hours in the heat without discomfort, who navigate through blinding mist without hesitation, who seem to know when a structure is about to shift or when a district is about to transition states.

It's not universal — you encounter people with no visible modification, or only minimal enhancement. But it's common enough to be unremarkable. The city selects for it, maybe. Or provides it. There are clinics tucked into the dense architecture where people go to have new interfaces installed, existing ones upgraded, damage repaired. The procedures are casual, almost social — you see people getting work done while chatting with neighbors, children receiving their first augmentations as casually as you might pierce an ear.

The enhancements seem to accumulate over lifetimes. Older people carry more modification, their bodies patchworked with decades of additions and upgrades. Not obviously cybernetic — the integration is too complete for that — but visibly *different*, their movements informed by senses and capabilities that baseline humans don't possess. They navigate not just the physical city but its data layers, reading information that bleeds from surfaces, interfacing with systems through proximity and intention.

The very old sometimes become strange, their augmentation approaching some threshold where humanity blurs into something else. You see them occasionally — ancients who move through the city like ghosts, barely physical, their attention clearly focused on layers of reality you can't perceive. They're treated with respect but not quite reverence, acknowledged but given space. They might be humans becoming something else, or they might be demonstrations of what happens when you integrate too deeply with the city's systems, when the boundary between person and place dissolves.

Children are everywhere, saturating the city in proportion to adults but more visible because of how they move.

They travel in loose groups that fragment and recombine, flowing through the architecture with even more fluidity than adults. They're louder, less modulated in their behavior, but still adapted to the density in ways that would be impossible elsewhere. A pack of children can move through a space at full speed, shouting and chasing, and somehow never collide with the adults conducting business, never disrupt the flow of foot traffic, as if they're operating in parallel channels that intersect the adult world without blocking it.

They're deeply integrated with the fauna. You see children riding the quadrupeds casually, hanging onto their flanks as they pad through the city. Feeding the rat-things, which swarm around children with a comfort they don't show around adults. Training the bird-things, or perhaps being trained by them — the relationship goes both ways, children learning to understand the metallic calls, to predict flock movements, to communicate through whistles and gestures that the creatures respond to.

Young children have less augmentation but adapt faster, learning the city's rhythms intuitively. By adolescence most have begun receiving interfaces, the process gradual, staged. First perhaps an enhancement to breathing, making the thick air easier to process. Then sensory upgrades — better hearing in the acoustic chaos, vision that penetrates mist. Later, neural interfaces that let them tap into the city's information systems, participate in the coordination that enables high-density living.

The staging seems to be tradition rather than law — no one enforces it, but everyone follows roughly the same progression. As if the community has learned through generations that this is the right speed to integrate human development with technological enhancement, that going too fast breaks something important in how personhood forms.

Adolescents are given more independence than you'd expect, allowed to range far through the city, to join work crews, to take on responsibilities that would be considered adult elsewhere. But they're also clearly monitored — you sense a diffuse awareness of their locations, adults knowing where young people are without appearing to track them directly. The city itself might be tracking them, or the fauna might be reporting their positions, or the adolescents themselves might be broadcasting location through their augmentation without realizing it.

Adults show tremendous variation in how they inhabit the city.

Some are clearly specialized — you can see it in their bodies, in how they move, in the tools integrated into their augmentation. People who work in the deep levels have enhanced heat tolerance, can spend hours in water hot enough to scald baseline humans. People who maintain the upper structures have modifications for climbing, for balance, hands and feet that grip slick surfaces with casual security. People who work with fauna have enhanced chemical sensing, can read pheromone trails, understand the biological signals that coordinate the animal communities.

But it's not rigid specialization. People move between functions over their lifetimes, their augmentation being modified to support new roles. Someone who spent years working in thermal management might transition to teaching, their enhancements being repurposed, their accumulated knowledge finding new application. The city needs flexibility as much as specialization — its constant turbulence requires people who can adapt, who can take on new functions as districts reconfigure and demands shift.

There's a substantial population that seems to exist between roles, not currently specialized into any particular function but available for whatever labor emerges. They congregate in certain districts, in structures that serve as informal labor exchanges, waiting for needs to arise. When a construction project requires extra hands, or a breakdown needs emergency repair, or a district transition requires moving resources, these unattached workers flow toward the need, spend hours or days on the task, then dissolve back into their floating state.

You can't quite call them unemployed — they're not desperate, not struggling. They seem maintained by the city somehow, housed and fed through mechanisms that aren't obvious. Perhaps they're performing functions you can't perceive, participating in the city's operation in ways that don't look like work. Or perhaps the city's productivity is so high that it can support a substantial population in reserve, holding them ready for surge capacity.

Many adults are modified for specific sensory experiences of the city. Some can perceive the electromagnetic fields that permeate everything, navigating by the topology of technological systems rather than physical architecture. Some can sense thermal gradients with precision, reading the city's energy flows directly. Some have enhanced temporal perception, experiencing the city's changes at different speeds, able to watch slow shifts in structure that occur over days compressed into perceptible motion.

These enhancements create parallel experiences of the same place. Two people standing in the same spot might be experiencing radically different versions of the city — one perceiving it as physical structure and human activity, another perceiving it as flowing energy and data, another perceiving it as slow geological change. This diversity of perception seems intentional, as if the city benefits from being experienced in multiple ways simultaneously, from being known through different sensory frameworks that each capture something true.

Family structures exist but are fluid, not bounded in conventional ways.

Children have genetic parents but are raised collectively, sleeping and eating in multi-family structures where care rotates among dozens of adults. The biological parents remain involved but aren't solely responsible, don't have exclusive claims. Adults form partnerships that might be sexual or romantic or purely companionate, combinations of any size and gender configuration, stable for years or fragmentary, the social forms as diverse as the architecture they inhabit.

The groupings that matter most seem to be work-based rather than kinship-based. People who labor together develop bonds that look like family, sharing resources and space and care, coordinating their lives around shared functions. A maintenance crew working a district might live communally, their individual sleeping spaces nested together, their meals shared, their children raised in common, even though they might have no genetic relationship.

This creates a strange effect where everyone seems distantly connected, social networks overlapping and interpenetrating until the city's population becomes one vast web of weak ties rather than clusters of strong ones. You're never more than a few intermediaries from anyone else, and information propagates through the social network with surprising speed. News spreads not through broadcasting but through chains of personal communication, each person telling a few others, the message mutating slightly with each transfer but maintaining enough coherence to coordinate collective action.

The elderly don't segregate themselves. They remain embedded in multi-generational structures, contributing what they can until they can't, then being supported by the community that grew around them. When someone becomes too old or sick to participate, they're maintained with casual grace, not hidden away but present in daily life, their needs met without the drama of caregiving as a separate category of labor.

Death, when it comes, is processed quickly. Bodies are taken into the deep levels, fed into systems that break them down, recycle their materials, return their substance to the city's flows. There's ritual around this but it's brief, pragmatic. The focus is on continuity rather than loss — the person is gone but their atoms remain, their augmentation is harvested for reuse, their role in the city's systems closes and redistributes to others. Grief happens but privately, or in small groups. Public mourning would be inefficient, would slow the city's necessary motion.

Some people are clearly visitors, not permanent residents, though the distinction is soft.

They move through the city with different energy, more attention to surroundings, less fluid integration into the crowd. They lack the deep augmentation, rely more on verbal communication, show discomfort with the heat and moisture that residents ignore. But they're not tourists in any simple sense — most seem to be here for work, for trade, for purposes that the city requires but doesn't generate internally.

They arrive through the marginal zones, where the city fades into whatever lies beyond. They stay for periods that might be days or months, conducting their business, then depart. Some return regularly, developing partial integration, learning to move with city rhythms even if they never fully commit to permanent residence. Others come once and never again, the experience too overwhelming or too alien to sustain.

The residents treat visitors with polite opacity — helpful when asked, but not volunteering information, not explaining systems that should be obvious to someone who belongs. As if true understanding of the city can only come through extended immersion, and trying to short-circuit that process with explanations would be futile or even harmful.

Occasionally you encounter people who are clearly from elsewhere, radically elsewhere — their augmentation is different, incompatible with local systems, or they have modifications that the city doesn't support. They move through carefully, using specialized equipment, breathing apparatus or cooling systems or filtration that suggests the city's atmosphere is toxic to them. The residents ignore them mostly, stepping around them as they would around a piece of infrastructure that requires slightly different navigation.

This implies that the Thermal Hollows is not isolated, that there are other places with other modes of life, other adaptations to whatever this world offers. But no one talks about elsewhere in ways you can understand. References to other cities or regions come wrapped in context you don't have, described through frameworks that don't translate. As if the basin contains all relevant reality, and what lies beyond is too different to be meaningful.

The work people do is endlessly varied but falls into recognizable categories.

Maintenance is continuous and employs a vast portion of the population. Not just repair — that's only a small part — but active tending of systems that degrade without attention. Clearing filters, adjusting flows, monitoring processes, responding to small failures before they cascade. The city is too complex to be fully automated, requires human judgment integrated into every level of its operation. Or perhaps it could be fully automated but has chosen not to be, has designed itself to need human participation as a way of binding people into its systems.

Manufacturing happens everywhere, but it's strange manufacturing, hard to distinguish from biology. Growth chambers where materials are synthesized by what might be engineered bacteria or might be molecular assemblers or might be something between those categories. Vats where the quadrupeds are bred, though "bred" implies more intentionality than you observe — they seem to reproduce on their own, with humans just managing the populations, culling excess, distributing the creatures to districts that need them. Workshops where components are assembled, but the components are so strange that you can't determine what they're for, and they're assembled by people whose hands move too fast to follow, enhanced speed and precision producing objects that might be technological or might be artistic or might be both.

Resource extraction happens in the deep levels — not mining, exactly, but something like harvesting, bringing up materials from the substrate that are then processed into usable forms. The people who do this work are heavily modified, their bodies adapted to heat and pressure and darkness, their senses reconfigured to navigate environments that would kill baseline humans in minutes.

Coordination and planning occupy another class of workers, though their labor is less visible. People who sit in rooms full of interfaces, their attention clearly focused on system states you can't perceive, making adjustments to keep everything flowing smoothly. Or people who move through the city observing, taking notes on devices that might be recording data or might be performing real-time analysis. They're maintaining some kind of oversight, ensuring that the distributed processes sum to functional whole rather than collapsing into chaos.

Teaching happens but is mostly informal, embedded in other activities. Children and adolescents work alongside adults, learning through participation. There are spaces that look like schools — rooms where young people gather with someone clearly instructing — but the pedagogy is strange, seems to involve a lot of sitting quietly with eyes unfocused, perhaps learning through direct interface transfer, downloading skills and knowledge that would take years to acquire through conventional instruction.

Art exists, though it's integrated into everything else rather than separated into a distinct practice. People decorate surfaces while maintaining them, incorporate aesthetic choices into manufacturing, create patterns in how they move and coordinate. There are districts where the decoration is denser, more intentional, structures covered in color and texture that serves no obvious function. But even these zones are inhabited and functional — the art is applied to surfaces that also shed water, that also house people, that also participate in the city's material systems.

Music is constant but distributed. No concert halls, no audiences sitting still while performers execute compositions. Instead, people make sound while doing other things — whistling while they work, drumming to coordinate labor, singing to mark transitions. The bird-things contribute their metallic calls, the rat-things add high-frequency components, the quadrupeds provide bass notes with their breathing and movement. All of it together creates an acoustic environment that is literally music, the city composing itself through the aggregated sound of its inhabitants.

Some people seem to do nothing but observe, witness, record. They sit in public spaces for hours, watching, their attention focused but not fixed on anything specific. They're documenting something, or processing something, or performing some function that looks passive but might be crucial. Perhaps they're part of the city's self-awareness, the nodes through which it knows itself, and their observing is actually a form of sensing, feeding information back into systems that use human consciousness as a distributed sensor network.

The religious or spiritual life of the city is oblique, hard to parse.

You see practices that might be worship — people gathering in certain structures at certain times, engaging in synchronized movement or sound-making. But it's unclear what's being venerated. Not gods in conventional sense — no statues, no representations, no mythology you can extract through observation. Maybe they're worshipping the city itself, or the processes that sustain it, or some principle of flow and turbulence that they've elevated to sacred status.

There are people who seem to be priests or shamans, marked by specific augmentation or by clothing that's slightly different from standard practical wear. They move through the city performing functions that look ceremonial — touching structures at specific points, pouring water in particular ways, speaking or singing in spaces that amplify sound into strange patterns. They're treated with respect but also ignored, as if what they do is important but not something that requires attention from others.

Occasionally you encounter spaces that feel sacred — not because they're decorated differently or separated from daily use, but because people behave differently in them. Moving more slowly, more deliberately. Speaking in quieter tones or not at all. Performing actions that have the quality of ritual even when they're ordinary maintenance or transit. As if certain locations in the city are charged with significance that you can't directly perceive but that residents respond to instinctively.

The spiritual practice might be inseparable from the practical, worship expressed through right action rather than separate ceremony. Maintaining the city properly might be a form of prayer. Moving with correct flow through the crowd might be meditation. The whole life here might be religious practice, every action potentially sacred if performed with proper attention and integration.

Or there might be no spiritual life at all, just practices that look religious to outsiders but are actually functional responses to the city's requirements, behaviors that emerged because they work rather than because they express belief. The gathering and synchronized movement might be calibrating augmented senses, the ceremonial touching might be interfacing with infrastructure, the singing might be testing acoustic conditions. Everything that looks like religion might be engineering you don't understand.

The emotional tenor of life here is hard to read.

People don't seem unhappy, but they don't seem joyful either. The predominant affect is neutral, focused, present. Laughter happens but briefly. Anger flares but is quickly modulated. Deep sorrow is rarely visible — either it's processed privately or the city somehow regulates emotional extremes, dampening them through augmentation or environmental factors.

Children show more emotional range, but even they seem modulated compared to children elsewhere. They play enthusiastically but not frantically. They cry when hurt but recover quickly. They display curiosity that seems almost calibrated, intense enough to motivate learning but not so intense that it disrupts necessary activity.

This flattening of affect might be adaptation to density — emotional extremes might be dangerous when people live in such tight proximity, might cascade through crowds and create instability. Or it might be cultural, learned behavior passed through generations. Or it might be direct intervention, the augmentation itself regulating neurochemistry, keeping people within ranges that support high-functioning participation in the city's systems.

But you catch glimpses of intensity underneath. In quiet moments, in private spaces, you see people experiencing things they don't express publicly. Grief being processed in silent tears. Joy breaking through in private smiles. Frustration visible in tight shoulders and clenched jaws. The emotions exist but are kept internal, managed, not allowed to ripple through the collective.

There's deep satisfaction visible in how people engage with work, with the city, with each other. Not happiness exactly, but fulfillment, the sense of being properly integrated into something meaningful. People who are maintaining systems seem content doing so, not eager to be elsewhere or doing otherwise. People who are raising children seem engaged with the task, not resentful of its demands. As if the city has succeeded in aligning individual purpose with collective need, making participation feel right at some deep level.

Or as if people who don't experience this alignment leave, migrate to the margins and beyond, and what remains is a self-selected population that finds satisfaction in exactly the kind of life the city requires. The place might be full of people who belong here precisely because they're constitutionally suited to its particular demands.

Conflict exists but is subtle, rarely explosive.

You see disagreements conducted through body language more than words — two people in a stance that suggests argument, but their voices remain level, their movements controlled. The dispute might be resolved through appeal to third parties, or through consultation with some system that adjudicates, or through simply separating until the issue becomes irrelevant.

Violence is rare enough that when it occurs it draws crowds, not to participate but to witness and contain. A fight breaking out causes people to converge, forming a circle that prevents expansion but doesn't necessarily stop the conflict. The combatants exhaust themselves against each other while being spatially limited, then are separated and taken somewhere — to be treated if injured, to be counseled or disciplined or recalibrated, you can't determine.

The rarity of violence might be regulatory, the augmentation making aggression difficult to sustain, or the city itself emitting signals that suppress it. Or it might be cultural, deeply ingrained prohibition enforced through social pressure. Or it might be practical — in a place this dense, violence would be too dangerous, would threaten too many people too quickly, and so has been selected against through generations until only people constitutionally calm remain.

Property seems fluid, not defended with the intensity you'd expect. People use spaces and objects that aren't clearly theirs, and others accept this without conflict. Personal possessions are minimal — people carry almost nothing, wear simple clothing that's standardized in style if not in exact form. What would be theft elsewhere is more like circulation here, objects and tools moving through the population based on need rather than ownership.

This might be enabled by abundance — the city produces enough that no one needs to hoard. Or it might be enforcement through monitoring — the city knows who has what, and taking more than your share would be detected and corrected. Or it might be deep cultural conditioning, the concept of exclusive ownership eroded through generations until what remains is a kind of collective stewardship where everyone has access to everything, mediated by informal rules about usage and return.

The political structure, if there is one, is invisible.

No evident hierarchy, no one clearly in command, no buildings that house government or administration. Decisions seem to be made through some distributed process, needs arising and being addressed without anyone directing the response. A structure needs repair — workers appear and fix it. A district needs reconfiguration — people gradually shift its usage until it transforms. Resources need redistribution — they flow through the city's systems to where they're required.

This might be anarchism, genuine self-organization at massive scale. Or it might be technocracy so complete that governance happens entirely through automated systems, with humans serving as sensing and response mechanisms within a larger artificial intelligence that is the city itself. Or it might be democracy so distributed that every augmented person is voting constantly on micro-decisions, their participation aggregated into collective choices that emerge without any individual determining them.

You see people who seem to have authority in specific contexts — someone directing a work crew, someone allocating resources in a manufacturing space, someone making decisions about care for an ill person. But the authority is contextual and temporary, granted for a situation and then dissolved. There are no titles, no permanent positions, no one whose role is to command rather than to participate.

The city might be governing itself, with humans as components in its decision-making but not the ultimate source of intention. Or the humans might be governing collectively at a level too distributed to perceive, their augmentation linking them into a group consciousness that makes decisions none of them individually control. The experience of living here would be similar in either case — a sense of being guided by something larger than yourself, participating in choices without making them, part of a collective will that emerges from the aggregation of individual actions and preferences.

There's no visible military, no police, no armed force of any kind. Security seems to be provided by the city itself — people who become threats somehow don't persist as threats, are removed or transformed or simply stop being problematic through mechanisms you can't observe. Maybe the augmentation allows direct intervention in someone becoming dangerous, shutting down aggression before it manifests. Maybe the fauna serves security functions, the rat-things and bird-things and quadrupeds monitoring for threats and responding in coordinated ways. Maybe the city just doesn't need security because it's selected for people who don't threaten it, and anyone who would is expelled before they can act.

The relationship between residents and visitors suggests permeability but also protection. Outsiders are allowed in, tolerated, even provided for, but they're also clearly tracked, limited in where they can go and what they can access. Some districts seem closed to visitors, the residents politely but firmly redirecting attempts to enter. Information is withheld, not through active secrecy but through passive opacity — questions get answered vaguely, details remain unclear, understanding stays superficial until you've been present long enough to integrate.

New arrivals — people who come intending to stay — go through something that might be orientation or might be transformation. They're taken into structures where they spend days or weeks, emerging different. Changed in ways that might be purely educational or might be physical, might involve augmentation installation or might be psychological conditioning or might be both. When they re-emerge, they move differently, participate more smoothly, seem to understand protocols and patterns that they couldn't have learned through simple observation.

This suggests the city actively shapes its inhabitants, doesn't just accept them but remakes them into forms that fit its requirements. Or that living here requires such specific adaptation that rapid transformation is necessary for survival, and the orientation is practical rather than ideological. Or that the city has memetic properties, changes minds through proximity and immersion, and the structured orientation just accelerates a process that would happen eventually anyway.

Long-term residents show progressive transformation, becoming more integrated over years and decades. Their augmentation becomes more extensive, their movement more fluid, their participation more seamless. They develop capabilities that newer residents lack — can navigate purely by sensing information fields, can interface with city systems through intention alone, can coordinate with others through mechanisms that look like telepathy but might be just very sophisticated communication technology.

The oldest residents, the ones you glimpse moving through the city like ghosts, might not be fully human anymore in any meaningful sense. They might be posthuman, or transhuman, or simply so deeply integrated with the city's systems that the category "human" no longer applies cleanly. They might be demonstrations of where the transformation leads if you stay long enough, integrate deeply enough, surrender sufficiently to the city's processes.

Or they might be warnings, examples of going too far, losing too much of whatever makes individual existence valuable. They're respected but also avoided, given space, not emulated by most residents who maintain some distance from full integration, preserving enough separation to remain recognizably themselves.

The children born here have never been anything else, don't have a baseline to compare against. They're native to the density, to the heat and moisture, to the augmentation and coordination and constant turbulence. They might be a new subspecies, adapted to this place so specifically that they couldn't easily live elsewhere. Or they might be completely standard humans just raised in radically different conditions, demonstrating how plastic human development actually is.

They seem comfortable in ways that would be impossible for someone who arrived as an adult. They sleep in the noise, move in the crowds, breathe the thick air without effort. They develop augmentation from early ages, integrating technology that shapes their neural development, their sensory systems growing around the interfaces rather than having interfaces added to already-formed senses.

These children, as they mature, will be more city than their parents, more integrated, more capable. Each generation likely shifts further toward whatever the city is optimizing for. In several more generations, the residents might be so transformed that they're effectively a different species, human-derived but specialized to this environment, unable to survive outside it, perfectly adapted to live nowhere else.

This might be the city's purpose — not just to house humans but to transform them, to create a new kind of being that exists in perfect symbiosis with high-density technological environments. Or it might be accidental, just the inevitable result of living in extreme conditions with powerful modification technology available, adaptation occurring without intention.

The people don't seem concerned about this trajectory, or maybe they don't perceive it. From inside the process, each change is small, practical, obviously beneficial. You augment to breathe easier, to navigate better, to participate more fully. Collectively, incrementally, you become something else, but there's never a single moment when the transformation is obvious or troubling.

Or maybe they do perceive it and embrace it, see the transformation as positive, as becoming more than baseline humanity, as evolving into something better suited to the universe they inhabit. Maybe the city is full of volunteers, people actively pursuing integration and transformation, eager to become posthuman if that's what flourishing in this place requires.

The alternative — trying to remain unchanged, preserving baseline human form and function — would require constant resistance to the city's pressures, would make life here much harder, maybe impossible. Adaptation is the path of least resistance, and the city makes adaptation easy, provides all the tools and support necessary to transform yourself into something that fits.

So people do transform, gradually, incrementally, generationally. And the city fills with beings that started human and remain human enough but are also becoming something else, something new, something that has no name yet because it's still in the process of becoming.

They move through the moisture and heat and noise and density with perfect comfort, these residents, these citizens, these components of a larger system that might be organism or might be machine or might have transcended that distinction entirely. They work and rest and reproduce and die and are recycled, and the city continues, and the basin holds it all, and the transformation proceeds, and whatever is emerging from the process remains unknown, even to those who are becoming it.

This is who lives in the Thermal Hollows. People who are becoming something else. People who might no longer be people, or might be more than people, or might be demonstrating what people can become when environment and technology and density combine in certain ways.

They would not recognize this description of themselves. From inside, they're just living, just participating, just existing in the only place and the only way that makes sense to them. The strangeness is only strange from outside. From inside, it's just life, just normal, just the way things are and have always been and will always continue to be, world without end, becoming without completion, process without resolution, sustained by heat and moisture and constant coordination through mechanisms too distributed to fully perceive, too embedded to separate from the simple fact of being alive here, now, together, in this low place where the air has weight and everything is always wet and the sound never stops.
