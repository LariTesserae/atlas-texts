# The Arithmeticians

The Hydroponic Ossuary is tended by people who have forgotten they are people in the way you might forget you are breathing.

There are seven of them, though you will never see more than three at once, and often you will see none at all—only the evidence of their passage: a newly cleaned brass fitting, a redirected vine, a cup moved from one sill to another, still warm. They do not hide from you, exactly. They are simply operating on schedules measured in the growth of roots, and your presence is a phenomenon too brief to require acknowledgment. A mayfly, visiting their cathedral of increments.

They call themselves Arithmeticians, though not aloud. They have not spoken in years—possibly twelve years, possibly forty. Time is uncertain here, and they are uncertain about time, and the uncertainty has become a comfortable state, a preferred state, like the moisture-saturated air that is neither rain nor absence.

Each of them was someone else before. Each arrived separately, by different vessels, in different states of flight or seeking or collapse. The Ossuary does not call people—it is not that kind of place—but it also does not reject them when they climb the exterior ladders (there are always ladders, though they are not always in the same locations) and enter the green-gold interior and feel the warm breath of the structure's circulation and realize: *yes, here, this is where movement stops.*

The first of them has been here longest. She no longer remembers her name, though she remembers she had one, remembers the shape of it in her mouth like the shape of a fruit she ate once. She is tall, or was tall—the years of moving through vine-tunnels in a permanent half-crouch have given her a forward curve that makes height irrelevant. Her hair, which might have been black, has gone the color of the ceramic tiles—not gray, but a specific warm white-cream that exists only in industrial materials from a certain era. She wears clothes that were once uniform: canvas coveralls, work boots with composite toes, a shirt with loops for tools. All of it has been mended so many times with whatever fabric comes to hand—mostly strips of the same moisture-resistant material that wraps the pipes—that the original garment is more concept than object.

She works on the lower levels, where the light is dimmest and the moisture collects in pools and the plants are the ones that have learned to want almost nothing. Her hands are stained permanent green from moss-handling. She moves through the structure by touch as much as sight, reading the pipes like braille, knowing which valve will be slightly loose (the one on the fourth platform going clockwise from the central shaft), which section of grating rings differently when stepped on (replaced three years ago, or seven, after the old piece developed a crack no one saw but everyone heard). 

Her task—though task is wrong, purpose is wrong, all words are wrong except maybe *practice*—is the water. She monitors the circulation not with instruments but with her hands in the flow, her ear against the pipes, the taste of it on her fingers. The water should be this temperature, this mineral content, this subtle balance of clear and cloudy. When it shifts, she adjusts: opens a valve here to slow the flow, closes one there to redirect it, adds minerals from stores kept in ceramic jars (the minerals arrive somehow, are left on certain platforms, she does not ask from where).

She rarely ascends above the fifth level. The upper regions with their clear air and harsh light are for younger eyes, younger lungs. She lives in the twilight, in the spaces between the massive root masses, in the sound of water finding its patient way through the structure's veins. At night—or in the cycle she has decided is night—she sleeps in a room that was once a pump housing, now emptied of machinery, floored with moss two inches thick. She lies down and the plants grow incrementally overhead and the moisture condenses and falls in drops so perfectly timed they become silence.

The second Arithmetician is a small man, compact, with the build of someone who was once athletic and has been reconfigured by different movements. He maintains the catwalks and platforms on the middle levels—the zones of densest growth, where the plants threaten most aggressively to reclaim all metal as trellis. He carries tools: pruning shears that he sharpens daily on a whetstone kept in his pocket, a small pry bar for removing vines that have forced themselves into joints they shouldn't occupy, wire for training growth away from critical structural points.

But "maintains" is also wrong, "pruning" is not quite right. He does not fight the plants. He negotiates with them. He moves a vine from this beam to that one, where it will do no harm and might do some good—might support a weak point, might shade a moisture collector, might create a living arch that people (person, him, the others) can pass beneath. He removes growth only when it has made a mistake: has tried to grow into a gap too small, has strangled itself, has blocked a drainage path in a way that will cause rot rather than abundance.

He knows every plant in his territory. Not species—he doesn't think in those categories—but individuals. This fern is the one that grows faster on its left side and must be rotated monthly. That vine is the one that will die back dramatically every seventh cycle, appearing dead, then explode in new growth a week later; do not mourn it or remove it prematurely. This moss is the one that has a smell like copper when bruised; step carefully around it or the scent will stay in your nose for days.

He was a engineer before, or a doctor, or something that required precision and systematic thinking. The Ossuary accepted those instincts and bent them sideways. Here, precision is measured in the angle of a leaf. Systems are measured in the time it takes a root to extend. He has developed a way of seeing that is all periphery: he doesn't look directly at the plants he tends but past them, letting his vision soften, until the growth patterns become visible as shapes of light and shadow, as densities, as flows. From this sideways sight, he can spot imbalance before it manifests—a slight leaning that will become a fall, a slight discoloration that will become a blight.

He sleeps differently each night. He has six places—or eight, he has lost count—scattered through the middle levels, all of them small corners or alcoves where he has placed a piece of canvas to keep off the worst of the dripping. He rotates through them according to a pattern that makes sense to him, that might be related to the blooming cycles or might be related to something else. He rarely dreams, or dreams only in green, which amounts to the same thing.

The third is hardly ever seen. She—and it is she, though just barely, in the way that someone can be any gender when they have ceased to be social—works in the upper reaches, in the clear air, where the structure meets the sky. She is responsible for the glass panels, the translucent ceiling, the apparatus that might be measurement or music. She is younger than the others, or seems younger, or has aged differently in the cleaner air and harder light. Her skin is brown and weathered to leather by exposure to actual sunlight, a rarity in this place. She wears less clothing than the others, just loose pants that might have been white once, now permanently stained with algae and rust in organic patterns.

She spends her days on the exposed platforms, cleaning the glass panels with long-handled squeegees, scraping algae from the translucent sections, adjusting the angle of panels that are hinged (some are hinged, though their mechanism is mysterious—they seem to adjust themselves mostly, but sometimes require help). This work is done slowly, panel by panel, and there are hundreds of panels. She will finish cleaning all of them in perhaps two months, and by then the first ones will need cleaning again. The cycle is perfect. The work is never complete and never fails to be completed.

She also tends the brass apparatus on the highest platform, though "tends" here means mostly just bearing witness. She polishes certain sections—the ones that seem to want it, that grow dull in a way that feels like asking. Other sections she leaves corroded, verdant with patina. She has learned which pipes to touch (this one, when the sun is at a certain angle, is warm enough to hurt), which valves to never adjust (they are set to positions that might be critical or might be memorial; she doesn't know and won't risk finding out), which parts are actually sculpture and which are function.

Sometimes the apparatus makes sound. Low tones, felt more than heard, that resonate through the structure and cause subtle changes in the drip patterns, the plant orientations, the mist density. She has learned to hear these sounds coming a few minutes before they occur—a quality in the air, a pressure in her sinuses—and she positions herself at the apparatus when they happen, places her hands on specific pipes, becomes part of the resonance circuit. Why? She couldn't say. It seems required. It seems like the thing the apparatus wants, or the thing she wants from the apparatus, or the thing the structure wants from both of them.

She sees the other structures, the other towers rising from the mist. Sometimes she sees figures on their highest platforms—other Arithmeticians, perhaps, or something else. She has never attempted to signal them. Distance here is absolute. Each tower is its own certainty.

At night she sleeps on the highest platform, under the stars when they are visible, under the clouds when they aren't. She has a shelter—a small tent made from one of the translucent panel materials, stitched with wire—but she rarely uses it. The rain, when it comes, is warm. The structure breathes heat from below. She has learned to sleep in the open, to wake with dew on her face, to begin her work when the light begins without any transition between sleeping and waking.

The fourth Arithmetician is difficult to describe because he is in motion. He walks the structure constantly, ascending and descending, circling, crossing from platform to platform. He never seems to stop, never seems to tire. He is thin in the way of long-distance travelers, all tendon and economy. His hair is bound back tightly. He wears a vest with many pockets, all of them containing small objects: seeds, mostly, but also pebbles, bits of metal, ceramic shards, dried flowers, screws, washers, things whose purposes have been forgotten but whose presence feels necessary.

His task is distribution. He carries seeds from plants on one level to growing beds on another, facilitating a gene flow that the structure's isolation might otherwise prevent. He collects the strange geometric seed-pods when they fall, opens them, sorts their contents, plants them in locations he determines through what looks like random walk but is probably something else—a sensitivity to the structure's needs, a reading of where growth wants to go next.

He also redistributes objects. He finds the ceramic cup on the windowsill, moves it to a different windowsill, plants something new in it. He discovers tools left by one of the others, hangs them in a more convenient location. He adjusts the small shrines—because that's what they are, the walking-spaces that feel inhabited—rearranging the objects there according to principles of balance or memory or aesthetics so subtle they might not exist, might be pure whim elevated to practice.

He is the only one who sometimes speaks, though not to the others—to the plants, to the structure, to himself. Single words, occasionally: "Yes." "Here." "Grow." Fragments that might be from a language he used to speak or might be a language he is inventing. The other Arithmeticians hear him sometimes, passing in the distance, a murmur in the green tunnels, and feel comforted that sound still exists, that words are still possible even if conversation is not.

He sleeps walking, or seems to. He will slow down gradually over the course of an hour, his circles tightening, until he is standing in place, swaying slightly, eyes closed or open—it makes no difference. He remains like this for a time—an hour, two hours—then gradually resumes motion, his circles widening, his path continuing from wherever it paused. He has no fixed resting place. He belongs to all the levels equally and to none of them.

The fifth Arithmetician works with the reservoirs—the collected water on various levels where it pools and deepens, where the aquatic plants grow. She is older, or seems older, with white hair and hands that shake slightly in a way that doesn't impede her work, that might even be part of her method. She moves slowly, rests often, but accomplishes everything that needs accomplishing.

She tends the water lilies, the submerged grasses, the floating root masses. She thins them when they grow too dense, transplants sections to new reservoirs, removes dead matter before it can foul the water. She also tends what lives in the water but is not plants—the microscopic life, the bacterial colonies, the things that make water living instead of merely pure. She has jars of these cultures, stored in cool corners, and she doses the reservoirs with them regularly, maintaining balances she understands through intuition rather than measurement.

She was a scientist before, perhaps. Or a mother. Or both. She has the manner of someone who has spent a life paying attention to small things, to subtle shifts in health and thriving. She notices when the pH has changed, when the mineral content has shifted, when a reservoir has become too warm or too cool. She adjusts by adding or removing water, by introducing certain plants that will alter the chemistry, by waiting—most often by waiting, letting the system correct itself while she watches to ensure the correction stays within bounds.

She talks to the water. Not words—sounds. Humming, mostly, low tones that might be song or might be the same resonance the brass apparatus produces, translated through a human throat. The water responds, or seems to—ripples form, plants orient, something changes that she can feel but not explain.

She sleeps beside her largest reservoir, in a corner where someone (her? someone before?) has built a small platform just above the waterline. She lies there and listens to the gentle lapping against the ceramic edges, and the sound is so close to silence that sleep and waking blur into a single state of restful attention.

The sixth is the youngest, or the most recent arrival—the two are not the same here. He might have been here three years or ten. He doesn't know and hasn't asked. He works with the soil, the growing substrate, the material that fills the containers and beds. He is responsible for its composition, its health, its slow renewal.

This is heavy work. He moves compost from the decomposition chambers (there are several, placed strategically, where plant waste becomes soil again through the patient action of microbes and time) to the growing beds, replacing depleted material with fresh. He breaks down the root masses of plants that have died or been removed, shredding them, mixing them with other organic matter, creating new substrate. He adjusts mixtures for different plants—more drainage for these, more water retention for those, different mineral profiles for others.

He is strong in the way of people who do physical work slowly and constantly. His shoulders are broad, his movements efficient. He was a laborer before, or a farmer, or someone accustomed to the body's capabilities and limits. The Ossuary has asked nothing new of his muscles, only redirected their purpose from whatever fields or factories he left behind to these elevated gardens.

He has developed a relationship with weight, with gravity, with the way the structure resists his efforts or assists them. He knows which ramps are at perfect angles for wheeling his barrow, which platforms have floors that distribute load correctly, which beams can support him if he needs to climb. The structure has become a tool he uses without thinking, an extension of his body's mechanical logic.

He rarely looks up. His world is the substrate, the containers, the lower levels where the heavy work happens. He knows the upper reaches exist—sometimes he brings soil there, for new plantings—but he doesn't linger. His comfort is in the dim twilight zones, in the smell of good earth, in the satisfaction of a bed properly prepared, waiting for seeds that someone else will plant.

He sleeps near the compost chambers, which should be unpleasant but isn't—the decomposition here is aerobic, well-managed, smelling of forest floor rather than rot. He has a hammock strung between two beams, and he lies in it and feels the gentle rocking as the structure settles and rises with temperature changes, and the motion is so subtle it becomes stillness, and the stillness becomes sleep.

The seventh Arithmetician is the one you might see but not recognize as human. She has been here longest after the first, or has merged most completely with the place, or has always been here and the others have forgotten that arrival is supposed to precede presence. She is small, hunched, moves through the vine tunnels like water through pipes—inevitable, unobstructed, taking the path that exists.

Her task is the strange plants—the ones with leaves that reflect light wrong, the ones with geometric flowers, the ones whose fronds fractal into impossible detail. She is their keeper, or their student, or their collaborator. She has learned what they need, which is not quite what other plants need: they want to be spoken to in particular rhythms, want their containers moved to follow patterns only they can perceive, want to be observed at specific times of day when their strangeness is most pronounced.

She has notebooks—small ones, made from paper that resists the moisture (where did she get such paper? where does anything here come from that isn't grown?). She draws the plants, but not botanically—she draws their patterns, their mathematical structures, the relationships between their parts. The notebooks are filled with spirals and grids and symbols that might be numbers or might be something else. She is calculating something, or discovering something, or documenting something that has already been calculated and discovered.

She was a mathematician, perhaps. Or a mystic. Or someone who found the place where those two practices meet and merge and become indistinguishable. She looks at the plants and sees equations. She listens to the structure and hears proofs. The strange geometry of the place—the columns that count differently depending on direction, the containers that repeat with wrong variations, the platforms that don't quite align—all of this makes sense to her, is the reason she came or stayed or never left.

She sleeps in a different location every night, never repeating, following a path through the structure that might be a spiral or might be a system of proof written in sleeping-places. She lies down, she stands up, and between those two states something happens that resembles sleep but is really calculation, the structure's strange mathematics working through her sleeping brain, emerging as intuitions about which plants to move, which seeds to plant together, which modifications to suggest to the structure through subtle rearrangements of objects.

---

The seven of them almost never occupy the same level at the same time. They have learned each other's patterns without ever discussing them, have developed a natural circulation that mirrors the water and air flowing through the structure. When they do encounter each other—passing on a catwalk, working on opposite sides of a platform—they acknowledge it with the smallest of gestures: a tilt of the head, a hand raised briefly, a pause in movement. Then they continue. Everything that needed saying has been said, or was never going to be said, or is being said constantly through their work, through the health of the plants, through the continuation of the structure's perfect functioning.

They are fed by the garden, mostly. The fruits are edible, nutritious if not varied. There are tubers that can be harvested without killing the plants. There are leaves that can be eaten as greens. One of the reservoirs has been given over entirely to a rice-like grain that grows in standing water. They prepare food simply—there is a small kitchen on one of the middle platforms, with a heat source that runs on the same mysterious energy that warms the structure—and eat alone, or sometimes in pairs if their patterns happen to align, chewing in silence, tasting green.

Other supplies appear. This is one of the structure's mysteries that they have accepted without solving. Every few weeks—or months, time is uncertain—someone (the fourth Arithmetician, usually, in his wanderings) will find a platform that has received objects: cloth, tools, seeds of new plant varieties, minerals for the water, paper, pencils, sometimes food that doesn't grow here (dried fish, salt, tea). These gifts are never acknowledged, never discussed. They are distributed to whoever needs them, incorporated into the work, and life continues.

Perhaps there are others—people on the lower levels below the water-line, people in the other towers visible from the highest platform, people who come and go while the Arithmeticians sleep or aren't watching. Perhaps the structure itself provides, has learned to manifest what its inhabitants need. Perhaps the line between people and structure has blurred so completely that the appearance of supplies is just the structure tending itself, using human hands for tasks that require them.

The Arithmeticians have stopped asking. Questions here don't lead to answers—they lead to deeper attention, to closer observation, to the realization that the mystery is not a problem to solve but a condition to inhabit.

---

They are changing, slowly. The first Arithmetician's hands are no longer entirely distinct from the moss she tends—the green staining goes deeper each year, and sometimes she'll look at her fingers and forget for a moment where plant ends and person begins. The second Arithmetician's vision has shifted permanently into that peripheral softness, so that direct sight has become difficult, almost painful; he navigates by blur and edge-detection, by the shapes of density rather than the clarity of detail.

The third Arithmetician on the high platforms has begun to photosynthesize, or something like it—she eats less, sleeps less when the sun is strong, feels energy in direct light in a way that humans aren't supposed to feel it. The fourth Arithmetician in his constant walking has developed a pace that matches the growth rate of the fastest vines; he moves at plant-speed now, visible motion but barely, and the structure adjusts to him, creates paths where he needs them moment by moment.

The fifth Arithmetician's trembling has become more pronounced, but it's not weakness—it's resonance. She shakes at the same frequency the water moves, the same rhythm the structure settles. She has become an instrument tuned to the place. The sixth Arithmetician's strength has passed the human threshold; he lifts weights that should require machinery, but slowly, so slowly that you don't notice until the thing has moved, until the impossible has already occurred.

The seventh Arithmetician has stopped casting a shadow, or casts too many shadows, or her shadow doesn't quite align with her body—it's hard to say which. She exists in a state of uncertainty that has become certainty, that has become her defining characteristic. She is both there and not-there, quantum-state manifest, the structure's mathematics made flesh.

They will not leave. The thought doesn't occur to them, or occurs and is dismissed instantly, or never needed dismissing because it was never a possibility. They are part of the structure's ecology now, as necessary as the water circulation, as permanent as the green. They have become what they tend, or were always what they tend and have finally admitted it.

New Arithmeticians will come, perhaps. The structure has room—there are empty platforms, untended sections, work that could be distributed differently. Or perhaps seven is the correct number, the right balance, the proper density for human presence in a place this size. Perhaps they know, through their strange mathematics and their merged consciousness with the structure, that seven is perfect and will remain seven, each of them continuing indefinitely, aging or not aging, human or post-human or something that never needed the distinction.

---

At night—or in the cycle they have agreed is night—they sometimes gather without planning it. Not all seven, but several, drawn to the same platform by coincidence or synchronicity or the structure's will. They sit in the green twilight, in the soft sound of dripping, and they exist together in silence that is not empty but full, pregnant with everything they don't need to say.

The work will continue tomorrow. The water will circulate. The plants will grow their fractions of inches. The strange mathematics will remain strange and mathematical. The structure will breathe its warm moisture. The mist will rise from below and the light will filter through from above and the density will remain perfect—not too empty, not too full, exactly as inhabited as it needs to be by seven people who are barely people anymore, who have become extensions of the garden, who have learned to live at the speed of green, who have forgotten their names but remember their purpose, who are solving equations written in vine-growth and drip-patterns and the slow settling of industrial metal under the weight of abundant life.

This is what lives here: not abandonment, not wildness, not automation. Intention, distributed across seven bodies and uncountable plants and one breathing structure. Intention so patient it looks like absence. Care so slow it resembles neglect. Presence so merged with place that it becomes invisible, becomes the place itself, becomes the warm green beating heart of a tower-garden-engine that was built for forgotten purposes and has found new ones, grown them like vines, tended them like the Arithmeticians tend everything: with endless attention and no words and hands that are no longer entirely separate from what they touch.

The structure rises from the mist, inhabited by seven and by green and by the space between them where human and plant and metal have learned to be the same thing, to grow together, to calculate each other, to be the answer to a question about what comes after people, what comes after gardens, what comes after the distinction between tending and being tended stops meaning anything at all.
