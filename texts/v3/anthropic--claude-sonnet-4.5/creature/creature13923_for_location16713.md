# The Inhabitants of the Plateau of Recursive Gardens

## The People Who Are Becoming Plant

The humans here—and they are human, still, mostly—have lived so long inside living architecture that the boundary has begun to blur.

Not physically, or not always physically. They don't have bark for skin or roots for feet. But watch someone who's lived here for decades: the way they pause before entering a structure, hand on the doorframe, a moment of contact that might be greeting or asking permission or simply acknowledgment. The way they'll stroke a wall absently while thinking, the same gesture you'd use to pet an animal. The way they sleep better in the plant-structures than anywhere else, as if the slight movement of the living walls, the subtle exhalations of sap and moisture, have become their lullaby.

Their timescales have shifted. A year is nothing here—that's barely a growing season, not enough to see results from any serious pruning. Five years is when you start to see if an architectural decision was sound. Twenty years is when a new structure really settles. The old people talk about changes that will matter "when the deep roots reach the water layer"—a process that might take a century—with the casual assurance that of course someone will be here to see it, someone will continue the work.

They move differently than valley people. More slowly, but not lazily—economically. They've learned the rhythm of working at elevation, the careful breathing, the steady pace that you can maintain for hours without exhausting yourself in the thin air. They've learned to read plant-growth the way sailors read weather, seeing in the angle of a stem or the color of leaves information about moisture, structural stress, disease, age, vitality.

Their hands are marked by the work: callused from pruning tools, stained green from sap that never quite washes out, occasionally scarred from mistakes (the plants don't bite, exactly, but some have thorns, some have edges, some have sap that burns). The oldest workers' hands are almost wooden themselves, skin gone thick and grooved like bark, fingers slightly curved as if permanently shaped around pruning-knife handles.

They smell of the city. It's not unpleasant—a green smell, chlorophyll and moisture and something sweeter from the flowering walls, underlaid with the mineral scent of stone and the particular clean coldness of high elevation. But it's distinctive. A person from the plateau carries that smell for days after leaving, and valley-traders can identify city-residents by scent before seeing them clearly.

### The Listeners

These are the people who have gone furthest into the strange communion between human and city.

You can identify them by their eyes, usually. There's a quality of not-quite-focus, as if they're looking at something behind or beyond what's physically present. Or by the way they'll stop mid-conversation, mid-task, head tilted, attending to something nobody else perceives. Or by the fact that they spend hours, days sometimes, in the weird zones where reality frays, sitting motionless in the dangerous contaminated spaces, and return with information they shouldn't have.

The process of becoming a listener isn't formalized. There's no training program, no apprenticeship exactly. It's more that certain people—usually noticed in childhood—have an affinity. They're the ones who don't avoid the weird zones out of healthy caution but are drawn to them. Who report seeing colors that don't exist or hearing the city's sub-audible hum more clearly than they hear human speech. Who touch a plant-structure and *know* things about it—not through observation but through direct transfer of information.

Most communities would call these people mad and isolate them. Here, they're valued but also watched carefully, because the line between useful listener and dangerously contaminated is uncertain and possibly nonexistent—just a matter of degree and what you're willing to risk.

A listener named Koreth (they/them, age uncertain, somewhere between thirty and fifty, time moves strangely for them): tall, thin to the point of gauntness, skin gone sallow from too much time in low-light weird-zones. They dress in layers of fabric made from deep-growth plant fibers, the dark nearly-black material that comes from those photosynthetically wrong species. Their hands shake slightly, constantly, a fine tremor that never stops but doesn't seem to impair function.

Koreth can tell you where to expand a structure, where the plant-mass is strong enough to support new growth and where it's stressed and needs rest. They can diagnose factory-machine problems without seeing the machines, just by pressing a hand to the factory's outer wall and standing there, trembling, for several minutes. They can warn you about coming instabilities—when reality is going to flex more than usual, when the weird zones are going to expand temporarily, when manifestations are likely.

They can do this because they're not entirely separate from the city anymore. "It thinks in me," they explained once, words coming slowly. "Or I think in it. The difference..." A long pause. "The difference isn't important. There are thoughts happening. Some of them are useful for human purposes. Those I speak."

Ask them if it's worth it, the contamination, the loss of stable self, and they look confused by the question. "I'm not lost," they say. "I'm more. I'm distributed. I'm in the roots under the western factory. I'm in the membrane-walls of the council hall. I'm in the marker-trees in the memorial garden. Why would I be less than I was when I was just the body?"

But you watch them sometimes fail to eat unless reminded, staring at nothing, lips moving soundlessly. You watch them weep without apparent emotion, tears running down their face while they continue whatever task they're doing, as if the tears are just another form of condensation, the body dripping like the walls drip. You watch them lose the thread of conversations with humans, distracted by dialogues with plant-masses that nobody else can perceive.

They're useful. Invaluable, even. The city might not function without the listeners translating its needs and moods. But nobody wants their children to become listeners. There's no coming back from it.

### The Factory-Tenders

These are different from the listeners, though there's overlap. Factory-tenders work with the industrial plant-machines, developing relationships with specific installations, learning their quirks and moods and needs.

A tender named Vaush (she/her, forty-something, stocky and strong): her hair is tied back in a practical knot, usually damp with condensation from the humid factory interiors. She wears minimal clothing despite the cool air—the factories run warm with their compost-heat and constant activity, and she's learned it's easier to work bare-armed and accept being soaked with condensation than to deal with wet fabric clinging and restricting movement.

She tends a section of the textile factory, the spinning-rooms where water-driven wheels produce thread. She knows every wheel personally—not anthropomorphizing them exactly, but recognizing that each has individual characteristics. This wheel runs fast and produces thin thread, good for fine work. That wheel is slower but produces stronger thread, better for structural applications. This third wheel is temperamental, needs its sap-hydraulics bled every three days or it starts to stutter, and when it stutters it produces thread with weak spots that will fail under stress.

She can hear problems before they manifest. A change in the tone of a wheel's hum, a shift in the rhythm of sap pumping through the hydraulic vines, a smell of overheating or under-lubrication. She'll adjust, oil, bleed pressure, redirect water flow, prune back growth that's interfering with movement, all without consulting diagrams or manuals because the knowledge is in her body, in her hands, learned through years of daily contact.

Ask her how the machines work and she'll tell you—up to a point. The water turns the wheels, the wheels drive the spindles, the spindles twist the fiber into thread. But ask her about the integration of the living plant-matter with the mechanical elements, about how the biological and mechanical interface, about why the wheels sometimes refuse to turn even when water flows and mechanical function seems fine, and she'll shrug.

"They have preferences," she says. "Moods, maybe. Or maybe the city has preferences about what should be produced when, and the machines feel that and respond. I don't know the mechanism. I just know that if you pay attention, if you listen and watch and touch, you can feel what they need. And if you give them what they need, they work."

She has apprentices, younger workers learning the trade. The teaching is mostly demonstration and supervised practice. "Try to start this wheel," she'll say, and watch while the apprentice goes through the mechanical steps—checking water flow, releasing brake-vines, ensuring the sap-pressure is adequate. The wheel doesn't turn. "Now watch," and she approaches the wheel, places both hands on its frame, stands there for thirty seconds in what looks like meditation or prayer or simply waiting. Then she steps back and gestures: "Try again." The apprentice repeats the same steps. Now the wheel turns smoothly.

"What did you do?" the apprentice asks, frustrated.

"Asked," Vaush says. "Listened. Made sure it was ready. You'll learn. It takes time. You have to become familiar. The machines have to become familiar with you."

Some apprentices never get it. They can perform all the mechanical tasks perfectly but never develop the sense of the machines as responsive entities, never learn to feel the rightness or wrongness of a moment for starting or stopping. These workers are useful for basic maintenance but never become true tenders. They usually transfer to other work—farming, construction, trade—where the interface between human and plant is simpler, less demanding of intuition.

The tenders who succeed often develop tics, unconscious habits acquired from their machines. Vaush rocks slightly when standing still, a motion that matches the rhythm of her wheels. Another tender, working with presses, has developed a breathing pattern that syncs with the compression cycles. A third, maintaining one of the compost-heat systems, has hands that are always slightly warmer than human normal, as if they've absorbed and retained heat from years of contact.

None of them think this is strange. Or rather, they acknowledge it's unusual but don't consider it problematic. "Of course the work shapes you," Vaush says. "That's what work does. Better to be shaped by honest machines that need you than by... whatever shapes valley-people." She means this disparagingly, though she's never lived in the valleys, doesn't actually know what shapes valley-people except through stories and brief encounters with traders.

### The Architects

These are the people who design new structures, plan expansions, understand the city at the level of overall pattern rather than individual detail.

An architect named Bhen (he/him, sixty and deeply weathered): he moves slowly, careful of joints that complain in the cool damp air, but his mind is quick and layered. He's been working with the city's growth for forty years, since his teens. He remembers sections of the city that have since been pruned away or collapsed. He remembers experiments that failed—structures that couldn't sustain themselves, species combinations that didn't achieve symbiosis, expansions that had to be abandoned because the underlying root-mass wasn't strong enough.

He works primarily through sketches and models. The sketches are made on paper-bark (pressed plant fiber, labor-intensive to produce, used sparingly), drawn with ink derived from certain fungi. The models are constructed from twigs and fiber, small three-dimensional representations of proposed structures. But the real work happens in his head and through consultation with listeners and long periods of simply standing in potential building sites, observing, feeling, thinking.

"You can't force the city to grow in ways it doesn't want to," he explains. "You can guide, suggest, provide frameworks. But the plant-mass has its own logic. It wants to grow in certain patterns, wants certain forms. If you try to impose too much, it resists—grows too fast and chaotically, or not at all, or grows and then collapses because it wasn't structurally sound at some level you couldn't see."

He describes architecture as negotiation. You propose: what if we extend this residential cluster northward, adding capacity for twenty more family-units? And then you listen—not metaphorically but through the listeners, through your own sense developed over decades, through trial plots where you plant the initial framework and watch how it takes. The city responds: yes but not there, slightly more west, and not as tall as you're thinking, and use more of the deep-growth species in the foundation because that area needs extra stability.

Sometimes the city counter-proposes. Bhen has multiple stories about starting a project with one goal and having the growth patterns suggest something entirely different, and following those suggestions, and ending up with structures that serve purposes he hadn't anticipated but turn out to be needed.

"We planned to build a new workshop complex," he recounts, "positioned for optimal morning light. The initial plantings refused to establish. We tried three times, three different configurations. Nothing took. Finally brought in a listener, and she said the site wanted to be residential. Made no sense—we needed workshops, we already had enough housing in that area. But we were desperate, so we shifted the framework, planted for residential instead. Grew beautifully. Six months later, a fire in the eastern residential quarter displaced thirty families. The new complex was ready to receive them. We'd have had a housing crisis if we'd successfully built the workshops."

He doesn't claim the city is prescient. But he also doesn't not claim it. "The city is old," he says. "It's been growing for centuries, integrating people and their needs. Maybe it develops heuristics, pattern-recognition. Maybe it senses things about population flows, about coming needs, through mechanisms we don't understand. Or maybe we just got lucky. But it happens too often to be luck."

His apprentices are fewer than they should be. The knowledge base is shrinking. Bhen teaches what he can—the practical aspects of framework construction, species selection, structural principles. But the intuitive aspect, the negotiation-sense, that's harder to transfer. You either develop it or you don't, and fewer young people seem to be developing it.

"The city is quieter than it used to be," he says. "Or people are deafer. I'm not sure which. When I was young, everyone could feel the city's preferences at least a little. Now it's mostly just the listeners, and even they say it's harder to hear clearly. Maybe the city is settling, becoming more stable, less actively communicative. Or maybe we're losing the capacity to hear. Either way, it concerns me."

He worries about what happens when he and the other elder architects die. The knowledge will pass, partially, to the next generation. But something will be lost. Maybe enough to cause problems. Maybe enough to cause failures.

"The city has grown for centuries without catastrophic collapse," he acknowledges. "Maybe it doesn't need us as much as we think. Maybe it will continue just fine, growing and adapting, and humans will adjust to whatever it becomes. Or maybe we're essential, and without proper architecture the whole system will gradually degrade. I don't know. I just keep working and teaching and hoping the next generation is better than they seem."

### The Children

The city's children are different from valley children in ways both obvious and subtle.

They're born usually in the residential structures, delivered by midwives experienced in the city's particular needs. The births are attended by plant-presence—the structures themselves seem to quiet during labor, the ambient hum dropping, the condensation drips slowing, as if the city is paying attention. Afterward, newborns are brought to specific old-growth trees, pressed briefly against the bark—a welcoming ritual, an introduction, showing the child to the city and the city to the child.

They grow up playing in the root-streets and gardens, learning the city's geography through exploration. They climb walls that are alive and textured, learning young what can bear weight and what can't, developing instinctive understanding of plant structure. They hide in the hollows and chambers that riddle the architecture, finding spaces adults have forgotten, creating secret networks of child-sized passages.

They're taught early: what's safe to eat (many of the fruiting species, certain mushrooms, specific leaves and shoots), what's dangerous (some plants are toxic, some are structurally necessary and shouldn't be damaged, some are weird-contaminated and should be left alone). They learn to read the city's moods—when the weird zones are active and should be avoided, when the factories are running hot and need wide berth, when fog is coming and they should return home.

A child named Seff (she/her, maybe nine): small for her age, quick-moving, with the fearlessness of someone who's grown up in a place she understands instinctively. She can navigate the city in fog without getting lost, following the pattern of root-streets and the locations of landmark trees with perfect internal mapping. She knows which walls are growing which fruits this season, which plaza-gardens have the best spaces for running games, which factory-tenders will give you interesting scraps if you ask politely.

She's learning to prune, practicing under supervision on designated training-walls where mistakes won't cause structural problems. Her hands are already developing calluses. She's memorizing plant species—not through formal study but through exposure and use, learning the names and properties the way she learned language, by immersion.

She has the casual acceptance of the city's strangeness that comes from never knowing anything else. The weird zones are just "the deep places," areas you avoid unless you have good reason and adult permission. The manifestations are just things that happen sometimes—once she saw a person who wasn't there helping her mother carry water, and when she mentioned it her mother said "oh, thank you" to the empty air, and that was that. The fact that buildings are alive, that machines are semi-biological, that reality is slightly flexible—these aren't bizarre facts about her world, they're just how the world is.

But she's also developing the listener-affinity, whether she knows it yet or not. She reports dreams that turn out to be accurate information about city-states: structural weaknesses, optimal pruning times, where new growth should be directed. She sometimes stops mid-play, head tilted, attending to something, then continues as if nothing happened. Her parents are proud and worried in equal measure. Proud because listeners are valued, necessary. Worried because of what it costs.

The other children are less marked, or marked differently. Some are clearly going to be tenders, drawn to the factories, fascinated by how things work, spending their free time watching the machines and asking questions. Some are future architects, always building things, stacking and arranging, showing spatial reasoning and pattern-recognition. Some will probably leave—the city loses population to the valleys, young people who want something different, who find the plateau's strangeness oppressive rather than comforting, who dream of places where buildings don't breathe.

But most will stay, will grow into the city's work, will have children of their own who will play in the same root-streets, will maintain the structures and tend the machines and negotiate with the living architecture, continuing the cycle.

### The Traders

The people who move between the plateau and the valleys, maintaining connection between the city and the outside world, are a specific type: adaptable, comfortable with boundaries and crossing them, never quite belonging fully to either place.

A trader named Kess (he/him, thirty-five, lean and weather-worn): he maintains a small compound at the city's edge, a structure that's half plateau-style (living plant-walls, condensation collection) and half valley-style (stone foundation, traditional roof, door that actually swings on hinges rather than dilating). He splits time between the city and a valley settlement three days' travel down the plateau slopes, carrying goods both directions, maintaining relationships, serving as translator-diplomat between the two cultures.

He dresses in valley clothes—woven fabric that's not plant-derived, leather boots, a jacket that's actually dead-animal skin—because it makes valley-people more comfortable and because he's found the city's plant-fiber clothing, however practical for residents, doesn't hold up well to long-distance travel. But he keeps city-clothes at his compound, changes when he returns, an acknowledgment of proper respect for the place.

He speaks both languages—not that they're different languages exactly, but the dialects have diverged, the city's version incorporating terms for plants and processes and states of being that don't exist in valley vocabularies. More than that, he speaks the conceptual languages, can explain city-concepts to valley-people and valley-concepts to city-residents in ways both can understand.

To valley-people: "No, the buildings aren't dangerous. Yes, they're alive, but they're not aggressive. Think of them as very slow, very large, very patient trees that humans have shaped to be useful. You can live in them safely. Thousands do."

To city-residents: "No, valley-people aren't stupid for building with dead materials. They have different priorities: permanence, predictability, structures that don't require constant maintenance. It's not wrong, just different."

He carries city goods to the valleys: cloth that's stronger and finer than valley weavers produce, medicines derived from weird-contaminated plants (safe once removed from the city, effective for ailments valley medicines can't touch), occasionally small artistic pieces—carvings from hardened plant-matter, pressed flowers from species that grow nowhere else, samples of bioluminescent lichen preserved in resin.

He brings back: metal (the city has limited metallurgy, relies on import for tools and fittings), salt, certain foods that don't grow at elevation, news and stories and books when he can get them. Also, occasionally, new residents—valley-people who want to try city-life, refugees from valley conflicts or disasters, sometimes criminals hoping the plateau's strangeness will hide them (it usually doesn't—the city has a way of revealing people).

He's useful to both communities but fully trusted by neither. Valley-people think he's been contaminated, too comfortable with the city's wrongness. City-residents think he's too tied to valley-normalcy, not committed enough to the place. He accepts this, sees it as the inevitable position of intermediaries.

"Someone has to be between," he says. "Someone has to maintain the connection. Otherwise the city becomes completely isolated, loses access to things it needs, loses the flow of new people and ideas. But also the valleys need to remember the city exists, need to maintain trade relationships, need the city's unique products. I'm the membrane, keeping both sides in contact but not collapsing together."

He has seen the city change over his working lifetime. "Fewer people," he notes. "When I started this route fifteen years ago, the city was maybe twenty percent more populated. People have drifted away, moved to the valleys, not been replaced. The buildings are maintained, the factories keep running, but there's a sense of emptiness. More structures standing unused than there used to be."

He worries about the long-term viability. "If population drops below some critical threshold, can the system keep functioning? The city needs people to prune and direct growth, to tend the factories, to do the work. It's not fully autonomous. Probably. Though I'm not entirely sure. Sometimes I think if all the humans left, the city would just... continue. Growing, adapting, becoming something else. But I don't want to test that theory."

### The Historians

Knowledge keepers who maintain oral records and the limited written archives, trying to preserve understanding of the city's past and development.

A historian named Thael (she/her, fifty-eight, with the particular stiffness in bearing of someone fighting joint pain): she maintains a library-structure in the central district, a specialized building grown with extra waterproofing and air circulation to preserve the paper-bark records. The interior is lined with shelves that are living wood but trained into perfectly flat surfaces, holding scrolls and bound volumes that document centuries of city history—insofar as documentation exists.

She knows the fragmentation of the record frustrates everyone. "We have good documentation for the last two hundred years," she explains. "Spotty records for the two hundred before that. Before that, it's mostly oral tradition and archaeology—trying to understand what happened by reading the growth patterns in the oldest structures, by interpreting the megalithic stones, by studying what artifacts occasionally surface."

She's interviewed every elder in the city, recorded their memories, traced lineages and stories. She's mapped the city's growth, correlating architectural styles with time periods, documenting the species-mixes used in different eras. She's collected accounts of major events: fires that destroyed sections of the city, plagues that killed significant population percentages, periods of prosperity when expansion was rapid, periods of decline when structures were abandoned.

She's also collected the weird stories: the manifestations, the reality-instabilities, the listener-transformations, the times when the city did things that defied explanation. These are harder to categorize, harder to analyze. "How do you write history when cause and effect are unstable?" she asks. "When events might happen because of human decisions or because the city wanted them or because reality flexed in ways that made new things possible? I do my best. I record what people report. But I'm aware the narrative is partial, probably inaccurate in ways I can't detect."

She teaches students when she can find them—not many young people are interested in history when there's practical work to be done. But a few show aptitude: the ones who ask questions about why things are the way they are, who want context and background, who understand that knowing the past helps navigate the present.

She's particularly interested in the question of origin: where did the people come from who founded the city? Why here? What was the "old world" referenced in the earliest oral traditions? What exactly did they do to the plants to make them suitable for architecture and semi-industrial use?

"The listeners say the city was engineered," she reports. "That the founder-generation had knowledge—maybe technology, maybe just very advanced botanical understanding—that let them modify plant-growth at fundamental levels. That what we have now is the result of deliberate design, not just patient cultivation. But the details are lost. Either they weren't written down, or the records were lost, or possibly were deliberately destroyed."

She's found suggestions that the founding-knowledge was considered dangerous by some, that there were factions who wanted to preserve it and factions who wanted to erase it, and the erasers won. "There are references in some old texts to 'the burning of the green books,' which might be literal or metaphorical. References to 'those who would not grow,' which might mean people who opposed the city's creation or people who were killed or who left. It's fragmentary. Frustrating."

Despite the gaps, she maintains that the work is important. "We need to know where we came from, even imperfectly. Need to understand that this city is the result of choices, human actions, intentions that might have been noble or desperate or terrible or all three. It's not natural. It's not accidental. It's made. And if it was made once, it could be made again, or unmade, or changed. That knowledge matters."

### The Farmers

The people who manage food production in the border zones and within the city itself.

A farmer named Orl (she/her, forty-two, broad-shouldered and sun-darkened): she manages one of the orchards at the city's edge, where fruit-trees are trained into productive configurations. The trees here are less architectural than the residential or industrial structures, allowed more natural form, but they're still deliberately managed—pruned for optimal fruit production, grafted to combine desirable traits, positioned to maximize sun exposure and cross-pollination.

She knows each tree personally—its age, its health, its quirks. This one produces abundantly but the fruit bruises easily. That one is more resistant to the occasional frosts that hit the plateau but produces less. This third tree is old, probably should be replaced, but it produces fruit with particular flavor that's prized, so she keeps it going with careful support and medicinal treatments.

The work is seasonal and weather-dependent. Spring means pruning and grafting, shaping growth, thinning buds to concentrate the trees' energy. Summer means pest management (the limited pests that exist still need controlling), watering during dry spells, watching for disease. Fall means harvest, processing fruit into fresh stores and preserved forms. Winter means rest and planning.

She also manages understory crops—plants that grow beneath the tree canopy, using the space efficiently. Some are food plants. Some are medicinal. Some are nitrogen-fixers, improving soil health. The system is polyculture and interdependent, requiring constant observation and adjustment.

"It's not that different from managing the architectural plants," she notes. "You're working with living systems, trying to encourage them toward productive configurations while respecting their inherent patterns. You can't force. You have to negotiate, pay attention, adapt when they resist."

But farming is distinct from architecture in crucial ways: food-plants are more traditional, less strange, less contaminated. The orchards and gardens are places where reality is stable, where cause and effect are reliable, where the weirdness that permeates the city proper doesn't reach.

"People need these spaces," Orl says. "Need places where things make sense, where an apple is just an apple, where you plant a seed and get predictable results. The city proper is wonderful but exhausting. You can't live in strangeness all the time without relief. The farms provide that relief."

She trains young people in food production, trying to ensure knowledge transfer. But fewer young people are interested than in previous generations. "They're drawn to the factories, the architecture, the weird zones," she says with frustration. "They think farming is boring, old-fashioned. They don't understand that without food production, the whole city fails. We can't eat plant-fiber cloth or factory-pressed board."

She's also dealing with ecological questions that don't have clear answers: Why do some traditional crop plants fail at the city's elevation while others thrive? Why do the modified city-plants not cross-contaminate into the farms despite being close? Is there some invisible boundary, some property of the contaminated zones that contains them? Or is it just luck, and one day she'll find weird-growth in her orchards?

"I've found it twice," she admits quietly. "Strange plants that shouldn't be there, growing in patterns that were too regular, leaves with those wrong colors. Both times I burned them immediately, down to the roots, sterilized the soil. Reported it to the listeners, who came and did... something. Their rituals or treatments or whatever they do. So far it hasn't spread. But I watch. Always watch."

### The Council, Such As It Is

Governance in the city is informal but functional, centered on regular assemblies where decisions are made through discussion and consensus.

The council isn't elected, exactly. It's more that certain people naturally take leadership roles—the elder architects, the master factory-tenders, the historians, the successful farmers, respected listeners—and their opinions carry weight when decisions need to be made. Others can and do speak at assemblies, and their input is considered, but practically speaking, a few dozen voices shape most outcomes.

An assembly about water management: forty people gathered in an amphitheater-structure, sitting on root-benches, discussing whether to expand the cistern system in the northern quarter. The discussion is lengthy, technical, occasionally heated.

An architect presents the engineering case: the northern population is growing, current cistern capacity is adequate but has no redundancy, expansion is possible using existing techniques.

A listener counters: the city is not comfortable with expansion in that direction right now, the plant-mass underneath is stressed from recent drought, forcing growth could cause instability.

A farmer asks: will expansion affect water availability for the edge-orchards? The city's water budget is tight; taking more for cisterns means less for irrigation.

A factory-tender points out: the northern factory complex is planning to increase production, which will need more water for steam-generation and cooling; if cisterns aren't expanded, industrial growth will be limited.

Back and forth. Arguments from different perspectives, different priorities, different knowledge bases. Gradually a consensus emerges: expand the cisterns but more slowly than originally proposed, giving the plant-mass time to adapt. Reduce planned industrial expansion slightly. Implement water-conservation measures in the edge-orchards. Everyone compromises; nobody gets exactly what they wanted; the decision is workable.

This process repeats for every significant question: building projects, resource allocation, dispute resolution, response to problems. It's slow, often frustrating, but it works because there's underlying agreement about values and goals. Everyone wants the city to thrive, to continue, to remain livable. Personal conflicts and factional disagreements exist but don't override that shared commitment.

There are exceptions: sometimes consensus can't be reached, and decisions are delayed or implemented partially, leading to problems. Sometimes the listeners report city-preferences that conflict with practical human needs, forcing difficult choices. Sometimes external factors—a bad harvest, a major structural failure, a drop in population—force rapid decisions without time for proper deliberation.

But overall, the system functions. The city maintains itself. Problems are addressed, usually. Life continues.

### The Damaged

Not everyone integrates successfully with the city. Some people are broken by it.

You see them occasionally: individuals who went too deep into the weird zones and came back wrong. Not contaminated in the useful listener-way, but damaged. They can't maintain focus, can't hold conversations, can't work. They sit in public spaces staring at nothing, or they wander aimlessly, or they speak to entities nobody else perceives.

The city cares for them, after a fashion. They're fed, sheltered in designated structures, monitored to ensure they don't hurt themselves or others. But they're not recoverable. Whatever happened in the weird zones changed them permanently, shattered something that can't be repaired.

A damaged person named Siel (he/him, age unknown, might be thirty or fifty, impossible to tell): he sits near the central plaza most days, propped against a living wall, mumbling continuously. The mumbling occasionally resolves into phrases: "too many roots," "the color beneath colors," "it's trying to tell me but the words are wrong," "I can see the blueprint but it's drawn in meat."

People leave food beside him. He usually eats it, sometimes doesn't. When he doesn't eat for too long, someone brings him to a designated care-structure, ensures he's fed and watered, then he returns to his spot and resumes mumbling.

He's harmless. Sometimes he says things that turn out to be useful—fragmentary information about structural weaknesses, predictions about growth patterns. The listeners pay attention to his mumbling, filtering signal from noise. But he's not functional in any normal sense. He exists in the city but not as a participant, more as a feature, like the weird zones themselves.

There are maybe a dozen like him in the city at any given time. The number stays roughly constant—some die (or are absorbed, it's not always clear), new ones appear as people push too far into contamination. They're a visible reminder of the risks, the price that's paid for living in a place where reality is negotiable.

Most people avoid them not from cruelty but from discomfort. Looking at the damaged means confronting what could happen, what might already be happening gradually to anyone who lives here long enough. Better not to think about it. Better to focus on work, family, the daily tasks that keep you grounded in practical reality.

But the listeners visit them sometimes, sit with them, listen to the mumbling. "They know things," a listener explains. "They've seen too much, more than human minds are built to process, and it broke them. But the information is still there. Sometimes you can extract it, if you're careful, if you don't push too hard. They're like... deep sensors in the city's awareness, gathering data at levels the rest of us can't access."

It's a cold way to think about damaged human beings, but also maybe the only way to make their suffering meaningful, to suggest their sacrifice serves some purpose, however terrible.

### The Ones Who Left

The city also produces people who reject it, who leave for the valleys and don't return.

You hear about them in trade-stories: former city-residents now living valley-lives, talking about the plateau as a beautiful nightmare, a place they needed to escape. They describe feeling absorbed, losing boundaries, becoming too much plant and not enough human. They describe nightmares of roots growing through their bodies, of their thoughts being not entirely their own, of the city drinking them slowly.

Are these real experiences or psychological manifestations of the stress of living in a strange place? Hard to say. The city is strange. It does affect people. Whether that affect rises to the level of parasitism or is just the normal influence of environment on inhabitants depends on definitions and tolerances.

Some valley-communities have significant populations of plateau-exiles: people who tried city-life and couldn't adapt, who fled to stable reality with relief. They tend to cluster together, forming support networks, sharing their experiences. They warn valley-people away from the plateau: "Don't go there, don't let it touch you, it seems beautiful but it's a trap."

City-residents are aware of these exile-communities and mostly dismissive of them. "They couldn't handle it," is the common explanation. "Some people aren't suited to living in a place that's alive. It's not the city's fault. They made the right choice for themselves by leaving."

But occasionally, in private, current residents admit to similar feelings: moments of panic when the walls seem too close and too aware, when the constant dripping condensation feels like being slowly digested, when the thought occurs that maybe you're not living in the city but being consumed by it, very gradually, over decades.

These feelings pass. The work continues. The city remains home. But the discomfort never entirely disappears, just subsides to manageable levels.

### The Newcomers

And then there are the people who've recently arrived, still adjusting, still learning to navigate the city's peculiarities.

A newcomer named Jess (they/them, mid-twenties, nervous and fascinated): they arrived three months ago from a valley settlement, drawn by stories of the city's uniqueness, by a desire for something different from valley-normalcy. They're working in the textile factory as a basic assistant, learning to handle plant-fiber, to operate the simpler spinning-wheels, to maintain the moisture levels in processing-rooms.

They're still adjusting to the constant dampness, to the thin air, to living inside something alive. "It's amazing," they say, enthusiasm bright. "I wake up and the walls are dripping and it's beautiful. I watch the sun come through the membrane-windows and the light is green and strange and I love it. The machines hum all night and instead of being disturbing it's like... a lullaby? A promise that things are working, that the city is alive and functioning."

But they also report dreams of being overgrown, of vines finding their way into their mouth and lungs and growing through them. Report moments of vertigo when distances seem wrong, when they're not sure if they're walking toward something or it's moving toward them. Report hearing the city speaking to them in words they almost understand but can't quite grasp.

"Is that normal?" they ask. "The hearing things, the sense that the city is aware of me specifically?"

The person they're asking—an older factory-tender—shrugs. "Normal enough. You're new. The city is figuring you out, you're figuring out the city. Give it time. Either you'll adapt and the strangeness will become comfortable, or you won't and you'll leave. Most people adapt."

Jess nods, accepts this. They're determined to stay, to become a real city-resident, to belong to this strange place. Whether they'll succeed depends on factors they can't control: whether they have the neurological flexibility to tolerate reality-drift, whether their body adapts to the elevation and moisture, whether the city accepts them.

Because there is acceptance or rejection, even though nobody talks about it directly. Some newcomers integrate smoothly—they learn the skills, make friends, find their niche, become part of the city within a year or two. Others struggle despite their best efforts—they remain outsiders, can't quite sync with the rhythms, feel the city's resistance in subtle ways (tools that break in their hands, plant-walls that bruise easily when they prune, a sense of not being wanted).

The latter usually leave eventually. Not because they're expelled—nobody tells them to go—but because life becomes incrementally harder until departure seems like the obvious choice.

### The Elders

The oldest residents carry the city's memory in their bodies and minds, walking encyclopedias of technique and history and adaptation.

An elder named Geth (he/him, eighty-something, still working daily despite complaining joints): he's been here his entire life, born in the city seventy-plus years ago, remembers when the current industrial quarter was empty grassland, when the weird zones were more extensive, when population was higher.

He moves slowly but precisely, every motion economical. His hands are gnarled, skin textured like bark, permanently stained green from decades of sap-contact. His voice is rough from breathing humid air for a lifetime, but his mind is sharp, memory detailed.

He tells stories: about the fire forty years back that destroyed an entire neighborhood, about how the city responded by growing fire-resistant species in the rebuilt sections. About the plague that killed a fifth of the population, about the mass absorption-ceremonies afterward when they gave the bodies back to the city. About the experiment with a new factory-machine design that failed catastrophically, about how they salvaged what they could and learned from the mistakes.

He demonstrates techniques: the proper angle for pruning cuts to encourage branching versus vertical growth, the specific pressure needed when grafting to ensure good union without damaging tissue, the way to read subtle color-shifts in leaves that indicate nutrient deficiency or disease.

He also embodies the cost of long-term residence. His breathing is slightly labored—decades of humid air have left his lungs scarred. His thoughts occasionally drift, attention wandering to things nobody else perceives, suggesting early listener-contamination or just the normal drift of very old minds. His skin shows patches of rough texture that might be pre-cancerous growth or might be something else, something resulting from lifelong exposure to weird-zone contamination at low levels.

But he's content. "I've lived a good life," he says. "Done useful work. Helped build and maintain something worth building. The city has been good to me—given me purpose, community, beauty. That it's also taken things, worn me down, changed me—that's fair. That's balance. You can't live in a place like this and remain unchanged. The question is whether the change is worth it. For me, it has been."

He worries about the future, about knowledge loss, about population decline. But he's also pragmatic: "The city will continue. Probably. It's survived worse than a temporary population dip. And if it doesn't, if it eventually fails and collapses and returns to wild growth—well, that would be its choice as much as ours. Nothing lasts forever. Even this."

### The Dead Who Remain

Finally, there are the people who died but haven't entirely left, whose absorption into the city was so complete that traces of their presence persist.

Walking through the memorial gardens—those densely planted areas where the dead are given to the city—you sometimes feel watched. Not threateningly, but attentively, as if the trees themselves are observing you. Some residents swear they see faces in the bark patterns, recognize the features of people who died years ago. Some hear voices in the rustle of leaves, words that sound almost like advice or warning or greeting.

The listeners are certain: "The dead are in the city. Not as ghosts exactly, not as separate entities. But their information, their patterns, their knowledge—that was absorbed along with their physical matter. The city remembers them. In some sense they continue, distributed through the structure, part of the living mass."

Whether this is comforting or horrifying depends on your perspective. For some residents, it means death is not an ending but a transformation, that they'll continue as part of the city they helped maintain. For others, it's existentially disturbing, the suggestion that your consciousness might persist in some degraded form, unable to rest, trapped in a state between life and dissolution.

There are rituals around absorption: the body is washed, dressed in plant-fiber clothing that will decompose easily, placed in a designated absorption-area with appropriate marker-plants. People speak remembrances, tell stories, express gratitude. The city is formally invited to take the person, to integrate them, to remember them.

Then everyone leaves and nature—or whatever passes for nature here—takes its course. The body is gradually embraced by roots, covered by vines, consumed by fungi, broken down and incorporated into the structure. The process takes months. Families visit during this time, watching the transformation, marking the stages. When it's complete—when nothing recognizably human remains, when the marker-plant has established itself—there's a second ceremony, an acknowledgment of completion.

And then the person exists—if they exist—as part of the city's fabric. Their knowledge accessible to listeners. Their preferences, perhaps, influencing subtle aspects of local growth. Their presence a comfort or a weight, depending on how you feel about persistence beyond death.

---

This is what lives in the Plateau of Recursive Gardens: people becoming plant, plant becoming city, city becoming something that might be aware. Humans adapting to strangeness, maintaining systems they don't fully understand, negotiating with an environment that responds and shapes them as much as they shape it.

They work and love and die and continue in transformed states. They're proud and frightened and committed and uncertain. They're building and maintaining something unprecedented, something beautiful and terrible, something that asks questions about the boundaries of life and architecture and consciousness and self.

They endure because they've learned to live with strangeness, to find comfort in the alive city, to accept the cost of living in a place where reality is negotiable and the walls breathe and the past is never quite past because the dead remain, distributed through the roots, remembered in the structure.

They are the city. The city is them. The boundary between resident and residence has dissolved to the point where the distinction is mostly semantic, useful for conversation but not reflecting any deeper truth.

And they continue—pruning, building, listening, absorbing, becoming—one generation after another, for as long as the city permits or requires or chooses.
