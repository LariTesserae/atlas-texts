# Who Lives in the Trestle Gardens

The people here call themselves tenants, not residents. Not owners. Never settlers, though that's what an outsider might think. The distinction matters to them in ways that take time to understand.

There are about forty of them, give or take. The population fluctuates gently—someone leaves for a season, someone new arrives with a letter of introduction from someone who left years ago. The number stays roughly constant through a process no one has explicitly organized, though everyone participates in.

They are, almost without exception, people who were looking for something specific without quite knowing what. The phrase you hear, when anyone gets reflective enough to explain, is "the ratio was wrong." One woman—Léan, who runs the workshop and keeps the generator—says it like this: "Out there, too much of one thing, not enough of its pair. Here, we measure."

## The Structuralists

There's a loose grouping, maybe a dozen people, who seem to have been drawn here by the patterns. They're the ones who maintain the copper plates, who understood immediately which building should go where when the last addition was planned, who can spend an hour watching how mist moves through the ravine and nod with satisfaction at something the rest can't quite see.

Mikel is the oldest of these—perhaps sixty, perhaps older, with the kind of weathered face that comes from decades of outdoor work. He was an architect before, or maybe an engineer. He deflects questions about his previous life with good humor, but you can see the training in how he moves through space, how he touches the ironwork of the trestle as if reading Braille. He maintains a journal, leather-bound, where he's been tracking the trestle's transformation. Not just sketches—measurements, calculations, predictions about where the birches will root next, which sections of rust are structural and which are superficial. He's been here longest, or at least that's the general understanding. When someone asks about the copper plates, they usually end up talking to Mikel, though his answers tend to generate more questions.

Sarai and Jun work as a pair, always. They're younger—thirties maybe—and they maintain the hanging gardens. Every basket, every placement. They have notebooks full of observations: which plants thrive in which positions, how the spacing affects growth, what patterns emerge in pollinator behavior. They're conducting research, clearly, but when you ask what the hypothesis is, they exchange glances and say things like "We're looking at what wants to balance" or "The iron has preferences." They might be romantic partners; they might just be research collaborators who've achieved perfect workflow synchrony. No one asks directly. That's not quite the culture here.

The structuralists—the name they don't use but everyone else does—tend to be quiet people. Observant. They're the ones who notice when something's slightly off, when a measurement doesn't match expectation. They speak in measurements and ratios and relationships. "The east garden is heavy this season." "The sound-focus has shifted upstream three feet." They're not cold, not unfriendly, but they're often distracted, attending to things just slightly outside normal perception.

## The Growers

Then there are the ones who came for the gardens themselves, for the act of cultivation in this particular place. They're more immediately warm, more likely to invite you to tea, more concerned with the practical abundance of things.

Thomas runs the vegetable planters in the square. He's broad-shouldered, soft-spoken, with hands that are always slightly dirty. He came here after what he describes only as "a collapse"—not his health, maybe his life, maybe just his willingness to continue as he had been. He grows food with an intensity that borders on devotion, but there's nothing desperate in it. He's feeding people, yes, but he's also studying what grows well here, what this soil and light and water pattern want to produce. He keeps seeds in carefully labeled jars in his quarters in the converted goods-shed, trades them occasionally with the others, experiments constantly with new varieties. When he talks about the kale or the beans, he uses language usually reserved for old friends. "The tomatoes are considering ripening." "The chard has opinions about that cold snap."

Vera manages most of the medicinal and culinary herbs, both the ones in the hanging gardens and the spiral beds around the settlement. She's precise, methodical, originally trained as a pharmacist—she says this freely, and adds that she left when she realized she was just "moving chemicals from box to box to person without understanding what any of it grew from." Here she understands the whole chain: seed to soil to leaf to effect. She makes tinctures and salves that the community uses, trades some to the occasional visitor. She has a theory, which she'll share after sufficient acquaintance, that the positioning of the hanging gardens affects the properties of the plants. "Rosemary from the north basket versus south basket—different volatile oil profiles. I've measured." Whether this is the ravine's microclimate or something else, she won't say. But she's measuring.

The growers are twelve or fifteen people, overlapping with other groupings. They're the ones most likely to have their hands in soil at any given moment, to smell of mint or tomato leaf, to have a smudge of dirt on their cheek during the midday meal. They talk about the plants like partners in an enterprise, not tools or possessions. There's a woman named Cait who manages the espaliered apples and speaks to them in what she claims is an old dialect—"Not for magic, just manners." There's a younger man, Petran, barely twenty, who tends the ferns in the ravine bottom and moves through that green space like water, never disturbing, always present.

## The Maintainers

Every place needs people who keep things working. Here, they form a distinct group, though they'd resist the categorization. These are the ones who understand metal and wood and wire, who keep the generator running, who rebuilt the pump, who maintain the electrical system and the lamps and the stairway to the ravine.

Léan, already mentioned, is central here. She's tall, rangy, with close-cropped gray hair and permanent grease under her fingernails. She came from industrial work—fabrication, heavy machinery—and she treats the trestle itself as an ongoing project. She's the one who installed most of the hanging gardens, rigging the chains and cables, understanding load and stress and how iron behaves when it's half-consumed by rust. She's protective of the structure, maintains it carefully, but not conservatively. She's willing to drill new holes, add new attachments, modify—but only after consideration, measurement, consultation with Mikel and the others. "The trestle's not done yet," she says. "Still becoming. We help."

Orin is her counterpart for wood and smaller-scale work. He's the carpenter, basically, though he rejects the simplification. He built most of the planters, maintains the buildings, constructed the cold-frames. He's younger than Léan, darker-skinned, with hands that are all scars and calluses. Quiet to the point of seeming shy, but he'll talk for hours about joinery, about how wood moves with moisture, about the conversation between built things and weather. He has a theory that the buildings' positions create something—not shelter exactly, but a kind of clarity. "You think better here," he says. "The angles are right." Whether he believes this architecturally or metaphysically is unclear. Possibly both.

There are others in this group—people who handle the electrical system, who manage the water collection, who keep the road maintained. Maybe six or eight of them, total, though everyone helps with maintenance when needed. They're practical people, but practical in a place where practicality includes measuring intervals and checking ratios and considering what the structure itself seems to want.

## The Cooks and Gatherers

Food comes from the gardens, but also from the surrounding countryside—the forests and fields beyond the ravine. There are four or five people who specialize in this interface between the settlement and the wider world.

Dima is the primary cook, running the communal kitchen in the converted pumphouse. She's middle-aged, comfortable in her body, constantly moving between counter and stove and garden. She came here because, she says, "I was cooking for people who didn't taste." Here, people taste. Meals are communal, eaten at long tables in the pumphouse or, in good weather, outside in the square. She works with what's available—the garden produce, the foraged goods, occasional supplies brought in from outside. She makes bread in a wood-fired oven Orin built, and the smell of it is one of the settlement's constants. She also keeps bees—three hives on the ravine's south wall, where they get sun—and speaks about them with the same affectionate frustration some people reserve for children.

Kostas forages. He's out most days, ranging through the countryside with a canvas bag, returning with mushrooms, wild greens, berries, herbs, occasionally fish from the creek or a nearby pond. He's thin, weather-worn, moves like someone who's spent most of his life outdoors. He might be fifty; he might be seventy. He teaches, informally—taking people out, showing them what's edible, what's medicinal, where things grow. He has a concern with "rightness"—taking only what's abundant, only what's offered, though he won't explain what he means by "offered." He just knows. He also tracks the animals—not for hunting, mostly, but for knowing. He can tell you about the foxes in the woods to the east, the deer paths, the heron that's claimed a section of creek upstream. He reports these things at meals like news, and people listen.

## The Record-Keepers

There are three people who seem concerned primarily with documentation, with capturing what happens here in various forms.

Elise paints. Watercolors, mostly—she's set up a studio in a corner of the long workshop building, north light, neat and organized. She paints the ravine, the trestle, the settlement, the people. Hundreds of paintings, probably, over the years she's been here. Not quite realistic, not quite abstract—she emphasizes certain elements, certain colors, in ways that make you see the subject differently. She sells some work, occasionally, to visitors or through a gallery contact outside, and this provides some income that flows into the community's shared resources. She's careful about light, about time of day, about how mist changes things. She talks about trying to paint the ratios, the relationships, not just the objects. "If I get it right," she says, "you should be able to feel why the gardens hang where they do."

Marcus writes. He might be a journalist, or might have been. He's younger, early thirties, carries notebooks everywhere, writes constantly. He's documenting the community, but also the place itself—the changes over time, the pattern of growth, the slow transformation of the trestle. He interviews people, records stories, tracks the small events that constitute life here. A new plant species appearing in the ravine. A section of iron finally giving way to rust. The generator's repair. Someone leaving, someone arriving. He's not writing for publication, or not primarily. He says he's "keeping the memory," which the community seems to understand as a necessary function. Every few months, he reads selections at the communal meal, and people listen in a particular attentive way, recognizing themselves and their home in his words.

Nina is the librarian, though the library is small—maybe three hundred books in a room off the main workshop. She manages it, maintains it, but more importantly she seems to hold the community's theoretical knowledge. She's the one people ask when they want to understand something about patterns, about why the settlement is arranged as it is, about what the copper plates might mean. She doesn't always answer directly—often she'll pull a book, mark a passage, hand it over without comment. She's maybe forty, careful in her movements, with very bright eyes that miss nothing. She came here after an academic career that ended in some unspecified disappointment. "Too much theory, not enough dirt under it," she says. Here, theory and dirt are in constant conversation.

## The Children

There are three children, which surprises some visitors. This doesn't seem like a place for children, initially—too quiet, too focused, too concerned with measurement and pattern. But the children are genuinely present, genuinely integrated.

Two are siblings—Yael and Tomas, maybe eight and ten—whose mother, Renata, is one of the growers. She came here pregnant with Tomas, fleeing some situation she doesn't discuss. The children have grown up here, and they move through the space with a comfort and knowledge that surpasses most adults. They know every path in the ravine, every good climbing spot on the trestle (which Léan has made safe with her modifications, though she'd never admit this is why she added certain handholds). They help in the gardens, they attend informal lessons with Nina, they fish in the creek, they're learning woodwork with Orin. They're being raised communally, to some extent—everyone teaches them, everyone watches them, everyone expects competence and offers help in equal measure.

The third child is Ada, younger—maybe five—daughter of two of the structuralists, who arrived as a family three years ago. Ada is still coming into language, but she's already showing the pattern-recognition that drew her parents here. She arranges her toys in careful positions, becomes distressed if they're moved randomly, settles if someone rearranges them according to some principle she can sense. Sarai and Jun have taken particular interest in her development, though they're careful not to overwhelm.

The children make the place feel less like an experiment and more like a life. Their laughter carries across the ravine. They get dirty, they need feeding, they ask endless questions, they have conflicts and reconciliations. They're also absorbing something—the attention to relationship, the measuring, the careful thought—as their baseline normal. Who knows what they'll become, growing up here.

## The Transients

Not everyone stays. There are usually two or three people in residence who are explicitly temporary—staying for a season, maybe a year, learning or helping or just being present before they continue elsewhere.

Currently, there's an older man named Paol who's recovering from something—illness or grief or breakdown, it's not clear. He's been here four months, helps in the gardens, mostly stays quiet. He's getting steadier, less haunted. This seems to be what happens here—people arrive wounded and the place (or the people, or the work, or the ratios) brings them back toward wholeness. He'll leave when he's ready.

There's a young woman, Isha, who's an apprentice to Vera, learning about medicinal plants. She's here for a year, maybe, then returning to some city to open an herbal practice. She's a bridge—learning the community's knowledge, planning to carry it elsewhere, adapt it to a different context.

And there's a man, probably mid-forties, who gives his name as Stephan and who's clearly running from something. He arrived two weeks ago with a letter of introduction from someone who left years before. He works hard, keeps his head down, asks few questions. The community accepts this—there's a deep respect for privacy here, for not-asking. He'll tell what he needs to tell when he's ready, or he won't. Meanwhile, he's useful, he's quiet, he fits into the work.

The transients are integrated but not permanently. They eat communally, they work, they participate in the evening conversations, but everyone understands they're in a different relationship with the place. They're visiting, even if the visit lasts months. And this is fine—the place seems to offer different things to different people at different durations.

## The Dynamics

The social structure is loose, non-hierarchical in obvious ways, but not structureless. Decisions are made communally, usually through conversation that spirals around a question until consensus emerges. Mikel's opinion carries weight on structural matters; Léan's on mechanical; Thomas's on cultivation. But it's not formal authority—more like natural deference to expertise, balanced by others' willingness to push back if something feels wrong.

They work in the mornings, mostly—garden work, maintenance, whatever needs doing. Afternoons are varied—some people keep working, some rest, some pursue individual projects. Evenings center on the communal meal, which is social time, conversation time. After dark, people often gather in the pumphouse or outside in good weather, talking, sometimes playing music (there's a fiddle, a guitar, someone has a small accordion). Not every night, but often.

There's an ethic of contribution, but it's flexible. When Paol first arrived, too shaken to work much, no one pushed. As he's recovered, he's naturally taken on more. Everyone contributes what they can, in the forms they can, when they can. The record-keepers contribute differently than the growers, but both are valued.

Money exists at the periphery. The community has some income—Elise's paintings, occasional sales of produce or herbs or crafts, rare consulting fees when Mikel advises on some outside project by correspondence. There are some expenses—supplies they can't produce, fuel for the generator, maintenance materials. The economics are opaque to outsiders, managed collectively, seemingly functional. No one appears wealthy or poor; there's a baseline material simplicity, but not deprivation.

Conflicts happen but stay small. Someone gets annoyed, voices are raised, then there's conversation, adjustment, resolution. The community is too small and too interdependent for rifts to deepen. There's a culture of talking things through, of assuming good intent, of recognizing that everyone here chose to be here and continues choosing.

## The Quality of Presence

What's most striking, spending time with these people, is a quality of attention. They're present in a way that's rare. When you talk to someone, they're genuinely listening, not waiting to speak or thinking about elsewhere. When they work, they're focused on the work—not frantically, but fully. There's a kind of temporal thickness to interactions here, like time is moving at a slightly different rate, or like each moment has more room in it.

They notice things. The shift in light when clouds pass. The change in the creek's voice when water rises. The first beetle of spring. The smell before rain. They point these things out to each other casually, a running commentary on the world's small changes. It's not performative mindfulness; it's just how they move through the day.

They're also comfortable with silence. Conversations have gaps. People work side by side without talking. The soundscape—creek, birds, wind, work-sounds—is often enough. There's no anxiety about empty air.

And there's a quality of humor, dry and present, often about the incongruities of their situation. Léan joking about explaining her career path to her former colleagues. Thomas describing a particularly opinionated squash plant. Mikel's deadpan observations about visitors who arrive expecting something mystical and are disappointed by the generator's diesel smell and the very real need to weed the gardens.

They're neither utopian nor cynical. They've built something specific, something that works for them, in this particular place. They know it's not replicable, not a model for everyone, not a solution to anything except their own particular need for a different ratio between elements of existence.

## The Relationship to the Place

Most striking is how they talk about the settlement and the ravine—not as property or even as home, exactly, but as a collaboration. The place has agency in their understanding. Not magical, not personified, but real. The trestle "wants" certain things. The ravine "offers" certain conditions. The patterns they're maintaining or discovering are not imposed but recognized, worked with.

Mikel talks about the trestle as an ongoing conversation. "We propose, through our modifications. It responds, through rust and growth. We adjust. It's patient, so we need to be patient."

Sarai describes the hanging gardens similarly. "We're not controlling what grows there. We're offering positions and seeing what flourishes. The plants tell us if we've positioned them correctly."

Even the more practical-minded speak this way. Thomas: "The soil here has opinions. You learn to ask."

There's a sense that they're tenants—that word again—in a larger system. They maintain, they contribute, they're part of the pattern, but they don't own it or control it. The place existed before them (well, the ravine did; the settlement is newer). It will exist after them. They're stewards, collaborators, participants.

This affects how they make decisions. When considering a new building or garden or modification, the questions are: Does this fit? Does this serve the pattern? Will this create the right relationship with what's already here? Not just: Is this useful? Do we want this?

## The Inexplicable Commitment

Why do they stay? The question comes up with visitors, sometimes among themselves. The work is hard. The winters are cold. The generator breaks. Weeds grow. Rust spreads. The place offers no obvious rewards beyond itself—no wealth, no fame, no conventional success.

The answers vary but circle around similar themes.

Léan: "My hands make sense here."

Thomas: "Out there, I was growing food I never saw anyone eat. Here, I grow food for people I have breakfast with."

Elise: "The light is right. I don't mean photographically. I mean existentially."

Vera: "For the first time, I understand the whole chain of what I'm doing."

Nina: "Theory and practice are in proper relationship."

Mikel, characteristically, is more cryptic: "The measurements match. That's rare."

There's a sense that they've found something—not a solution or an answer, but a proper question, or a proper way of standing in relation to questions. The place allows a kind of life, a kind of attention, a kind of work that fits them in a way their previous lives didn't.

And there's the pattern itself—the underlying structure, the ratios, the relationships that everyone here is at least dimly aware of. For some, like the structuralists, this is primary: they're here to understand, to maintain, to participate in something they sense but can't fully articulate. For others, it's more atmospheric, but still present. Everyone feels that there's something here, some rightness to the arrangement, that goes beyond the merely practical or aesthetic.

They don't worship it. They're not mystical about it, mostly. But they acknowledge it, work with it, measure it, tend it. It's the invisible architecture beneath the visible settlement, and maintaining that architecture—whatever it is, whatever it does—has become their collective work.

## The Faces

If you visit, here's who you might meet:

Mikel, weathered and precise, touching the trestle's ironwork with fingertips that read metal like text, asking you questions about ratios that you can't quite answer but that lodge in your mind afterward.

Léan in her workshop, grease-stained and competent, explaining some modification to the trestle's structure with a enthusiasm that makes engineering beautiful.

Thomas in the garden, hands in soil, talking to plants or maybe just talking while plants listen, looking up with a smile that's entirely without hurry.

Sarai and Jun moving in their synchronized way through the hanging gardens, adjusting, observing, recording, a partnership so complete it's like watching one organism with two bodies.

Vera at her work table, surrounded by jars and plants and notebooks, explaining the properties of some herb with a precision that's almost devotional.

Dima in the kitchen, knife moving with practiced speed, turning vegetables into a meal, calling over her shoulder about who needs to set the tables.

Kostas returning from a foraging trip, bag full of mushrooms, stopping to tell you about the heron he saw, his delight in the observation as fresh as a child's.

Elise in her studio, brush moving across paper, the painting emerging in layers that somehow capture not just the trestle but the relationship between iron and air.

Marcus in a corner with his notebook, watching, writing, occasionally asking a question that makes you think about your own presence here.

Nina in the library, pulling a book, her eyes bright with something between knowledge and mischief, saying "Read this. Tell me what you think."

The children—Yael and Tomas racing through the ravine, competent and wild, or Ada arranging stones in careful patterns, or all of them at the meal table, integrated and belonging.

And the others, each distinct, each carrying some expertise or gift or wound or question that brought them here.

They'll welcome you, feed you, answer your questions or deflect them with good humor. They'll show you around, put you to work if you want work, give you space if you need space. They'll treat you as neither intruder nor convert—just someone who's here now, for whatever reason, for however long.

And if you stay more than a day or two, if you eat with them and work with them and listen to the creek and watch how light moves through the ravine, you might start to feel it—that sense of ratio, of relationship, of pattern. The thing that drew them and keeps them. The reason this ravine, this trestle, this strange small settlement feels like something more than its parts.

You probably won't stay. Most people don't. The life is too particular, too demanding of presence, too far from what the wider world offers and requires.

But you might leave differently than you arrived. With a sense that there are other ways to arrange a life, other ratios between work and rest, built and grown, self and community, human and more-than-human. That somewhere, in a ravine you could probably find again if you really needed to, forty-some people are measuring and growing and maintaining something they believe matters, even if they can't fully explain why.

And that the trestle is still rusting, still being grown through by birch roots, still holding the hanging gardens above rushing water. Still becoming whatever it's becoming, in conversation with the people who tend it and the processes that transform it.

Still home to swallows and ferns and careful humans, all finding their positions in a pattern that no one designed but everyone maintains.
