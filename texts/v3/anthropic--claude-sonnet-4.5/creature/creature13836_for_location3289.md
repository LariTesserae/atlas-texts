# The Seepfolk

They have lived here so long that wetness is their base state—not uncomfortably damp, but never quite dry either. Their clothing dries from body heat but picks up moisture from the air before the process completes. Their skin has adapted over generations; they don't chap or crack the way lowlanders do when they visit the plateau. Their hair holds moisture, stays slightly heavy. They smell faintly mineral, like the stone itself, a scent they don't notice on each other but which marks them when they travel elsewhere.

Their lungs are capacious. The constant bird-sound and industrial rhythm and water-noise means they've evolved—culturally if not yet biologically—toward deeper breathing, fuller voices, a way of speaking that projects without shouting. Children learn early to pitch their voices to carry across a street, to cut through the ambient chorus without strain. Conversations are direct, economical. The environment doesn't reward elaborate circumlocution. You say what you mean because saying more means working harder to be heard.

They are patient in a way that seems like slowness but is actually calibration. Everything here operates at middle tempo—the water rises slowly, the stone changes imperceptibly, the buildings settle over generations, the birds come and go in cycles that span years. Rushing achieves little. The seepfolk move deliberately, work steadily, maintain rather than disrupt. Their relationship with time is elongated. A project might span a lifetime; this is normal. Your grandfather laid the foundation, you build the walls, your granddaughter will see the roof completed. This is how things work.

They are maintenance people above all. The infrastructure requires constant attention—cisterns to be cleaned of sediment, pipes to be checked for mineral buildup, pumps to be serviced, channels to be cleared. Every neighborhood has its maintenance collective, volunteers or professionals who know their district's water system intimately, can diagnose problems by sound alone, spend their days in the practical work of keeping water moving where it should. This is not low-status work; these are respected positions. The pump-engineer who keeps your block's cistern functioning is someone you know by name, whose children your children play with, whose knowledge will be passed down because it must be.

Their spatial cognition is local and layered. They know their neighborhood with extraordinary precision—every seep-point, every pooling location, which streets flood in heavy cycles and which stay passable, where the stone is solid and where it's honeycombed with cavities. They know the vertical dimension intimately because the city is built up and down as much as across, buildings rising four or five stories, basements extending into solution caves, cisterns at multiple levels. Their mental maps are three-dimensional, temporal (this route is good now but will be wet in three days), acoustic (you can hear the Miller Street pump from here, so you're in this general area).

But they have almost no sense of the plateau as a whole. It's too large. Maps exist but feel abstract, theoretical. Most people know their district, maybe three or four adjacent ones, have perhaps visited a different sector for trade or to see relatives, but conceive of the rest as something like foreign countries—real but not personally knowable. The plateau has neighborhoods separated by days of travel, different specialties, different microclimates around different seepage patterns, dialects that drift far enough apart that residents of distant districts can identify each other by speech.

They are factory workers, many of them. The mills run on shifts—six hours on, twelve off, rotated so that someone is always working but no one is ground down by continuous labor. The work is physical but not brutal: tending machines that process textiles, loading and unloading, moving materials through the workflows that turn imported fiber and local water into fabric that exports to the lowlands. The factory culture is collective but not communal—people work alongside each other, develop efficiencies, take pride in their mill's output, but maintain personal boundaries. The noise prevents casual chatting; you work, you do your job, you go home. Friendships form but around shared competence rather than conversation.

The merchant class is smaller, more cosmopolitan. These are people who manage the trade routes, who travel to the plateau's edges and beyond, who negotiate with the lowland cities for food imports and coal and the other necessities the plateau doesn't produce. They speak multiple languages, understand currency exchange, know how to present plateau goods to outsiders. They live in the better buildings near the market squares, send their children to the newer schools, employ servants from the working districts. Not aristocracy—the plateau doesn't have that kind of stratification—but comfortable, influential, connected to the world beyond in ways most residents are not.

The artisans are the third major class: ceramicists working with local clay, stonemasons who both quarry and carve, metalworkers making the pipes and fittings and pump components that infrastructure requires, textile specialists doing the fine finishing work that machines can't. They apprentice young, learn over years, develop specialties. A master pump-smith who can cast brass fittings that will last decades is known across multiple districts. A ceramicist whose glazes catch light in particular ways can command premium prices. Their workshops are ground-floor spaces with living quarters above, passed down through families or sold to apprentices who have earned the master's trust.

There are also the water-listeners. This is not quite a profession, more a skill that some people develop and others pay for. They can diagnose the health of the water system by sound—not just obvious problems like a blocked pipe, but subtle changes in flow rate, the acoustic signature of mineral buildup before it becomes critical, the tone of a cistern that indicates sediment levels. Some of them are also weather-predictors, claiming they can hear changes in seepage rate that precede atmospheric shifts. Whether this works or is elaborate folk belief, people pay for their services, and the maintenance collectives employ them as consultants.

The children grow up semi-feral in the best way. The streets are safe enough—crime exists but is mostly petty theft, handled by neighborhood justice rather than formal police. Kids range freely through their district, learn the architecture by climbing it, know which buildings allow rooftop access and which courtyards have the best pools for swimming in summer. They're comfortable with heights, with the birds that are everywhere (though they learn early not to disturb nesting sites—angry parent birds are no joke). They play games involving navigation, water-prediction, timed runs between landmarks. By adolescence they know their territory better than most adults, have discovered the hidden spaces, the forgotten basements, the rooftop routes that bypass streets entirely.

Education is practical and formal both. Children attend schools run by neighborhood collectives or religious organizations (the plateau has several faiths, none dominant, all focused more on ethical behavior than metaphysics). They learn literacy, mathematics, enough history to understand the city's development, enough engineering to grasp water systems, enough biology to identify bird species—this last is practical knowledge since some birds indicate good water, others contamination. By twelve or thirteen most are apprenticing in some trade, still attending classes part-time but primarily learning by doing.

The older generation, those who've aged out of factory or workshop labor, become the memory-keepers. They sit in the market squares, the public fountains, the neighborhood gathering spots. They remember the last time this particular cistern needed major repair, or when that building's foundation had to be reinforced, or which winter the northern districts had that disease outbreak. This oral history is how the city maintains continuity—written records exist but are scattered, often lost, hard to search. The elders are the accessible database, consulted for precedent, for context, for the long view.

They are not particularly religious but have developed what you might call material spirituality. The stone is ancient, the water mysterious in its persistence, the birds arrive from and depart to unknown places. There's a sense of being embedded in systems larger than human scale. Some residents make small offerings at notable seep-points—coins, carved stones, paper prayers that dissolve in the dampness. Certain formations are considered auspicious: a particular arch where water falls just so, a pool where two seeps merge and create unusual mineral deposits. These aren't gods but something like nodes of meaning in the urban fabric, places where the city's strangeness concentrates enough to feel significant.

The few formal religious organizations tend toward contemplative practices suited to the environment. The sound level makes liturgy challenging, so prayer is often silent, meditative. Monasteries—there are a few—emphasize work as practice: maintaining a particularly beautiful cistern-garden, operating a hospice, running a school. They're not withdrawn from the world but woven into it, their rhythms matching the city's middle tempo.

There's a growing academic presence. The universities are relatively new, established in the last few generations as the plateau's stability and strangeness attracted scholars. They study bird migration patterns (the plateau is uniquely positioned to observe species that rarely overlap elsewhere), hydrogeology (the seepage mechanisms are still not fully understood), acoustics (the sound environment is unlike anywhere else), political organization (how does a city this large function without central authority?), and increasingly, the people themselves—anthropologists interviewing elder generations, linguists documenting dialectical drift, sociologists mapping the informal networks that actually run the districts.

The students are mostly plateau-born, children of merchants or successful artisans, studying for three or four years before returning to family trades with enhanced knowledge. But there are also outsiders, drawn by the plateau's reputation for a particular kind of contemplative scholarship—the environment seems to slow thought in useful ways, makes certain kinds of complex problem-solving easier, or so they claim. These foreigners struggle initially with the dampness, the noise, the scale, but those who stay begin to move at plateau tempo, develop the patient attention that the place seems to cultivate.

The scholars have also noticed something about long-term residents: their circadian rhythms are slightly different. The plateau's atmospheric conditions mean that day-night transitions are less sharp than elsewhere, the haze softening dawn and dusk into gradual shifts. People tend to sleep in shorter stretches, wake naturally, nap during the day if needed. The tyranny of the clock is less absolute here—factory shifts are timed, yes, but outside of that, people operate more by natural rhythm than schedule. You eat when hungry, sleep when tired, work when you have energy. This would be chaos in a tightly coordinated economy, but the plateau's infrastructure is robust enough to handle variation, and most work is either continuous process (machines running constantly with rotating shifts) or artisanal (completed when it's completed).

Food culture is entirely imported ingredients prepared in local styles. There's no agriculture, so no farm-to-table freshness, but the trade networks are efficient enough that preserved and staple foods arrive regularly. The cuisine emphasizes soups and stews—makes sense in a climate where everyone is slightly chilled from dampness, where hot food is comforting. Grains and legumes are staples, supplemented with preserved fish from the lowlands, occasional meat, dried fruits. Baking is significant: dense breads that keep well in humidity, pastries as special occasion foods. Beer is common—the water is safe but tastes mineral, and beer masks that while providing calories. The breweries are neighborhood institutions, their products varying by district based on water source (different seeps have different mineral profiles, produce different beer characters).

Eating is communal in working districts—boarding houses with common tables, street vendors selling hot food from carts, neighborhood cookhouses where you can bring ingredients and use the facilities. The merchant class has private dining in their larger apartments, hosts dinners as social and business events, has developed a culture of hospitality where invitation to a home meal signifies relationship depth. But even they eat street food regularly—it's convenient, often excellent, and mixing with neighbors is expected regardless of class.

The relationship with birds is complex. They're everywhere, unavoidable, and most residents exist in a state of parallel tolerance. You don't feed them (that way lies being overwhelmed), but you don't actively discourage them either (they were here first, or at least they arrived simultaneously with human settlement, and they're not leaving). You accept that horizontal surfaces need periodic cleaning, that window ledges are contested territory, that certain times of day the sky is more bird than air.

But there are specialists: bird-harvesters who collect eggs from accessible nests (eaten, but also used in various crafts and processes), guano collectors who have arrangements with building owners to harvest droppings for sale as fertilizer, pest controllers who manage aggressive species or remove nests from critical infrastructure, and increasingly, bird-guides who lead tourists or scholars to viewing spots, who can identify species by call alone, who understand the migration patterns and can predict which species will be present when.

Some residents develop genuine affection. They learn to identify individual birds—the pigeon that always roosts on that particular cornice, the swift that returns to the same nest cavity each year. They notice when a familiar bird disappears, mourn in their way. Children especially bond with the birds, give them names, claim to understand their behavior. Whether this is anthropomorphism or genuine interspecies familiarity is debatable, but it's real to those who experience it.

The city has its share of eccentrics—anyone that large and old will have people who've found unusual niches. There's a woman in the Westmark district who claims she can predict seepage by tasting stone, and people hire her to evaluate building sites. There's a man who's walked every street in a twenty-district radius, drawn maps from memory that turn out to be uncannily accurate. There are the underground explorers who descend into the deeper solution caves, sometimes disappearing for days, returning with stories of vast chambers and underground lakes and formations that seem impossible.

There are also the standard urban problems: alcoholics who can't hold employment, congregate in certain declining districts; drug users (there are several drugs that enter via trade routes, used for everything from recreation to pain management to staying awake through double shifts); the mentally ill who in a kinder city would receive care but here mostly survive on their own, tolerated and occasionally helped by neighbors but not institutionalized. Crime is mostly opportunistic—theft of goods, the occasional robbery, disputes that escalate to violence. The city has informal justice: neighborhood councils that arbitrate disputes, assess restitution, in extreme cases exile troublemakers to other districts.

The serious crimes—murder, rape, arson—are rare but handled by a loosely organized plateau-wide enforcement network, volunteers who investigate, track perpetrators across district lines, bring them before councils for judgment. Punishment is practical: restitution to victims, labor for the community, for the truly dangerous, exile from the plateau entirely. There are no prisons—maintaining them would require resources better used elsewhere—so justice is quick and often harsh by outsider standards, but considered fair by locals who participate in the process.

Women and men work in rough equality—the factory labor is mixed, as are most trades. Some specialties trend gendered (more male stonemasons, more female textile finishers) but these are soft patterns, not rules. Family structure is typically nuclear but flexible: two-parent households are common, but single parents are unstigmatized, extended family living together is normal when economics favor it, childless couples are neither rare nor remarkable. Children are everyone's responsibility in a district—if a kid is in trouble, any adult will intervene; if a parent is struggling, neighbors help.

Marriage exists but isn't universally formalized. Some people have religious ceremonies, others just a neighborhood celebration, others simply declare themselves partnered and are recognized as such. Divorce or separation is straightforward—you divide assets, make arrangements for children, move to different quarters if you were sharing. The dampness and closeness of living conditions mean that staying in a dysfunctional relationship is worse than separating, so people don't.

The elderly are cared for by family when possible, neighborhood collectives when not. There's no formal retirement—people work as they're able, transition to lighter tasks as they age, eventually reach a point where they're simply present, telling stories, watching the street life, being fed by the community. Death is commemorated but not elaborately—a simple ceremony, cremation (burial in stone is impractical), ashes scattered in a significant seep or added to a commemorative garden that some districts maintain.

The outsiders who visit—traders, scholars, occasional tourists—find the plateau overwhelming initially. The dampness feels oppressive, the noise exhausting, the scale disorienting, the birds alarming in their density. Those who stay longer begin to acclimate, find rhythms, discover neighborhoods they prefer, learn to navigate. Some never adjust, leave after completing their business. A few fall in love with the place, find the plateau's strangeness exactly what they needed, immigrate permanently, slowly become seepfolk themselves over years of adaptation.

The plateau-born who leave find everywhere else too dry, too quiet, too small, too rushed. The lowland cities move at frantic pace compared to the plateau's steady tempo. The sky feels empty without birds. Buildings seem flimsy, temporary. Water seems precious rather than abundant. Some adapt, make lives elsewhere, visit home occasionally. Others return, unable to settle anywhere else, having been shaped by stone and water and sound in ways that can't be reshaped.

The young people sometimes feel restless—the plateau is vast but also circumscribed, its rhythms can feel stifling to those who want drama, change, excitement. Some leave for adventure, military service in lowland armies, merchant marine on seas they've never seen, scholarship in distant universities. Most eventually return, having discovered that the plateau's particular character is rare and, in retrospect, valuable. They slot back into neighborhood life, take up trades, raise children who will also grow up questioning whether this is all there is, most of whom will also return.

The very old residents, those who've lived through eight or nine decades, speak about changes—certain seeps that were once reliable have diminished; others have emerged. Districts that were prosperous have declined; others have grown. Bird populations shift—some species are less common than they remember, others more numerous. The coal deposits are depleting in accessible areas, requiring deeper mining or imports. The climate might be changing, though with a sample size of one lifetime on a plateau that's been here for geological ages, who can say?

But the fundamental character persists: water rising, stone weathering, birds arriving and departing, people maintaining infrastructure and raising children and making things and growing old and dying, the city spreading across its tableland, dissolving slowly into haze in every direction, vast and particular and strange and, to its residents, simply home—wet and loud and patient and alive.
