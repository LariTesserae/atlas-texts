# What Lives There

The axolotls are citizens of the deep yards, but not equally. There are the drifters and there are the territory-holders, and the difference is legible in how they move.

The drifters are mostly young, sleek, still growing into their colors. They hover in the open water between pillars, moving in loose aggregations that aren't quite schools—more like neighborhoods of convenience. Six or seven will occupy the same cylinder of water for hours, not touching, barely acknowledging each other, but somehow coordinated. They face the same direction. They rise and sink together. When one moves to investigate something—a falling rust flake, a swimming invertebrate—the others don't follow, but they notice. Their external gills pulse in patterns that might be language, that might be just respiration, that might be both.

The territory-holders are larger, older, their colors fully saturated into improbable hues. There's one the color of thunderclouds, nearly two feet long, that occupies the space around the fourth pillar on the east rank. Another, pure white with eyes like pink opals, holds dominion over a section of elevated track where the water is shallowest, barely covering the rails. A third, so dark purple it's nearly black, iridescent at certain angles, has claimed the space directly beneath one of the crane hooks—the one with the fabric pendant—and is rarely seen anywhere else.

They defend these territories not with violence but with presence. When a drifter enters, the territory-holder simply positions itself between the intruder and the best hunting spots, the most sheltered resting places. The display is subtle: gills extended to maximum spread, body oriented to show size, a slight advance. It's almost always enough. The drifters withdraw without confrontation. But occasionally, a drifter of unusual size or confidence will hold position, and then the clicking begins—rapid, staccato, aggressive. The sounds echo through the space in cascading waves. Other axolotls stop moving. The crickets go silent. Even the bats pause in their roosts. The two amphibians hang in the water, clicking, for thirty seconds, a minute, two minutes. Then one, usually the challenger, turns and drifts away, and the normal acoustic pattern resumes.

The territories themselves are works of long curation. The thundercloud-gray one has cleared its pillar base of all debris except for three perfectly positioned rocks—limestone chunks that must have been moved there deliberately, too heavy to have arrived by chance—that create channels where water flows slightly faster, where prey must pass. The white one's elevated track segment has had moss encouraged on the north side, creating a backdrop that renders the axolotl's pale body nearly invisible against it. The purple-black one beneath the crane hook has let a specific colony of the fern-things grow on one side of its pillar, while keeping the other side clear—the plants create an asymmetry that somehow makes the space more defensible, more defined.

They hunt at different hours, or perhaps hunt constantly but feed at different hours. You see it in cycles: a period when many are active, mouths opening and closing, bodies turning with sudden precision, then a period when most are still, digesting, only their gills moving. The clicks and chirps increase during feeding times—communication, perhaps, warnings to avoid the same prey, or calls to coordinate without meaning to, or simply excitement vocalized.

Some bear scars, or rather, marks—regeneration in progress. An axolotl with a gill frond that's shorter on one side, the new growth visible as a paler pink against the established deep red. Another with a hind leg that's slightly smaller, fresher, newer. They regenerate slowly here, in the cool water, or perhaps that's the normal speed and it only seems slow to human perception. The scars aren't from violence among themselves—they're too cooperative, too patient for that. Perhaps from the crabs, in disputes over food. Perhaps from something falling from above. Perhaps from the fish, in rare ambitious moments.

There are gravid females, their bodies thickened, moving with particular deliberation. They seek out the most hidden spaces—behind equipment housings, in the gaps between misaligned rails, beneath overhangs of concrete. They disappear for days at a time. Then they emerge, noticeably slimmer, and in those hidden spaces, if you looked, you would find eggs: hundreds of them, in gelatinous masses, adhering to whatever surface was chosen. Most won't survive. The ecosystem is balanced, which means it's also ruthless. But some will, enough will, because the population maintains itself, has maintained itself for however long this place has been like this.

The juveniles are barely visible, thumbnail-sized, translucent except for their internal organs showing dark through the skin. They occupy the shallowest margins, the places where water meets concrete in a film only inches deep. They feed on things invisible to human eyes—microorganisms, algae too sparse to notice, particles of detritus. They're vulnerable to everything: crabs, fish, adult axolotls in hungry moments, beetles that fall from above. Most die. The ones that survive grow slowly, imperceptibly, until one day they're large enough to drift out into deeper water, to join the drifters, to begin the long process of finding their color.

The fish are foreigners who've become natives.

They were never meant to be here—too far from any natural cave system, too isolated—but someone introduced them or their ancestors, intentionally or by accident, decades ago. Aquarium releases, perhaps. Bait bucket dumping. An experiment by someone who worked here when it was still transitional, still partially in use, before the water found its final level.

They're cavefish, or becoming cavefish, or several species collapsing toward the same solution. Elongated bodies, whip-thin. Eyes that range from large and functional to clouded to absent, depending on lineage. Some are pure white, pigmentless. Others retain ghost patterns of what they used to be—faint stripes, shadowy spots, the memory of color.

They move in groups of four to six, navigating the rails with such precision they must be using lateral lines, sensing pressure waves, building mental maps of the space through sound and touch rather than vision. They know where every rail runs, where every pillar stands, where the floor rises and falls. They swim in formation, maintaining exact distances from each other, never colliding despite the darkness and obstacles.

Their mouths are slightly upturned, adapted for surface feeding, but the surface here offers little. Instead they've learned to scrape—they attach to concrete, to metal, to the wooden elements, rasping away biofilm, algae, bacterial mats too thin to see but substantial enough to sustain. You hear them sometimes if you're very still: a faint scritch-scratch-scritch as dozens of tiny mouths work across surfaces.

They spawn in the drainage channels, where water movement is strongest. You find them there sometimes, packed together in struggling masses, dozens of individuals in a space the size of a footlocker, their bodies flashing pale in the dimness. The eggs they leave adhere to the concrete channels and hatch into larvae even more attenuated than the adults, transparent threads that drift for weeks before settling.

The largest one—there's always a largest one—is over a foot long but no thicker than two fingers. It moves alone, slowly, following a route around the entire perimeter of the space. It takes days to complete this circuit. You see it in one corner, recognize it by size and by a specific damage to its dorsal fin, then don't see it again for three days, four days, until it appears at the opposite end, still moving, still following its patrol. Why it does this, whether it's feeding or guarding or simply expressing some deep behavioral program, is unknown. But it's been doing it long enough that the other fish yield to it, that the axolotls track its movement with their eyes.

There are smaller fish too, barely longer than your thumb, that school in open water rather than following the rails. They're the most active, constantly moving, feeding on whatever drifts in the water column—particles, microorganisms, axolotl waste. They're food for everything else. The bats take them when they venture too near the surface. The axolotls take them constantly. The crabs scavenge their bodies when they die and sink. They survive through sheer fecundity, spawning year-round in tiny batches, producing just enough young to offset the predation.

One species—or what's becoming its own species—has adapted to feed on the axolotl eggs. They're small, quick, with mouths full of tiny teeth. They wait near the spawning sites, and when a female leaves her egg mass unguarded, they dart in, biting through the jelly, consuming dozens of embryos before the female returns and drives them off with lunging attacks that always miss. It's a tax, an expected loss. The axolotls lay enough eggs to absorb it. The fish have shaped their entire lifecycle around this one resource, timing their own reproduction to follow the axolotls' cycles by a few weeks, so their young grow up when egg-feeding is possible.

The crickets are the most ordinary and therefore the strangest.

They're just crickets—*Acheta domesticus* or close relatives, the same species that lives in basements and feed stores and pet shops across the surface world. Brown-black bodies, long antennae, powerful hind legs. But there are thousands of them here, tens of thousands maybe, in a space that shouldn't support them.

They live on the dry surfaces, which are plentiful: the platforms, the catwalk gratings, the tops of equipment housings, the exposed rails, anywhere water doesn't reach. They shelter in every crack and crevice—behind panels, inside junction boxes, in the gap between an I-beam and the ceiling. During the dimmer hours they emerge and feed.

What they feed on is the mystery, or would be if you thought about it too carefully. There's fungus, yes—they graze on the bracket fungi, on patches of mold growing on organic materials. There's detritus falling from above—particles of leaves and seeds and surface debris that drift down through the ventilation shafts. There's algae growing in the thin damp film where water and concrete meet. There's each other, occasionally—cannibalism is part of the balance.

But mostly there's guano. The bats produce it in quantity, small pellets that accumulate beneath roosts, and the crickets converge on these deposits, processing them, breaking them down, returning nutrients to the system. They're decomposers, janitors, the foundation of the food web. Without them, the whole ecology would collapse under its own waste.

They chirp individually, rarely in chorus. Each male holds a tiny territory—a few square feet of platform, a section of rail, the top of a gear housing—and calls from there. The calls are spaced out, stochastic, creating that scattered soundscape of individual voices. Occasionally two neighbors will call in response to each other, a brief duet, then fall silent. Occasionally a male will call continuously for minutes at a time, urgent, seeking. Sometimes a female approaches, sometimes not.

Mating happens in darkness, in privacy, in crevices. You don't see it, but you see the results: females with ovipositors extended, probing into cracks in concrete, into soft wood, into accumulated organic debris, depositing eggs. The eggs hatch into nymphs that look like miniature adults, wingless, and the nymphs grow through molts, shedding exoskeletons that accumulate in corners like scattered armor.

The crickets have predators everywhere. The bats are most obvious—swooping attacks that end in a brief crunch, wings folding around a body. But the crabs take them too, ambushing from behind equipment. The fish take them when they fall or jump into water. Even the axolotls will eat them if they land on the surface and can't escape before a mouth rises from below.

And yet the population thrives, because each female lays hundreds of eggs, because the generation time is weeks rather than months, because they're efficient and adaptable and nearly indestructible as a species. You could remove half the crickets and in six months the population would recover. You could flood their breeding sites and they'd find new ones. They're survivors, urban adapters, and this drowned switchyard is just another city to them, another set of surfaces to occupy.

Some are malformed—legs at wrong angles, wings that don't close properly, antennae of mismatched lengths. Inbreeding, perhaps, in an isolated population. Or radiation from some old industrial source. Or just the normal rate of developmental errors, more visible because you're looking closely. They survive anyway, mostly. The really damaged ones get eaten quickly, but the slightly off ones manage, reproducing, passing on their variations, contributing to whatever evolutionary trajectory this population is following.

There are albino crickets, pure white, eyeless or nearly so. Not many—maybe one in five hundred—but they're there, ghostly presences on dark metal. They survive poorly in the open, too visible, but in the deepest crevices where darkness is complete, their disadvantage vanishes. Perhaps in a thousand generations this population will split—normal crickets on the surfaces, cave crickets in the depths, two species where there was one.

The bats emerge at dusk, probably.

Time is unreliable here, but they have their rhythms, and their rhythms likely link to the surface world through the ventilation shafts—temperature shifts, air pressure changes, cues too subtle for humans but clear to senses shaped by millions of years of twilight hunting.

They're little brown bats, *Myotis lucifugus*, or something closely related. Small—body the size of your thumb, wings that span a hand when extended. They roost in the highest corners, hanging upside down from beams and ceiling irregularities, clustered in groups of twenty to forty. Not huge colonies, but enough for social warmth, for information exchange, for the comfort that comes from bodies nearby.

When they wake, they groom first. You hear it if you're listening: the rustle of wings being stretched, the faint sounds of tiny claws combing through fur, the social murmuring—ultrasonic calls pitched too high for human ears but almost-audible as a sense of presence, of communication. Then one drops, and another, and another, until the air is full of wings.

They're hunting the crickets primarily, but also midges and other small flying insects that live here—tiny moths that eat the fungus, gnats that breed in the organic debris, things so small you'd never notice them until you see a bat's trajectory curve, strike, continue in one fluid motion.

The bats know the space perfectly. They've echolocated every surface, every pillar, every hanging chain, every I-beam. They fly at full speed through the darkness, through the forest of vertical obstacles, never hesitating, calling constantly, building real-time sonar maps in brains the size of peas. Sometimes they fly low over the water, drinking on the wing—belly almost touching the surface, tongue extended, a quick lap of cool mineral water, then up again.

They take crickets off vertical surfaces, plucking them in flight with feet or mouth. They take them from the air when crickets jump. They even, occasionally, take them from the water's surface—a cricket that fell or jumped poorly, struggling, and a bat swoops down in a maneuver that seems impossible, snatching it without getting wet, or getting wet but not caring, and then up to a feeding perch.

Feeding perches are specific locations—a particular beam, a jutting piece of equipment—where bats land to consume larger prey. These perches accumulate debris: wing cases, legs, heads. The guano concentrates here too, forming deposits that the crickets then harvest, closing the loop.

They mate in the high corners, in late summer or whenever their bodies tell them late summer has arrived. The females become pregnant, but implantation delays, embryos waiting through the winter, through whatever passes for winter here—perhaps just a slightly cooler period when the surface world grows cold and less heat reaches down through the shafts. Then the embryos develop, quickly, and pups are born in late spring, naked and helpless and clinging to their mothers.

The mothers hunt while carrying pups, at least when they're small. You see them flying weighted, asymmetrical, and yet still capable of those impossible maneuvers. When the pups grow too large, the mothers leave them clinging to roosts while they hunt, returning to nurse. The pups vocalize constantly when alone, high distress calls, and the mothers answer, threading back through the darkness to exactly the right spot among dozens of pups.

Not all pups survive. Some fall, dropping into the water where they drown or are eaten. Some sicken, fail to thrive, are eventually rejected or abandoned. The mortality rate is high but sustainable. Enough survive that the population maintains itself, generation after generation, a colony that's been here as long as the water has.

One bat is ancient, or seems ancient. Larger than the others, grizzled, with a face that looks wise in that way old animals sometimes do—not wise, of course, just old, experienced, lucky. It roosts alone, separate from the main colony, in a corner near one of the ventilation shafts. It hunts less than the others, but when it hunts, its strikes are certain, efficient. It's been here long enough to remember when things were different, perhaps, when the water was still rising, when the space was still adapting. Or perhaps it arrived last season and just looks old. Bat years are compressed, fast-lived. But it holds itself differently, moves with a kind of authority or weariness, and the others give it space.

The crabs are hermit crabs who've forgotten the sea.

They're land hermit crabs, *Coenobita clypeatus*, probably escapees from the pet trade or their descendants, but they've been here long enough to adapt, to shift, to become something specific to this place. Their gills need humidity, which they have. They need calcium, which they find in the concrete and limestone. They need shells, which they've reimagined.

Instead of seashells, they wear salvage. Industrial debris has become their architecture, their armor, their identity. One wears a brass valve fitting, the threads still visible. Another has claimed a section of copper pipe, polished to a bright finish by its own movement. A third carries the bell housing of an old telephone, the receiver-cradle mechanism still attached, trailing behind like a strange tail. Another has wedged itself into a cylindrical section of machinery, some bearing housing or bushing, too perfectly fitted to be anything but deliberate choice.

The shells are too heavy, you'd think, but the crabs manage. They're strong—one the size of your palm can lift twice its own weight. The heavy shells make them slow, deliberate, but that's acceptable here. Nothing moves quickly. Everything has time.

They emerge from crevices at intervals that might be night on the surface, or might be their own internal clock, or might be triggered by hunger and opportunity. They scavenge obsessively, comprehensively. A dead cricket is found and consumed within an hour, processed by multiple crabs working in sequence—one cracks the exoskeleton, another feeds on the organs, a third cleans up fragments. A fish that dies and sinks is located by chemical senses and reduced to bones over the course of a day.

They eat the algae, scraping it from surfaces with specialized mouthparts. They eat detritus—any organic particle that falls or accumulates. They eat each other's molts, recycling the calcium. They eat bat guano, cricket frass, anything that contains nutrients. They're the cleanup crew, the disposers, walking stomachs in borrowed shells.

They're also vulnerable. During molting, they must leave their shells, expose their soft bodies, shed the old exoskeleton and wait for the new one to harden. This takes hours, sometimes days, and during that time they hide in the deepest crevices, the most protected spaces. Sometimes they don't survive—a bat finds them, or an axolotl, or another crab. But usually they do, emerging larger, immediately seeking a new shell to match their increased size.

Shell acquisition is competitive. There aren't enough good shells for every crab, so hierarchies develop. Larger crabs evict smaller ones from desirable shells, forcing them into less optimal housing. Sometimes you witness this: a large crab approaches a smaller one, taps it repeatedly with claws, uses its greater mass to tip the smaller crab's shell, eventually forcing it out. The smaller crab flees, naked or in some inadequate temporary shelter, seeking anything it can fit into. The victor claims the prize—a particularly well-fitted pipe section, a lightweight but strong aluminum cap.

They make sounds—not vocalizations, but mechanical sounds. The scrape of shell on concrete, the click of claws on metal, the tap-tap-tap of communication or aggression. They gesture with their claws—threats, displays, signals. Two crabs meeting will often engage in a ritual: claws raised, bodies oriented, a moment of assessment, then one backs down and they pass each other peacefully.

There's a social structure, loose but real. Certain crabs are dominant, holding the best scavenging territories—the areas beneath the largest bat roosts, the places where crickets are most abundant, the zones near axolotl territories where food is reliable. Other crabs are peripheral, scraping by in marginal areas. Juveniles are everywhere and nowhere, tiny things in tiny shells—bottle caps, small gears, sections of rubber hose—scuttling through forests of adult legs.

One crab has no shell. Its original shell shattered, perhaps, or it lost a dominance battle at a crucial moment and has been unable to secure a replacement. It lives in a crack in the concrete, rarely emerging, surviving on whatever drifts or crawls near its hiding spot. Its body is pale, soft, vulnerable, but it's been alive this way for weeks at least. Eventually it will find a shell or it will die, but for now it persists, one of the space's many small survival stories.

The fungi are patient empires.

The bracket fungi on the wooden elements are the most visible—those pale shelves stacking like plates, growing at the rate of millimeters per year. Each one is a reproductive structure, a fruiting body, supported by mycelia networks running through the wood beneath. The wood is being digested, converted, turned into fungal body mass over timescales that make trees seem hasty.

They reproduce through spores, released in quantities that defy imaging—trillions of spores per fruiting body, drifting through the air, settling on surfaces, almost never finding suitable substrate. But "almost never" is enough when you're releasing trillions. New colonies establish themselves on any wood that remains: forgotten timber, old ties, platform planking. The space is slowly being digested, transformed from dead wood to fungal matter to nutrients that leach into the water, feeding algae, feeding bacterial mats, feeding the base of the food web.

There are other fungi, less visible. Molds growing on organic debris, on cricket carcasses, on bat guano. They bloom in colors—white, green, black, orange—tiny forests visible only when you look closely. They smell musty, earthy, sweet sometimes. They're decomposers, essential, breaking down complex organic molecules into simpler compounds.

There's a slime mold, a plasmodium, living in one of the wet corners. It moves—actually moves, flowing over surfaces at rates measurable in hours, seeking bacteria to consume. It's bright yellow, startling against the gray-green surroundings, a hand-sized blob that pulses slowly, almost breathing. It's not a fungus, not really—more closely related to amoebas—but it occupies the same conceptual space: strange, simple, ancient, patient.

Some fungi are bioluminescent. Not brightly—you'd never notice unless you were in complete darkness and your eyes were fully adjusted. But in the deepest corners, in crevices where no surface light reaches, there's a faint glow. Greenish, ghostly, outlining the networks of mycelium spreading across surfaces. It serves no obvious purpose—perhaps attracting spore-dispersing insects in other environments, though there are few flying insects here. Perhaps just a metabolic byproduct, energy released as light because the biochemistry involved can't help it.

The fungi and the moss exist in careful relationship. The moss needs clear surface for light access. The fungi need organic matter to digest. Where moss dies back, fungi move in, consuming the dead plant material. Where fungi digest their substrate too thoroughly, moss colonizes the enriched surface. They trade space across seasons or years, cycling, maintaining balance.

There are lichens too, though they're easily mistaken for moss or stain. Symbiotic partnerships between fungi and algae or cyanobacteria, they grow on the most exposed surfaces—high on pillars where a little more light reaches, on steel beams where nothing else can establish. They grow even more slowly than the bracket fungi, expanding their territories by millimeters per decade. Some of the largest patches are probably centuries old, started when this space was new, before it drowned, persisting through every transition.

The moss is a library of water.

It holds moisture, releases it, mediates between the dripping ceiling and the humid air. The thick carpet on the north wall contains gallons of water at any moment, suspended in the matrix of plant tissue and empty space. It drips gently, constantly, feeding itself and everything below.

It's not one species but several, growing in layers, in succession. At the bottom, where moisture is constant but light is dimmest, a dark green species that's almost black, that grows in tight mats like velvet. Above that, a looser growth, more emerald, with tiny leaves on threadlike stems. At the top, where occasional light reaches, a species that's almost silvery, reflective, maximizing what little illumination it receives.

They reproduce through spores, through fragmentation, through creeping growth. A piece breaks off, falls into water, drifts until it catches on something, establishes if conditions are right. The moss is slowly colonizing every surface that stays damp, gradually transforming the space into something greener, softer, more organic.

Invertebrates live in the moss. Springtails, tiny and translucent, feeding on bacteria and detritus. Mites, microscopic, grazing on the moss itself or hunting the springtails. Worms, pale and thread-thin, moving through the moist tissue. The moss is an ecosystem in itself, a miniature rainforest where the canopy is millimeters high and populations are measured in thousands per square foot.

Water bears—tardigrades—live there too, though you'd need a microscope to see them. They're feeding, reproducing, going through their strange life cycles, capable of suspending all metabolism if the moss dries out (it rarely does), waiting decades if necessary, reviving when moisture returns.

The moss smells faintly of earth, of rain, of green growing things. It's one of the few smells here that connects to the surface world, to forests and gardens. Everything else smells of stone and metal and time. The moss smells alive.

Where the moss is thickest, the crickets visit to drink, to graze on the invertebrates living within it. The crabs scrape its edges, harvesting both plant matter and the tiny animals it hosts. The water that drips from it carries nutrients down into the standing water, feeding the algae, feeding the bacterial mats, feeding the base of the aquatic food web.

It grows toward the light, what little there is. The thickest growths are always oriented toward the nearest ventilation shaft, toward the source of illumination. Over years it shifts, very slowly, following changes in light patterns as seasons change above, as the sun's angle shifts, as surface vegetation grows and blocks or opens new pathways for light.

The fern-things are probably not ferns at all.

They look like ferns—fronds, rosette growth pattern, that essential fernness—but they're thicker, darker, more succulent than any surface fern. Perhaps they're some other plant entirely, some species from a different lineage that has converged on the fern form. Perhaps they're ferns that have adapted so heavily they've become something new.

They grow at the margins, in the shallow water, in the interface zone. Their roots, if they are roots, anchor in cracks, in accumulated sediment, in the spaces where concrete has degraded enough to offer purchase. They're scattered, individual, each rosette separate from its neighbors by careful distance, as if they've negotiated territories.

They reproduce through spores, probably—there are structures that look like sporangia on the undersides of fronds—but those spores are released rarely, in response to conditions unclear. Most of the time the plants are just there, existing, photosynthesizing when light is available, dormant or nearly so when it's not.

One plant is much larger than the others, nearly three feet across, its fronds spreading like dark green hands. It grows in a corner where three different light shafts converge during part of the day, where it receives more illumination than anywhere else in the space. It's probably the oldest plant here, the survivor that established when conditions were right and has held on through whatever changes have occurred since.

The smaller ones cluster near it, as if it's a parent, though they're probably not offspring—too large to be recent, too far away for spore dispersal to be likely. Perhaps they're just maximizing the same favorable conditions, converging on the same optimal location.

Invertebrates graze on them—mites, tiny snails that rasp at the frond surfaces, caterpillars of some small moth species that lives here in tiny numbers. The plants tolerate this grazing, having evolved with it or adapted to it. The damage is never severe, never threatening. The grazers are sparse enough, controlled by their own predators, that the plants can afford the loss.

The axolotls use them as cover sometimes, hovering behind the fronds, using them to break their silhouettes when hunting. The fish navigate around them carefully, treating them as obstacles to be respected. The crabs climb them occasionally, reaching up the fronds to access algae growing on concrete just above the waterline.

They're beautiful in a subtle way—the dark green against the gray stone, the regular geometry of the fronds against the irregular geometry of the infrastructure. They're one of the few things here that look intentional, designed, as if someone planted a garden and then left it to mature. But no one planted them. They arrived by chance and stayed by adaptation, one of the many small miracles of the space.

The willow is impossible and present.

It shouldn't survive—too dark, too cool, wrong environment entirely. But it's there, in its corner, six feet high, branches trailing to touch the water, leaves sparse but undeniably alive.

The trunk, if you can call it that, is thin—no thicker than your wrist—and twisted, as if it's grown while struggling with something, fighting some constant pressure or deficiency. The bark is pale gray-brown, smooth, with vertical fissures that weep sap occasionally, clear droplets that catch what little light exists.

The branches are willowish—thin, flexible, hanging down under their own weight. There are maybe twenty main branches, each two or three feet long, each bearing those distinctive narrow leaves. The leaves are yellowish, chlorotic, obviously stressed, but they're there, they're photosynthesizing something, converting whatever light reaches them into growth energy.

The roots must run deep and far. There's no other explanation. The crack it grows from is small, no bigger than your hand at the surface, but below it must widen, must connect to channels that lead upward toward light, toward water sources, toward whatever combination of conditions makes this survival possible.

It moves constantly, those branches swaying in air currents too gentle to feel. It's hypnotic if you watch it—that slow waving, that underwater-kelp motion, that sense of something alive and responding. The movement affects other things: water ripples spreading from where branches touch the surface, creating circles that interfere with other circles, generating patterns. Insects land on it, rest, take off. Occasionally a bat strikes at an insect on the willow, the branch bending dramatically from the impact, then slowly resuming its wave.

It grows, presumably, though the rate must be glacial. A millimeter per year? A centimeter? Enough to maintain itself, to replace dead branches with new ones, to stay in balance with its harsh environment.

There are other plants nearby, smaller, less dramatic—what might be sedges growing in the shallowest water, what might be some kind of adapted grass on a dry ledge. But the willow dominates, conceptually if not physically. It's the thing that should not be here, and therefore the thing you remember, the thing that makes the space feel enchanted or cursed or simply stranger than it already is.

Birds could nest in it, but probably don't—no birds down here, no way for them to enter or leave. But it has the structure of a nesting tree, those thin branches perfect for weaving between. Perhaps in another timeline, another version of this space, birds do nest there, using the willow as an anchor point for their architecture.

Sometimes, rarely, the willow blooms. Tiny flowers, barely visible, on catkins that hang from the branches for a week or two before dispersing seeds that float on the water, drift in the air, almost certainly find no suitable substrate. The blooming seems to follow some internal calendar rather than external cues. Once a year, maybe twice, without warning, the flowers appear, attempt their reproduction, fail, vanish.

It's the most alive thing here, or seems so, or is tied with the axolotls for that status. Everything else is patient, slow, adapted to the stillness. The willow is active, moving, visibly growing if you had the patience to watch for years. It's a bridge between the surface world and this subterranean reality, a reminder that above this ceiling, the sun shines and wind blows and seasons change. Down here, those things are theoretical, reported only by this one stubborn tree.

There are smaller lives, too small to mention individually but too numerous to ignore collectively.

The water is full of microscopic organisms—paramecia, rotifers, copepods, daphnia, all the usual suspects of freshwater plankton. They bloom and crash in cycles, responding to nutrient levels, light availability, predation pressure. They're invisible to the naked eye but their effects are visible: the slight cloudiness in the water where they're abundant, the crystal clarity where they've been filtered out by the fish and axolotls.

Bacteria are everywhere, in quantities that defy comprehension. Billions per milliliter of water, trillions per square centimeter of surface. They're breaking down organic matter, fixing nitrogen, oxidizing sulfur, mediating every chemical process that keeps the ecosystem functioning. They form biofilms on every surface, slick invisible coatings that are actually vast cities of microbial life.

Algae grow wherever light reaches water or damp surfaces. Single-celled species, colonial species, filamentous species forming tiny green threads. They're photosynthesizing, producing oxygen, forming the base of the food web. Everything else is eating them or eating things that eat them or eating things that eat things that eat them.

Protozoans hunt through this microscopic jungle—amoebas, ciliates, flagellates, predators on the bacterial scale, prey on the scale of anything larger. They're diversifying, evolving, speciating probably, though you'd need a taxonomist and a microscope to confirm it.

Nematodes—roundworms—live in the sediment, in the biofilms, in the moss, in the water. They're everywhere multicellular life is possible, feeding on bacteria, on algae, on each other, being fed upon in turn by anything with a mouth small enough to target them. There are probably hundreds of species, though they all look the same to human eyes: tiny transparent threads, thrashing or gliding depending on environment.

Aquatic insects are present in small numbers. Midge larvae, tiny worms in the sediment, building tubes from silk and particles. They'll eventually pupate and emerge as flying adults, which will mate in the brief air above the water, lay eggs, and die, completing cycles that have probably been running for decades, isolated from surface populations, evolving into something new.

There are flatworms gliding across submerged surfaces, gray and transparent, hunting other invertebrates. There are water mites, tiny red dots, parasitizing the fish and axolotls, embedding in their skin, feeding on blood. There are leeches—just a few, hidden in crevices, emerging to attach to any warm-blooded animal that enters the water, which is to say almost never, so they wait, patient as stones, for opportunities that rarely come.

Snails rasp their way across every surface, harvesting algae and biofilm. They're small—the largest no bigger than a pea—and varied in shell shape and color. Some are native to caves, some are escapees from aquariums, some are surface species that found their way here and adapted. They reproduce in clusters, laying eggs that hatch into miniature copies, adding to the population slowly, steadily.

All these small lives add up to a biomass that probably exceeds the larger animals. If you could filter the water, scrape the surfaces, collect and weigh everything, the invisible world would dominate. But it's the visible world that catches attention, that defines the space in memory: the axolotls and their impossible colors, the crickets and their scattered songs, the crabs in their industrial armor.

---

And then there are the visitors.

Rats, occasionally. They come down through the access corridors, exploring, seeking. They drink from the water—cautiously, as if they know it's strange. They catch crickets, eat dead fish, investigate the crabs with interest that sometimes ends in violence. They don't stay long. The space doesn't offer enough of what rats need—no garbage, no grain, no human detritus worth the name. They come, they explore, they leave.

Raccoons, more rarely. They wade into the shallow water, their hands searching, touching everything. They catch axolotls and eat them—efficiently, methodically, biting off the head and consuming the body. They're excellent predators, too competent, and if they stayed they might crash the axolotl population. But they don't stay. Like the rats, they find the space interesting but insufficient, and they return to wherever they came from.

Possums, once in a while, bumbling through in their nearsighted way, eating whatever they find, unable to catch the quick-moving prey but happy to scavenge anything dead. They're gentle presences, unhurried, unbothered by the strangeness.

Feral cats, very rarely. They hate the water but are fascinated by the crickets, the bats, the possibilities. They perch on dry platforms and hunt, taking bats in flight if they're lucky, settling for crickets if they're not. They're unsuccessful here, usually—too reliant on stealth, and the echo-locating bats detect them easily, the alert crickets hop away. They leave frustrated.

Humans, perhaps once every few years, perhaps more often in certain eras. Urban explorers, workers checking old infrastructure, homeless people seeking shelter. They wade through, flash lights across the water, disturb the equilibrium. The axolotls flee. The crickets go silent. The bats press themselves against the ceiling, hoping to disappear. Then the humans leave, and over hours, over days, the space settles back into itself.

But humans have mostly forgotten this place exists. The access points are sealed or hidden, the knowledge of the space preserved only in old municipal records and the memories of retired workers. It's been years since the last visitor, maybe decades. The space is isolated, removed from the human world despite being underneath it, close enough to hear if you knew where to listen but far enough to remain unknown.

---

The equilibrium holds, but it's not static. Things change slowly.

The water level drops slightly over years as the water table shifts, as distant pumping operations affect underground flows, as climate patterns change on the surface. Sections that were submerged become exposed. Axolotls adapt by contracting their territories. The fish populations shift. New surfaces become available for crickets and crabs.

The concrete degrades, imperceptibly. Alkaline surfaces becoming neutral, becoming slightly acidic as carbonic acid in the water slowly dissolves them. The chemical changes affect what can grow, what can live. Some species benefit, some decline. The balance shifts, finds a new balance.

The ventilation shafts are slowly closing. Vegetation growing on the surface sends roots down into them. Debris accumulates—leaves, branches, urban trash blown by wind. Less light reaches the space each year, each decade. The fern-things struggle. The moss expands to compensate, being more efficient in low light. The willow grows paler, its leaves more chlorotic, but it persists.

The infrastructure continues its slow decay. The rust spreads, weakens, but hasn't yet reached the point of collapse. The I-beams still hold. The pillars still stand. The space has centuries of integrity left, probably, barring some catastrophic event above.

New species arrive, occasionally. An insect drifts down through a shaft. A seed finds its way in. An aquatic invertebrate is carried in on a rat's foot or in a bird's gut (though birds don't come here, so perhaps on the foot of some other visitor). Most arrivals die—wrong environment, no suitable food, unlucky. But occasionally one survives, establishes, joins the community. The ecosystem becomes incrementally more complex.

Species adapt, evolve. The fish are becoming more eyeless, more elongated, more cave-specialized. The crickets are diverging into subtypes—platform crickets and crevice crickets, starting to differ in size and behavior. The axolotls are becoming more efficient at low temperatures, their metabolisms shifting, their growth patterns changing. In a thousand years, they might be a new species. In ten thousand years, they certainly will be.

The space is evolving toward something. Not toward purpose—evolution doesn't have goals—but toward greater specificity, better fit, deeper integration. Everything here is becoming more itself, more adapted to these exact conditions. The space is creating its own ecology, its own rules, its own balance.

---

Underneath it all, in the water, in the biofilms, in the chemical gradients and nutrient cycles, there's a metabolism. The space breathes, slowly. Organic matter enters—from the surface, from the visitors, from the plants dying and regrowing. It's processed, broken down, transformed. Energy flows through the system, from light to plants to herbivores to predators to decomposers and back to nutrients. Entropy increases, but slowly, gracefully, in ways that maintain order even as they increase disorder.

The space is not trying to survive. It just does. Every organism here is trying to survive, and their collective trying results in this stable, strange, balanced system. The axolotls eat the small animals. The small animals eat the smaller animals. The smaller animals eat the bacteria and algae. The bacteria and algae eat the light and nutrients. The nutrients come from decay. The decay comes from death. Death comes from life. The circle is complete, self-sustaining, fragile but persistent.

It's been like this for decades. It will be like this for decades more, probably longer. Barring intervention—the space being drained, being filled, being sealed completely, being flooded with toxic runoff from some surface disaster—it will continue. The equilibrium will hold because equilibriums do, when they've had time to establish, when every component has adapted to every other component.

The drowned switchyard lives. Not as an organism—it's not coherent enough, intentional enough for that—but as a community, an ecology, a metabolism. It processes, transforms, endures. The axolotls drift in their dark water. The crickets sing their scattered songs. The crabs scuttle in their salvaged shells. The willow waves its branches. The moss holds water and releases it. The fungi digest the past. The bacteria mediate the chemistry of survival.

And in the cool constant temperature, in the dim light, in the black water still as glass, the space continues being itself: patient, strange, balanced, alive.
