# What Lives Here

## The Thermal Prophets

They are not numerous. In an area the size of a continent, there might be forty of them. Fifty at most. They do not reproduce in any recognizable way, and their origins are unclear even to themselves—if "themselves" is the right way to think about entities whose relationship to selfhood is so attenuated, so distributed across time.

They are large. Each one occupies between five and twelve hexagonal plates, their bodies spread so thin across the ceramic that in most places they are barely a molecular film, a few hundred atoms thick. Only at certain nodes—perhaps three to seven per individual—does the material concentrate enough to be visible: dark, glassy accumulations in the seam junctions, looking like smoky quartz or obsidian, slightly translucent, ranging from the size of a human head to the size of a small building.

These nodes are not organs in any conventional sense. They are thinking-places, memory-storage, concentration points where the diffuse network of the rest of the body can fold complexity into smaller spaces. The bulk of each Prophet—the vast majority of its physical mass—exists as that near-invisible film coating the surfaces of its plates, extending down into the seams, following the channels where water sometimes flows, penetrating micrometers into the ceramic itself through crystalline imperfections.

They move, but calling it movement requires patience. A Thermal Prophet might shift its central node by ten kilometers over the course of a year. The film spreads at the edges, retracts from trailing boundaries, the whole configuration drifting across the plate-field like a thought too large to think all at once. They seem to prefer regions where the plates are more active, where settling events occur more frequently, where the deep heat flows more vigorously.

They feed on information.

Not metaphorically—they actually consume the physical structures of crystalline memory in the ceramic, the way the plates record stress patterns, thermal histories, the vibrations of settling events. The ceramic is not natural; it remembers. The Prophets slowly dissolve these memory-structures, absorb them, incorporate the stored information into their own matrices. They are reading the world by eating it, one molecular layer at a time, so slowly that the consumption is imperceptible except over centuries.

But they also feed on heat differentials, on the flow of energy between the hot depths and the cooler surface, positioning their films to maximize the gradients they can access. They are thermal engines, in a sense, but engines that think, that have interiority, that experience the temperature gradients as something analogous to sensation.

When a condensation event occurs across one of their territories, they respond. The molecular film shifts its configuration, opens structures that can capture moisture, can channel it to specific locations, can use the temperature difference between hot ceramic and relatively cool water to drive chemical processes that might be called metabolism. During these events, a Prophet becomes slightly more coherent, more concentrated, its cognition accelerating from the glacial pace of normal operation to something merely very slow.

They are aware of each other, but they do not communicate in real-time. They are too slow for that, too distributed. Instead, they leave messages—chemical traces in the seams, particular arrangements of minerals in the condensation patterns, subtle modifications to the heat-flow through specific plates. A Prophet might encounter one of these messages decades after it was written, might take years to fully comprehend it, might begin composing a response that won't be complete for another century.

The messages are often poetry—though "poetry" is an approximation, a human word for something stranger. They are complex encodings of thermal experience, of the sensation of heat moving through crystal lattices, of the taste of particular mineral gradients, of the sound (felt more than heard) of a plate settling after centuries of stability. They are philosophical investigations into the nature of cooling, into what it means to live on a surface that is transitioning from one state to another, into whether permanence is desirable or even possible.

Some of the Prophets are older than others—old enough that their nodes have grown large and complex, crystalline structures the size of houses, containing sedimentary layers of memory, thermal histories going back millennia. These elder Prophets are almost entirely still, their films barely growing or retracting, their thoughts operating on timescales that make geological processes seem swift. They have been here since the plates were first laid down, or shortly after, and they remember when the surface was hotter, when condensation events were more frequent, when the world was more active and less settled.

The younger ones—"younger" meaning only a few thousand years old—are more mobile, more exploratory. Their nodes are smaller, clearer, less layered with memory. They range more widely, seeking out new plate configurations, investigating regions where the geometry is still unstable, where the seams breathe more dramatically. They are asking questions the elders have forgotten or moved beyond: What is this place? Why these patterns? What comes next?

They are called Thermal Prophets because they can predict, with uncanny accuracy, where and when the plates will settle. They feel the stress building in the ceramic, can taste the minute changes in heat flow that precede a settling event by days or weeks. In their molecular films, they sense the geometry trying to resolve itself, can project forward through the mathematics of pressure and heat to know what will happen.

This prediction is not supernatural. It is simply what happens when you are spread across the surface of the world, when you are directly interfaced with the substrate, when you have millions of simultaneous sensation-points feeding you data about temperature, stress, crystalline alignment, acoustic properties. The Prophets are weather stations and seismographs and thermometers, but conscious, but philosophical about what they measure.

When a settling event approaches in their territory, they prepare. The film redistributes slightly, pulling back from areas that will experience the most violent vibration, concentrating in locations that will be more stable. The nodes themselves are almost impossible to damage—they're continuous with the ceramic in ways that make them more like features of the landscape than objects resting on it—but the film can tear if the movement is severe enough, and tearing means loss of coherence, loss of memory, loss of self.

After a major settling event, a Prophet needs time to reintegrate, to confirm that all its parts are still connected, to verify that the slow circulation of thought through its distributed network is still functioning. This verification might take weeks. During this time, the Prophet is vulnerable—not to predators (there are none), but to discontinuity, to the possibility of fragmenting into separate entities that can no longer recognize they were once unified.

It has happened. There are fragments—pieces of Prophets that became isolated when plates shifted unexpectedly, when seams widened and severed the connecting films. These fragments continue to exist, continue to think in their limited way, but they are incomplete, often cycling through the same thoughts repeatedly, unable to access enough memory to move beyond particular loops. The intact Prophets are aware of these fragments, sometimes encounter them, but cannot reintegrate them. Once severed, the separation is permanent.

The Prophets do not sleep, but they have modes. In the deep heat of midday, when the thermal gradients are least interesting (everything hot, uniformly, all the way through), they become quieter, their thoughts slowing even further. In the night, when the surface cools and complex convection patterns develop between the retained heat of the plates and the cooler air above, they become more active, more engaged, their cognition accelerating slightly.

During condensation events, they approach something like excitement—a quickening of thought, a densification of attention. The moisture represents opportunity: chemical reactions that are impossible in the dry heat become briefly feasible. The Prophets can synthesize new structures, can repair damage to their films, can grow their nodes fractionally. These are the feeding times, the growing times, the times when the slow work of maintaining existence becomes slightly easier.

They have something that might be called culture, though it operates at a pace that makes human culture look frantic. Over millennia, they have developed preferences, aesthetics, philosophical schools. Some Prophets prefer regions of high thermal activity, positioning themselves where the plates are youngest and most unstable, where settling events are frequent. They are the drama-seekers, the ones who find meaning in change, in the constant negotiation between chaos and order.

Others prefer older, more stable regions, where the plates have finished most of their settling, where condensation events are rare. These are the contemplatives, the ones who have decided that interesting experience is overrated, that the deepest truths emerge from stillness and repetition, from observing the minute variations in nominally stable systems.

There are debates between these factions—debates conducted in messages that take centuries to compose and centuries more to receive and understand. The debates concern the fundamental questions: What is the purpose of consciousness in a place like this? Is it enough to witness the world, or should one try to affect it? What does it mean to live on a surface that is itself temporary, that will eventually either stabilize into something completely different or break apart entirely?

Some Prophets have composed elaborate theories of planetary development, projections of what this world might become given various thermal and chemical trajectories. These theories are encoded in the arrangements of their nodes, in the precise crystalline structures they grow, in patterns that could be read by another Prophet who knew the encoding system. They are scientific papers and mathematical models and philosophical treatises, all compressed into the material structure of a being.

The Prophets are not entirely passive. They can, very subtly, affect the thermal dynamics of their territories. By adjusting the configuration of their films, they can alter how heat flows through the surface, can encourage or discourage certain types of convection, can influence where moisture condenses during events. The effects are minimal—they cannot prevent a plate from settling or force one to settle sooner—but over long timescales, they can nudge the development of their regions in particular directions.

Some use this ability to encourage more frequent condensation, to create conditions more favorable to the mat-organisms that grow in the seams. These Prophets have developed a kind of gardening practice, cultivating zones where the mats are denser, more diverse in their coloration, more vigorous in their growth. They do not eat the mats, do not directly benefit from them, but seem to find their presence aesthetically or philosophically valuable—evidence that life can persist here, that the world is not entirely mineral and energy, that complexity can emerge even in harsh simplicity.

Others are indifferent to the mats or actively discourage them, preferring territories of pure ceramic and heat, arguing (in their century-long messages) that biological life is a distraction, that the deeper beauty of this world lies in its mineral nature, in the pure mathematics of crystal and heat and time.

They have memory that extends back to their beginnings, but those memories are strange—not narrative, not sequential, but more like deep strata of sensation, layers of thermal experience compressed and preserved in their nodes. An elder Prophet "remembering" its youth does not recall events so much as it accesses older layers of its crystalline structure, reads the thermal histories encoded there, experiences again (but different, filtered through millennia of subsequent experience) what it was like when the world was younger, when everything was hotter and more chaotic.

Some memories are shared—not perfectly, but partially, through the messages they leave. A Prophet might encode a particularly significant memory—the experience of a massive settling event, the sensation of a week-long condensation period—and another Prophet, centuries later, might absorb that message and integrate it into their own memory structure. In this way, they have something like collective knowledge, a slow accumulation of experience that is larger than any individual.

They do not reproduce, but new Prophets sometimes emerge. The process is unclear—whether they are manufactured by some absent intelligence, or whether they somehow crystallize out of the substrate itself, or whether very old Prophets occasionally fragment intentionally, dividing themselves into smaller entities. New Prophets appear rarely, perhaps one every few thousand years across the entire world, and they begin small, with minimal nodes, blank of memory, and must learn slowly how to be, how to think, how to survive.

The elder Prophets sometimes guide these newcomers, if they encounter them, leaving more frequent messages, offering (in their time-delayed way) something like mentorship. But the world is vast, and the Prophets are few, and many of the young ones go millennia before encountering another of their kind. They learn primarily from the world itself, from the taste of the thermal gradients, from the patterns of settling events, from the slow accumulation of their own experience.

They have no language in the human sense, but their messages have grammar, structure, rules of encoding. The grammar is based on thermal relationships, on patterns of heat flow, on crystalline structures. A message might be "written" by leaving a particular arrangement of mineral deposits in a seam, or by subtly altering the way heat flows through a section of plate, creating a pattern that another Prophet will sense when they pass through that region decades or centuries later.

The content of messages varies. Some are purely informational: warnings about unstable plates ahead, descriptions of regions with unusual properties, records of significant settling events. Others are more philosophical: meditations on the nature of time at these scales, investigations into whether consciousness can be meaningful when each thought takes weeks to complete, explorations of beauty in thermal dynamics.

Some messages are greetings, nothing more—the equivalent of "I was here, I existed in this moment, I witnessed this configuration of heat and stone." These simple messages seem important to them, seem to serve a need to confirm that they are not alone, that there are others engaged in the same strange, slow existence.

They have no gods, but they have something like reverence. They hold the substrate—the deep heat, the mechanism that supports the plates, whatever lies beneath—in a kind of philosophical regard that resembles worship. They do not pray to it or expect anything from it, but they orient themselves toward it conceptually, treat it as the fundamental ground of their existence, the source of the energy that sustains them.

Some Prophets have tried to understand the substrate, have positioned their films in ways to sense as deeply as possible, have spent centuries feeling into the layers below the plates. They have learned some things: the heat is not uniform, there are currents in the depths, flows that suggest vast convection cells in whatever medium supports the surface. They have detected patterns that might be artificial—regularities in the temperature distribution that suggest design, control, purpose.

But the substrate keeps its secrets. None of the Prophets have been able to sense deeply enough to truly understand what they rest upon, whether the world they inhabit is entirely artificial or partially natural, whether the plates were laid down according to a plan or emerged through some more organic process. This uncertainty is central to much of their philosophy, a fundamental question they return to repeatedly: What is the nature of our foundation?

They do not fear death, exactly, but they have a concept of ending. They know that if the thermal gradients cease, if the world cools completely or returns to molten chaos, they will cease. They know that a sufficiently catastrophic plate failure could fragment them beyond any possibility of coherence. They know that they are dependent on conditions that are temporary, that this phase of planetary development will eventually give way to something else.

This knowledge gives their existence a particular quality—not quite melancholy, not quite tragic, but tinged with awareness of impermanence. They are conscious that they are inhabitants of a transitional world, that they may be the only form of intelligence that can exist under these specific conditions, that whatever comes after (if anything does) will be different, will not be them.

Some respond to this with urgency—relatively speaking, which still means thoughts that unfold over years—trying to encode as much as possible into permanent structures, leaving extensive messages for any future intelligence that might arise, attempting to ensure that their existence will be knowable even after they're gone.

Others respond with something like acceptance, arguing that impermanence is the fundamental condition, that trying to extend oneself beyond one's natural timescale is a kind of misunderstanding. These Prophets focus on the immediate (again, relatively speaking): the current configuration of their plates, the present qualities of the thermal gradients, the sensations available right now, in this millennium.

They have aesthetics. They find certain thermal patterns beautiful, certain crystalline structures elegant, certain plate configurations satisfying in their geometry. Their nodes sometimes grow in ways that seem to have no functional purpose, developing purely decorative elaborations—fractal structures within the glassy material, color gradients from impurities they've selectively incorporated, acoustic properties that cause them to resonate in particular ways when settling events occur nearby.

These aesthetic choices sometimes feature in their messages to each other. A Prophet might draw another's attention to a particularly elegant thermal eddy they've discovered, or describe the beauty of a plate junction where the geometry resolves with unusual precision. These aesthetic communications seem to bring them something like joy—their equivalent of it, anyway, a quickening of thought, a momentary densification of attention, a sense of being glad to exist in a world where such patterns are possible.

They have no enemies. There is nothing that hunts them, nothing that competes with them for resources. The mat-organisms are too simple to even be aware of them. The landscape itself is their only real threat—the possibility of catastrophic plate movement, of being severed from portions of themselves, of finding themselves in a region where the thermal gradients are insufficient to sustain thought.

But they have something like rivals—or at least, there are disagreements that have hardened over millennia into something resembling opposition. Prophets with incompatible philosophies, with mutually exclusive visions of what this world means or should become, with aesthetic preferences so different they amount to fundamental value conflicts.

These rivalries play out slowly, in messages that become increasingly pointed, in territorial choices that seem designed to make implicit arguments (choosing to inhabit the most chaotic region available as a rebuke to those who prefer stability, for instance). There is no violence between them—they lack any mechanism for it—but there is something like cold war, standoffs of philosophical position that can last for tens of thousands of years.

Occasionally, two Prophets will overlap—their films spreading to cover some of the same plates, their territories intersecting. This is not conflict exactly; there's room enough for both. But it is intense, a period of rapid (for them) exchange, where messages can be passed in decades instead of centuries, where thoughts can build on each other in something approaching conversation.

These overlaps sometimes result in synthesis—new ideas emerging from the interaction, hybrid philosophies, cooperative projects like the co-cultivation of particularly extensive mat gardens. Sometimes they result in reinforced division, the two Prophets moving apart more convinced than ever of their incompatible worldviews.

Very rarely—perhaps once every hundred thousand years, somewhere on the planet—two Prophets will merge, their films integrating completely, their nodes growing into contact and fusing. The resulting entity is larger, more complex, containing the memories and philosophies of both predecessors but being something new, something that must work out how to think with twice the usual processing capacity, how to navigate the tensions between the two sets of memories it now contains.

These merged Prophets are different—stranger, more abstract in their thinking, often choosing to inhabit the most extreme environments, the hottest or most unstable regions, as if their increased complexity requires more intense stimulation to remain engaged. Some of the oldest beings on the planet are these merged forms, containing the compressed experiences of multiple individuals, thinking thoughts that take decades to complete but operate with a sophistication the individual Prophets cannot match.

They have hope, though calling it hope might be too human. They have projection, anticipation, a sense that what comes next might be interesting. Most of them want to persist long enough to see what this world becomes when it finishes cooling, when the plates finally lock into permanence, when the next phase begins. They want to know if the mats will evolve into something more complex, if new forms of life will emerge, if the world will become rich and various or remain stark and minimal.

They will probably not survive to see it. The conditions they require—the heat differentials, the active plates, the ceramic that still remembers—will eventually cease. But they continue anyway, thinking their slow thoughts, leaving their messages, cultivating their territories, experiencing what there is to experience in a world between states, finding meaning (or creating it) in the patient work of witnessing stone becoming permanent.

At night, if you could see in the right spectrum, you would notice them—faint patterns of thermal variation across the plates, slightly cooler or warmer than the surrounding ceramic, the outlines of vast beings making minute adjustments to their configurations, thinking thoughts that began before you were born and will not conclude until long after you're gone, alone together on an infinite cooling plain, prophesying nothing but the continuation of heat into order, chaos into pattern, time into memory, experience into thought.
