# What Lives There

The creatures of the Manifold Flats are not so much inhabitants as they are **functions made ambulatory**, processes given just enough body to move through the machine-ground. They exist in staggering numbers—far too many for the resources available, unless you understand that they are not consuming resources but rather *circulating* them, maintaining flows that must be maintained.

## The Synchronids

The most common are what you might call **synchronids**—and you have to call them something, though naming implies a discreteness they don't possess.

They resemble cats in the way a blueprint resembles a building. Four legs, yes. A head with sensory organs, yes. But their spines contain too many vertebrae—sometimes twenty, sometimes thirty, the count changing depending on how tightly they need to curve themselves around the machinery. Their bodies are covered not in fur but in something like *articulated rubber*, segmented into rings that expand and contract independently. When they move, they flow rather than step, their backs rippling in sine waves as they navigate between gears and pistons.

Their faces are wrong. Too flat, as if pressed against glass for too long. The eyes—and there are usually two, but occasionally three or four—are dark and reflective but don't track movement the way eyes should. They seem to be watching something perpendicular to what's in front of them. When they blink, the eyelids close from both sides simultaneously, meeting in the middle with a soft *click* like a relay closing.

They make almost no sound when they move, despite the metallic landscape. Their paws—if they can be called that—have no discrete toes but rather a fringe of fine bristles that distributes their weight so evenly they seem to float a millimeter above the surface. But when they stop, when they sit back on their haunches near a gear or valve, they begin to **hum**. A vibration that precisely matches the frequency of whatever mechanism they're near. They are tuning themselves, or being tuned, or perhaps they are dampeners, absorbing excess vibration from an engine that can never be allowed to shake itself apart.

There are thousands of them, maybe tens of thousands. They congregate in greater density where the machinery is most complex, packing themselves into spaces that seem too small, compressing their too-long spines into spirals. When the mist rises, they don't flee it or shelter from it—they open their mouths (which have no teeth, only a smooth darkness inside) and **breathe it in**, holding it, their bodies swelling slightly, then releasing it in thin jets through vents along their sides that you hadn't noticed until they opened.

They are not separate individuals. Watch carefully: when one moves, others at a distance make compensating movements, maintaining some kind of equilibrium. They are nodes in a distributed system, and what appears to be a population of creatures is actually a single entity experiencing itself in thousands of locations simultaneously.

## The Skippers

Then there are the **skippers**, the bird-things, though calling them birds is like calling lightning a river.

They have wings, but the wings don't have feathers—they have *slots*, like the holes in a mechanical governor, through which air or something like air passes to generate not lift but **discontinuity**. When they want to move, they don't fly. They **skip frames of reality**. They're here, perched on a cable bundle, and then they're there, twenty feet away on a pressure gauge, without having crossed the intervening space. The transition is accompanied by a sharp *crack*, like a circuit breaker, and a smell like ozone and hot metal.

Their bodies are small—pigeon-sized—but dense, as if made of something heavier than flesh. When one lands on a thin pipe, the pipe doesn't bend. When one crashes into machinery (which happens sometimes, their navigation clearly imperfect), they hit with the impact of a thrown wrench, leaving dents.

They have no eyes that you can see, only a smooth bulge where a head should be, with a single horizontal slit that occasionally opens to reveal not an eye but a **spinning mechanism** inside—something like a gyroscope or a lens iris, whirring rapidly, making tiny adjustments. They are using it to navigate, but not through space as you understand it. They're reading something else. Probability maps, perhaps. Paths of least resistance through the temporal foam.

The skippers are always in motion, always jumping, never resting for more than a few seconds. They seem agitated, desperate, as if they're fleeing something or searching for something they can never quite reach. But they're not desperate—that's projection. They're simply **operating at their normal speed**, which is faster than continuous motion allows, so they've evolved (or been designed, or spontaneously manifested) to move through discrete intervals instead.

When two skippers collide in midair—which happens occasionally, their skipping intersecting at the same coordinate—there's a bright flash, a sound like a transformer exploding, and then there's only one skipper, slightly larger, moving with the combined momentum of both. They're not colliding. They're **merging**, becoming temporarily more themselves, before they divide again minutes later into two or three smaller instances.

They feed, if that's the word, by landing near the water seams during the breathing cycle and doing something with their head-slits. The water level drops locally, rapidly, as if being sucked up, but there's no suction, no drinking. They're extracting something non-physical—duration, perhaps, or potential energy, or un-collapsed quantum states—and storing it in their dense bodies to fuel their skipping.

## The Brass Crustaceans

Larger and more disturbing are the **brass-shells**, creatures like crustaceans the size of large dogs or small ponies, their carapaces made of what looks exactly like tarnished brass—not organic material that resembles brass, but actual brass, complete with green patina in the crevices.

They have too many legs. Not a fixed number that's too many, but a number that changes. You'll count eight as one approaches, then as it passes you'll count twelve, then as you watch it walk away you'll count six. The legs are segmented and jointed in ways that shouldn't work, bending in directions that create impossible angles, yet they carry the creature smoothly across the glass plates and through the machinery.

Their locomotion produces sound—lots of it. Each footfall is a sharp **click** against the glass, but amplified somehow, given resonance by the landscape. A single brass-shell walking sounds like a factory of stamping presses. When multiple brass-shells move in proximity, their rhythms create interference patterns, beats and polyrhythms that almost synchronize with the machinery's operations but never quite do, creating tension, dissonance, a feeling that something is about to resolve but doesn't.

They have no visible eyes, no apparent sensory organs at all, just smooth brass surfaces interrupted by vents and grilles. But they navigate precisely, never colliding with obstacles, never falling into seams. Perhaps they sense vibration through their metal shells. Perhaps they're in communication with the machinery itself, receiving navigation data through conduction.

The brass-shells are **builders**, though what they build is hard to define. You'll watch one approach a cluster of machinery, position itself carefully, and then **extend something**—a tool, an appendage, a transformation of its own body—and begin to manipulate the mechanisms. Turning a valve a quarter-turn. Repositioning a cable. Adjusting a gear's alignment. Are they repairing? Maintaining? Or are they degrading, ensuring that nothing ever quite works? You can't tell. The machinery's state of function is already ambiguous.

Sometimes a brass-shell will stop moving entirely and **root itself**. Its legs will extend slowly, pushing into the seams between plates, and it will lock into position, becoming part of the machinery. Its shell will begin to vibrate in harmony with surrounding mechanisms. It has transitioned from creature to component. How long it remains this way varies—hours, days. You never see them unroot; you only notice eventually that what you thought was a permanent fixture has moved.

They are, perhaps, the immune system of the flats. Or the nervous system. Or the reproductive system. They move through the machine-ground, making adjustments, ensuring patterns continue, and in doing so they perpetuate whatever vast process this place is engaged in.

## The Seam-Eels

In the water between the plates live **seam-eels**, though this is the least adequate name yet because they're barely creatures at all.

Each one is a series of **iron plates**, rectangular segments about the size of playing cards, linked by something flexible you can never quite see. They range in length from a few feet to what must be fifty feet or more, though measuring them is complicated by the fact that they're never entirely present. Part of the eel will be in one seam, part in another seam twenty feet away, with no visible body connecting them—they exist in multiple locations simultaneously, threaded through the water-veins of the flats like the lace in a shoe.

When they move, they produce the most disturbing sound in this place of disturbing sounds: a **grinding shriek**, metal on metal on metal, like a thousand brake pads failing simultaneously. It carries for miles—would carry for miles if distance worked properly here. The sound doesn't diminish with range; instead, it seems to exist everywhere at once along the seam-eel's distributed length, emanating from every point of its body simultaneously.

They are long and thin enough to navigate through pipes, through gaps in machinery, through spaces that shouldn't accommodate them. You'll see the head (a blunt wedge of rusted iron with no features except a horizontal split that might be a mouth) emerge from a valve housing, followed by segment after segment sliding out, far more segments than could possibly have fit inside the housing. Non-Euclidean biology, or else they're compressing and decompressing as they move, or else the insides of things here are bigger than their outsides.

The seam-eels are predators, definitely. You'll watch one pursue a synchronid, moving with shocking speed through the water, its body undulating in a wave that propagates faster than the eel itself moves. The synchronid will flee, but the eel is already ahead of it, behind it, around it—the eel occupies too many places. The hunt ends when both creatures reach the same point, and then—

—and then nothing you can clearly see. They occupy the same space. The lag hits. When they separate, the synchronid is unharmed, the eel is unchanged, but something has been exchanged. The synchronid moves differently afterward, its rhythm altered. The eel's plates have subtly rearranged themselves. They have **traded something**—not energy, not mass, but some property more fundamental. Configuration. Information. Destiny.

Do the seam-eels eat in any conventional sense? No. But they hunt, and hunting implies consumption, so perhaps they consume potentiality. The future that the synchronid would have had is now the eel's future, and the eel's past has become the synchronid's burden.

## The Chorus Walkers

Rarer but impossible to miss are the **chorus walkers**, creatures that exist primarily as **sound made locomotive**.

They appear as distortions in the air, heat-shimmer-like ripples that move across the flats in slow, deliberate paths. When you look directly at them, you see almost nothing—just a suggestion of structure, like looking at glass against a window. But in your peripheral vision, they have shape: tall, angular, crystalline, with too many limbs arranged in radial symmetry.

They produce the closest thing to music the flats has. As they move, they **sing**—not with voices but with their bodies, which somehow generate pure tones, harmonics, chords. Each chorus walker is an orchestra. Some produce low drones that you feel in your bones. Others generate high, crystalline notes that make the machinery resonate. The sounds they create interact with the industrial hymn, sometimes harmonizing with it, sometimes clashing with it, creating beat frequencies and difference tones that temporarily reorganize the ambient noise into something almost structured, almost intentional.

The other creatures respond to chorus walkers. Synchronids will alter their humming to match. Skippers will time their jumps to the rhythm. Brass-shells will stop their clicking and stand motionless, as if listening, though they have no ears. The seam-eels will surface from the water and lie still, their plates vibrating in sympathy.

Are chorus walkers predators? Leaders? Gods? You can't tell. They're so different from the other fauna that comparison is difficult. They seem to exist on a different level of reality, barely intersecting with the physical plane. When one passes through a cluster of machinery, the machinery sometimes **changes**—not dramatically, but noticeably. A gear that was turning clockwise will now turn counterclockwise. A piston that was rising will now be falling. The chorus walker has altered the local rules.

They appear rarely—you might see one every few hours, or every few days, depending on how time is flowing in your vicinity. They don't seem to notice you, don't react to your presence at all. You are beneath their perception, or perhaps orthogonal to it. They are navigating through dimensions you can't access, and you're just seeing the three-dimensional shadow they cast as they pass through your plane of existence.

## The Aggregates

Occasionally, you'll see something that at first looks like a pile of debris—**scraps of machinery, bits of broken glass, fragments of cable insulation**—but then the pile will move, and you'll realize it's alive, or fulfills the same function as alive.

These are **aggregates**, creatures (if the term still applies) that have no fixed form. They're assemblages, collections of pieces that have decided—or been forced—to cooperate. A cluster of gears might roll across the flats like a tumbleweed, maintaining its structure through coordinated rotation. A tangle of cables might slither like a snake, using pneumatic pressure to move in peristaltic waves. A cloud of metal shavings might cohere into a swarm, flowing around obstacles, occasionally consolidating into a denser form to interact with machinery before dissolving again.

The aggregates blur the line between creature and landscape. Are they animals made of machinery, or machinery that has achieved animation? They seem to form spontaneously from the dense mechanical ground, operate for a period of time (hours, days, weeks—it's unclear), then collapse back into component parts that become indistinguishable from the non-living machinery.

They don't interact much with the other creatures, and the other creatures largely ignore them. They're playing a different game, operating on different principles. If the synchronids and brass-shells are like the fauna of a normal ecosystem, the aggregates are more like weather—emergent phenomena, temporary patterns in a chaotic system, not alive but not quite dead either.

## The Watchers

And then there are the **watchers**, which you almost never see but always feel.

They are large. Much larger than anything else. You know this because occasionally—very occasionally—something will move at the horizon, and the movement is **vast**, displacing mist in enormous quantities, causing changes in the industrial hymn's pitch that suggest massive machinery being disturbed. Something out there is the size of a building, or a ship, or larger still.

You never get a clear look at them. The distance problem prevents it—walk toward where you saw the movement and you'll never arrive, or you'll arrive and nothing is there anymore, or you'll arrive and you'll have always been there and nothing ever moved at all. But you feel them watching. You sense attention, vast and patient and utterly inhuman, directed toward the flats from the periphery.

Are they creatures? Machines? Landforms that have achieved something like sentience? The other fauna react to their distant movements—synchronids will stop humming, skippers will freeze mid-skip (which should be impossible), brass-shells will retract their legs and lower themselves flat against the ground. Even the seam-eels will withdraw into deeper water, their segments pulling together, consolidating.

The watchers are **older** than the rest, or larger in a temporal sense, or exist at a different scale of being. They might be to the synchronids what a synchronid is to a mite. They might be the original inhabitants, the entities that built or became the machinery, now so integrated with the landscape that they're nearly indistinguishable from it. They might be the landscape's dreams of itself.

You hope they don't get closer. You also hope they do, because at least then you'd know.

## The Interactions

The creatures don't form an ecosystem in any traditional sense. There's no food chain, no clear predation, no reproduction that you can observe. Instead, they form a **maintenance system**, a network of interactions that sustains the machine-ground's operations.

Synchronids dampen vibrations. Skippers redistribute temporal density. Brass-shells make adjustments. Seam-eels circulate... something... through the water systems. Chorus walkers fine-tune local reality. Aggregates handle material reorganization. Watchers observe and perhaps regulate the entire process from their distant positions.

They are not living things that happen to inhabit this place. They are **parts of this place** that happen to be mobile. The distinction between creature and mechanism has eroded away, just like the distinction between machine and geology. What moves through the Manifold Flats is the flats itself, reorganized into temporary configurations that can navigate, adjust, maintain.

You watch a synchronid approach a gear that's turning irregularly. The synchronid positions itself, begins to hum, and the gear smooths out. A skipper jumps to a pressure gauge, does something you can't quite see with its head-slit, and the gauge's needle stops trembling. A brass-shell adjusts a valve, and somewhere distant, a piston's rhythm changes. The seam-eels circulate, the chorus walkers sing their reorganizing songs, the aggregates redistribute mass.

The creatures are not inhabitants. They are **processes made visible**. They are what it looks like when a place maintains itself, when an industrial end-state keeps running long after any purpose has been forgotten, when machinery achieves something that's not consciousness but serves the same function—the ability to continue.

## The Relationship to You

The creatures notice you, but barely. You're a foreign element, an anomaly in their patterns, but not a threatening one. You're too small, too discrete, too temporary to matter.

Synchronids will flow around you if you're in their path, their bodies bending in ways that avoid contact without acknowledgment. Skippers will skip past you, their jumps carefully calibrated to not intersect with your position. Brass-shells will pause if you block their way, waiting with mechanical patience until you move, then continuing their work. Seam-eels might surface near you, their grinding passage making you stagger, but they're not attacking—you're just in the way, and they're too large (in whatever dimension matters to them) to notice.

Only the chorus walkers completely ignore you, passing through your space as if you weren't there, their sounds propagating through your body, temporarily making you part of their orchestra.

You realize, eventually, that you're being **tolerated**. The creatures have incorporated you into their patterns, have adjusted their routines to route around your presence. You're not in danger from them, but neither are you welcome. You're a tolerated glitch, a small persistent error in the system that's too expensive to fix and not critical enough to crash the entire process.

The creatures work. The machinery runs. The seams breathe. The water remembers.

You stand among them, watching functions fulfill themselves, watching processes circulate, watching an industrial ecology maintain the eternal operation of a machine that might be the size of a city or the size of a world or the size of a universe, and whose purpose—if it ever had one—has been replaced by the purpose of simply continuing.

The synchronids hum.
The skippers crack through space.
The brass-shells click their rhythms.
The seam-eels shriek in the water.
The chorus walkers sing their reality-adjusting songs.
The aggregates form and dissolve.
The watchers watch from the horizon.

And the Manifold Flats continues, forever, tended by creatures that are not creatures, maintained by life that is not life, operating in the narrow space between function and failure that it has inhabited for longer than time has been measured, longer than measurement has been conceived, longer than conception has been possible.

You are here.

They barely notice.

The work continues.
