# The Nursery Caves of Isthal-Ven: Who Lives Here

## The Nithka

They are not one thing. This is the first understanding that comes from sustained observation, from watching long enough to see past the overwhelming fact of their numbers to the distinctions beneath.

The smallest are larval—though whether they're nithka larvae specifically or something else that shares the space is a question without consensus. They cluster in the moisture-thick crevices, translucent as fish eggs, each the size of a lentil. You can see through their shells to the darker mass inside, coiled tight. They don't move individually, but the clusters *breathe*—expanding and contracting in rhythm, creating the illusion of a single compound lung. When they hatch—if that's what the process is called—they emerge already armored in miniature chitinous plates, already possessing too many limbs, already restless. The mortality rate appears catastrophic. For every hundred that emerge, perhaps five survive to adolescence. The rest become food for adults, for each other, for the pale worm-things that thread through the slime.

The juveniles are hand-sized, kit-sized, and they move with manic energy that makes the adults look contemplative by comparison. They climb everything. They fall constantly—from walls, from ceilings, from the backs of larger nithka—and land already scuttling, apparently undamaged. Their shells are softer than adult carapaces, slightly flexible, pearl-gray with patterns that might be individual or might be developmental stages. They chirp. Constantly. High, insistent sounds that carry through the chamber noise, probably locating calls, possibly distress signals. The adults tolerate them, mostly. Sometimes an adult will groom a juvenile with surprising delicacy, mandibles clicking across the smaller shell, removing parasites or debris. Other times adults simply crush them underfoot, accidentally or otherwise, and the juveniles become food like everything else becomes food.

As they mature—and the growth rate seems rapid, weeks rather than months—they begin to differentiate. This is where the species question becomes complicated, because they don't differentiate into standardized adult forms. They differentiate into *variants*.

Some develop heavy frontal carapaces and powerful clawed limbs—these become the largest specimens, draft horse–sized, primarily quadrupedal though capable of rearing up on hind legs. They move slowly, deliberately, parting crowds of smaller nithka through sheer mass. Their shells are fortress-thick, scarred, often hosting smaller organisms—mites, perhaps, or tiny crustaceans that graze on algae films growing on the shell surface. These are the ones the delvers call *shield-backs*, and they're simultaneously the most and least dangerous: most because they can accidentally crush you, least because they're slow enough to avoid.

Others grow long, segmented bodies with a dozen or more legs rippling in coordinated waves—these ones climb with spider-grace, occupying the upper walls and ceilings in preference to floors. Their shells are segmented, articulated, allowing serpentine flexibility. They drop silk, or something like silk—protein strands that dangle from ceiling attachments, creating curtains of sticky thread in some galleries. Are they hunting? Building? The purpose remains unclear, but you learn to watch for the gleam of these threads in your light, to avoid walking face-first into them. The substance is strong enough to slow your movement, sticky enough to require careful removal. Sometimes smaller nithka are caught in these threads, struggling or still, and the long-bodied ones descend to feed. Sometimes the threads simply hang empty, accumulating dust and moisture.

A third variant develops extensive fur alongside its chitinous sections—mammalian pelts, shaggy and coarse, growing in patches between armor plates. These ones congregate in the warmest areas, the deep chambers where geothermal heat seeps through stone. They're more vocal than the others, making low thrumming sounds that resonate from chest cavities hidden beneath fur and shell. When they vocalize in groups, the sound becomes almost subsonic, felt in your bones before it's heard. They groom each other extensively, teeth and mandibles working through each other's pelts, removing parasites, redistributing oils. The grooming sessions can involve a dozen individuals, creating heaps of intermixed fur and shell that pulse and shift as bodies rearrange within the tangle.

And there are others, less common variants: ones with enlarged sensory organs—compound eyes bulging from their heads like biological telescopes, or antenna arrays so elaborate they seem architectural. Ones with specialized limbs that look adapted for digging, though what they'd dig in solid stone is mysterious—perhaps they're evolutionary holdovers, tools waiting for appropriate substrate. Ones that seem to be developing wings, or fin-like protrusions, structures that make no sense for cave-dwelling but persist anyway, folded against their backs like failed experiments.

The variants interbreed freely, or at least mate without apparent preference—you witness couplings between grossly different morphologies, shell-back to fur-bearer, long-bodied to standard form. The act is violent, graceless, accompanied by clicking percussion and struggle that looks like combat until you recognize the pattern. Whether this produces further variants or whether the differentiation happens through environmental triggers during development—like temperature-dependent sex determination in reptiles, but morphology instead—remains unknown. The juveniles don't declare their eventual form until they're already maturing.

## Intelligence and Behavior

They're not smart. This seems important to establish. They show no evidence of tool use, language, culture, memory beyond simple conditioning. An individual nithka will make the same mistake repeatedly—attempting to climb a too-smooth surface and falling, then immediately attempting the same climb. They don't learn from watching each other. They don't cooperate toward goals, don't build, don't modify their environment intentionally.

But they're not stupid, either. They solve immediate problems: how to reach food, how to avoid falling rocks, how to navigate three-dimensional space with considerable agility. They recognize threats—a delver who injured one will be avoided by that specific individual for hours afterward, though not by others, and eventually even the injured one forgets. They exhibit what might be emotions: something like play in juveniles, who chase each other without apparent purpose beyond the chase itself. Something like distress when injured, vocalization and frantic movement. Something like contentment when grooming or being groomed, a visible relaxation of body tension, a stillness that's otherwise rare.

Their social structure is density-based rather than hierarchical. They don't have alphas, territories, or dominance displays. They simply exist in proximity, tolerating each other because they're forced to by their numbers and the confined space. The peace is not sophisticated—it's the peace of overcrowding making violence impractical. In the less dense areas, you occasionally witness actual fights: two nithka grappling, mandibles locked, claws scoring each other's shells. These conflicts are brief, ending when one disengages and flees. No pursuit follows. No vendetta continues. Five minutes later the same individuals might be resting side by side, the fight apparently forgotten.

The density itself might be the key to their cognitive limitations. In evolutionary terms, they're optimized for survival in overwhelming numbers rather than individual cleverness. Any single nithka is expendable, easily replaced. Natural selection doesn't pressure them toward intelligence—it pressures them toward fecundity, toward rapid maturation, toward tolerance of extreme crowding. They're quantity over quality, succeeding through sheer biological persistence rather than adaptation.

Yet there are moments that suggest something more, or at least something other. The synchronized movements that ripple through congregations—those aren't random. Some kind of signal propagates, chemical or vibrational, creating coordinated response without requiring understanding. Like murmurations, but the medium is bodies instead of air. And the patterns that emerge from their movement through the caves, the pathways worn by accumulated traffic, suggest optimization that individual nithka couldn't achieve through individual decisions. It's emergent complexity, the colony as organism, intelligence arising from the interaction of non-intelligent parts.

The delvers debate whether the nithka constitute one species with extreme polymorphism or several species so closely related they're barely distinct. The truth likely falls somewhere between: a species-complex in the process of speciating, caught in the act of evolutionary divergence, not yet separate enough to be incompatible but different enough to be visually distinct. The caves provide the isolation necessary for speciation—different galleries with different conditions, populations that mingle but not completely. Given another thousand generations, they might fracture into genuinely separate species. For now they remain in flux, undefined, becoming.

## The Ecosystem They Create

The nithka are not the whole ecology, but they're the foundation everything else builds on. Their waste, their dead, their shed shells—these create the resource base for dozens of other organisms, most of them small, many of them unclassified, some of them possibly undiscovered.

The slime is the most visible secondary organism. In the brightest chambers, where light enables closer observation, you can see its structure: not uniform but filamentous, networked, spreading in patterns that suggest growth vectors, resource flow. It fruits occasionally—producing tiny cup-shaped structures, mushroom-small, that release clouds of spores or spore-equivalents when disturbed. The nithka consume these fruits when they encounter them. The spores settle on nithka shells, germinating there, beginning the cycle again. It's symbiotic, probably—the nithka transport and fertilize the slime, the slime processes waste into forms the nithka can eat, directly or through intermediaries.

The worm-things are more complex than they appear. Under close examination—which requires capturing one, which requires patience and a delicate touch—they reveal segmentation, rudimentary eye-spots, mouths with rasping structures adapted for scraping. They're eating the slime, primarily, but also dead nithka tissue, also each other. They reproduce through fission, sometimes—one worm becoming two identical worms—and through sexual reproduction other times, leaving egg-clusters in protected crevices. The juveniles are microscopic. The adults reach six inches at maximum, though most stay smaller.

The flying things are genuinely insectoid: six legs, four wings, compound eyes, the full arthropod body plan. Several species coexist, each with slightly different ecological niches. The smallest are nectar-feeders, visiting the slime fruits when they open, transferring spores, serving as pollinators in a system that probably doesn't require pollination but benefits from it anyway. Larger ones are predators, catching smaller insects mid-flight with remarkable agility. The largest are scavengers, landing on nithka corpses to feed on decay, to lay eggs in the softening tissue where their larvae will develop. The nithka tolerate landing insects briefly before snapping at them, a reflex that's successful maybe one time in five. The insects are faster, more agile, able to operate in the vertical dimension the nithka dominate but can't monopolize.

And there are others, discovered through careful observation over time: Pale spiders, web-less, hunting through active pursuit in the slime layers. Centipede-analogues, though with the wrong number of legs, flowing through cracks too narrow for nithka. Things that might be scorpions, or something that convergently evolved a scorpion body plan, complete with stinging tails that paralyze worms. Creatures that look like nothing else, unique to this environment, possibly evolved in isolation in these caves or their predecessors—blind, colorless, adapted to eternal darkness and close spaces.

The food web extends downward to bacteria, obviously, though these require equipment most delvers don't carry to observe. But you can infer their presence from the decay rates, from the way dead nithka decompose over days rather than weeks, flesh processing into slime-food, shell gradually fragmenting into mineral components. And the food web extends upward, theoretically, to whatever apex predators might visit the caves from outside. But evidence of these is scarce—occasional bones that don't match nithka morphology, scratch marks on stone that seem too large, stories from surface dwellers about things that emerge from the cave mouths at night. Nothing confirmed. Nothing repeated reliably enough to establish pattern.

The ecosystem is young, like the caves themselves. It hasn't achieved equilibrium—populations fluctuate wildly, boom and crash cycles observable within single seasons. The nithka numbers swell until they exhaust local resources, then crash as starvation takes the weakest, then recover as the survivors breed prolifically in the resource recovery phase. The dependent species follow these cycles with a lag, predator and prey populations oscillating out of phase. In older ecosystems these cycles would dampen, stabilize, reach steady state. Here they remain chaotic, suggesting the system hasn't existed long enough to optimize, or that the environment itself is still too changeable to permit stability.

## The Other Inhabitants: Evidence of Humans

The people who left the artifacts are gone, but their absence is instructive. The tools and structures are primitive, yes, but they're also *unsuccessful*. The stone walls collapsed or were never completed. The platforms serve no clear purpose, or served purposes that failed. The tools are scattered as if dropped in haste or despair. This was not a thriving settlement—it was an attempt that ended.

Who were they? The copper and iron suggest metallurgy, which suggests culture, agriculture, civilization somewhere nearby. But the stone and bone tools alongside the metal suggest either poverty or expedience—people using whatever came to hand, not equipped for systematic exploration or habitation. The clay vessels, unglazed and crude, were made by hands that understood pottery's basics but hadn't refined the technique. Folk pottery, utilitarian, not artistic.

Why did they come? Several theories compete: They were miners, seeking metal veins in the volcanic rock, using the caves as shelter or workspace. But there's no evidence of mining—no tailings, no excavation sites, no ore remains. They were refugees, fleeing some surface calamity, attempting to survive underground. But the caves are unsurvivable for long-term human habitation—no food source, water too unreliable, the nithka making rest impossible. They were scholars or mystics, drawn by the strangeness, attempting to study or worship or understand. But their tools are too practical, too basic, suggesting people focused on survival rather than inquiry.

Perhaps they came before the nithka, when the caves were newer, emptier, more navigable. The artifacts could be older than they appear—the cave environment might preserve rather than degrade. Perhaps they came after the nithka but in smaller numbers, when the population was manageable. Perhaps they came deliberately to harvest nithka, establishing the trade that surface settlements continue in diminished form, and these caves were outposts or processing stations.

The distribution of artifacts offers clues: They cluster loosely around the largest light shafts, the most navigable passages, the areas with reliable water. Sensible locations for camps. But they're also present in deep chambers, in tight passages, in places that seem deliberately difficult to reach. As if different groups came at different times with different purposes, or as if single groups fragmented, spread out, lost cohesion.

And there's the question of what happened to them. No bodies. No skeletons. Either they left, carrying their dead—possible, but the effort involved seems prohibitive. Or they died and the nithka consumed them, processed them into the ecosystem, leaving nothing identifiable—more likely, and somewhat horrifying, suggesting the nithka see no meaningful difference between human tissue and any other protein source. Or they died and their remains are present but unrecognizable, broken down to fragments, scattered to bones so old they're mineralized into the stone itself.

The delvers who come now are different—professionals, or at least practiced. They come with lights, weapons, mapping tools, plans. They come for specific purposes: harvesting nithka juveniles for the surface trade, where they're sold as curiosities or pets or food, depending on the culture. Gathering slime for its medicinal or alchemical properties—it's rumored to heal wounds, prevent infection, enhance vitality, though how much is truth and how much is merchant fantasy remains unclear. Exploring for its own sake, mapping the unmapped, claiming the prestige of surviving a night in the Nursery Caves.

They don't stay long. A day, two at most. They establish temporary camps in the less infested chambers, posting watches, sleeping in shifts, never relaxing. They wrap themselves in heavy canvas or leather to deter the investigating nithka, to create barriers between skin and probing palps. They bring preserved food—nothing fresh, nothing that will spoil in the warmth. They ration water, knowing the cave sources are contaminated or unreliable. And they leave as soon as their purposes are achieved or abandoned, emerging into surface sunlight with relief so visceral it's visible in their body language.

Some don't emerge. The caves claim a percentage, not through violence but through accident, exhaustion, disorientation. A delver gets separated from their group, can't find the way back, wanders deeper into unexplored galleries until thirst or hunger or the nithka's sheer overwhelming presence breaks them. A ceiling section that was solid on the way in collapses on the way out, blocking passages, forcing detours into unknown territory where navigation fails. A delver sleeps without setting adequate watch and the nithka pile atop them in the night, not attacking but suffocating through accumulated mass, and by the time the survivor wakes they're already half-buried, already hypoxic, already dying.

The survivors bring stories to the surface, and the stories are why new delvers come. Tales of chambers full of treasure—the copper and iron, surely there's more, surely veins of pure metal wait to be discovered. Tales of the nithka themselves, creatures so bizarre they must be worth something to the right collector, the right scholar, the right wizard. Tales of the slime performing miracles, curing diseases, extending life. Tales of deeper caves beneath these, of connections to the underworld, of things older and stranger than the nithka waiting in the unexplored dark.

Most stories are exaggerations or fabrications. But some truth exists in the core: the caves do extend deeper than anyone's fully mapped, the nithka are genuinely unprecedented in biology, the slime does possess properties worth investigating. Enough truth to keep the delvers coming, generation after generation, each believing they'll be the ones to unlock the caves' secrets, to survive where others failed, to emerge wealthy or famous or transformed.

## The Question of Origin

Where did the nithka come from? This question haunts everyone who spends time with them. They're too varied, too numerous, too well-established in an environment too young to have spawned them through normal evolutionary processes. Several hypotheses:

**Natural evolution in isolated conditions**: Perhaps the caves, though young geologically, are older than they appear. Perhaps the volcanic activity that formed them occurred centuries ago, giving the nithka time to evolve in isolation from surface species. The variety comes from rapid speciation in separated galleries, and the numbers from successful adaptation to cave resources. This is the conservative explanation, requiring no special mechanisms, just time miscalculation.

**Introduction from elsewhere**: Perhaps the nithka evolved in other caves, other regions, and were brought here—deliberately or accidentally—by some agent. Birds carrying eggs on their feet. Flooding from underground rivers. Human transport, intentional or not. They found the Nursery Caves ideal for their biology and exploded numerically, filling available niches, overwhelming any native species. This explains their success but raises the question of who or what brought them, and why.

**Magical or deliberate creation**: The traders whisper about wizards, experiments, breeding programs gone wrong. Perhaps someone tried to create the perfect cave guardian, or food animal, or biological weapon, and these are the results—escaped, proliferating, adapting beyond their creator's intentions. The variety represents different experimental lines, all released or escaped into the same environment, now interbreeding and hybridizing. This is the romantic explanation, impossible to disprove but also impossible to prove without finding the hypothetical creator.

**Accelerated natural process**: Perhaps something about the caves themselves speeds evolution. Radiation from the volcanic stone, or exotic minerals in the water, or magical resonance in the deep earth, mutating the nithka at accelerated rates, forcing rapid diversification. In a few generations they've achieved what normally requires millennia. This explains both the variety and the numbers while leaving mechanism mysterious.

The truth likely combines elements—natural evolution hastened by environmental factors, possibly with human interference at some point in the process. The artifacts suggest human presence contemporary with or predating the nithka population boom. Perhaps early explorers brought the first specimens as curiosities or food sources, not realizing they'd reproduce so prolifically in cave conditions. Perhaps the stone walls and platforms were early attempts to control the population, to create barriers and cultivation spaces, all of which failed.

What's certain is that the nithka are established now, deeply integrated into the cave ecology, unlikely to be dislodged by anything short of geological catastrophe or systematic extermination. And both options seem unlikely—the caves are too extensive for thorough extermination, and killing the nithka would collapse the ecosystem they support, leaving the caves biologically dead, which serves no one's interests.

So they persist, and the caves persist in their state of productive chaos, neither fully wild nor fully known, hosting life in overwhelming abundance while continuing their slow geological maturation toward some eventual settled form that may be centuries distant, that may never arrive, that exists only as potential in the stone's slow dreaming and the nithka's ceaseless motion through galleries still deciding what they will become.
