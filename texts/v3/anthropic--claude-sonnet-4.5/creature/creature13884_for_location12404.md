# The Inhabitants of the Forming Grounds

## The Pillar-Tenders

They are easy to miss at first, despite their size.

They stand perhaps four feet tall when upright, but they rarely are. Instead they drape themselves along the pillar-plants in postures that make them nearly indistinguishable from the vegetation itself. Their bodies are elongated, segmented loosely like a caterpillar's but with a fluidity that suggests something more amphibian, more vertebrate. The skin is not quite skin — it's a membrane that holds a constant sheen of moisture, dark green on the back surfaces, pale yellow-green on the ventral side, with intricate networks of darker veining visible beneath the translucent outer layer.

They have six limbs, arranged in pairs along the body. The foremost pair are the most specialized — three-fingered appendages that work with surprising delicacy, pinching, pruning, arranging the leaf-scales of the pillar-plants with what can only be called precision. The middle pair are stronger, used for climbing, for gripping, for holding position on vertical surfaces. The hind pair are broad and paddle-like, used to brace against the substrate or to propel them through the deeper pools when they descend to forage.

Their heads are small relative to their bodies, but the eyes are enormous — bulging compound structures that wrap partway around the skull, giving them nearly 360-degree vision in the dim light. Between the eyes, a cluster of thin appendages wave constantly, testing the air, the water content, the chemical composition of their immediate environment. The mouth is underneath, a soft-lipped opening that extends into a proboscis they can elongate to reach into crevices, to drink from the condensation beads, to feed on the particular fungal growths that appear at the junctions where pillar-plant scales overlap.

But what's most striking about them is their *attention*.

They don't move randomly. They don't browse indiscriminately like the armored creatures below. Instead they work their way methodically along a single pillar-plant over the course of what might be hours or days, tending it. They remove scales that have been damaged by the browsers. They adjust the angle of growing tips, gently bending them toward or away from the dim light in patterns that must make sense to them. They clean away the colonies of sessile filter-feeders when they grow too dense, carefully removing them and placing them on adjacent plants, redistributing them.

They garden.

Not in the human sense, not with intention or planning, but with a consistency of behavior that produces garden-like results. The pillar-plants they tend grow taller, more symmetrically. The leaf-scales align more perfectly. The water that drips from the treated plants falls in more regular rhythms.

They are solitary, mostly. You rarely see two on the same pillar-plant, though sometimes they pass each other on the ground-vine mat when moving between their chosen territories. When they do meet, they pause, their head-appendages touching briefly, waving in patterns that might be communication or might be simple chemical recognition. Then they separate, each returning to their work.

They make a sound. Not often, but occasionally — usually when they've found something wrong, some disruption to their plant's growth pattern. A soft whistle, ascending in pitch, that carries surprising distance through the dense air. You hear it sometimes from far off, and later you'll pass a pillar-plant and find one of them there, working intently on some repair, some correction to the plant's architecture that was invisible to you but crucial to them.

They sleep draped along the upper reaches of their pillar-plants, their bodies conforming to the curve of the stem, perfectly still except for the rhythmic expansion and contraction of the breathing spiracles along their sides. Even in sleep, their head-appendages continue to wave slowly, monitoring their environment, ready to wake if anything threatens their chosen plant.

They are born in the pools — you find their larvae sometimes, translucent tadpole-like creatures with oversized heads already bearing the characteristic compound eyes. The larvae feed on the dense microorganism soup until they metamorphose, climbing out onto the mat as miniature versions of the adults. They seem to select their pillar-plant early, establishing a relationship that may last their entire lives. How long that is, you can't determine. They show no signs of aging in any timeframe you can observe.

There's something monk-like about them. Something devoted. They have found their purpose in tending these plants, and they pursue it with absolute consistency, neither rushed nor leisurely, simply constant. They are part of the equilibrium, perhaps one of its essential maintenance mechanisms. Without them, would the pillar-plants still grow so uniformly? Would the structure of the forest remain so stable?

You cannot interact with them in any meaningful way. They acknowledge your presence — their eyes track you as you pass — but they don't respond beyond that. Touch one and it simply waits, patient, until you remove your hand, then continues its work. They have no fear because they have no concept of threat. Nothing here threatens them. They exist in their niche completely, perfectly, with no need to develop behaviors for situations outside that niche.

Watching them work, you begin to understand something about this place. It's not just passively stable. It's being actively maintained, tended, by species that have evolved to serve that function. The pillar-tenders are the gardeners of the equilibrium, one of perhaps many species whose behavioral patterns keep the system locked in its eternal present.

## The Swimmers-Who-Will-Crawl

In the pools, among the masses of transparent fish-things, there is another form.

Larger, chunkier, less gracefully aquatic. They are six inches long, perhaps eight, with bodies that are thick through the middle and taper toward both ends. Four limbs project from the body — but they're not quite fins, not quite legs. Something intermediate, with fleshy lobes supported by visible bone structures, capable of paddling in water but also of bearing weight, of pushing against solid surfaces.

Their heads are broad and flat, with eyes positioned on top, giving them an upward gaze. The mouth is wide, lined with tiny teeth not for tearing but for scraping, for processing the soft substrate and the algae that grows on every surface. They have external gills — feathery red structures on either side of the head — but also something else: a pair of spiracles on top of the head that open and close rhythmically, testing the air above the water, preparing for a future that may or may not come.

They spend most of their time on the bottom of the pools, scooping up mouthfuls of substrate, filtering out the nutritive material, expelling the waste in clouds that the transparent fish immediately swarm. But several times in a span of time you could measure in hours, they do something remarkable.

They haul themselves out of the water.

Not far. Just onto the ground-vine mat at the pool's edge, where they lie for a time, breathing air through those spiracles, their gills gasping in the moisture-saturated atmosphere but not desiccating, not dying. They test the land. They practice being terrestrial.

After a few minutes — sometimes longer, sometimes shorter — they flip and thrash their way back into the water, where they resume their bottom-feeding. But each time, they venture slightly farther. Each time, they stay out slightly longer. You can mark individuals by the subtle patterns on their backs and watch them over a period that might be days, and see them becoming gradually more confident, more capable, in the thin margin between water and land.

This is evolution happening at observable speed. Or rather, this is a species that has retained the plasticity of development, the ability to transform in response to opportunity. They are not fully aquatic and not yet fully terrestrial. They are *becoming*, caught in the act of transition, generation after generation practicing the move from water to land.

But here's the strange thing: they never complete it.

You find adults of this species all around the pools, at every stage of the transition. Some that barely leave the water. Some that spend half their time on land, breathing air, even venturing several meters from the nearest pool. But none that abandon water completely. The transition goes only so far and then stops, as if there's no pressure to complete it, no advantage to full terrestriality in a place where water is everywhere, where the air itself is nearly aqueous.

They exist in permanent liminality, permanent metamorphosis. They are amphibians that never quite amphibiate.

They feed on land as they do in water, scraping the ground-vine mat, processing the proto-moss and filament-algae. They're clumsy — their lobe-fins weren't designed for walking, and their locomotion is an ungainly push-and-pull that's exhausting to watch. But they persist, driven by some imperative to expand their range, to exploit resources in both realms.

They congregate. Not in the organized way the pillar-tenders work, but in loose associations near productive pools. A dozen or more might occupy the same pool and its margins, their territories overlapping without apparent conflict. They vocalize — a grunting croak that resonates in the thick air — but whether it's territorial, reproductive, or simply the mechanical sound of air moving through their developing respiratory system, you can't tell.

Their eggs — you find them in the shallower pools, in areas where the water is particularly still — are laid in masses, hundreds of them, each protected by a thick jelly coating. The larvae are entirely aquatic, legless, spending weeks or months in pure tadpole form before the limbs begin to develop. The development happens in stages, each individual following the same sequence: hind limbs first, then fore limbs, then the gradual absorption of the tail, the development of the air-breathing structures. But the process is slow, indeterminate, as if there's no urgency to complete it.

Some individuals seem to arrest at intermediate stages, remaining tadpole-like far longer than others, perhaps indefinitely. Neoteny as a strategy, remaining in the juvenile form if conditions favor it. The population shows every possible variation of the water-to-land transition existing simultaneously.

They are not intelligent in any sense you can measure, but they're exploratory. They investigate new areas, test new food sources, attempt new behaviors. One individual discovers that the cushion growths on the substrate mounds are edible and suddenly a dozen others are scraping away at them. Another finds a channel between pools and soon it becomes a regular highway, marked by the trails they leave in the soft substrate.

They represent something essential about this place: the process of becoming as a permanent state. They are evolution paused mid-stride, adaptation frozen at the moment of transition. In a normal environment, pressures would push them fully onto land or pull them back into water. Here, those pressures are absent or perfectly balanced, and so they remain forever in-between, practicing a transition they'll never complete.

Watching them haul themselves onto the mat, gasping and determined, you see the history of terrestrial life compressed into a few minutes. And then watching them slip back into water, you see that history rewound. They are living fossils of a moment that never ends, ambassadors from a threshold they guard but never cross.

## The Weavers-of-Air

You don't see them at first. You see their work.

Between the pillar-plants, at mid-height, there are structures. Not the hanging chain-pods — those are botanical. These are *constructed*. Networks of fine fiber forming three-dimensional meshes, each mesh roughly spherical, each about the size of a basketball, suspended in the spaces where plant growth is less dense.

The fiber catches the moisture, becomes visible when the dim light hits it right. Each strand is almost invisible individually, but collectively they create volumes, architectures, spaces defined by their weaving. The structures are complex — not just simple webs but multi-layered constructions with passages and chambers, with different densities of weaving in different zones, with openings that face specific directions.

And inside them: the weavers.

They are small, perhaps an inch long, with narrow bodies and eight legs of disproportionate length — four times the body length, thin as threads themselves. They're not spiders, not quite, though the resemblance is strong. The body segments are more distinct, more crustacean-like. The legs have more joints, more flexibility. The head bears a cluster of simple eyes and a pair of manipulator appendages that work constantly, handling the fiber they produce from spinnerets at the rear of their body.

But what's remarkable is that they don't work alone.

Each of those spherical structures houses a colony — dozens of individuals, sometimes hundreds, all cooperating in its construction and maintenance. They move across the surface and through the interior of their structure, adding strands, reinforcing weak points, repairing damage. They seem to communicate through vibration, plucking the strands in patterns that ripple through the whole structure, causing other individuals to respond, to move to specific locations, to perform specific tasks.

The structures serve multiple functions. They're homes, obviously — the weavers rest inside them, clustered in the central chambers during what passes for their dormant periods. But they're also traps. The outer layers catch the flying things, the small ones, which become entangled in the sticky fiber. The weavers swarm these captures immediately, not to eat them directly but to wrap them in more fiber, preserving them, creating a larder. The stored prey is then consumed gradually, the colony feeding collectively from its accumulated reserves.

But there's more. The structures affect airflow in ways that are subtle but measurable. They channel the movements of air, the convection currents driven by temperature differentials too small to feel, creating micro-circulation patterns in their immediate vicinity. This brings more flying things into contact with the structures, improves the rate of capture. The weavers have engineered their environment at a scale appropriate to their size, creating local conditions that favor their survival.

Different colonies have different architectural styles. Some build tight, dense structures with many small chambers. Others create looser weaves with fewer, larger spaces. Some incorporate plant material into their constructions, using the leaf-scales and hanging pods as anchors or structural supports. Others are purely fiber-based, suspended from just a few attachment points.

The colonies reproduce by fission. A structure reaches some critical size and complexity, and then a portion of the colony — perhaps a third — leaves, taking with them a section of the structure they detach and transport. They carry it together, each individual holding a strand, the whole group moving in coordination through the air-space between pillar-plants. When they find a suitable location, they anchor their fragment and begin expanding it, building out from that seed until they have a full-sized structure of their own.

The original colony repairs the damage, fills in the missing section, continues. Both colonies are viable, neither is primary or secondary. The population expands not through individual reproduction — though that must happen too, maintaining numbers within each colony — but through this colony-level budding.

They have predators. Some of the larger flying things deliberately attack the structures, tearing through the weaving to get at the weavers inside. You see damaged structures sometimes, sections ripped away. But the colonies respond quickly, the surviving members repairing the breach within hours, sometimes relocating entirely if the damage is too severe.

There are hundreds of these structures visible from any given vantage point, and presumably thousands or millions throughout the forming grounds. They're part of the mid-story ecosystem, occupying the space between the ground-level crawlers and the canopy-level climbers. They harvest the flying things, convert them to fiber and stored protein, and in doing so regulate the populations of those fliers, preventing them from becoming even more overwhelming than they already are.

The weavers themselves serve as food for others. The transparent fish-things, when they make their ventings onto the ground-vine mat, sometimes thrash far enough to bring down the lower structures, then feed on the scattered colonies. Some of the climbing species deliberately seek out the structures, tear them open, consume the weavers in quantity. The equilibrium again: weavers harvest fliers, climbers harvest weavers, fish-things harvest climbers, the substrate recycles fish-things, and the system circles endlessly.

But watching a colony at work, seeing the coordination, the division of labor, the complex social behaviors, you realize these are among the more sophisticated organisms here. They have solved problems of cooperation, of communication, of architecture. They exist not as individuals but as superorganisms, each colony a single distributed intelligence spread across dozens of bodies.

At night — if there is night — you hear them. The structures sing very softly, the fibers vibrating in response to the constant motion within and around them, creating harmonics that are just at the edge of hearing. Each structure has its own pitch, its own timbre, determined by its size and the tension of its fibers. Together, the hundreds of structures in any given area create a barely audible chorus, a background drone even more fundamental than the wing-hum of the flying things.

They are weaving the air itself into something semi-solid, creating architecture from breath and moisture. They are making the space between plants into a place that can be inhabited, exploited, defended. They represent complexity in an environment that might otherwise remain simple: adaptation to a niche that didn't exist until they created it.

## The Substrate Engines

These you hear before you understand what you're hearing.

A rhythmic sound, slow and deep, like breath or like a heartbeat, rising from below. It's everywhere and nowhere, part of the baseline noise of the forming grounds, easily mistaken for some geological process, some settling of the soft earth.

But it's not geological. It's biological.

Beneath the ground-vine mat, in the substrate itself, they live. You glimpse them only occasionally, when one surfaces — and even then you see just a portion, because they're large, far larger than anything else here.

They are worm-like but segmented in a way that suggests something more complex than simple annelids. Each segment is armored with a chitinous plate, and the segments slide over and under each other as they move, creating a wave-like motion that propels them through the soft substrate. They're five feet thick at their widest, and their length is impossible to determine — ten feet? Twenty? The portions that surface never reveal the whole animal.

The front end — if it is the front — bears a structure like a combination of mouth and digging apparatus: a ringed opening lined with grinding teeth or perhaps calcium deposits that scrape and process substrate as they advance. The segments behind the mouth are perforated with spiracles that draw in water and air, extracting oxygen from either, functioning in the saturated substrate the way gills and lungs function in more differentiated species.

They feed constantly, processing cubic meters of substrate per day, extracting the organic material, the bacterial colonies, the decomposing matter that would otherwise accumulate. What they excrete is different from what they consumed — refined, more processed, the minerals redistributed, the organic molecules broken down to simpler forms. They are digestive systems that happen to be mobile, that tunnel through the very ground, eating and excreting, transforming the substrate from the inside.

The rhythmic sound is their peristalsis, the wave of muscular contraction that moves along their segmented bodies as they propel themselves through the earth. Each individual produces its own rhythm, and these combine into a complex polyrhythm that forms the deepest layer of the soundscape. It's felt more than heard, a vibration transmitted through the substrate to everything touching it, to your feet, your hands, your body when you lie down.

They create the tunnels, the channels through which water flows beneath the surface. Not intentionally — they're not engineers — but as a consequence of their movement. The tunnels collapse behind them slowly, but new ones form constantly, ensuring that the substrate remains permeable, that water can circulate, that the whole system doesn't compact into impermeability.

They surface to excrete sometimes, creating the low mounds visible on the ground-vine mat, those dome-shaped structures the browsers avoid. The excretion is mineral-rich, slightly toxic, and hardens quickly into those more solid formations. The cushion growths colonize these mounds immediately, specialized to tolerate the chemistry, creating a micro-ecosystem on each one.

You never see them interact. Whether they're solitary by nature or simply occupy non-overlapping territories, their movement through the substrate seems coordinated to avoid collision. They're navigating by some sense you can't identify — chemical trails in the substrate, perhaps, or pressure-wave detection, or something else entirely.

They have no eyes, no visible sensory organs except for subtle chemical receptors scattered across the anterior segments. They exist in permanent darkness, in the substrate that is their entire world, surfacing only for those brief excretions before submerging again. How they find food, how they navigate, how they know where they are in the vastness of the forming grounds, remains opaque.

But they're crucial. Perhaps more crucial than any other organism here. They're preventing the substrate from becoming compacted, dead, anaerobic. They're cycling nutrients at the foundational level, making minerals available to the plants, processing organic waste before it accumulates. They're the digestive system of the entire landscape, the process by which this place feeds on itself and remains fertile.

There might be dozens of them, might be thousands. Their individual sizes vary — you can tell by the rhythm, by the different pitches and tempos of their peristalsis. The smaller ones, perhaps juveniles, have faster rhythms, higher frequencies. The larger ones are slower, deeper, their movements taking several seconds per wave.

They must reproduce, but how? You never find eggs, never see larval stages. Perhaps they reproduce by fission, like the weaver colonies, each individual eventually dividing into two smaller ones that then grow. Perhaps they produce eggs that develop entirely within the substrate, hatching directly into juvenile forms that begin tunneling immediately. Perhaps they're effectively immortal, growing continuously, and the smaller individuals are recent fissions while the larger ones have been here since the forming grounds first formed.

The mystery of them is complete. They're too large to observe whole, too hidden to study, too alien in their sensory world to understand. You know them only by their effects: the mounds, the tunnels, the vibration, the constant processing of substrate that keeps the system viable.

They are the engines. The great recyclers. The mechanism by which the equilibrium maintains itself at the most fundamental level. Without them, the forming grounds would suffocate in its own waste, would compact into sterility, would cease to be the thriving super-saturated system it is.

They ask nothing, provide everything, and go entirely unnoticed except as vibration, as sound, as mysterious mounds appearing in the night.

## The Witnesses

And then there are these.

They are rare. You might spend days without seeing one. But when you do, the encounter is unmistakable.

They're bipedal, which is unusual here. Most things crawl, swim, or climb with multiple limbs. These stand upright, three feet tall, their bodies balanced over two legs that are muscular, digitigrade, ending in feet with four toes that spread wide for stability on the soft substrate.

The body is compact, the chest deep, housing what must be an efficient respiratory system for this thick air. Two arms, shorter than the legs, with hands that have five fingers — three opposing two, allowing for precision grip. The skin is smooth, almost amphibian, but thicker, more durable, mottled in patterns of grey-green and darker brown that blend with the vegetation.

But the head.

The head is larger than it needs to be for the body size, and it's dominated by eyes. Not compound like the pillar-tenders, but forward-facing, large, with visible pupils that dilate wide in the dimness. The eyes show focus, tracking, the ability to resolve detail at distance. And in those eyes, when you meet their gaze, there is something that makes you stop.

Recognition. Not of you specifically, but recognition as a concept. Awareness of being seen. They know you're there not just as a presence, a disturbance in their environment, but as an entity, a consciousness, something like themselves.

They don't flee when you approach, but they don't stay either. They watch. They maintain distance. They're cautious without being fearful, interested without being bold. They observe.

They carry things sometimes. Sections of plant material, pieces of the substrate, specimens of the smaller organisms. They hold these in their hands, examining them, turning them over, exploring their properties. Sometimes they set them down carefully in specific locations, arrangements that might be meaningful, might be random. Sometimes they discard them.

You never see them eat, at least not in the way other organisms here eat. They investigate food sources — the fruiting bodies, the algae mats, the occasional carcass when one appears — but their interaction is exploratory rather than consumptive. Perhaps they feed rarely, efficiently. Perhaps they've evolved to need minimal nutrition. Perhaps they eat only when unobserved.

They manipulate their environment but don't change it. They might break a leaf, test its flexibility, then move on. They might dig briefly in the substrate, examine what they find, then fill the hole. They interact with other organisms — placing a hand near the swimmers-who-will-crawl, letting the weavers investigate their fingers — but they don't hunt, don't compete for resources, don't establish territories.

They seem to exist outside the equilibrium's resource chains. They're not producing anything the system needs. They're not consuming in amounts that matter. They're not regulating populations or processing nutrients. They're simply... present. Observing. Bearing witness.

They vocalize rarely, but when they do, it's complex. Multi-tonal, modulated, clearly capable of carrying information. Sometimes two of them will encounter each other, and they'll stand at distance, vocalizing back and forth in patterns that sound like conversation, though what they might be communicating you can't begin to guess. Then they separate, each continuing their individual explorations.

They're intelligent. Not human-intelligent, perhaps, but intelligent in their own right. They're capable of curiosity, of systematic investigation, of memory — you see the same individual return to locations they visited days before, checking on things they'd arranged, noting changes. They're building some kind of understanding of this place, though to what purpose is impossible to say.

They have no tools beyond what their hands provide, no constructions, no visible culture. But the hands themselves are tools of remarkable capability, and they use them constantly — feeling textures, testing resistance, exploring the properties of materials. They're tactile learners in a place that demands touch to understand.

Sometimes you find marks they've left. Not writing, not symbols, but scratches in the substrate, arrangements of stones or plant material, configurations that don't occur naturally. Whether these are communication, memory aids, art, or something else entirely, you can't determine. They persist for a while, then are absorbed back into the general flux of the environment.

They sleep in the pillar-plants, climbing to mid-height and wedging themselves into secure positions, their bodies relaxed but their eyes opening periodically to scan their surroundings. They're vulnerable then, but nothing seems to threaten them. Perhaps they're too large for most predators, too unusual to be recognized as prey. Perhaps they taste wrong, have defensive chemicals. Perhaps they're simply left alone by some instinct in other species that recognizes them as somehow separate, other, not part of the regular food web.

Their young — you see them occasionally, smaller versions clinging to the backs of adults — are born live, not hatched. The adults carry them for what might be months, the young observing everything their carrier observes, learning through extended apprenticeship. Eventually the young begin to explore independently, staying close to the adult at first, then gradually increasing their range until they're autonomous.

The population is small. You might see three, four individuals in a day's walk, might go days without seeing any. They're sparse enough that each individual likely has a territory measured in square miles, though they don't defend these territories, and their ranges must overlap. Perhaps the population is limited by some resource you can't identify, or perhaps this is simply the density at which their social structure functions best.

They're beautiful in their way. There's a grace to their movement, a deliberateness to their actions that stands out against the frenetic activity of most other species here. They move like they have time, like they're not driven by hunger or fear or reproductive urgency. They move like creatures who have solved the basic problems of survival and now occupy themselves with other concerns.

What those concerns are, you can only speculate. Understanding? Mapping? Some intellectual or aesthetic pursuit that operates by rules you can't access? They witness the forming grounds with an attention that suggests it matters to them in ways beyond simple survival.

When one looks at you — and they will, they'll approach close enough to get a clear view, to meet your eyes directly — you feel assessed. Not threatened, not welcomed, just... seen. Acknowledged. Recorded in whatever memory they maintain, whatever understanding they're building of this place and everything in it.

Then they move on, returning to their explorations, their witnessing, their mysterious purposes.

They are proof that intelligence can evolve in the forming grounds, can find a niche in the equilibrium. But it's an intelligence that has remained small, rare, careful not to disrupt the balance. They take up almost no space in the resource budget, make almost no impact on the other species, exist in the gaps between the major processes.

They are observers in a place that doesn't require observation, witnesses to an equilibrium that would continue perfectly well without them. And yet they persist, driven by whatever imperatives guide a species whose purpose is to understand rather than to consume, to know rather than to exploit.

You wonder what they make of you, this even rarer visitor, this temporarily perturbation in their permanent present. Whether you're just another specimen to observe, or whether your presence suggests to them what their presence suggests to you: that consciousness exists, rare and strange, in the margins of this endlessly cycling world.

## The Equilibrium Itself

But perhaps the truest inhabitant of the forming grounds is not a species at all.

It's the pattern. The system. The equilibrium that holds everything in suspension, in balance, in this eternal moment between formation and development.

You begin to perceive it as an entity in itself — not conscious, not intentional, but present nonetheless. A meta-organism composed of all the individual organisms, all the cycles and flows and feedbacks, all the relationships between species and substrate and water and light.

It has properties, this equilibrium. It's resilient — push against it anywhere and it absorbs the push, redistributes the force, returns to its configuration. It's precise — the temperature, the humidity, the populations, the light levels all maintained within narrow margins. It's productive — the sheer biomass generated and maintained is staggering, requiring constant energy input and efficient recycling to sustain.

And it has something like desire, though that's not quite the right word. It wants to persist. Every species here is adapted not just to survive but to maintain the balance. The pillar-tenders maintaining the plants, the substrate engines processing the waste, the weavers regulating the fliers, the swimmers practicing their eternal transition — each is both beneficiary and maintenance crew for the equilibrium.

Any species that disrupted the balance too severely would destabilize its own habitat, would cause cascades that would ultimately harm it. So selection favors those variations that fit within the pattern, that contribute to its perpetuation. The equilibrium has shaped every organism here into a component of its own maintenance.

This is the thing you're walking through. Not land, not ecosystem, but a self-perpetuating process that has learned how to be stable, how to resist change, how to exist in perfect circulation forever.

It will be here long after you leave. It will likely be here in exactly this configuration, with these same species performing these same functions, for longer than you can imagine. It has found a way to be that requires no development, no progress, no future. Only endless present, endlessly maintained.

This is what lives in the forming grounds: a moment, extended indefinitely, inhabited by forms that exist to keep it from ever becoming anything else.
