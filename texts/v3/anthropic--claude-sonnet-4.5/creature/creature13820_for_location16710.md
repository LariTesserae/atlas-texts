# The Inhabitants

What lives here exists in the spaces between presence and absence, between inhabitant and infrastructure.

## The Moss Choir

They are not a they, not properly. But the moss that forms the structural foundation of this place has achieved something beyond individual existence. In the deepest, most ancient accumulations—those two-foot-thick vertical meadows, those compressed peat-like masses—the moss has become less a collection of organisms and more a single distributed entity with distributed awareness. Not consciousness. Never that. But something like memory encoded in growth patterns, something like intention expressed through chemical gradients.

The oldest sections, where moss has built upon moss death for centuries, maintain corridors through their mass—not hollow spaces but zones of different density that allow moisture, nutrients, and chemical signals to move in preferential paths. These paths shift over seasons, closing here, opening there, creating a slow circulatory system through the vegetative walls. The moss responds to minute changes: a shift in humidity, a change in the mineral content of water trickling down from above, the presence of certain insects or their absence. These responses propagate through the mass at the speed of diffusion, one section's adjustment influencing its neighbors over the course of days or weeks.

In the deepest layers, where light never penetrates and moss lives off the nutrients released by its own decay above, there are structures that might be fossils or might still be alive—compressed forms that hold the shape of things that fell against the walls decades ago and were slowly incorporated. A bird's skeleton, entirely sleeved in moss filaments. The leather remnants of what might have been work gloves. The moss grows into these things, through them, around them, preserving their forms while slowly extracting whatever nutrition they offer.

The moss knows—though knowing isn't the right word—the architecture of moisture in this place. It knows which beams will drip and when, which corners will stay damp through dry periods, where condensation will form as temperature changes. It has shaped itself to these patterns, grown thicker where water is reliable, thinner where conditions challenge survival. The result is a kind of map: the moss's varying thickness traces the hydrology of the space as surely as contour lines trace elevation.

## The Traverse Keepers

They are rats, but rats that have been here so long they've become something more specific to this place. Smaller than their city cousins who live in sewers and subways, with fur that's gone a peculiar gray-brown that matches the moss almost perfectly. Their paws have adapted to surfaces of compressed vegetation—the pads are wider, the claws slightly longer, shaped for gripping plant matter rather than concrete or metal.

There are perhaps two dozen of them in this chamber, distributed through the hollow building-cores and the spaces between walls and floors. They maintain the paths—not consciously maintaining, but their repeated passage keeps certain routes compressed, certain doorways clear. They move through the space with intimate knowledge, taking the same routes generation after generation, so that what began as expedient passage has become tradition, and tradition has become groove, literal grooves worn into the moss by countless tiny feet.

They nest in the drier spaces—inside the old control panels, in gaps behind the colonized radiators, in the upper reaches of the metal staircases where the steps meet the walls. Their nests are constructed from what's available: shredded moss, the papery bark from certain vines, threads pulled from ancient fabric caught in machinery, fibers from their own shed fur. These nests are maintained indefinitely, repaired rather than rebuilt, becoming over time nearly as structural as the moss itself—compressed, layered, with the oldest material at the bottom turned to something almost like felt.

They eat the sparse animal life—insects mostly, the occasional moth or beetle—and supplement this with the fungal bodies that fruit in the darkest, dampest sections of the building-cores. There are mushrooms here that grow nowhere else, adapted to this specific substrate of moss-decay and rust, and the rats know where each type appears, when it's safe to eat. They cache nothing. In this place of steady cool and reliable moisture, food doesn't keep well, but neither does it ever truly disappear. Something is always available.

The rats communicate through the structure itself. They've learned which metal pipes carry sound best, which hollow spaces amplify their vocalizations. A rat on the ground floor can send a message—a specific pattern of scratches and squeaks against a particular pipe—that will reach individuals six stories up. These communications aren't frequent. The rats mostly operate independently. But when something changes—when a new section of wall collapses, when water begins flowing in a new pattern—the information propagates through the population within hours, each individual incorporating the update into their movement patterns.

They have territories, but these overlap and shift. A given rat might consider three different nesting sites theirs depending on season or mood, and might share feeding areas with half a dozen others without conflict. The population stays stable not through aggression but through something more like collective agreement—when resources tighten, birth rates drop; when conditions improve, more young survive. They are woven into this place's carrying capacity, regulated by the same forces that regulate moss growth and water flow.

The rats occasionally die in visible places, but never for long. The moss doesn't exactly consume the body, but moisture and decomposers make short work of soft tissue, and what remains—bones, teeth—are quickly grown over. Within a season, a rat's skeleton becomes a slight bump in a path, a textured patch on a wall. Within a year, it's indistinguishable from the substrate. They leave no permanent mark of individual existence, but their collective presence is written in every maintained pathway, every clear threshold.

## The Lichen Dialects

On every surface the moss hasn't completely claimed—and even on some it has—lichen grows in patterns complex enough to suggest language. Different species occupy different ecological positions, different chemical environments, different relationships with moisture and light. On the ceramic insulators in the transformer building, seven distinct lichen species have established what appears to be a single compound organism, each species occupying its optimal zone on the porcelain surface, together creating a complete coverage that allows no space for competitors.

The lichens grow slowly enough that they might as well be permanent features, yet they do grow, advancing millimeters per year, competing at the boundaries where different species meet. These boundary zones show evidence of chemical warfare conducted at the speed of diffusion—one species releases compounds that inhibit its neighbor, which responds with its own defensive chemistry, which prompts adaptation, which leads to detente or victory or years-long stalemate. The result is boundaries that are sometimes sharp as cut lines, sometimes graduating over a centimeter of mixing, sometimes forming complex interdigitated patterns like fingers laced together.

Certain lichen colonies on exposed metal beams have been there long enough that the oxidation patterns beneath them differ from adjacent areas. The beam's rust has been shaped by the lichen's chemistry, creating subtle variations in color and texture that will persist even if the lichen dies. The lichen, in turn, has adapted to the specific mineral content of this particular iron alloy, this specific rust chemistry. Remove it and place it elsewhere, and it would struggle. It has become specific to this beam, this place.

The lichens are the longest-lived things here. While moss grows and dies and regrows in measurable cycles, while rats live their few years, while even the structural plants turn over their leaves and sometimes die back and regenerate, the lichens simply persist. The oldest colonies—great circular growths on brick faces that have remained perpetually dry enough for their survival—might be centuries old, pre-dating the garden phase of this place, having colonized the buildings while they were still merely abandoned, not yet composted.

They respond to air quality with such sensitivity that their patterns map invisible gradients. Where certain pollutants accumulate—residues from the industrial past, slowly leaching from old equipment—some lichen species cannot survive, while others thrive. The result is a readable map for anyone who knows the language: this patch of orange crust means heavy metal concentration in the substrate, that area of gray-green foliose growth indicates particularly pure moisture, those crustose species barely distinguishable from the rock itself mark the driest zones.

## The Intermittent Visitors

The birds that pass through—they are visitors, not residents, but such regular visitors that they might as well be counted among the inhabitants. Thrushes mostly, and sparrows of a type that nests in cavities, and occasionally something larger—a crow that perches on upper beams and watches the space below with interest that seems taxonomic, as if cataloging.

They come for the insects and the water and the shelter, but they don't stay. No bird nests here in the central chamber, though you can find evidence of nesting in some of the more enclosed building-cores, tucked into gaps between moss-covered machinery and walls. The birds that visit are transients, stopping during migrations or daily rounds, resting on beams that must seem to them oddly soft, drinking from the saturated zones, occasionally catching an insect or picking at the fungal bodies that fruit in season.

Their songs sound different here than outside—the acoustics of moss and moisture and enclosed space soften every note, remove the sharp edges, extend the decay. A thrush's song becomes something more liquid, less structured. The birds seem aware of this; they often pause after singing, as if listening to how the space has transformed their sound, then adjust their next phrase. Not consciously, perhaps, but birds are creatures of pattern and repetition, and patterns here require different emphasis.

When they land, their small weight compresses the moss on beams and ledges, leaving temporary impressions that slowly reinflate over hours after they've gone. These compression marks are the most ephemeral evidence of passage—they exist just long enough to overlap if another bird lands in the same spot soon after, creating a momentary density map of avian preference, gone by evening.

## The Fungal Negotiators

Beneath everything visible, in the dark accumulated layers of moss-death and moisture, the fungal network does its essential work. Mycelial threads extend through every substrate, connecting moss to decaying wood to rust to brick to more moss. The fungi break down what needs breaking, transfer nutrients from where they're abundant to where they're needed, create the chemical conditions that allow certain plants to thrive while excluding others.

Some of the fungi fruit when conditions align—after periods of sustained moisture, when temperature stays in a particular range. The fruiting bodies appear suddenly: small brown mushrooms no larger than a fingertip, emerging from cracks in moss or wood or from the leaf litter equivalent that accumulates in corners. They last only days before collapsing back into spore-bearing puddles that the moss absorbs. But the mycelial networks that produced them are permanent, or as permanent as anything here, persisting year after year, growing incrementally, connecting with other networks, sometimes competing, sometimes merging into super-organisms that span multiple buildings.

The fungi determine what can grow where through their relationship with plant roots. They are selective partners, providing certain plants with enhanced access to nutrients and water while denying others. This partnership shapes the visible landscape—why certain ferns thrive in particular spots while struggling mere meters away, why some vines can colonize certain beams but not others. The fungi are invisible architects, working in chemical gradients and symbiotic arrangements, their decisions playing out in the patterns of green growth above.

Some of the fungi digest metal, or have learned to. They concentrate on the rust, breaking down iron oxide into forms plants can use, slowly converting the industrial substrate into nutrition. The rate is imperceptible—a beam might lose a micrometer of thickness per year—but it's happening, continuous, unstoppable. Eventually, perhaps centuries from now, certain beams will have been entirely consumed, converted from iron to rust to fungal biomass to plant nutrition to moss to decay to fungus again, completing a cycle that incorporates metal into the living system.

## The Bacterial Weather

In the saturated zones, in the water film that covers every surface, in the droplets that hang from beams and accumulate in moss pockets, bacteria live in numbers that make them the place's true dominant inhabitants by sheer population. They are uncountable, unimaginable in their quantity, and they do the fundamental work: fixing nitrogen from the mist, breaking down the complex organic molecules of decay into simpler forms plants can use, processing toxins and heavy metals, maintaining the chemical balance that allows everything else to survive.

They exist in consortia—hundreds of species working in metabolic chains where one species' waste product is another's food source, creating cycles of transformation that run continuously. These bacterial communities differ by location: the populations in standing water differ from those in moss, which differ from those colonizing rust, which differ from those in the vertical cores of the buildings. Each microenvironment hosts its specialized community, adapted to that particular combination of moisture, nutrients, pH, and chemical challenges.

The bacteria are the place's true memory. While moss holds a kind of structural memory, while rats pass behavioral memory through generations, the bacteria hold chemical memory. They are constantly evolving, adapting to the specific conditions of this place, becoming increasingly specialized and increasingly efficient at the transformations this environment requires. They can break down pollutants specific to this industrial site because they've had generations to evolve the metabolic pathways. They can tolerate the specific heavy metals present in this particular collection of machinery because selection has preserved the individuals with appropriate resistance.

When conditions change—when a new source of water opens up, when a section of building collapses and creates new chemistry—bacterial populations shift with remarkable speed. Within days, new species come to dominance, having been present in low numbers but ready to exploit new opportunities. The system self-regulates through bacterial competition and cooperation, finding new equilibria as circumstances demand.

## The Insect Specialists

The insect life is sparse but essential. There are springtails in the moss, countless tiny arthropods that graze on the fungi and bacteria, keeping populations in check, recycling nutrients at the smallest scales. They are so small and so numerous that they form a nearly invisible living layer, present everywhere but noticed nowhere.

The moths that rest on brick faces in perfect camouflage—they are here as adults for feeding and reproduction, but their larvae live in the moss, eating the accumulated detritus, helping to break down the layers that would otherwise compact too completely. Different moth species specialize in different layers: some larvae graze the living moss surface, others tunnel into the dead zones, others target the woody vines where they climb the walls.

There are beetles that live in the drier spaces, behind loose bark on old wooden beams that still exist in some building cores. They are small, dark, adapted to low food availability. They eat fungal spores, dead insects, anything organic they can find. Their populations are small—perhaps a few hundred in the entire chamber—but stable, held at carrying capacity by the limited resources available.

Cave crickets, pale and long-legged, live in the darkest building cores, emerging at night to scavenge. They have elongated antennae that sweep the darkness, finding food by touch and chemical sense. They are nearly blind—light here is too sparse and indirect to make vision worth maintaining. Instead, they've invested in those antennae, in sensitive leg hairs that detect the slightest vibration, in chemical receptors that can locate a dead springtail from meters away.

The insects are the place's circulatory system, moving nutrients from where they accumulate to where they're needed, carrying pollen from the rare flowers, dispersing fungal spores, breaking down the dead, feeding the living. Without them, the system would stagnate, would collapse under its own accumulated waste. With them, everything flows, slowly but continuously, maintaining the dynamic equilibrium that appears—to human observation—as stable, unchanging quiet.

## The Watchers

And there is something else. Something harder to define, harder even to be certain of. In the deepest building-cores, where light penetrates only as a dim suggestion, where the moss grows thickest and water drips constantly, there are spaces that feel watched. Not by rats or birds—you learn quickly to recognize when those creatures observe you. This is something different.

Perhaps it's the accumulated result of all the living systems here, some emergent property of a place where every surface hosts life, where chemical signals pass through moss and water and air, where the same processes have run for so long that the place itself has achieved a kind of meta-stability, a system-wide awareness that isn't consciousness but isn't quite separate from it either.

Or perhaps it's simpler: in a place this quiet, this enclosed, this saturated with moisture and life, your own awareness changes. You become more sensitive. You notice things you'd normally miss—the way light shifts, the pattern of drips, the small sounds of settling and growth. This heightened sensitivity creates the feeling of being observed, when really you're just observing more carefully, and the feedback loop between you and the space creates a reciprocal quality, a sense of exchange.

But there are moments—entering a particularly deep building-core, or standing in the central space as mist settles and light fades—when the feeling becomes specific enough to have location, to seem to come from somewhere. From the oldest moss masses, perhaps, those compressed centuries of growth. From the fungal networks that connect everything to everything else. From the collective result of all the small lives here, their aggregate existence creating something that's more than the sum of individual organisms.

It's never threatening, this watched feeling. If anything, it's assessing. The space is taking your measure, deciding whether you belong, whether you're compatible with its careful equilibrium. Stand still long enough, breathe the cool damp air, let moisture settle on your skin, and the feeling shifts from assessment to acceptance. You're permitted. Not welcomed—the place doesn't care enough for welcome—but permitted to be here, to observe, to exist for a time within this breathing garden where industry has become infrastructure for life, and life has become architecture, and something in between watches over the transformation with patience measured in generations of moss.
