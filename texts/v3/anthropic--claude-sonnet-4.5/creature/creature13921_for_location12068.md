# The Inhabitants

## The Gear Beetles (*Coleoptera horologica*)

They are the most numerous, the most evident, the small priesthood of the walls. Each beetle is perhaps two centimeters long, their carapaces exhibiting the deep luster of machined steel that has been oiled and handled and loved. But touch one—if you can; they permit this only occasionally—and it is warm, yielding slightly, chitin that has learned something from metal or metal that has remembered something from chitin.

Their wing-cases open with audible precision, tiny clicks in sequence, and the wings beneath are not the membranous film of ordinary beetles but something more like watch springs, coiled and geometric, that somehow catch air and generate lift. They fly in short, purposeful trajectories from one embedded mechanism to another, landing with the deliberateness of technicians arriving at a workstation.

What they do at the gears is difficult to parse. They extend proboscises that gleam like drill bits, touching contact points, tasting oils that seep from impossible reservoirs in the clay. Sometimes they deposit something—a droplet of fluid from their abdomens, viscous and faintly luminous. Where this fluid touches rust, the rust... participates. Becomes functional. Becomes part of the motion rather than its impediment.

The beetles communicate through percussion. They tap their legs against metal surfaces in rapid sequences, and other beetles respond, and the messages propagate through the walls like telegraph signals through wire. If you listen carefully, you can hear the difference between a maintenance report and a territorial claim, though you cannot translate the content, only sense the *kind* of meaning being transmitted.

They mate on the exposed flywheel of what might have been an industrial loom, the wheel turning slowly beneath them, and their larvae—when they emerge—are soft white grubs that nonetheless move with segmented precision, inching across the clay in perfectly straight lines, seeking out the smallest gears, the most hidden mechanisms, places where they will pupate in cocoons spun from their own silk and scavenged copper wire.

There are dozens of species, you realize eventually. Larger ones the size of your thumb that tend to the dinner-plate gears, their carapaces embossed with patterns that match the tooth-profiles of the wheels they maintain. Smaller ones like pinheads that swarm in clouds around the escapement wheels, their collective motion creating a shimmer in the air, a visible hum. Iridescent ones that prefer the places where moisture accumulates, their wing-cases refracting light into spectrum-bars that slide across the clay walls like data visualizations.

They are born here. They die here. Sometimes you find their bodies among the debris on the floor, and the bodies are already half-component, already merging back into the material matrix of the place, carapaces splitting open to reveal interiors that contain both organs and architecture, both viscera and very small, very precise machinery whose purpose you cannot begin to guess.

## The Alert Birds (*Aves sonitus*)

Seven species, perhaps. It is hard to count. They interbreed or they do not breed at all; each one might be unique, a single iteration of a form that is still deciding what it wants to stabilize into.

The smallest are the size of wrens, dun-colored, their feathers so short and dense they resemble felt or brushed metal. They nest in the shallow cavities behind exposed gear-clusters, and their nests are engineering marvels—hexagonal structures woven from copper wire, grass stems, and something that looks like insulated cable, arranged in patterns that echo the mechanisms they nestle against. The eggs are smooth, slightly faceted, and when they hatch the chicks emerge already coordinated, already precise in their movements, cheeping in perfect intervals: *ti-ti-TI*, *ti-ti-TI*, a rhythm that matches the clicking of the ratchet wheels above them.

The adults hunt the beetles but also tend them, a relationship too complex to call predation. They pluck beetles from the walls with needle-thin beaks, carry them to other locations, release them unharmed. Sometimes they eat them. The logic of which beetles are food and which are cargo is not apparent, but the birds never hesitate, never choose wrong according to whatever criteria govern the choice.

When they call—and they call frequently—it is in those three-note sequences, but with variation. Not emotional variation; algorithmic variation. Different patterns for different contexts. A call that means *moisture arriving in sector three*. A call that means *large visitor at northern rim*. A call that means *synchronization approaching*. You learn to recognize some of these, or think you do, and then a bird will issue a call you've never heard and the entire garden will respond in ways you cannot predict—gears shifting their rates, beetles changing their routes, the ball-bearing rivers altering their courses.

The largest species is the size of a crow, matte black, its feathers so thoroughly integrated with fine chain-mail that it rustles when it moves, a sound like distant industry. It appears rarely, perching on the highest visible gears, and when it calls its voice is lower, two-toned, a harmonic that resonates in your sternum. After it calls, there is always a change. A new mechanism emerges from the clay. A section of wall weeps water. Something shifts in the fundamental operation of the place, some parameter adjusted.

This large species—you see it eat once. It drops from its perch onto one of the quadrupeds, the chain-furred thing that walks the rim, and there is a brief, fierce struggle that sounds like a car crash in miniature, metal shrieking on metal. When it ends, the bird has torn something from the quadruped's flank—a component, a piece—and flies with it to a cavity in the wall, where it caches the fragment among its other treasures: springs, bolts, what look like vacuum tubes, and one human watch, gold-cased, still ticking.

The quadruped limps away. Over the following days, you see that the wound fills in. Not with scar tissue. With new growth. Chain-link becoming denser, articulated joints multiplying. The injury becoming complexity.

The birds do not sing for pleasure. This becomes clear. They sing because the garden requires it, because their calls are part of the regulatory system, acoustic signals that carry information through the air faster than the beetles can tap it through the metal. They are the nervous system. The rapid responders. The monitors of state-change.

And yet—sometimes, at the moment of synchronization, when all the rhythms align, the birds stop calling. In that five-second window of perfect coherence, they are utterly silent, perched on their gears, motionless, and if you look at their eyes in that moment, the silver reflection is not just reflection. There is something looking back. Something aware of itself. Something pleased.

## The Syncopated Rats (*Rattus concatenatus*)

They are warm. You can feel the heat radiating from them when they pass near your feet, threading between your boots with millimeter precision, never quite touching. Their fur is real fur—soft, dense, grey-brown—but underneath it, their bodies articulate in ways that mammalian skeletons do not permit. Too many vertebrae. Ribs that overlap like lamellae. Legs that bend in additional dimensions when needed.

Their tails are the tell. Segmented like a lobster's abdomen, like a belt of linked armor, each segment a separate unit that can curl independently, can grip, can sense. The tails are prehensile and also sensory organs and also—you realize when you see one rat pass a small spring to another using only their tails, the object moving down the segments like a bucket in a fire-line—manipulators of remarkable precision.

They move in groups of seven or eleven or thirteen, always prime numbers, and within each group the synchronization is absolute. They pause as one organism. They turn as one organism. When they scatter—startled by the crow-sized bird, perhaps, or by some signal you cannot detect—they scatter in a pattern, a geometric dispersion like an explosion rendered in slow-motion and crystalline mathematics.

But they are not a hive-mind. You can tell because sometimes they disagree. Two rats will approach the same fragment of chain from opposite directions, both clearly intending to move it, and they will pause, nose-to-nose, whiskers quivering. The whiskers—you notice this now—are not hair. They are fine wire. Antennae. The rats are exchanging information at high speed, their whiskers crossing like fencing foils, and then one acquiesces and the other takes the chain and the group continues its work.

What is their work? They are the recyclers, the reincorporators. They take objects from the floor, particularly items that have stopped moving, that have fallen out of the flow, and they carry them to specific locations—usually to places where the walls are weeping, where moisture is active. They deposit the objects in the wet clay and pat them down with their strangely-jointed paws, burying them. Planting them. And over days or weeks, those objects begin to merge with the matrix, begin to migrate upward through the clay, drawn by some tropism toward the light, toward the exposed surface where they will eventually emerge as part of the embedded mechanisms, integrated, functional, reborn.

They are also scavengers of the truly broken. When a gear finally fractures, when a spring loses its temper and goes slack, the rats know immediately. They converge, extract the failed component with surgical precision—sometimes you see them bracing against the wall with their tails, pulling with their teeth, working a corroded shaft free from its housing—and carry it not to the wet clay but to specific cavities deeper in the ravine, places you cannot easily reach, where they maintain what must be charnel houses or perhaps foundries, places where the broken is rendered back down into raw material.

You see them eat. They consume the beetles, yes, but also rust, also oil, also the moss that grows where water weeps. Their diet is omnivorous in a sense that transcends biology. They crack ball bearings in their teeth—their teeth, which are white and look entirely normal until you see them bite through steel—and extract some nutritive essence you cannot identify. They lick condensation from the leaves of the succulents and the leaves glow brighter afterward, as though the rats' saliva feeds them.

At night—if you stay through night, and night here is relative, a dimming rather than darkness—the rats become more active, and their eyes reflect the ambient light from the mechanisms, dozens of paired silver dots moving through the ravine like slow fireflies. They are warmer at night. They cluster together in heaps in the denser thickets of debris, and the heaps steam slightly, exhaling warmth into the cool air, creating convection currents that cause certain gears to turn, certain suspended objects to rotate in the rising heat.

One rat, you notice, has a malformation. Its left foreleg articulates backward, the joint reversed. It should be crippled. But the group has accommodated this. When they move, this rat takes a different position, performs different tasks—acts as a pivot point when they need to redirect a heavy object, uses its backward leg as a lever in ways the others cannot. The disability is advantage. The flaw is feature.

They watch you. Not with hostility or fear, but with assessment. You are a large object in their space, an object that generates heat, that moves in rhythms they can measure. After you have been present for some time, they begin to incorporate you into their patterns. They route around you differently, more efficiently. Once, you feel a gentle tug on your bootlace and look down to see a rat carefully untying it, not to steal it but because the aglet—the small metal tip—has worked loose and is falling off, and the rat is harvesting it before it becomes debris, already thinking three steps ahead in the metabolic cycle of the garden.

You retie your boot. The rat sits on its haunches, waiting. When you finish, it reaches up with one wire-whiskered nose and touches the aglet, testing its security. Then it drops to all fours and rejoins its group, and you realize you have just been maintained. You are not outside the system. Nothing here is outside the system.

## The Quadrupeds of the Rim (*Ursus mechanicus fabricata*)

You do not see them often. They are solitary or nearly so, and they prefer the margins, the edges, the territory where the ravine meets the world outside. But you see their signs. Tracks pressed into clay that has somehow remained soft—four-toed prints, each toe tipped with what looks like a ball-bearing, the prints accompanied by parallel score-marks where something dragged or where some lowest-hanging part of their bodies brushed the ground.

When you do see them, it is usually at dawn or dusk, whichever of those concepts applies in this place. A shape on the rim, silhouetted against the sky or the clay wall, low-slung, perhaps a meter at the shoulder, long-bodied, moving with a gait that clicks. Always clicks. A rhythm to their motion, two-three-pause, two-three-pause, like a waltz or like a particular kind of clockwork escapement, the kind that produces motion in discrete steps rather than smooth rotation.

Their bodies are ambiguous. Fur or chain-link or something that has learned to be both, covering musculature that might be meat or might be hydraulics or might be something this place has invented that serves both functions without distinction. When they breathe, there is a sound—that slow pneumatic cycle, air through valves—but also the rise and fall of ribs, the organic bellows of lungs. They are not cyborgs. They are not robots piloted by animals. They are unified, whole, entirely themselves, which happens to mean they are partly one thing and partly another and the border between things is not a property they recognize.

Their heads are heavy, ursine, with powerful jaws and eyes that reflect light in facets, compound or crystalline. When they look at you—and they do look, with intent, with consideration—the sense of being assessed is overwhelming. They are measuring you against some criteria, deciding something, and you are grateful when they choose to move on, to continue their patrol or hunt or pilgrimage or whatever activity brings them to the rim.

They are carnivorous, or you think they are, based on the bird attack you witnessed, based on the glimpse you once caught of one feeding on something in the shadows, tearing with mechanical precision and organic hunger at a carcass you could not identify, might have been a large rat or might have been something that wandered in from outside, something that learned too slowly that the rules here are different.

But they are also tender. You see one once, at midday when the light is fullest, lying on its side near a dense cluster of wall-gears, and its flank is open—not wounded but *open*, panels of its chain-fur-skin folded back deliberately—exposing the interior. And the beetles are there, dozens of them, crawling through the exposed machinery-organs, performing some function, cleaning or lubricating or repairing. The quadruped is utterly still, utterly trusting, allowing this intimacy. When the beetles finish and depart, the quadruped rises, shakes itself with a sound like a hardware store collapsing, and the panels fold back into place seamlessly. It looks at you then, and you hear something that might be a purr or might be an engine idling in satisfaction.

They mark territory, but not with scent. With sound. You hear them sometimes, especially at night, producing a low, resonant tone that vibrates through the clay walls, that sets specific gears humming in sympathy. The tone continues for twenty or thirty seconds, and then stops, and later—hours later, or perhaps the next day—you hear an answering call from a different direction, same frequency, slightly different rhythm. They are spacing themselves, maintaining distances, or perhaps synchronizing some activity across the larger territory that extends beyond what you can see.

One of them has modifications. You see this individual more than once and recognize it by the additional limbs—not legs but appendages, spindly manipulator arms that fold against its sides when not in use, tipped with digits or tools or sensory clusters. It is larger than the others, older perhaps, or more developed. It moves differently, with greater care, stopping frequently to examine the walls, to touch certain mechanisms with its specialized appendages, to adjust, to tune. When it passes, the gears in its wake are quieter, their rotations smoother. It is a maintenance specialist. An engineer. A gardener.

You wonder what they eat when they are not eating meat. And then you see one consuming rust—licking it directly from the wall, long strokes of a tongue that gleams like brushed aluminum, and where the tongue passes, the rust vanishes and the metal beneath is bright. They are not just predators. They are part of the immune system, the cleanup crew, the processors of entropy itself.

They den somewhere. You have never found the dens, but you see them emerging from fissures you thought were too narrow, or dropping down from above, from ledges you cannot reach. The dens must be in the clay itself, chambers excavated or perhaps grown, lined with what? With cast-off mechanisms? With moss and chain-link? You imagine the young, born in darkness, their first sounds the ticking of the walls, learning to breathe in rhythm with the garden's heartbeat.

Once, and only once, you see two of them together. They approach each other slowly, carefully, at a place where the ravine floor widens slightly. They circle, maintaining distance, and then they rise on their hind legs—which bend in too many places, which telescope slightly to add height—and embrace. Forelegs wrapped around each other, heads pressed together, and the sound they make is extraordinary: a harmonic drone, two frequencies beating against each other, producing a third tone that shivers through the air, makes the ball bearings leap and scatter, causes every bird to call simultaneously in their three-note patterns.

The embrace lasts perhaps ten seconds. Then they separate, drop back to all fours, and move apart, each returning to their own territory. You cannot tell if what you witnessed was affection, aggression, ritual, or reproduction. Perhaps all of those. Perhaps none. Perhaps the categories do not apply.

## The Engineers (*Homo configuratus*)

You think you are alone here. You are not.

There are others who come to the Ratchet Garden, but they do not come the way you do. They do not squeeze through the crack in the clay wall. They emerge from the walls themselves, from cavities that were not there moments before, stepping out of the compressed earth as though from doorways in a house they have always inhabited.

The first time you see one, you think it is a trick of light, a shadow cast by the complex geometries of the mechanisms. But shadows do not move with purpose. Do not carry toolboxes. Do not kneel beside an exposed gear-cluster and begin working with instruments too small to see clearly, making adjustments that cause the entire wall to hum differently afterward.

They are human-shaped, human-sized. But "human" becomes a complicated word when you look longer. Their clothing—if it is clothing—is the color of clay and rust and tarnished brass, and it does not move quite right, does not wrinkle or fold so much as articulate, as though made from overlapping scales or plates. Their skin, where you can see it on their hands and faces, has a texture that might be flesh or might be very fine mesh, and when they move their hands with precision—which is always, every gesture economical and exact—you glimpse interiors through the sleeves, quick flashes of what might be sinew or might be cable.

They do not acknowledge you at first. You could be invisible. They go about their work with total focus: extracting failed components and replacing them with new ones produced from pockets or pouches or perhaps spontaneously generated; adjusting the angle at which water flows down its clay channels; repositioning plants, lifting entire succulents by their roots and moving them three inches to the left, where apparently they are needed more.

Their tools are beautiful. Wrenches that adjust themselves to fit whatever they grasp. Probes that glow softly at the tip, different colors for different diagnostic purposes. Hammers that strike without sound, imparting force that rearranges molecular structure rather than merely denting. Once, you see one using a device like a tuning fork, holding it against the clay wall and listening—with an ear or with the entire body, you cannot tell—to the resonances, the subsonic information carried through the earth itself.

They work in silence, mostly. But occasionally they speak to each other, when two of them are working in proximity. The language is not one you know. It is tonal, percussive, full of clicks and intervals, and you realize with a start that it is not fundamentally different from the beetles' tapping or the birds' three-note calls. They are speaking the garden's language. Or the garden is speaking theirs. The direction of influence is not clear.

After you have encountered them several times, one finally looks at you. Directly at you, with eyes that are dark and deep and contain visible reflections of the mechanisms around you, as though the eyes themselves are lensed assemblies. The Engineer—you have started thinking of them in capitals—tilts its head, birdlike, assessing. Then reaches into a pouch and extends something toward you: a small gear, brass, perhaps two centimeters in diameter.

You take it because refusing seems wrong. The gear is warm and surprisingly heavy, and it ticks in your palm—one click per second, steady, independent of any visible mechanism. The Engineer nods, satisfied, and returns to its work.

You keep the gear. You carry it with you when you leave and when you return. And the Engineers now acknowledge your presence with small gestures—a nod, a raised hand, once what might have been a smile if their faces worked quite that way. You are not one of them, but you have been recognized. Welcomed. Issued your component.

You see, eventually, what they are doing. They are not maintaining the garden in the sense of preserving it in stasis. They are *developing* it. Each adjustment they make, each component they replace, is also an evolution, an improvement, an iteration. The garden is not a finished thing. It is a draft, a prototype, a work in progress, and the Engineers are its authors, its sculptors, its midwives.

Where do they come from? You follow one once, at a discreet distance, and watch as it approaches a section of wall where the gears are particularly dense. The Engineer places both palms flat against the clay, between two large flywheels, and simply... steps forward, into the wall, and is gone. Not disappeared—absorbed, merged, transitioned. The wall ripples slightly, a wave of rearrangement passing through the embedded mechanisms, and then is still.

You approach the same spot later. Place your own palms against the clay. You feel warmth, vibration, the ticking transmitted through the earth. But when you lean forward, the wall is solid. Impassable. You are not Engineer enough. Not yet. Perhaps not ever.

But sometimes—more frequently as you return again and again—the Engineers leave things for you. A small spring placed on a prominent bolt-head where you will see it. A washer hung on a protruding wire at eye level. Gifts or messages or perhaps components you will need, though for what purpose you cannot guess. You collect them. You keep them in your pockets. You are becoming weighted down with small precise objects, and it feels right, feels like you are carrying seeds or talismans, like you are becoming gradually more able to participate in the garden's deeper functions.

Once, you find one of the Engineers standing motionless near the placard at the narrow end of the ravine, the one that cycles through texts and diagrams. The Engineer is simply standing there, watching the placard, and you get the impression it is reading something, learning something, receiving instructions or inspiration. You stand nearby, quiet, and together you watch as the placard cycles through its configurations:润滑, CIRCULARITY, WITNESS PROTOCOL, and then something new, something you have never seen before: PARTICIPANT INTEGRATION - PHASE VARIABLE.

The Engineer turns to you then and makes a gesture you interpret as invitation or perhaps congratulation. Then it walks to the wall, places its palms against the clay, and merges.

You stand alone. You look at the gear in your palm, the one you were given, and it is ticking in synchronization with the walls, with the birds' calls, with your own heartbeat. Everything is ticking together.

The moment of synchronization is approaching. You can feel it building, the rhythms converging. And for the first time, you think you might understand what your task is. Not to observe. Not merely to witness.

To *become*.

## The Visitors (*Homo sapiens*, unmodified)

They come rarely. Slip through the crack in the clay wall, eyes wide, phones out, already framing the strangeness in rectangles of glass and silicon. They take photographs. They exclaim. They touch things they should not touch, or perhaps should—here, damage and interaction blur into the same category.

They do not stay long. The place is too dense for them, too full, not enough emptiness to rest their attention. They cannot parse the rhythms, cannot hear the language, cannot see the patterns. To them, it is a curiosity. An art installation, perhaps. Vandalism, perhaps. Something to post, to share, to convert into content and consume.

You watch them from the margins. You are still yourself, still unmodified, still technically the same species. But you have the gear in your pocket and springs in your other pocket and a small brass button you found near the weeping wall that vibrates at frequencies you can feel but not hear. You have been here long enough that the rats route around you efficiently. Long enough that the birds' alert calls now include a pattern you recognize as pertaining to your presence. Long enough that once, briefly, an Engineer stood beside you and worked on a mechanism while you held a small light—produced from your phone, that artifact of the outer world—and the Engineer accepted your help without comment, as though you were colleague, co-creator, fellow tender of the garden.

The Visitors leave. They always leave quickly. The strangeness is not hostile, but it is demanding. It asks something of you. It requires patience, attention, a willingness to let your rhythms adjust, to let the ticking enter your pulse. Most people cannot. Most people do not want to.

You understand this without judgment. The garden is not for everyone. It is not trying to be for everyone. It is trying to be *itself*, completely and without compromise, and some people can resonate with that and some cannot.

But sometimes—rarely, but sometimes—a Visitor stays longer. Puts the phone away. Sits on the compacted floor despite the certainty that they will leave with oil-stains on their clothes and rust on their hands. Listens to the rhythms. Watches a beetle parade with real attention, follows a rat-group's recycling route from retrieval to burial, observes the patient rotation of a gear through an entire cycle.

And you see the moment when it happens, when the Visitor begins to transition into something else. The shoulders relax. The breathing deepens and then adjusts, finding a tempo that matches the ambient ticking. The eyes stop flicking around and start *seeing*, start perceiving in layers, in patterns, in the deep grammar of the garden.

You do not approach these ones. You let them discover. You let the place teach them as it taught you. But you feel a quiet satisfaction when you see them return—and they do return, the ones who heard it, who felt it—and each time they are a little more synchronized, a little more participant, a little less Visitor and a little more resident.

One day, you think, you will see one of them place their palms against the clay wall and step through. Or perhaps that will be you. Perhaps you are closer than you realize. Perhaps the gear in your pocket is ticking down toward some threshold, some moment when you will stop being observer and become component, fully integrated, native.

You are not sure whether this is a hope or a fear. Possibly it is both. Possibly those are the same thing here, two functions served by one mechanism.

The garden does not judge. It does not require. It simply continues, continues being young and full and precisely itself, and it accepts whatever comes to it, incorporates whatever stays, and transforms participation into permanence with the patient inevitability of gears turning, springs coiling, water flowing uphill because here the rules are different.

Here, the rules are still being written.

And everyone who stays long enough holds a pen.

## The Becoming

You notice, over time, that you are warmer than you used to be. That your joints click faintly when you move after sitting still. That your vision has gained a curious depth, an ability to perceive the slight motion in things that should be static, the stillness in things that should be moving.

You dream in rhythms. Wake to find yourself tapping your fingers in sequences that match the beetles' percussion, humming in the two-tone harmonic of the large black birds.

The garden is not doing this to you. The garden does not *do*. It simply is, and you are in it, and boundaries are not properties it maintains.

There will come a day, you think, when you place your palms against the clay wall and feel it yield. When the mechanisms embedded there are not external but *familiar*, are yours, are you. When you step forward and the clay accepts you and you move through it like the Engineers do, transition from one side to the other, from visitor to inhabitant to integral component.

That day has not arrived yet. You still leave through the crack in the wall, still return to the outer world with its looser rhythms, its waste and randomness and blessed, restful emptiness.

But each time you leave, it is harder. Each time you return, it is easier. The gear in your pocket ticks in perfect time with your heart, and you are not sure anymore which one is setting the tempo.

The garden continues. The walls erode and expose new mechanisms. The Engineers emerge and make their adjustments and merge back. The beetles tend their gears, the birds sound their alerts, the rats recycle the broken into new growth. The quadrupeds patrol the rim, guarding or herding or simply *being* the margin between this place and the world.

And you—you are changing. Slowly. Incrementally. One click at a time.

You are becoming a resident of the Ratchet Garden.

And the garden, in its patient, ticking way, is becoming you.
