# Who Lives Here

## The Stillwater People

They call themselves the Stillwater People, though the name is more recent than their lineage. Before the flooding, their ancestors were the plateau folk, quarry workers and stonemasons who lived in these buildings when they stood dry and whole. When the water came—not catastrophically but gradually, over a single lifetime, as the climate shifted and the basin filled—most left. Some stayed. These are their descendants.

They number perhaps thirty-five in the current season. Three extended family groups, with enough connection between them to maintain social cohesion but enough distinction to preserve identity. The eldest among them is a woman named Taiva who remembers her grandmother's stories of when the lowest street still had air above it, when you could walk through doorways that now require diving. She's perhaps sixty, which here means she's lived long enough to see three full cycles of the water's subtle rising and falling, long enough to know which buildings will last and which are losing their long battle with time.

They're small people, compact and efficient. Generations of swimming, diving, climbing, and negotiating vertical space have selected for bodies that move well through water and across platforms: broad shoulders, strong legs, narrow hips. Their lungs are capacious—the children learn to hold breath almost before they learn to speak. By adolescence, most can dive to the old street level and work there for two, three minutes before surfacing. The best divers, like Taiva's grandson Mir, can reach five minutes on a working dive, longer if they're just resting on the bottom in meditation.

Their skin stays cool and slightly damp from constant contact with water and humid air. They don't tan darkly despite the open sky, because the moisture in the air softens the light, but they have a particular weathered look, especially on their hands and feet, skin that's adapted to being wet more often than dry. Many have scars from fish spines, from scraping against stone underwater, from the small accidents of a life lived in three dimensions where a misstep means falling into water rather than onto ground.

They dress minimally. In the warm season, children wear nothing at all until they reach an age of modesty—around ten or eleven. Adults wear simple wraps: woven reed cloth or softened fish leather, garments that can be removed quickly for swimming, that dry fast, that don't restrict movement. In the cool season, they add layers: felted plant fiber cloaks, woven reed capes that shed rain. Footwear is rare except on the roughest platforms—bare feet grip wet wood better, and shoes are a liability underwater.

Their hands are always busy. Even in conversation, they're mending nets, weaving cordage, carving fish hooks from bone, processing plant fibers. Stillness here means fingers working while the body rests. They have the particular grace of people who've grown up moving carefully—there's no clumsiness, no wasted motion, because mistakes here mean falling into water at best, serious injury at worst.

## The Divers

Among the Stillwater People, the divers hold particular status. Everyone swims, everyone dives for daily needs, but the divers—the ones who go deep, who enter the most submerged buildings, who retrieve things from the old floors—these are specialists.

Mir is the best of the current generation. He's twenty-three, unmarried, and spends more time underwater than above it. His body shows this: his chest is barrel-like, his neck thick with muscle, his ear passages scarred from repeated pressure changes. He can equalize deeper than anyone else, can navigate the darkest interior chambers by touch and memory, can work at depth with his hands while others can only look.

He knows the underwater city better than anyone living. He's mapped it in his mind: which buildings have strong floors, which have collapsed interiors, where the largest fish hide, where objects from the old world still lie waiting. He retrieves things: pottery that's intact, metal tools, stone carvings, anything useful or interesting from the time before water. These objects give the Stillwater People connection to their past, and Mir is the one who brings them up from darkness into light.

But diving changes you. Mir is quiet above water, more comfortable in the green silence below. He speaks little, responds to questions with gestures as often as words. Other divers are similar—there's something about spending so much time in breath-held concentration that makes surface chatter feel frivolous. They develop a meditative quality, a stillness that's different from lethargy, more like a frog's waiting-stillness before it strikes.

The oldest diver is a man named Kael, now forty-seven and past his deep-diving years. His ears have suffered too much, his lungs no longer hold what they once did, but he teaches the young ones. He's the one who shows them how to relax into the dive rather than fighting it, how to read water pressure and their body's signals, how to navigate by the quality of light in murky conditions, which plants to grab for orientation and which will come loose in your hand. He's saved three lives over the years—young divers who went too deep or stayed too long and needed pulling back to surface, needed someone to breathe life back into them on the platforms.

The divers have their own small traditions: they won't dive in the early morning without touching water to their forehead first, a gesture that might be prayer or might be practical—wetting the face before the dive, waking the reflex. They leave offerings in certain deep places, small things—a carved fish, a woven bracelet—dropped into rooms that no one visits except them, a way of marking territory or showing respect to whatever lives in the deepest dark.

## The Weavers and Makers

Taiva is the master weaver, though several others share the skill. She's the one who understands plant fibers best, who knows which reeds to harvest when, how long to ret them, how to process them into thread and cordage. Her hands are never still: she weaves while walking, while talking, while sitting at the evening fire. The platform where she lives is festooned with hanging work—half-finished baskets, rope in progress, reed mats drying.

She makes the finest waterproof baskets, woven so tightly they hold water without leaking, coated inside with a resin she processes from certain plant saps. These baskets are valuable—they're used to carry water to gardens, to store fish, to hold collected foods. A good basket lasts years and is passed down, repaired repeatedly until the original weaving is barely visible under the patches.

Her daughter, Sela, works primarily with fish leather. She's developed a process for tanning fish skins—the largest carp and similar species—into a material that's waterproof, flexible, and surprisingly strong. She makes bags, water containers, clothing, even cordage from strips of fish leather braided together. The smell of her workspace is particular: fish oil, tanning agents made from bark, smoke from the low fires she uses to cure the leather. It's not pleasant, but it's the smell of valuable work, and no one complains.

The men who aren't primarily divers spend their time on construction and maintenance. There's always something that needs repair: platforms that need new boards, railings that need replacing, bridges that have developed dangerous sags. They work with stone tools primarily—the Stillwater People have some metal tools from the old times, precious and carefully maintained, but most daily work is done with flaked stone blades, bone awls, wooden mallets.

One man, Tovan, has specialized in understanding the plants. Not formally—there are no titles here, no official positions—but everyone knows he's the one who knows which plants can be pruned, which must be left alone, where new growth should be encouraged to provide future scaffolding. He walks the platforms every few days, checking the health of the major stems that support the walkways, looking for disease or damage. He's the one who first noticed that certain plant species, when wounded, release a sap that hardens into a waterproof seal, and now that sap is harvested carefully for coating baskets and patching boat hulls.

## The Children

The children are half-fish. They swim before they can run properly, dive before they can climb high platforms safely. By age five, they're navigating the underwater streets, playing games of chase through the drowned buildings, learning to hold their breath by competing with each other for duration and depth.

There are eight children currently, ranging from three to thirteen. They travel in a loose pack, supervised casually by whichever adults are nearby but given remarkable freedom. They know the safe areas and the dangerous ones—every child learns early which buildings are stable and which are death-traps, which platforms will hold their weight and which are for adults only.

Their games are adaptations of universal child-games, modified for the environment. Tag becomes a three-dimensional affair, chaser and chased diving through windows, surfacing on different sides of buildings, climbing to platforms to escape being caught in water. Hide-and-seek uses the submerged rooms as hiding places—you have to find someone before your breath runs out. They build small boats from scraps and race them through the channels. They fish with simple lines, competing for the largest catch.

The youngest, a boy of three named Piro, is just learning to dive properly. He can hold his breath for perhaps thirty seconds, enough to submerge his head and grab something from a few feet down. He's constantly testing his limits, much to his mother's anxiety, ducking under the platform edge and staying down until he absolutely must surface, then bursting up with a huge gasp and a proud grin. In two years, he'll be competent. In five, he'll be teaching someone else.

The oldest girl, Lina, is thirteen and approaching her recognition ceremony—the rite where she'll demonstrate adult skills and be acknowledged as a full member of the community. She's been working with Sela on fish leather processing, and she's become quite good. She's also an excellent diver, though not as strong as Mir. She's in the awkward stage of being too old for child games but not quite ready for full adult responsibility, and she spends much of her time alone, swimming slowly through the quieter areas, thinking about what her life will become.

## The Elders

Besides Taiva, there are three other people who've reached what passes for old age here. Old age comes earlier in the Stillwater—most people don't see fifty, worn down by the constant physical demands, by diving accidents, by the infections that can follow from even small wounds in this perpetually wet environment. Those who do survive to become elders have clearly done something right, and their knowledge is respected.

Ventar is perhaps fifty-five, the oldest man. He was a great diver in his youth but now his joints are swollen with what they call water-sickness—arthritis brought on by years of cold water and pressure changes. He moves carefully, with pain visible in his face, but he still works: he carves bone and wood, making the fishhooks and needles and small tools that everyone needs. His hands hurt but they still remember their skill. He sits in the same spot most days, on a platform that catches morning sun, and people bring him materials and take away finished tools.

Two women, both in their late fifties—Imara and Dess—are sisters who've lived their entire lives here. They remember the time when there were more people, when three other families lived in the Stillwater before deciding it was too hard, before moving to the lowland settlements. They're the memory-keepers, the ones who tell the stories on long evenings, who maintain the oral history of who married whom, which family owns which traditional platforms, what happened in the great storm twenty years ago.

They also know medicine, such as it is. They know which plants reduce fever, which poultice stops infection, how to set a broken bone, how to treat the ear problems that plague divers. Their knowledge is practical and empirical—this works, this doesn't, we don't know why. They've seen enough injuries and illnesses over the decades to have developed a pharmacopeia of treatments, most of them plant-based, some of them using animal products. When someone is seriously hurt or ill, these two are the ones who sit with them, applying treatments, making decisions about whether someone needs to be taken to the lowland for help.

## The Visitors

The Stillwater People aren't completely isolated. Every few weeks, traders come from the lowland settlements, paddling up through the flooded valleys in larger boats loaded with goods the Stillwater can't produce: grain, salt, certain tools, cloth woven from sheep wool. They trade for what the Stillwater has in abundance: dried fish, fish leather, the finest waterproof baskets, occasionally an artifact retrieved from the deep—old pottery or stonework that lowlanders value for reasons of tradition or status.

The traders are a different people, larger-bodied, softer from lives that don't require constant swimming. They're uncomfortable here at first, nervous about the platforms and the deep water, careful where they step. The Stillwater People find this slightly amusing—these are adults who can't dive, who don't know how to move through a flooded building, who treat water as an obstacle rather than a medium.

But the traders bring news, stories from other places, and the Stillwater People value this. Evenings when traders are present become storytelling sessions, the visitors sharing news of marriages and deaths in the lowland towns, of conflicts with other settlements, of strange weather or unusual catches. The Stillwater People reciprocate with their own stories—what the divers have found recently, any odd behavior from the fish or birds, changes in the water level.

Occasionally, a young person from the lowland will ask to stay, fascinated by the unique life of the Stillwater. A few times this has worked—if they can learn to dive, learn to navigate the vertical world, they can be accepted. More often, they discover that what seemed romantic from outside is actually very difficult, and they leave within a season. The Stillwater life requires starting young, developing the skills and the body before adolescence. Adults can learn, but rarely well enough.

Very occasionally, someone from the Stillwater leaves permanently. Usually it's a young person who's restless, who wants to see more than the same few hundred feet of drowned buildings. This is understood—the Stillwater can only support so many people anyway, and if someone wants to try life elsewhere, they're not prevented. But most come back. One woman left at seventeen, married a lowland farmer, lived there for five years, then returned divorced and re-adapted to the water. She said she couldn't sleep on solid ground anymore, couldn't breathe in air that wasn't moist, missed the sound of water constantly moving.

## The Relationship with Animals

The Stillwater People live in constant intimacy with the other creatures of this place, but it's a practical intimacy, not sentimental.

The fish are food, primarily. Every day, multiple people fish—with lines, with nets, with spears underwater. They know the fish as individuals sometimes: the very large carp that lives in the old temple building, the ancient one with the scarred mouth, is left alone not from sentiment but from practicality—he's too smart to catch, and he's probably tough and unpleasant to eat anyway. Better to leave him as breeding stock. But his offspring are fair game, and delicious.

The fishing is sustainable by practice and necessity. The Stillwater People can't overfish their home—they depend on the fish replenishing themselves, and they've learned through generations what level of harvest the system can support. Certain areas are fished less frequently, allowed to recover. During breeding seasons, fishing is reduced. The smallest fish are released. It's not conservation in an ideological sense, just practical management of a resource you can't afford to exhaust.

The birds are mostly left alone, though occasionally someone will catch a duck or similar waterfowl for meat. The effort isn't usually worth it—fish are easier. But the birds serve another purpose: they're indicators. When the herons suddenly leave, something's wrong—water quality changing, fish moving, something. When the cormorants are diving frequently in an area, it means fish are schooling there, and human fishers should pay attention. The Stillwater People read the birds like instruments, using their behavior to understand what's happening below the surface.

The otters are tolerated with mild irritation. They're competitors for fish, and occasionally they damage nets, but they're also beautiful and entertaining, and the children love watching them. An unspoken agreement exists: the otters fish the deeper areas, humans fish the shallows and the areas near platforms. Sometimes an otter will steal a catch right off someone's line, and this produces cursing but not serious anger. They're part of the community, in a way.

The frogs are just there, part of the soundscape. No one eats them—there's not enough meat to be worth the effort. Their calling tells you the time of day, the season, the weather. When the frogs are silent, something is very wrong.

One person, a young man named Savo, has developed an odd relationship with the turtles. He feeds them scraps, and now several turtles will swim up to him specifically, will take food from his hand. It's not useful particularly, but it's a small magic in a pragmatic life, and people enjoy watching him interact with them. The turtles recognize him—they'll swim away from others but approach him—and this suggests something about consciousness and memory that everyone finds interesting even if they don't articulate it.

## Social Structure and Decision-Making

The Stillwater People have no formal leaders, no chiefs or councils, but structure exists in the form of respect and competence. When decisions need to be made—where to build a new platform, whether to move someone's living space, how to respond to a trader's offer—the people involved gather and talk it through. Those with relevant expertise speak more, are listened to more carefully. Taiva's opinion on anything involving plant materials carries weight. Mir's knowledge of the deep areas makes him the authority on what's safe to attempt underwater. Kael's experience with diving accidents means everyone listens when he says something is too dangerous.

Arguments happen, sometimes heated, but they rarely become destructive because everyone knows they have to keep living together in very close quarters. Grudges can't be maintained when you're sharing platforms, when your safety depends on your neighbors. There's a social pressure toward resolution, toward compromise, toward finding solutions that work well enough even if they don't make everyone perfectly happy.

For serious disputes—rare but not unknown—the eldest are consulted. Taiva, Ventar, Imara, and Dess together represent something approaching institutional authority, not because they have power but because they have memory. They know what's been tried before, what worked and what didn't, what the consequences of various choices might be. Their age has bought them perspective, and perspective is valued in a place where most people are too busy with immediate survival to think long-term.

Children are raised communally to a degree. Everyone watches everyone's children, corrects them, teaches them, feeds them if they're hungry. Parents have primary responsibility, but on a given day a child might eat with three different families, learn fishing from their uncle, diving from Kael, weaving from Taiva, and their parents are fine with this. It takes a village, and this is a village.

Marriages happen but are informal. Two people decide they want to live together, they build or modify a platform for shared space, they start being referred to as a pair. There's usually a celebration—a feast, drinking if there's alcohol from the traders, dancing on the platforms—but no ceremony in a formal sense. Divorces happen similarly: someone moves their sleeping mat to a different platform, and that's that. Children usually stay with the mother but maintain relationships with the father, and everyone continues to cooperate because they must.

## Daily Rhythm and Seasonal Patterns

The day begins with first light, when the birds start calling and the morning shift of fish begins moving through the shallows. People wake on their sleeping platforms—simple structures, often just a raised wooden platform with a woven mat, maybe a reed screen for privacy, open to the air. In the warm season this is comfortable. In the cool season, people pile on felted blankets and sometimes share body heat.

Morning is for fishing. The fish are most active at dawn, and the light is good for seeing them in the water. Multiple people are out with lines or nets, or diving with spears. By mid-morning, the day's catch is usually sufficient, and attention turns to other work.

The middle of the day is for tasks: repairing platforms, processing fish, weaving, crafting tools, maintaining boats. If it's hot, people take breaks in the water, cooling off, swimming for pleasure as much as purpose. The children are always in and around the water, their shouts and splashes a constant background.

Afternoon might bring rain—the daily shower that comes from the altitude and the moisture in the air. When rain comes, people either shelter under the thatched roofs or ignore it, continuing to work wet. You're wet half the time anyway here; rain is just another form of water.

Evening is communal. The main cooking area—a large stone platform with permanent fire pits—becomes the gathering place. Fish are grilled or boiled in pots. Plant foods—collected greens, roots from the floating gardens, grain from the traders—are prepared. People eat together, talking, sharing the day's small events. The children are told to settle down, though they rarely do completely.

As darkness comes, people retire to their sleeping platforms. Sometimes there's evening work by firelight—mending nets, crafting, storytelling. Sometimes there's music: simple drums, flutes made from reed, singing. Sometimes people sit quietly, listening to the frogs, watching the stars, resting from the physical demands of the day.

Every few days, there's a deeper cleaning: platforms are scrubbed, sleeping mats are taken apart and re-woven or dried in sun, accumulated debris is cleared. The place requires constant maintenance—wood rots, cordage weakens, platforms accumulate slippery algae. Neglect is dangerous here, so vigilance is habitual.

Seasonally, the rhythms change. The wet season, when rain comes more frequently and the water level rises, is a time of more construction—new platforms must be built higher, old ones must be reinforced or abandoned to the rising water. Fishing is good because the high water brings nutrients and the fish spawn. But travel to the lowland becomes difficult, and the Stillwater People become more isolated.

The dry season, when water levels drop and certain areas become more accessible, is when major retrieval projects happen—Mir and the other divers go into the deepest areas that are usually too far down, bringing up whatever they can find. It's also when traders come most frequently, and when anyone who wants to visit the lowland settlements does so.

## Skills and Knowledge

Every person here develops a core set of skills: swimming, diving, fishing, basic carpentry, cordage work, plant knowledge. These are survival fundamentals. Beyond that, people specialize according to aptitude and interest.

The divers develop extraordinary breath control and body awareness. They learn to read water pressure, to navigate by touch in darkness, to work efficiently underwater where every motion costs precious oxygen. They learn the architecture of the drowned city intimately, memorizing layouts of buildings, understanding structural stability, knowing where dangers lurk.

The weavers develop fine motor skills and plant knowledge—which reeds are strongest, which most flexible, how to process fibers to achieve different qualities. They understand structural patterns in weaving, how to create strength in different directions, how to make waterproof seals. Their knowledge is geometric and material at once.

The fish-workers understand anatomy and preservation. They can butcher a fish quickly and efficiently, wasting nothing. They know which parts are best eaten fresh, which should be dried, which can be used for other purposes—swim bladders for cordage, bones for tools, skin for leather, guts for bait. They understand the curing process, how salt and smoke preserve meat, how to prevent spoilage in humid conditions.

The builders understand leverage and load, balance and material strength. They can look at a plant stem and know if it will support a platform. They understand how to distribute weight, how to anchor to unstable surfaces, how to build structures that flex with wind and water movement rather than fighting it. Their knowledge is practical physics, learned through experience and occasional failure.

Everyone learns the water itself—how current flows through the buildings, how temperature layers form, where the water is safest and where it's problematic. They learn to read the sky for weather, to predict the daily rain, to know when larger storms are coming. They learn the seasonal patterns of water level, plant growth, fish behavior, bird migration.

There's also negative knowledge—what they don't know, having been isolated here. They don't know how to work large-scale agriculture, how to manage large animals, how to build permanent structures on dry land. Some of the traders' goods are mysterious to them—processed foods, woven textiles from sheep wool, metal tools made with techniques they don't understand. The young people are particularly aware of this gap, that there's a larger world with knowledge and technologies they lack.

## Relationship to the Old World

The buildings beneath them are the old world—the world before water. Objects brought up from the depths are treasures partly for their utility but also for their connection to that time, that place, those ancestors who lived here when air filled the rooms that now hold water.

The Stillwater People maintain an ambiguous relationship with this past. On one hand, they're proud descendants of the people who built these structures, who worked stone and made these buildings that stand even drowned. The architecture represents their heritage, their identity. On the other hand, they're aware that their current life is radically different from what their ancestors lived, that they've adapted to conditions that would have seemed like disaster to those earlier people.

When Mir brings up an intact pot, a carved stone, a metal knife, these objects are handled with reverence. The pot might be used—functional items return to function—but it will be used carefully, with awareness that it connects to the old world. Decorative items, carvings, things that served no practical purpose, these are kept on shelves in the communal spaces, arranged where people can see them, touch them, remember.

Some of the objects are mysterious. Mir once found a small bronze wheel, beautifully made, with no clear purpose. It's become a kind of toy—children spin it on platforms, adults puzzle over what it might have been part of. No one knows, and the not-knowing is itself interesting, a reminder that the past was more complex than the present in some ways.

The old world also represents caution. Some buildings collapsed as the water rose, killing people. The flood itself—though gradual—forced most families to leave, to abandon everything they couldn't carry. The lesson the Stillwater People take from this is that the world changes, that permanence is illusion, that you must adapt or die. They've adapted. They're proud of this. But they also know that adaptation required loss, that the old world with its greater population and more complex life is gone, replaced by this smaller, simpler, wetter existence.

Occasionally, someone—usually young—will ask why they don't leave, go to the lowland, live on solid ground. The answer varies by who's asking but generally includes: this is our place, our home, our inheritance. We know how to live here better than anywhere else. The water doesn't scare us—it sustains us. And besides, where would we go? The lowlands are crowded, and we'd be strangers there, without status or position. Here we're the Stillwater People, and everyone knows what that means.

## Psychological Characteristics

Living in the Stillwater does something to consciousness, shapes it in particular ways.

There's a calmness here, a lack of rushing. The physical environment doesn't permit rushing—hurrying through water is inefficient, burning oxygen you need for the dive, creating turbulence that scares fish. Hurrying on platforms risks slipping, falling, injury. So people move deliberately, economically, taking the time needed to do things properly. This physical deliberation becomes mental: people think things through, consider before acting, maintain emotional steadiness.

But there's also a constant alertness. The environment is unforgiving of inattention—a moment's distraction while diving and you've used too much breath, stayed too deep too long. A careless step on a wet platform and you've broken an ankle. The animals require attention—fish to notice, birds to watch, changes in behavior that signal danger. So underneath the calmness is a vigilant awareness, a monitoring of self and surroundings that never quite shuts off.

The Stillwater People are comfortable with water in ways that outsiders find uncanny. They sleep to the sound of water, wake to humidity, spend half their time immersed. Water isn't an element they're in—it's an element they're of. This creates a particular body-confidence: they trust their ability to swim, to hold breath, to survive if they fall in. The water isn't dangerous to them in the way it is to non-swimmers; it's home.

They're also comfortable with smaller social groups and limited space. The same thirty-five people, the same five hundred feet of platforms and buildings, every day for years. This could be claustrophobic, but they've adapted: they know when to be social and when to give each other space, when to talk and when to work in companionable silence. They've developed tolerance for each other's quirks and habits, acceptance that this is the community you have, not the one you might wish for.

There's a particular relationship with risk here. Danger is constant—drowning, injury, infection—but it's manageable danger, danger that can be reduced through skill and care. The Stillwater People aren't reckless, but they're not fearful either. They assess risk practically: this dive is worth attempting, that one isn't. This platform is safe enough, that one needs repair before use. Risk is part of life, to be managed, not eliminated.

The children grow up fearless in ways that terrify the traders. A five-year-old diving through a window into a dark room, disappearing from sight for thirty seconds, then popping up on the other side of the building, grinning—this is normal here, horrifying to outsiders. But the children aren't being reckless by their standards; they're operating within parameters they understand, with skills they've been developing since they could swim.

Finally, there's a sense of verticality in their thinking. The world isn't just horizontal for them—it's up and down, surface and depth, aerial platforms and submerged streets. They think three-dimensionally in ways that people from flat-ground settlements don't. When describing locations, they specify depth or height as naturally as direction. "Meet me at the western platform, two fathoms down at the window" is a perfectly sensible instruction here.

## What They Create

Beyond survival necessities, the Stillwater People make beautiful things, not from luxury but from the human need to make meaning and mark existence.

The baskets and woven work are often decorated—patterns worked into the weave, geometric designs that have been passed down and modified over generations. Each weaver has their characteristic patterns, recognizable to the community. Taiva's work has a particular spiral motif. Sela marks her fish-leather pieces with a stamped design of overlapping scales. These marks are signatures, pride in craftsmanship.

Someone—Ventar, mostly—carves small figures from bone and wood: fish, birds, turtles, human forms. These aren't idols exactly, not religious objects, but they serve some similar function. They're placed in various spots around the platforms, small presences watching over the space. When someone dies, a carved figure is dropped into the deep water as a memorial, a sending-off.

The children make toys from whatever's available: small boats from bark, dolls from reed bundles wrapped and tied, miniature fishing spears. Their play-tools are remarkably sophisticated—a child's fishing spear might be crude but it's functional, capable of actually catching small fish, not just pretending.

Music exists, though limited by available instruments. Drums from hollow logs and stretched fish leather. Flutes from reed, producing airy, breathy tones that blend with the wind-in-plants sound of the place. Singing, always—work songs while processing fish, lullabies for children, longer ballads in the evening that tell stories of the ancestors, of the great flood, of notable events in community history.

The songs are mostly unaccompanied or lightly accompanied by percussion. The musical style is modal, using scales that sound slightly unfamiliar to ears trained in other traditions. The rhythm is often like water—flowing, cyclical, without hard edges. Listening to Stillwater music is like listening to the place itself, the sound of water and wind and living things transformed into human voice.

Stories are the major art form. The evening storytelling sessions are entertainment but also education, cultural transmission, identity formation. The stories everyone knows: how the water came, gradually, season by season, the old people arguing about whether to stay or go. The story of Taiva's grandfather, who was the first to understand that the plants could be used as scaffolding, who built the first aerial platforms. The story of the great storm, when wind destroyed half the platforms and three people died. Humorous stories about fish that got away, about diving accidents that were frightening at the time but funny now, about visitors from the lowland who were terrified of water.

New stories are added constantly—Mir's latest deep dive and what he found, a strange bird that appeared one day and stayed for a season, the time a child got lost in the underwater buildings and was found hours later, calm and unworried, playing in a submerged room. These stories are tested in the telling: some get repeated and refined, becoming part of the permanent repertoire, others are told once and forgotten.

The physical environment itself becomes a canvas, sometimes. Someone has carved designs into one of the platform posts, abstract patterns that catch shadow. A section of wall has been marked with hand prints in white clay, a dozen hands of different sizes, a record of who's here now. These aren't formal art but spontaneous marking, the human impulse to say "I was here, I made something, I exist."

## Their Future

The Stillwater People don't think much about distant future—survival is too immediate for that—but they think about the next generation, about continuity.

The children will grow up, will become the divers and weavers and builders. Some will stay, some will leave. The population might grow slightly if conditions are good, might shrink if the water rises further or if disease strikes. The community is resilient but fragile, large enough to sustain itself but small enough that a few deaths or departures matter significantly.

The water level is the great uncertainty. If it continues rising, as it has slowly over generations, eventually the current platforms will be submerged, and new ones will need to be built higher. There's a limit to how high they can go—at some point, the plant growth won't support platforms, the ruins won't provide anchoring, and the Stillwater life will become impossible. If that happens, they'll have to leave, become lowland people, lose their identity.

But if the water stabilizes or even drops slightly, the community could thrive. More platforms could be built, more areas could become accessible, perhaps more people could be supported. The ruins below contain resources for generations—stone, materials, artifacts. The fish population can support more harvesting if needed. The plants continue growing, providing material and structure. There's a possible future where the Stillwater grows, where this adaptation becomes not just survival but success.

The young people feel the uncertainty more than the elders. Lina thinks about this when she's alone, swimming slowly through the quiet places: will she have children here? Will her children have children here, or will they be the last generation, the ones who finally abandon the ruins to the fish and the plants? She doesn't have answers, but she knows that if anyone can maintain this life, it's her people, the ones who learned to breathe water and walk on stems, who made a home in a drowned city and called it good.

And maybe that's enough: not certainty, but capability. Not knowing the future, but being ready for it, whatever it brings. Being the people who stayed when others left, who adapted when adaptation seemed impossible, who took a disaster—the slow drowning of their ancestral home—and transformed it into something strange and beautiful and sustainable. Being the Stillwater People, for as long as the water holds them, and the gardens hang suspended between surface and depth, and the buildings stand patient beneath the green.
