# What Lives There

## The Drift-Synod

They are the most numerous, and thus they are what defines life here: radially symmetrical beings, disk-shaped or bell-shaped, their bodies organized around a central mouth that might also be an anus, or perhaps something that has transcended that binary. Each individual carries within its translucent flesh a series of hard crescents—not bone, not cartilage, but something crystalline that the cold water has never managed to dissolve. These crescents give them structure, allow them to hold form against the pressure and turbulence. Under the dim light, if you look closely as one passes near, you can see these internal architectures: geometric arrangements that vary from individual to individual like fingerprints, or like the floor plans of houses long abandoned.

They are called the Drift-Synod in your mind, though they have no name for themselves, possibly no concept that names are things that should exist.

Their fringe-appendages—forty to sixty per individual, depending on age and damage—are not tentacles in the grasping sense. They are sense organs and propulsion and communication organs and possibly something else. Each appendage contains thousands of cilia that can beat in coordinated waves, allowing the creature to pulse gently in one direction or another. But more often, the appendages simply extend into the current, reading it. They can detect pressure changes across their length, temperature variations of a tenth of a degree, chemical traces at nearly homeopathic dilution. The appendages quiver constantly, processing the water, and if you could see what they perceive you would understand that they navigate not through space but through a constantly updating model of flow—they know where the current is going five seconds from now, ten seconds, and they position themselves within it accordingly.

When two Drift-Synod meet and intertwine, appendages weaving together in patterns that last for minutes, they are exchanging something. Not genetic material—that happens differently, in the deep times, in ways you don't witness. What they exchange is current-knowledge. One has drifted through a region where thermoclines crossed in a particular way; another has felt the pressure-signature of something large moving through a distant section of lattice. They braid their appendages and pulse gentle chemical signals back and forth, and when they separate, both know more about the lattice's state than either did before. They are creating a distributed map, a collective consciousness of flow that no individual could hold alone.

Some are larger, much larger—three meters across, their bells thick and their appendages grown long and elaborately branched. These are the Synod-elders, perhaps, though age here might not mean what it means elsewhere. They drift more slowly, seem to make decisions more carefully. When a current-vortex forms, the smaller Drift-Synod scatter or spin with it chaotically, but the large ones position themselves deliberately, angling their bodies to slice through the turbulence at specific vectors, and they always emerge exactly where they seem to have intended. You suspect they have been mapping these currents for a very long time.

They eat—they must eat—but you never see it happen directly. Occasionally you observe one positioned with its central mouth pressed against a beam, appendages spread wide, holding station against the current for long minutes. When it finally releases and drifts away, there is no visible change to the beam's surface, no mark, no residue. Perhaps they are filter-feeding, extracting minerals that precipitate from the water at the beam's surface. Perhaps they are consuming something so thin, so dispersed, that it leaves no trace. Or perhaps they are eating time itself, sustaining themselves on the slow radioactive decay of elements in the stone, on the minimal energy released as ancient ceramics finally achieve their ultimate equilibrium.

They reproduce, occasionally. You find their young—tiny disks the size of coins, with only eight or ten appendages, drifting in clusters near certain platforms. They seem to prefer regions where multiple currents meet and cancel out, creating zones of relative calm. The adults drift through these nursery areas but do not stop; there is no parenting here, no guarding. The young either learn the currents or they don't. Most don't—you see their bodies sometimes, no longer pulsing, drifting as particulate matter, their internal crescents catching the light as they tumble slowly into the depths. But enough survive. The population persists at some equilibrium with the lattice and its flows.

Some have developed colorations, barely: a blush of pink in their flesh, a pale yellow tint to their appendages. These seem to be older individuals, and the color comes from mineral accumulation in their tissues—iron, sulfur, trace elements filtered from the water over decades or centuries of drift. They are becoming geology, slowly. Eventually they will become too heavy with their collected minerals and will sink into the depths, still pulsing weakly, adding their crystalline crescents to whatever layer of sediment might exist at the lattice's bottom.

## The Segment-Walkers

Less common, but regular enough to be part of the lattice's normal state: arthropod-like creatures the length of your forearm, segmented into a dozen sections, each bearing a pair of legs that end in adhesive pads. They walk along the beams with mechanical precision, each leg lifting and placing in perfect rhythm. Their bodies are translucent grey, hard-shelled, revealing nothing of their internal workings. At the front end: a cluster of simple eyes, probably only capable of detecting light and shadow. At the rear end: nothing, just a sealed terminal segment.

They are called the Segment-Walkers in your mind, and they are the lattice's custodians, though they might not know it.

Where the Drift-Synod navigate the water, the Segment-Walkers navigate the stone. They walk endlessly along the beams, following the geometric paths, ascending spiral staircases that lead nowhere, crossing bridges between platforms. They never stop for long. You might observe one paused for a moment at a beam junction, its front segments lifted, eyes angled at different paths, making some kind of decision. Then it chooses and continues, its legs moving in their unwavering rhythm: click-click-click-click against the smooth ceramic surface.

What are they doing? The beams show no sign of fouling, no growth that would need to be grazed away—but perhaps that's because the Segment-Walkers are constantly cleaning them. Their adhesive pads might secrete something, a chemical or enzyme that breaks down the microscopic layer of precipitate that tries to form on the stone. Or perhaps they're doing something stranger: reading the structure itself, checking for damage, reporting to some node or center that no longer exists but which their species still remembers in their behavioral patterns.

They eat, definitely. You see one encounter a fragment of organic matter—probably a Drift-Synod's discarded appendage, or a piece of deceased young—and it stops. Its front segment opens to reveal a complex mouth, more cutting than chewing, all hard edges and mechanical precision. It processes the fragment methodically, reducing it to smaller pieces, extracting what nutrients exist. The remainder, inorganic minerals and hard parts, it leaves behind: tiny debris that the current immediately catches and carries away into the lattice. The Segment-Walker continues its patrol.

They can swim, after a fashion. When they reach the end of a beam that doesn't connect to another, they don't turn back. Instead they release their grip and extend their legs wide, and the segments begin to undulate in coordinated waves. They look absurd doing this, rigid creatures trying to move through water with anatomy designed for walking, but it works. They flutter awkwardly through the current, often tumbling, sometimes taking long minutes to cross a gap of ten meters. But they reach the next beam eventually, legs finding purchase, and resume their walking as if nothing had interrupted them.

You never see more than one on the same beam. When two Segment-Walkers approach each other on a collision course, one always stops, reverses, and takes a different path. There seems to be a hierarchy—larger individuals have right of way, or older ones, or perhaps it's determined by some signal you can't perceive. They communicate, probably, through vibrations transmitted through the stone itself, through the substrate they walk on. The beams might carry their tapping to others far away, letting them coordinate their movements across the lattice so that every surface gets walked, every path maintained, without overlap or waste.

Their reproduction is strange. You discover a cluster of them—twelve individuals, all converged at a particular node where six beams meet. They are arranged in a circle, their front ends pointing inward, and they are perfectly still. This is so unusual that you drift closer to observe. Hours pass. They don't move. Then, suddenly, each one's terminal segment opens—something you didn't know was possible—and they release into the center of their circle a cloud of material: eggs perhaps, or spores, or cells, something too small to see individually but visible in aggregate as a pale haze. The haze drifts in the current for a moment, particles finding each other, aggregating, and then the twelve adults break their circle and walk away in different directions, their terminal segments sealed again.

The particle cloud persists for minutes, then begins to settle, individual motes drifting down to land on the beams below. Most are probably lost, swept away by the current or consumed by passing Drift-Synod. But some must survive, must grow, must eventually become new Segment-Walkers that take up the endless patrol. You find tiny ones occasionally—only three or four segments long, their legs still soft—making their way along the beams with the same mechanical determination as their elders, already committed to the task they'll perform for their entire existence.

## The Tanglers

These are rarer, and more disturbing. You don't see them often—perhaps one every few hours of observation—but when you do, they command attention.

They are masses of tentacles, each tentacle perhaps two meters long, and there might be twenty or thirty tentacles per individual, all emerging from a central body that you never quite see clearly. The body itself is hidden in the mass of limbs, which writhe constantly, never quite synchronized. The tentacles are pale grey, segmented like the Segment-Walkers but flexible, covered in tiny hooked barbs that catch the light.

They are called the Tanglers in your mind, and you do not want them to touch you.

They move through the lattice in a way that seems wrong, too purposeful, too aggressive for this place of slow drift and gentle collision. When a Tangler approaches, the Drift-Synod scatter, pulsing hard to escape into different currents. The Segment-Walkers freeze on their beams, becoming as still as the stone they walk on. Even the water seems to move differently around the Tanglers, swirling in their wake with unusual turbulence.

They are predators, clearly. You watch one intercept a Drift-Synod that wasn't quick enough. The tentacles lash out, not all at once but in waves, three or four reaching, grabbing, and when they make contact with the Drift-Synod's bell, the barbs hook in. The Drift-Synod pulses frantically, its appendages beating in panic, but more tentacles are wrapping around it now, pulling it toward the central body. The mass of limbs becomes a feeding knot, contracting, and you hear—or imagine you hear—a sound like ice cracking. When the tentacles uncoil and continue their patrol, the Drift-Synod is gone. Not even its crystalline crescents remain. The Tangler has consumed everything, dissolved it, incorporated it.

But they don't only hunt the fauna. You observe one wrapped around a beam, its tentacles spread wide, tips pressed against the ceramic surface. It holds this position for a long time, and when it finally releases and swims away, the beam looks different: duller somehow, less smooth. The Tangler has been eating the beam itself, somehow breaking down the ancient material that nothing else can affect. Perhaps they secrete an acid, or perhaps they operate through some other chemistry. Either way, they are the only thing in the lattice capable of damaging the structure, of actually changing it rather than just existing within it.

This makes them terrible and important. If the Tanglers are eroding the lattice—slowly, minimally, but measurably—then the lattice isn't eternal after all. Given enough time, enough Tanglers, the beams would be consumed, dissolved into the water, and the entire geometry would collapse back into randomness. The lattice would end.

But there aren't many Tanglers, and they seem to prefer eating the fauna when it's available. Perhaps they only turn to eating stone when desperate, when they can't catch enough Drift-Synod to sustain themselves. Perhaps there's an equilibrium here too: the Drift-Synod population supports a certain number of Tanglers, and that number is small enough that their damage to the lattice is negligible on any timescale that matters.

You never see Tanglers interact with each other. Each one appears alone, hunts alone, disappears back into the blue-grey distance alone. Either they're solitary by nature, or their population density is so low that encounters are rare. You have no idea how they reproduce. Perhaps they bud, each tentacle eventually separating to become a new individual. Perhaps they lay eggs in the deep places, down where the pressure and darkness increase beyond tolerance. Perhaps they don't reproduce at all, and the ones you see are the last of their kind, remnant predators from some earlier era when the lattice teemed with more abundant prey.

## The Deepform

You only see this once, and you're not sure you saw it at all.

You've descended deeper than before, following platforms down level by level, watching the light fade until you're navigating by touch as much as sight. The cold is more intense here, or seems to be—perhaps it's psychological. The fauna are fewer; most of the Drift-Synod stay in the upper reaches where the light is better. But there are still some, and their bioluminescence—you realize now that they do have it, just extremely faint—provides occasional reference points in the darkness.

You're resting against a beam, conserving energy, when the water changes. The pressure shifts in a way that has nothing to do with currents. It's a presence, massive, moving through the lattice far below. You look down and see nothing, then something, then you're not sure again.

It might be many creatures moving in coordination, or it might be one creature with many parts. The scale is wrong for you to process—it seems to extend across multiple platform levels, to occupy a volume of water that should contain nothing so organized. You see segments that might be tentacles, but each tentacle is meters thick. You see shapes that might be bells or disks, but they're the size of the platforms themselves. There is a geometry to it, a symmetry, but not radial—something else, something more complex.

It's moving upward through the lattice, ascending slowly, and you realize with a jolt of primal terror that it's going to pass near you, perhaps directly through your position. You push off from the beam and swim laterally, frantically, no longer caring about the current or maintaining orientation. You need to be elsewhere, anywhere but in the path of this thing.

You watch it rise through the level below yours, and in the moment when it's closest, you see—or think you see—eyes. Dozens of them, each one the size of your head, arranged in patterns along what might be limbs or might be body segments. The eyes are not looking at you. They're not looking at anything. They're open and functioning, but their gaze is directed inward, or backward, or into some dimension you can't perceive.

Then it's past, continuing upward, and you feel the water pressure normalize. The normal currents resume. A Segment-Walker on a nearby beam, which had frozen during the passage, continues its patrol as if nothing happened.

You don't know what you saw. The Deepform is what you call it later, trying to make sense of the memory. Perhaps it's something that lives at the lattice's bottom, in the crushing darkness, and occasionally rises to feed or breed or simply because its nature requires movement. Perhaps it is the lattice, some final transformation where the ancient structure and the fauna that inhabit it have merged into a single entity, stone and flesh becoming one. Perhaps it was an hallucination, your mind creating pattern from random shadows and the play of dim light on water.

You don't descend that deep again.

## The Polyp-Clouds

These are barely fauna, possibly not fauna at all. They exist at the boundary between life and chemistry.

In certain locations—always at structural nodes, always where multiple beams join—you find clusters of tiny polyps, each one smaller than your fingernail, anchored to the stone. They are translucent to the point of invisibility, only noticeable because they refract light slightly differently than water. Each polyp has a simple body: a base that adheres to the stone, a tubular section, a crown of hair-like cilia at the top.

Individually, they do nothing interesting. They wave their cilia in the current, presumably filter-feeding on minerals or on particles too small for you to see. They don't move, don't react to stimuli, might as well be plants except that no plants exist here.

But collectively, at certain times—you can't determine the trigger—they release. All at once, thousands of polyps let go of their anchors and drift free, and their cilia begin beating in synchrony. The cluster becomes a cloud, a swarm, and it moves through the lattice with apparent coordination. The cloud can be ten meters across, a pale haze in the water, and it flows around obstacles, divides to pass through narrow gaps between beams, reunites on the other side.

The Drift-Synod are attracted to these clouds. You watch them converge, dozens of them, swimming into the polyp-swarm and allowing themselves to be engulfed. The polyps land on the Drift-Synod's bells and appendages, attach temporarily, and seem to feed on something—sloughed cells perhaps, or mineral deposits in the Drift-Synod's tissues. The Drift-Synod don't seem harmed by this; they pulse gently while covered in hundreds of tiny polyps, perhaps even enjoying the sensation, a cleaning service in exchange for nutrients.

After minutes or hours—the duration varies—the polyps release again, drifting away from their temporary hosts. The cloud disperses gradually, individual polyps settling onto beams and platforms throughout the lattice, reanchoring themselves, returning to their immobile state. The Drift-Synod swim away looking cleaner somehow, their translucence more pure.

You wonder if the polyps are actually a life stage of something else—perhaps juvenile Drift-Synod, or a symbiotic organism that spends part of its life anchored and part free-floating. Or perhaps they're truly independent, a species that has evolved to exploit the other fauna without quite being fauna themselves, occupying some strange category between sessile and mobile, between individual and collective.

## The Stragglers

These don't belong here, and they know it.

Occasionally, very occasionally, you encounter something that is clearly from outside the lattice: a fish, recognizably vertebrate, with fins and scales and the bilateral symmetry of the upper world. It's struggling, always struggling, trying to navigate the turbulent currents with anatomy designed for different water. Its movements are panicked compared to the serene drift of the Drift-Synod.

These are stragglers from the surface, you realize, fish that descended or were pulled down into the lattice and can't find their way back out. The cold is killing them—you can see it in their sluggish movements, in the way their fins move without coordination. They have hours left, perhaps, or minutes. The lattice is lethal to them.

The native fauna ignore them. A Drift-Synod might bump into a dying fish and simply pulse away, uninterested. The Segment-Walkers march past without pausing. Even the Tanglers don't seem to register them as prey—perhaps the fish's chemistry is wrong, incompatible with the Tangler's digestive processes.

You watch a fish—something that might have been a type of cod once, before the ocean rose and changed everything—spiral slowly downward, its buoyancy failing, until it disappears into the darkness below. Its body will settle on some platform or beam, will be slowly processed by bacteria if any still exist at these temperatures, will eventually be reduced to minerals and dissolved back into the water. Perhaps a Segment-Walker will find it and extract what nutrition it can. Perhaps it will simply lie there, desiccating in the cold, until nothing remains but bones that the water will slowly dissolve over decades.

The stragglers remind you that the lattice is not the whole ocean, that above this drowned structure there must be a surface world with light and warmth and the kinds of life you remember. But that world is far away, and everything here has adapted to its absence, has found ways to exist in cold and darkness and turbulence that would kill normal life.

The lattice's fauna don't need the sun. They have transcended it, or forgotten it, or never knew it existed in the first place.

## The Coordination

After enough time observing, you begin to notice something that might be pattern or might be apophenia—the human tendency to find meaning in randomness.

The Drift-Synod's movements, which seem individual and purposeless, start to look like they have larger organization. When a current-vortex forms, they don't scatter randomly—they position themselves in specific relation to each other, maintaining distances and angles that seem too precise to be accidental. When they intertwine and exchange current-knowledge, the information seems to propagate through the population in waves, each individual passing data to several others in turn, until within an hour the entire visible population has been updated with whatever was learned.

The Segment-Walkers' patrols, which appear to be independent, might actually be coordinated across the entire lattice. You never see the same beam walked by two different Segment-Walkers in quick succession; there's always a long interval, as if they're spacing out their maintenance checks. And occasionally you notice one change its path abruptly, as if responding to information from elsewhere, taking a different spiral staircase than it seemed to intend.

Even the Tanglers, solitary and predatory, might be part of something larger. Their appearances seem clustered—you'll see three in one day, then none for a week, then two more. Perhaps they coordinate their hunting to avoid depleting local Drift-Synod populations too quickly, maintaining the prey species at sustainable levels.

And underlying it all, the lattice itself: the structure's geometry creating predictable turbulence patterns, the beams and platforms channeling currents in ways that create habitable zones and feeding opportunities and nursery areas. The fauna have evolved not just to survive in the lattice but to use it, to exploit its geometry and flow patterns. The lattice is their habitat and their tool, their landscape and their library.

You start to wonder if the lattice was meant for this. If whoever built it—in those ancient air-times before the drowning—somehow knew or predicted that it would eventually be submerged, and they designed it to support life in exactly this way. The spacing of platforms, the angles of beams, the placement of spiral staircases: all calculated to create the turbulence patterns that the Drift-Synod need, the walking surfaces that the Segment-Walkers require, the three-dimensional space that the Tanglers hunt through.

Or perhaps it's the opposite: perhaps the fauna created this coordination themselves, evolved over millions of years to exploit whatever geometry the drowned lattice happened to have, and the apparent design is just the result of life finding every possible niche, filling every available space with purpose. Perhaps in another million years they'll have evolved further, become even more integrated with the structure, until fauna and lattice are truly one thing, indistinguishable, a fusion of biology and architecture.

## What Lives There

In the end, what lives in the Drowned Lattice is adaptation itself—life that has found a way to exist in conditions that should be impossible, in cold that should halt all chemistry, in darkness that should starve all food chains, in turbulence that should batter and destroy.

The Drift-Synod have become the water's partners, reading its movements and moving with them, sustaining themselves on almost nothing and creating a distributed intelligence that spans kilometers. They are thoughts drifting through the lattice-mind, each individual a neuron firing in patterns that create something larger than themselves.

The Segment-Walkers have become the lattice's maintenance crew, its custodians, the species that keeps the ancient structure clean and documented even though the structure needs no cleaning and there's no one left to read their documentation. They march on because marching is what they are, because their ancestors walked these beams when they still divided air, and some memory persists in their genetic code of a purpose that no longer exists.

The Tanglers have become death-necessary-death, the predators that keep populations in check, that recycle the minerals locked in Drift-Synod bodies back into the water, that even dare to eat the eternal stone itself. They are feared and necessary, the shadow that makes the drift-life precious.

The Deepform—if it exists—is the mystery that keeps everything humble, the reminder that the lattice extends beyond what you can observe, that there are scales and organizations you cannot comprehend from your limited perspective.

And the polyp-clouds are the edge of life, the place where chemistry becomes coordination, where the minimum requirements for "alive" are met and nothing more is added, efficient to the point of disappearing.

Together they have created an ecosystem from nothing, from mineral-water and ancient stone and dim light that barely reaches. They prove that life doesn't need abundance—it needs stability. Give it a constant environment and enough time, and life will elaborate itself into every possible form, will find every way to extract energy from what's available, will create relationships and dependencies and coordinations that eventually become indistinguishable from purpose.

The lattice is stable. The cold is constant. The currents will churn forever or until the ocean evaporates, whichever comes first. And so the fauna persist, in their sparse abundance, in their dim proliferation, neither thriving nor declining but existing in perfect equilibrium with their impossible home.

What lives there is what survives there. And what survives there is what has learned to need nothing more than what the lattice provides: stone to walk on, water to drift through, cold to slow all processes until they require almost no energy, darkness to hide in or ignore, and time—endless, patient time—to become whatever evolution suggests might work infinitesimally better than what came before.

They are the inheritors of the drowned city, the species that emerged after the drowning, and they will persist long after the reason for the lattice has been forgotten even by stone, long after the last human word has dissolved back into constituent atoms, long after everything that seemed permanent has changed.

The lattice endures. And so they endure. And the two facts are the same fact, have become the same fact, will remain the same fact until the universe grows cold enough that even this cold seems warm by comparison, until the lattice and its fauna and the water that connects them all achieve true equilibrium and stop, finally, their restless motion through the dark.
