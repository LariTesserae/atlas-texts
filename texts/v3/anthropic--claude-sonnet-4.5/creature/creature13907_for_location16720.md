# The Inhabitants of the Lung Valleys

What lives here does not *occupy* the Lung Valleys—it *participates* in them.

## The Tenders

You don't see them at first. They move when the light is most intense, when your eyes are already overwhelmed by the white-green radiance, and they move with a stillness that paradoxically makes them invisible. But after hours in the valleys, your vision adapts not to darkness but to *brightness*, and you begin to perceive them: figures among the succulent walls, so perfectly integrated with the plant tissue that they might be extensions of it.

They are tall—two and a half meters, perhaps three—and narrow in a way that suggests efficiency rather than fragility. Their bodies are vertical, mostly upright, though they can bend at more points than you have joints. The silhouette is vaguely humanoid: a central column, four limbs, a distinct head region. But the proportions are wrong for humanity. The legs are too long and have too many segments, folding in ways that allow them to navigate the three-dimensional maze of vegetation with an insectile precision. The arms—if they are arms—are thin and divide at the elbow into multiple forearm-equivalents, giving them a branching, twig-like quality. Three or four "hands" at the end of each arm, each hand capable of independent operation.

Their skin—if you can call it skin—is the same translucent white-green as the youngest plant tissue. Not camouflage exactly, because it isn't mimicry; it's *kinship*. Their bodies are semi-transparent in the right light, and you can see hints of internal structure: something like veins, something like bones, all suspended in that crystalline green flesh. They don't have hair or scales or any obvious texture. Their surfaces are smooth, waxy, with the same quality as the succulent walls—that sense of holding water internally, of being barely contained liquid.

The heads are elongated, sloping back from a face that has no features you would call expressive. There are eyes—you think they're eyes—two large ones, very dark, with a wet sheen to them, positioned where eyes might be but somehow taking up more of the face than eyes should. They don't blink. Between them, there might be other sensory organs; the surface of the face has subtle variations in texture, patches that could be for smell or heat-sensing or something you don't have a word for.

No mouths that you can see. No ears. No obvious sexual characteristics, no clothing to hide or reveal such things.

They move through the valleys with absolute certainty, never hesitating at intersections, never retracing steps. One hand-cluster will reach out and touch a succulent wall, press into it slightly, and you realize they're not *checking* the plant—they're *communicating* with it, or reading it, or adjusting it at some chemical level. The wall responds, imperceptibly, some internal process shifting in response to that touch.

You watch one of them approach a section of wall where the plant tissue has grown too thick, blocking what might be an important passage. The Tender doesn't cut it or break it. Instead, it places multiple hands against the surface and simply waits. Ten minutes pass. Twenty. The plant tissue begins to thin from the inside, withdrawing, redistributing its mass to other areas. The Tender moves through the resulting gap as it forms, never stopping, as if the timing was known in advance.

They are gardeners, you realize. But not gardeners who impose will upon plants—gardeners who negotiate, who suggest, who collaborate with the vegetable mass in maintaining whatever pattern this place is supposed to hold.

How many are there? Impossible to say. You might see the same individual multiple times and not know it, or see a dozen different ones and think it's the same one. They don't cluster or gather. Each one seems to occupy a territory of several valleys, moving through their space with purpose that never becomes routine because the space itself is always changing.

Sometimes you'll find evidence of their presence: a platform of woven vine that wasn't there before, positioned at exactly the right height to access a cluster of ceiling pods. A standing stone, grown from compressed plant matter, that serves as a marker or a tool or a meditation, you can't tell. A chamber hollowed inside a succulent wall, lined with that soft bioluminescent tissue, empty now but clearly maintained.

They are aware of you. You know this because once, when you reached out to touch a particularly beautiful section of translucent plant flesh, one of them was suddenly beside you—you never saw it approach—and it gently, without force, redirected your hand to a different section of the same wall. The touch of its multiple fingers was cool, slightly damp, utterly certain. Then it was gone, folding itself back into the vertical maze of growth.

The message was clear: *Not there. There.*

You touched where it indicated. The plant tissue was firmer, less delicate. You would not have damaged the other section—probably—but the Tender knew the difference between what could be touched and what was too new, too tender, too essential to whatever process was unfolding.

They don't speak. You never hear vocalizations from them, no calls or signals. But they must communicate somehow, because their work is too coordinated to be coincidental. The structures appear in patterns. The vegetation is managed with a consistency that suggests shared knowledge, shared intention.

Perhaps they communicate through the plants themselves, using the vast interconnected tissue as a medium. Perhaps the touch-based interaction you witnessed is how they exchange information, pressing meaning into plant flesh and reading it back later. Perhaps they have some sense you don't possess, operating on a spectrum or dimension you can't perceive.

Their relationship with the animals is strange. The mantis-things, the translucent spherical feeders, the pale eel-forms—none of them flee from the Tenders, but none of them approach either. They coexist in parallel, each moving through the ecosystem according to their nature. Once, you watched a Tender carefully position a fallen ceiling pod so that its split would drain into a substrate depression, creating a new pool. Within hours, the floating disc-plants had established themselves there, and the translucent sphere-creatures were tending them. Coincidence? Or husbandry at a remove, setting up conditions for other lives to fulfill themselves?

## The Visitors

There are others. Not residents—visitors. You can tell the difference.

They are more like you: smaller, more varied, with the quality of coming from somewhere else. They appear irregularly, alone or in small groups, and they don't move with the Tenders' certainty. They explore, investigate, sometimes take samples of plant tissue or substrate, sometimes sit for long hours in observation, sometimes interact with the standing stones or platforms or chambers in ways that suggest familiarity but not ownership.

Some are human, or human-enough. Others are not.

One figure you encounter is humanoid but wrong in subtle ways—joints that bend slightly backward, hands with six fingers, eyes that reflect the bioluminescent light with a metallic sheen. They wear clothing of a sort: a wrap or robe of material that has the same translucent quality as the plants, but clearly manufactured, with seams and edges. They carry a container made of hardened plant matter, and they're collecting samples of the vine secretions, carefully, with a set of delicate tools that might be biological, might be mechanical, you can't tell.

They nod at you when you pass. A gesture of acknowledgment, nothing more. They don't speak. Maybe they can't. Maybe you lack the organs to hear if they do.

Another time, you see something that is definitely not humanoid: a low, long form like a centipede scaled up to the size of a large dog, but with translucent carapace segments and too many eyes. It's examining one of the standing stones with several mouth-parts or sensor-appendages, running them over the regular grooves, pausing at intervals as if reading information. It notices you but continues its work, unconcerned.

The Visitors come, they observe or collect or interact, and they leave. You never see how they arrive or depart—the valleys are vast, and there are passages you haven't explored, gaps in the ceiling membrane that might lead elsewhere. They treat the place with a kind of reverence, or at least care. No one damages anything. No one leaves waste. They take only small samples, touch only what seems touchable.

Once, you find a group of them—three humanoids of different configurations, one of the centipede-forms, and something else that's mostly a mass of sensory tendrils in a roughly spherical arrangement—gathered around one of the chambers-inside-walls. They're not doing anything, just *being* there, together, in that soft bioluminescent space. After a while, they disperse, each moving off in a different direction through the valleys.

A meeting? A ceremony? A coincidence of timing? You have no way to know.

The Tenders tolerate the Visitors, or perhaps "tolerate" is the wrong word—they're aware of them, account for them, work around them. Once, you see a Visitor attempt to touch a section of wall that's clearly too new, too unstable, and a Tender materializes just as it did for you, redirecting the touch. The Visitor accepts the correction without resistance, as if they expected it, as if this interaction is understood by both parties.

## The Symbionts

Then there are the ones who live *in* the Tenders.

You don't notice at first. The Tenders' translucent flesh hides much, reveals much, and your eyes aren't trained to distinguish between the Tenders' internal structures and other things that move within them.

But eventually, you see: small, dark shapes visible through the skin, moving independently of the Tender's own motion. They cluster near what you think might be the Tenders' equivalent of a spine, or along the major limb-branches. They're about the size of your thumb, elongated, with multiple legs or cilia, constantly in motion inside their host.

Parasites? No—the relationship seems too stable, too long-established. The Tenders show no sign of distress. If anything, the presence of these internal creatures seems to correlate with the Tenders' health. The ones with the most symbionts move with the greatest certainty, touch the plants with the most effective adjustments.

You watch a Tender press multiple hands against a succulent wall, and you see—barely—the symbionts within its arms shifting position, clustering near where the hands make contact. Some kind of information transfer? Are the symbionts doing the actual communication with the plants, and the Tenders are merely the vehicles?

Or perhaps it's simpler: perhaps the symbionts process nutrients, or manage the Tenders' internal chemistry, or perform some other metabolic function that allows the Tenders to survive in this hot, oxygen-rich, water-saturated environment.

You never see the symbionts outside a Tender. They might be unable to survive independent existence, or perhaps they simply never leave, generation after generation living out their entire lifecycles inside these tall, translucent gardeners.

The Tenders, for their part, sometimes pause in their work and seem to... attend to the symbionts. A hand will press against their own torso, or along a leg, and you'll see the dark shapes within responding, clustering toward the touch. Care? Communication? Mutual grooming in some chemical sense?

The relationship goes both ways. The Tenders need them. They need the Tenders. Neither is complete without the other.

## The Architects (Hypothesis)

And then there's what you don't see. What might not be there to see.

Because something designed this place. The substrate didn't crystallize in this particular formation by accident. The plants didn't evolve—couldn't have evolved, not yet—to fill these specific niches this perfectly. The bioluminescent ceiling, the heat, the water cycle, the whole improbable system: it's too complete, too functional, too *young* to have developed through any process you understand.

Someone or something built the Lung Valleys. Or is building them, in the present continuous tense.

Are the Architects among the Visitors? Is one of those strange forms that passes through occasionally actually the maker, checking on their creation? Or are the Architects the Tenders themselves, in some capacity beyond their visible gardening—do they shape the place at a level deeper than pruning and encouragement?

Or—and this seems somehow most likely, though you can't say why—are the Architects something that exists at a different scale entirely? Not individuals but processes, not beings but systems. Perhaps the plants themselves are the Architects, and the valleys are self-organizing according to principles you don't possess. Perhaps the substrate is still *active* at some level, still forming, responding to pressures and templates laid down by whoever or whatever initiated this place, following instructions written in crystal patterns and biochemical gradients.

You find yourself studying the standing stones with this question in mind. The regular grooves in their surfaces—are they writing? Decoration? Or are they something else: templates, perhaps, instructions encoded in physical form. The stones are made of compressed plant matter, which means the plants *can* be manipulated to hold shapes, to retain information. Are the grooves a record of how the place should develop? Or a record of how it has developed, a history written by the system itself?

You try to match the patterns to the layout of the valleys, to the structure of the vegetation, to the distribution of the Tenders' maintained structures. Sometimes you think you see correlations. Other times it all seems random.

The platforms, the chambers, the stones—they might be facilities, created for some purpose that will only become clear when the valleys mature further. Or they might be messages: "We are here. We do this work. This is how the system functions."

Or they might be both at once.

## The Ecology (Reconsidered)

Once you accept that intelligence—multiple forms of it—is woven into the Lung Valleys, the ecosystem snaps into a different focus.

This isn't a natural place waiting to be inhabited. It's an *intentional* place being actively maintained. Every element has a role: the huge succulent walls storing water and structuring space, the vines filtering air and providing vertical habitat, the hanging pods serving some reproductive or nutrient-distribution function, the substrate providing anchorage and perhaps slow-releasing minerals or energy.

The animals aren't just fauna—they're workers, components. The mantis-forms might be pollinators or pest-controllers. The sphere-creatures are clearly tending something, managing the pool ecosystems. The eel-things might be cleaning services, or they might be distributing something the plants need through their secretions.

And the Tenders sit at the center of it all, not as rulers but as coordinators, as the intelligence that keeps the various systems in balance during this critical establishment phase.

You watch one of them work for hours. It moves through its territory, touching walls here, adjusting a vine tangle there, redirecting the fall of condensation into a new channel by subtly altering the angle of a plant surface. Small actions, each one barely significant. But cumulatively, they maintain the pattern. They keep the valleys breathing properly.

This is husbandry at the scale of landscape. This is gardening that includes geology.

And you—what are you? The Tenders don't exclude you, but they don't include you either. The Visitors treat you as one of their own, roughly speaking. You're here under some kind of informal permission, some understanding that the Lung Valleys, while maintained, are not *closed*. They can be witnessed. Perhaps they're meant to be witnessed.

## The Future (Implied)

None of this is stable in the long term. It can't be. The valleys are too young, too energetic, too perfect. Something will shift.

Perhaps the Tenders are temporary, a bootstrap species that will fade away once the ecosystem is established enough to self-maintain. Perhaps they're permanent, and what you're seeing is the beginning of a civilization that will exist here for millennia, tending these valleys as their sole purpose and way of being.

Perhaps more will come. The valleys might be early-stage terraforming, preparing a space for some other form of life that hasn't arrived yet. The Visitors might multiply, become residents, diversify the intelligence that inhabits this place.

Or perhaps this is as big as it gets: a few square kilometers of managed subsurface ecosystem, a proof of concept, a small miracle of coordination between plant and tender and substrate. Not meant to expand, just meant to *be*, demonstrating that such a thing is possible.

The Tenders know, probably. The Architects definitely know. But they're not telling you, and perhaps can't tell you, and perhaps it doesn't matter because the knowledge is written in the grooves of the standing stones, in the arrangement of the platforms, in the way the light concentrates and diffuses through layers of translucent plant flesh—written in a language that you're learning to read just by being here, just by breathing this too-rich air and walking these corridors of green and watching the tall, translucent figures move with their impossible certainty through this world that is still learning how to be.

You press your hand against a succulent wall, feeling the warmth of it, the slight dampness, the sense of something living very intensely just beneath the surface.

For a moment—maybe you imagine it—you feel something press back.

Not a Tender's hand redirecting you. Something else. The wall itself, acknowledging. The vast plant-network, aware of you through some sense you don't possess.

Or maybe the symbionts within a Tender passing nearby, their chemical signatures bleeding through the plant tissue.

Or maybe nothing at all, just your own hope that this place, for all its strangeness, recognizes that you're trying to understand it, trying to see what lives here, trying to participate—however briefly, however inadequately—in the slow, hot, luminous breathing of the Lung Valleys.
