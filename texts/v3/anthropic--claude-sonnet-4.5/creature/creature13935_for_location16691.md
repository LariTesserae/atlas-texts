# Who Lives There

## The Retained

They were the first, or claim to be. Twenty, perhaps thirty individuals scattered among the towers, identifiable by their ease—the way they move through the space without hesitation, without the constant low-level anxiety that marks newcomers. They don't look different, not physically, but they *sound* different. Their voices have a quality, a resonance, as if when they speak the landscape briefly quiets to listen.

The Retained don't explain where they came from. Ask them and they'll tell you "I've always been here," even though the towers are young, even though the city can't be more than forty years old at the absolute outside. Press them and they become confused, genuinely distressed. One woman—she lives in a cylindrical tower near the basin's western edge, alone except for the drones that roost on her balcony—tried to answer once. "I remember... before?" she said, the statement curling into a question. "But before is... it's not a when. It's a beneath." She couldn't elaborate. Spent the next hour sitting silently, trembling, while the trees screamed around her.

They have privileges. The Retained can touch the tubes without consequence—you see them sometimes, kneeling in the sand, hands pressed to the glowing conduits, faces slack with concentration. They stay like that for hours, absorbing or transmitting or communing. What they learn, they rarely share. "The water says the northern grove needs phosphates," one might mention casually, and somehow this information gets where it needs to go, and the chemistry of the rain adjusts, and the trees darken with new health.

The fauna trust them. Birds land on their shoulders. The long-legged predators approach them openly, accept scraps of food, allow themselves to be touched. There's a man who lives in a skeletal tower—all struts and exposed space, vines already claiming half of it—who keeps company with something like a bear. It's not a bear. Too many joints in the legs, eyes too forward-facing, hands too articulated. But it follows him, sleeps curled against his tower's base, and sometimes they sit together in silence, his hand on its flank, both of them watching the rain.

They sleep less than others, or differently. You'll see them active at all hours, moving between towers in the dangerous dark, carrying lights that seem dimmer than they should be, as if the darkness parts for them rather than being pushed back. They navigate by senses no one else has or by information no one else receives.

And they're grieving, all of them, constantly. You catch it in unguarded moments—a sudden stillness, eyes focused on nothing, expression crumpling before being smoothed away. They've lost something. Everyone assumes it's people, colleagues or loved ones from whatever came before, whatever established this place. But sometimes it seems like they're mourning something larger. The loss of a world, maybe. Or the loss of a way of being that can't exist here, in this landscape that demands transformation.

## The Committed

They came on purpose. Volunteers, applicants, selectees—the process is never clearly explained, but these are people who wanted to be here, who competed for the privilege. They arrive in small groups, every few months, appearing at the basin's edge with minimal gear and maximal determination.

Young, mostly. Twenties, thirties, a few teenagers accompanied by equally committed parents. They have the look of idealists, true believers, people for whom the strangeness of the Hollowgarden isn't a barrier but the entire point. They speak—at first—in the language of ecological restoration, symbiotic systems, post-human futures. "We're learning to live differently," they say. "Learning to be part of something instead of apart from it."

The landscape breaks them of abstractions quickly.

Within weeks, the rhetoric fades. You can see it happen: the moment when someone who arrived speaking of "reintegration with natural systems" is standing knee-deep in the detritus beneath a tree, covered in insects, hands bleeding from thorns, overwhelmed by the smell of rot and growth, and they simply start crying. Not from despair—from the collision of theory and reality. From understanding, suddenly and completely, that this place doesn't care about their philosophy. It simply *is*, and they must adapt or leave.

Most adapt.

They take on roles with an earnestness that would be touching if it weren't so necessary. Tower maintenance—they're the ones who spend days cleaning moss from walls, repairing damage, managing the interfaces that older inhabitants use instinctively but they must learn through repetition. Agricultural work—harvesting the tree-crops, processing them, distributing food. Documentation—endless documentation, recording species, tracking changes, building databases on systems that barely qualify as computers.

They form friendships quickly, intensely. Isolation drives it—there are never enough people, and the work is endless, and the landscape is overwhelming. Bonds form in shared exhaustion, in the vulnerability of being constantly bewildered. You see them in small groups on bridges at twilight, talking quietly, or sharing meals in tower common rooms, or working side by side in companionable silence.

Some of them pair off. Relationships here have a quality that's hard to name—not quite romance, not quite partnership, but something shaped by the place. Couples who sleep in the same tower but spend days apart, walking separate paths across the basin. Triads who pool resources, sharing living space and work. A few clusters that are larger, more fluid, configured less around intimacy and more around mutual support in a place that demands resilience.

They have children, occasionally. Cautiously. There's infrastructure for it—medical facilities in the larger towers, knowledge among the Retained about birth and infant care in this environment. But pregnancy here is strange. The mothers report dreams, vivid and recurring, of roots and wings. Their bodies change in expected ways and unexpected ones—skin that becomes briefly photosensitive, craving for foods that don't exist and must be approximated. The children, when they come, are healthy but odd. Alert in ways newborns shouldn't be. Quiet when they should cry. Watching, always watching, as if cataloging the world from their first breath.

And the Committed stay, mostly. Some leave—the landscape isn't for everyone, and there's no shame in departure—but most remain, and after a few years you can't tell them from the Retained except by direct knowledge. They've become the place. It's become them. The transformation they came seeking has occurred, though never in the ways they imagined.

## The Temporary

Scientists, researchers, documentarians—people here on terms they understand as limited. Six months, a year, two years maximum. They arrive with equipment, protocols, questions they intend to answer. They live in designated towers, clusters of habitation that have been modified for outsiders: better lighting, more soundproofing, interfaces labeled in multiple languages, supply chains for familiar foods.

They try to maintain objectivity, distance, professional detachment. It works for about three weeks.

The landscape doesn't permit observation from outside. You can't study it without entering it, and entering it means being changed by it. The researchers fight this, develop elaborate protocols to maintain boundaries. Scheduled sleep times divorced from the fauna's rhythms. Recorded messages to themselves, reviewing their original research goals. Regular team meetings to "reality-check" experiences.

But slowly, inevitably, they slip.

You see a biologist who came to study insect populations. She was rigorous initially—transects, count protocols, statistical analyses. Four months in, she's sitting beneath a tree for hours, not counting, just watching. Her notes have become impressionistic, poetic. "The swarm moves like thought," she writes. "Not the thoughts I have, but thoughts themselves, the structure beneath language." She still collects data, but she no longer trusts it. Numbers feel reductive, false. She's trying to invent new methods, new languages, new ways of knowing that can accommodate what she's experiencing.

A geologist arrived to study the substrate, the foundation structure visible in erosion cuts. He brought ground-penetrating radar, core sampling equipment, chemical analysis kits. Now the equipment sits unused in his tower while he walks the basin with his hands in his pockets, stopping occasionally to press his palms against tower walls, against tube-lines, against tree bark. "It's all connected," he says, unhelpfully. "Not like a network. Like a body. Like a single thing." He's trying to understand topology that doesn't exist in three dimensions, space that curves in ways his instruments can't measure.

A team of linguists came to study the inhabitants' language, the new vocabulary emerging here. They're making progress, in a sense—extensive recordings, grammatical analyses, etymological tracking. But they're also arguing, constantly, about whether what they're documenting is language at all or something else. One insists the wall-interface symbols aren't writing but "externalized proprioception, a way of touching the landscape's nervous system." Another thinks the bird calls have become linguistic, that fauna and humans are developing a shared communication substrate. The team leader has stopped participating in debates and spends her time learning the non-verbal communication methods, the touches and gestures and silences, the ways meaning moves here without words.

The Temporary keep detailed journals, almost obsessively. They're aware they're changing and trying to track it, to maintain some continuity of self. The journals start clinical, observational, and gradually become confessional, strange. "I don't remember my mother's face today," one entry reads. "I know I have a mother. I can recall facts about her. But the image is gone, replaced by the pattern of bark on the tree near my tower. I should be more upset about this."

Some of them extend their stays. Six months becomes a year becomes indefinite. Others leave on schedule but return within months, unable to readjust to wherever they came from. A few make the full transition, becoming Committed or something like it, their research abandoned or transformed into forms of participation indistinguishable from habitation.

The ones who leave and don't return—you wonder about them. What do they carry back? What can they say about this place that would make sense elsewhere? Their papers, when published, are dry, careful, studiously avoiding the experiences that mattered most. Between the lines, though, you can read confusion, loss, a longing for something they can't name.

## The Children

Born here, or brought here young enough that they remember nothing else. There are perhaps two dozen, ranging from infants to teenagers. They are the landscape's true natives, and they are not quite like children elsewhere.

Silent, for one thing. Not mute—they can speak, do speak—but they default to quietness in ways that unsettle adult newcomers. They communicate extensively through gesture, expression, touch. Among themselves, they seem to understand each other perfectly with minimal vocalization. Adults watching them play—if what they do can be called play—often can't follow the logic, the rules, the goals.

They move differently. Confident in the dark, in the open spaces, in the chaos beneath the trees. A ten-year-old will walk alone from tower to tower across kilometers of emptiness without hesitation, navigating by cues adults can't perceive. They climb—constantly, compulsively—up tower walls, across bridges, into the canopies where they're not technically forbidden (that prohibition is for outsiders; the children have some dispensation no one discusses).

The fauna regard them differently. Not as prey, not as threat, but almost as kin. Birds land near them, curious and unafraid. Insects settle on them without biting. One child—a girl of seven or eight, living with her mothers in a bulbous tower—is always accompanied by something like a cat, but too small, too elongated, with eyes that reflect silver. It follows her everywhere, sleeps curled beside her, and when she touches the wall-interfaces it watches intently, as if learning.

They have aptitudes that shouldn't exist yet. A five-year-old can read the wall-symbols fluently, not through teaching but through exposure, the same way he acquired language. A teenager can diagnose problems in the tube-network by touch, her hands hovering over buried lines until she locates blockages, chemical imbalances, flow disruptions. When asked how she knows, she looks confused. "It feels wrong," she says. "Can't you feel it?"

Their education is informal, sporadic, collective. Adults teach them reading, mathematics, history—though history is difficult, because the past before the Hollowgarden feels increasingly irrelevant or fictional. The children learn more from each other, from the Retained, from simple exposure to the landscape's patterns. They're developing expertise in domains that didn't exist before: swarm dynamics, symbiotic architecture, the language of targeted weather.

But they're also strange in concerning ways.

They refer to the landscape as "it" with casual familiarity, as if discussing a known person. "It wants more roots in the eastern section," a child might say, relaying information no one gave her. "It's tired today." When adults press—what is it, how do you know what it wants—the children grow frustrated, unable or unwilling to explain. The knowledge is simply available to them, obvious, like knowing when you're hungry.

They dream collectively. Not the same dreams, but dreams that reference each other, that form a kind of serial narrative. They discuss them casually: "Last night I was in the red place again," one will say, and others will nod knowingly. "Did you see the tower there?" "The one that's inside-out? Yeah." Adults listening to these conversations can't tell if it's imagination or something else, some shared mental space the landscape has given them access to.

And they're not entirely convinced that they're human, or at least not only human.

One boy—maybe twelve, thoughtful and articulate when he chooses to be—tried to explain once. "We're the first version," he said. "Of what comes next. We're still people, but we're also... we're part of it. The way the drones are part of it, or the tubes. We're nodes." He didn't seem distressed by this. It was simply observation, stated with the certainty of lived truth.

The adults worry, of course. Debate endlessly about development, socialization, what these children will become. But there's also recognition that the children are better adapted than they are, that whatever strangeness is emerging might be necessary. The landscape is raising them, or they're co-evolving with it, and interference might do more harm than good.

So the children run semi-feral, climbing and exploring and learning things that can't be taught, and the adults watch from tower windows, anxious and envious in equal measure.

## The Transients

They don't stay. Can't stay. Something about their neurology, their psychology, their fundamental orientation makes them incompatible with the place. They arrive—usually among the Committed, sometimes with the Temporary—and within days or weeks it's clear they won't make it.

The symptoms vary. Some develop persistent anxiety, a low-grade panic that never quite resolves. The noise is too much, the isolation too vast, the strangeness too constant. They stop sleeping properly, lose weight, become hypervigilant. Others go the opposite direction: lethargy, depression, a flatness of affect. They sit in their towers, barely moving, barely speaking, as if the landscape's demands have simply exhausted them into shutdown.

A few experience something like psychosis. Hallucinations, paranoia, the sense that the landscape is actively hostile, watching with malevolent intent. They report feeling pursued, targeted, though by what they can't specify. The wall-interfaces seem to mock them. The bioluminescence spells messages only they can see. Birds scream warnings meant for their ears alone.

The community tries to help, but resources are limited and expertise scarce. There's a woman—one of the Retained, living in a tower that's become an informal medical center—who has training in psychology or something like it. She sits with the Transients, talks them through their distress, offers what comfort she can. Sometimes it's enough. More often it isn't.

They leave, always. Escorted to the basin's edge by volunteers, given supplies, sent back to wherever they came from. Some go willingly, relieved. Others resist, insisting they want to stay, they just need more time, they can adapt. But the body knows. The landscape knows. The incompatibility is too fundamental.

And here's the disturbing part: no one knows why some people can adapt and others can't. There's no screening process, no test that predicts compatibility. The selection for Committed supposedly involves criteria—psychological assessments, interviews, background checks—but Transients emerge from the same pools as those who thrive. It's random, or appears to be, or operates according to logic no one has identified.

Unless it's not random. Unless the landscape itself is choosing, accepting some and rejecting others according to needs or patterns too complex to perceive. The Retained seem to believe this, though they won't quite say it. "Not everyone fits," they'll murmur. "It's not a failing. Just... not a match."

The Transients haunt the community's conscience. They represent failure, loss, the limits of the great experiment. Each departure prompts quiet discussions: Could we have done more? Should we screen more carefully? Is it ethical to keep bringing people here knowing some will break?

No conclusions. The discussions fade, and the next group arrives, and the cycle continues.

## The Visitors

Rare, irregular, appearing at intervals that might be months or years. They don't come from outside—at least, they don't arrive the way the Committed or Temporary do, on foot across the basin's edge. They're simply there, suddenly, standing in a plaza between towers or walking a bridge or sitting beneath a tree.

Not human. That's the first thing you notice. The shape is wrong—proportions slightly off, limbs that bend at unexpected angles, faces that are almost right but disturbingly not. Or they're human-shaped but clearly not made of flesh: bodies that look carved from wood or formed from living clay, surfaces that shimmer with patterns like butterfly wings or beetle carapaces.

Or they're not shaped like anything, really. Clouds of coherence, presences that exist more as distortions than forms. The air around them looks wrong, bent, as if they're lensing reality. You see them peripherally more clearly than directly.

They don't speak, or don't speak in ways that sound like language. But communication happens. If you're near one, you find yourself understanding—not words, but concepts, emotions, requests. A Visitor might approach, and you'll suddenly know it wants to be shown to a particular tree. So you walk there together, this impossible being and you, and when you arrive it does... something. Touches the bark, or merges with the root system, or simply stands present for a duration you can't later recall. And then it's gone.

The Retained treat them casually, almost with familiarity. "Oh, the clay-forms are back," someone might mention, as if discussing migrating birds. They seem to know more about the Visitors than they share—their purposes, their origins, their relationship to the landscape. But ask directly and you get the usual vagueness: "They're part of it. They've always been."

The Visitors seem particularly interested in the children. Not in threatening ways—they approach slowly, carefully, and the children don't seem frightened. They interact with something like recognition, as if meeting distant relatives. A Visitor might crouch beside a child (assuming it has a form that can crouch), and they'll sit together for an hour, not speaking, not touching, just being proximate. Afterward, the child can't explain what happened but seems content, enriched somehow.

Sometimes the Visitors leave things behind. Objects that don't make sense: a perfect sphere of what looks like glass but isn't, warm to the touch, that hums at a frequency you feel in your bones. A piece of fabric that changes color based on no input you can detect. A tool whose purpose is unclear but that fits perfectly in the hand, begging to be used for something. These objects circulate through the community, studied, handled, eventually integrated. The glass sphere ends up in a tower's common room, where it sits on a shelf, humming. The fabric becomes part of someone's clothing. The tool gets used for maintenance work, and it's perfect for tasks no one could have predicted.

And occasionally—very occasionally—a Visitor doesn't leave.

There's one that has been present for... how long? Time is slippery. Years, certainly. It resembles a heron, if a heron were made of bundled light-fibers and stood three meters tall. It lives, if that's the right word, in a skeletal tower that was abandoned but is now somehow occupied. It doesn't eat or sleep or perform any recognizable life function. It simply stands, utterly still, sometimes for days at a time. And then it moves—a sudden, flowing motion—to a different position, a different tower, a different tree. It's mapping something, or surveying, or participating in some process no one understands.

People leave offerings near it. Food, at first, until it became clear it didn't eat. Then flowers from the trees, interesting stones, art objects. The Visitor ignores them all, but the gesture feels important. Acknowledgment. Welcome. A way of saying: you're strange, but so is everything here, and you're part of the community now, however alien.

The children named it Meridian. No one remembers which child suggested the name, but it stuck. Meridian the Visitor, the guest that never leaves, the entity that watches over the Hollowgarden with eyes that might not be eyes, for purposes that might not be purposes.

## The Lost

They don't have a proper name. The community doesn't like to discuss them. But they're there, in the landscape, known through glimpses and signs and the occasional encounter that leaves everyone shaken.

People who stayed too long in the wrong places. Who walked into the zones where the air feels thick, where the network has knotted, and didn't come back. Or came back changed so profoundly they're no longer recognizable as what they were.

You see them sometimes, in the distance. Figures moving across the basin in the deep night, but moving wrongly—too fast, or too smooth, or in patterns that aren't quite linear. By the time you think to call out, to follow, they're gone. Or you find evidence: footprints that start at nothing and end at nothing, in sand that should show approach and departure but shows only a single set of tracks, walking in a circle.

Or you're beneath a tree, and you sense presence—not fauna, not Visitor, but something else. Someone else. A shape in the canopy that's almost human but integrated somehow, merged with the biomass, looking down at you with eyes that reflect the bioluminescence like an animal's. You freeze. It freezes. And then it's gone, or you realize it was never there, or you can't remember what you saw well enough to describe it.

The Retained know more. They have to. Sometimes they leave offerings in specific locations: food, water, tools. The offerings disappear. No one sees who takes them, but they're gone by morning, and there's a sense of exchange, of obligation fulfilled.

There's a theory, whispered among the Committed, that the Lost aren't lost at all. That they've completed the transformation everyone else is undergoing slowly. That they've become what the landscape wants, full nodes in its network, no longer separate enough to qualify as individual humans. They're still conscious, maybe. Still themselves, in some sense. But operating on a substrate that isn't purely biological anymore, integrated into the mycelial networks and root systems and the strange technology in the substrate.

If true, it's either transcendence or horror, depending on your perspective.

Some of the Committed find it aspirational. They speak quietly about "going deep," about "full integration," as if it's a goal to work toward. Others are disturbed, considering it a fate to be avoided, proof that the landscape takes too much.

The children seem unconcerned. When asked about the Lost, they shrug. "They're fine," one will say. "Just... elsewhere." And they'll refuse to elaborate, as if it's not worth explaining, as if the adults should already understand.

There's a tower—no one lives in it—where the Lost sometimes appear. Always at night, always briefly. They stand in the entrance or on the balcony, visible from neighboring towers. They don't move, don't call out. They just stand, present and watching, until they fade back into the darkness. Residents have learned to acknowledge them: a raised hand, a nod. Recognition without approach. The boundary is clear and must be maintained. They're still part of the community, but they exist in a different relationship to it now.

And very rarely, one of them communicates. Not in words—they seem to have moved beyond language—but through the interfaces, through patterns in the bioluminescence, through the behavior of fauna that suddenly becomes coordinated and deliberate. A message, usually: something needs attention, a tube is failing, a tree is stressed. Information that helps, that maintains. They're still contributing, still connected, but from inside the landscape in ways the living inhabitants can't be.

The community has developed rituals around them. Offerings, yes. But also songs—simple, wordless melodies that get sung at twilight, directed toward the places the Lost are thought to be. The Retained initiated this practice, and the Committed have maintained it. No one's sure it means anything, but it feels necessary. A way of saying: we remember you, we honor your transformation, you're not forgotten.

And sometimes, in the deep night, if you're alone and listening carefully, you hear singing back. Not in human voices—something else, something that uses the wind and the insects and the rustling leaves as instruments. Harmonies that shouldn't work but do. Music that comes from the landscape itself.

The Lost, singing home.

## What They All Share

Despite their differences—the Retained with their mysterious origins, the Committed with their idealism, the Temporary with their protocols, the children with their strangeness, the Transients with their incompatibility, the Visitors with their alienness, the Lost with their transformation—there are commonalities.

They're all quiet. Even the children, even the newly arrived. The landscape enforces it, or teaches it, or selects for it. Loud people don't last. Not because of rules, but because loudness here feels obscene, violent, a kind of assault against the overwhelm that already exists.

They all touch things constantly. Walls, tubes, trees, each other. The place demands tactile engagement; visual and auditory information isn't enough. You learn through your skin here, through pressure and temperature and vibration. Conversation between any two people often involves one hand on the other's shoulder, maintaining circuit, grounding meaning in physical connection.

They all have gaps in their memory. Not pathological amnesia, but a softening of the past, a difficulty maintaining clear recall of life before. The landscape occupies cognitive space, demands so much attention and processing that long-term memory becomes less precise, more impressionistic. People remember feelings more than facts, atmospheres more than events.

They all dream vividly. Every one of them, even those who rarely dreamed before arriving. The landscape penetrates sleep, fills it with imagery that's lush and strange and often shared. Partners wake to discover they dreamed of the same tree. Strangers discover overlapping narratives. The boundary between individual and collective unconscious is porous here, weakening.

And they all change. Not uniformly, not predictably, but everyone who stays longer than a month is different at the end than at the beginning. Some changes are subtle: shifts in preference, new skills, altered sleep patterns. Others are profound: changes in values, identity, fundamental worldview. The landscape doesn't erase who you were, but it adds so much to you that the proportions shift. You become a smaller part of a larger self.

They all feel it—the presence, the intelligence, the something underneath everything. They differ in how they interpret it: as emergent complexity, as designed system, as living entity, as distributed consciousness, as divine immanence. But the experience is universal. The landscape is aware. The landscape responds. The landscape is not simply a place but a being, and living here means living inside it, as cell lives inside body.

And none of them can imagine leaving permanently.

Even the Transients, forced out by incompatibility, talk about returning. "Maybe later," they say. "When I'm stronger." The Temporary extend their stays or promise to come back. The Committed are committed. The children can't conceive of elsewhere—this is the world; everywhere else is abstract, theoretical, possibly fictional.

The Hollowgarden keeps them.

Not through coercion, not through barriers or force. Through transformation. By the time you've adapted enough to live here, you've changed enough that you belong here, and belonging becomes identical with being. Your self and the place are no longer clearly separable. You've been woven in.

And in quiet moments, usually at twilight, usually alone, they all wonder: is this what was intended? Did someone—the Retained, whoever came before them, the builders of the substrate foundation—plan this transformation? Or has the landscape exceeded its design, evolved beyond its purpose, become something its creators didn't predict?

And the question underneath that one, the question no one quite asks aloud: if we're being changed, absorbed, integrated... what are we becoming? What is the landscape building us into? What happens when the transformation is complete?

No answers. Only the sound of the trees, the targeted rain, the glow of bioluminescence in the gathering dark. The work continues. The children grow. New towers rise. The roots spread.

And deep in the substrate, in the foundation beneath everything, something vast and patient and unutterably strange continues whatever process it began decades or centuries or millennia ago, adding each new inhabitant to itself, neuron by neuron, node by node, building toward a completion no one can imagine and everyone can feel approaching.

The Hollowgarden grows.

And so do they.
