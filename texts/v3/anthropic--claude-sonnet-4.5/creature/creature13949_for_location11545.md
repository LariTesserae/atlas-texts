# The Pneumatics

They are not quite human anymore, though they remain pneumatic—breath-driven, air-centered, their lives organized around respiration in ways that would seem pathological anywhere else. Here, in the perpetual humidity, the constant condensation, the pressure-shifting hollow, breathing has become something more than autonomic function. It is how they mark time, measure distance, communicate status, navigate space. They are the people the cathedral made, shaped across generations by its particular atmosphere.

There are eleven of them currently. The number oscillates between eight and fourteen, has done so for as long as the group's oral memory extends—which is not particularly far, since they don't value history the way outsiders do. An individual who can no longer remember their childhood is still young here. Memory before breath is irrelevant.

They are tall and thin in a way that suggests not malnutrition but optimization. Their ribcages are disproportionately large, barrel-shaped, the intercostal spaces wide. When they breathe deeply—which is often—you can count ribs through their skin. Their shoulders are narrow but their backs are broad, all the width in the posterior thorax, accommodation for lungs that have expanded over generations to process the thick, moisture-laden air more efficiently.

Their skin: pale brown-gray, the color of the aged root-wood, matte-textured. It doesn't seem to produce much oil. In the constant humidity, this matters—they don't become slick or pungent the way unacclimatized visitors do. Sweat when it comes is watery, evaporates quickly despite the dampness, as if their pores have learned a trick of local thermodynamics. Their hair is fine and sparse, worn long by some, cropped short by others, no apparent pattern or meaning to the choice. Color varies from dark brown to gray-black, occasionally a strange greenish-tinted black that might be staining from constant contact with wet moss rather than pigmentation.

Their eyes are disproportionately large, the sclera faintly blue-white like skim milk, the irises amber or gray-green. They can see in the filtered low light with uncanny precision—they navigate the hollow in near-darkness without hesitation, place their feet on the root-floor with confidence when visitors would be shuffling and uncertain. But in full sunlight, if they ever encountered it, they would likely be pained, night-adapted as they are to the cathedral's perpetual dimness.

Their faces are long, with pronounced nasal structures—not broad-nostrilled but projecting, ridged, complex internally. You can sometimes hear a faint whistling when they breathe through their noses, as if the air is being shaped, filtered, processed through baffles and channels. Their mouths are wide, lips thin, teeth—when they smile, which is rare but not unknown—surprisingly small and even, more like a child's permanent teeth than an adult's.

They move with deliberate slowness, not from weakness but from precision. Each step is placed after a pause, however brief, to assess the root-floor's surface. They flow between the pillars like the condensation mist, rarely in straight lines, following paths that seem to track acoustic nodes or air pressure gradients rather than geometric efficiency. Watching them move, you realize they're reading the space in ways you cannot, navigating by cues invisible to the unacclimatized.

Their clothing is minimal and strange. Most wear what looks like a single garment—a wrapped length of cloth, undyed, that might be cotton or might be beaten bark-fiber, it's hard to tell. The fabric is always slightly damp, darkened by absorbed moisture, molded to their bodies. Some wear it as a skirt-like wrap around the waist and legs. Others drape it over one shoulder and across the torso. A few go effectively naked except for a narrow strip around the hips. There seems to be no modesty concern, no sexual differentiation in dress—the choices appear purely pragmatic, related to the wearer's current activity or preference.

They wear no shoes. Their feet are broad, the toes prehensile, gripping the root-floor with visible strength. The soles are thick-callused but not crude—you can watch their toes articulate independently, feeling for purchase, adapting to the wood's contours. When they climb—which they do frequently, ascending the pillars using barely-visible irregularities in the bark—their feet are as functional as their hands.

Their hands: long-fingered, the joints prominent, always slightly damp like everything else. The fingertips are broad, almost spatulate, the nails kept short by constant wear rather than cutting. They touch things constantly—running fingers along a pillar as they pass, trailing a hand through condensation, brushing moss, touching each other briefly on the shoulder, the arm, the back. The touching seems communicative but not linguistic, more like echolocation made tactile.

They speak, but not frequently. Their language—if it is a single language—consists of words that sound like breathing exercises. Long aspirated syllables, clicks formed far back in the throat, hummed tones held for several seconds. Many of their utterances seem to occur on the inhale rather than the exhale, a reversed speech that gives their conversations a gasping, tidal quality. They can communicate quite complex ideas this way, but they choose not to most of the time. They are comfortable with silence the way fish are comfortable with water.

When they do speak, their voices are quiet, pitched to carry in the hollow's strange acoustics. They've learned which spots amplify sound, which deaden it. A conversation might involve two people standing thirty meters apart, speaking in whispers that reach each other clearly via reflections off the pillars, while someone standing between them hears nothing. They play the space like an instrument.

They sing, occasionally. Not songs as an outsider would recognize them—no melody, no rhythm you could tap your foot to. Long sustained tones that shift gradually in pitch, overlapping with other voices to create slow-moving chords. The effect is less like music than like hearing the wind-rush from above concentrated and purified into pure tone. They sing to mark certain moments—dawn, though the light barely changes; dusk, same caveat; the generator starting up; the return of the bats. Sometimes they sing for no apparent reason, a single voice starting, others joining or not, the sound building and fading over ten or fifteen minutes.

Their singing attracts the macaques, who fall silent and watch. The parrots sometimes mimic fragments of it, their vocal apparatus producing garbled approximations that the Pneumatics seem to find either funny or pleasing—they smile, anyway, or do the thing with their faces that might be smiling.

They sleep communally, piled together on the broader bracket fungi or in the fern masses, limbs entangled, breathing synchronized. Watch them sleep and you see their chests rise and fall in unison, as if they're sharing a single respiratory system. They don't seem to dream—no rapid eye movement, no sleep talking or twitching. They drop into sleep completely and rise from it the same way, the transition nearly instantaneous.

Their diet: vegetarian by necessity, this space provides little meat. They eat the mushrooms extensively, raw or briefly cooked on heated stones. They harvest the orchids, eating both flowers and the thick pseudo-bulbs at their base. They collect fruits from the canopy—climbing up into that green darkness and returning hours later with bags woven from liana fiber, full of small acidic berries and larger starchy pods. They eat constantly but in tiny amounts, grazing rather than sitting down to meals. You rarely see one eating alone; they pass food between them, offering pieces to each other, to the macaques, occasionally even to visitors.

The fiddleheads when they emerge are apparently a delicacy. The Pneumatics gather them by the hundreds, blanch them briefly in water pumped fresh and heated in metal bowls scavenged from whoever left the machines. They eat them in handfuls, the green juice running down their chins, and for a few weeks become almost animated, chattering more, moving faster.

They drink constantly. Not from the pump usually, but from the condensation pools, from water collected in bromeliad cups, from moisture squeezed out of moss. They cup their hands and drink, they lower their faces and lap like animals, they suck water from saturated cloth. The humidity is insufficient for their needs apparently; they require actual liquid, liters per day, more than seems reasonable for people who move so little.

Their metabolism must be strange. They generate little heat—you can stand next to one and feel almost no warmth radiating from their body. Yet they don't shiver in the cool hollow. They seem to exist at a lower operating temperature than normal humans, their biochemistry adapted to run efficiently in the constant coolness. When the generator runs and they stand near it, warming themselves, they become sluggish, almost drowsy, as if the heat is intoxicating.

They maintain the machines, though "maintain" might be too strong a word. They operate them, certainly. You'll see one approach the generator, spend several minutes adjusting things—opening the fuel petcock, advancing the throttle, checking the oil level with a wooden dipstick. Then they'll turn the crank, that explosive moment of ignition, and the generator will stutter to life. The Pneumatic will stand there for a moment, breathing in the exhaust fumes—which seems unhealthy but they actively seek it out, as if the diesel smoke provides something they need. Then they'll walk away, leaving the generator to run unattended until it consumes its fuel load and stops.

The elevators: they ride them individually, never in groups. One person will approach a platform, start the engine with a practiced sequence of adjustments and pulls, then stand motionless as the platform shudders upward. They ride with absolute stillness, not holding the cage bars, just standing centered, breathing steadily. Up into the canopy they go, disappearing into the green darkness. What they do up there is unclear—gathering food presumably, or tending to something, or perhaps just spending time in that other space, that upper cathedral in the canopy where light is different and the wind is direct rather than filtered.

They descend the same way, alone, the engine hammering, the platform shuddering. They step off and walk away without apparent accomplishment or satisfaction. The act seems to be its own purpose.

The pump gets less use. They prefer collected water to pumped water, for reasons they haven't explained and probably couldn't in any language available. But occasionally one will spend an hour at the pump, working the long handle in a steady rhythm, breathing synchronized to the motion. Inhale as the handle rises, exhale as it falls. The water gushes from the spout, runs across the stone platform, drains away through gaps in the root-floor. Where it goes, what purpose this serves—unclear. The Pneumatic pumping will eventually stop, step back, breathe heavily for a few moments, then walk away. The pump drips. The stone platform stays wet.

They are not incurious, but their curiosity is specific. They examine the fungi closely, bending down to peer at the gills of a new mushroom, touching it gently, sometimes bringing it to their nose or mouth. They watch the macaques attentively, following their movements, occasionally mimicking their vocalizations—not mockingly, experimentally, as if trying to understand the grammar of hooting. They listen to the birds intensely, their heads tilted, their eyes tracking invisible sounds.

But they don't seem interested in where things come from or why things are as they are. The machines exist, so they operate them. Food grows, so they eat it. The hollow is the hollow. Questions about origins or purposes don't appear to arise. They live entirely in the present tense, in the immediate conditions of the space.

They are not hostile to visitors, but they're not precisely welcoming either. An outsider arriving in the hollow will be observed but not greeted. The Pneumatics will go about their activities—eating, singing, climbing, operating machinery—as if the visitor were another tree rat, notable but not alarming. If the visitor speaks to them, they might respond, though the response will be in their breath-language, incomprehensible. If the visitor stays long enough, one of the Pneumatics might approach, might touch them briefly, then walk away. This seems to be the extent of the social protocol.

Over time—days, weeks—the Pneumatics will slowly incorporate a long-term visitor into their awareness. They'll offer food unprompted. They'll make space in their sleeping pile. They'll include the visitor in their singing, making space in the chord for another voice even if that voice can't properly produce the required tones. But they won't explain anything. There's no orientation, no cultural transmission, no teaching. You learn by observing, by attempting, by slowly attuning to the same cues they use—the acoustic nodes, the air pressure shifts, the quality of light at different hours.

Some visitors stay. This seems to happen unpredictably. Someone arrives, spends time in the hollow, and simply doesn't leave. They begin to change—walking slower, breathing more deliberately, their speech becoming less frequent. Over months they grow thinner, paler. Their eyes adjust. They start sleeping in the communal pile. Within a year or two, they're indistinguishable from the others, one more Pneumatic moving between the pillars.

Others leave. They spend a few days or weeks, then depart, resuming whatever life they had outside. The Pneumatics don't prevent this. They don't encourage it either. People come and go like the parrots, like the weather. The hollow continues.

Children are rare. In the current group of eleven, there are no infants, one child of perhaps seven or eight years old, a few adolescents. The rest are adults of indeterminate age—they could be thirty or sixty, it's genuinely hard to tell. They age differently here, or perhaps they age normally but the markers are different. No sun damage, no weathering, just a gradual thinning, a slight increase in joint prominence, hair going gray-green earlier than expected.

The child moves like the adults, with the same deliberation, the same acoustic awareness. They seem to emerge already acculturated, already knowing the hollow's rules. There's no visible parenting, no instruction. The child eats with whoever's eating, sleeps in the pile, climbs the pillars with startling competence. They're quieter than children elsewhere, but not unhappy. They sing with a pure, piercing tone that cuts through the adults' harmonics.

Where new Pneumatics come from—whether they're born here or only arrive from outside and transform—isn't clear. Probably both. The group needs genetic input from somewhere, and isolated populations don't sustain themselves across generations. But tracking lineages would require the kind of record-keeping the Pneumatics don't do. Ask one about their parents and they might point at another person, or at the hollow itself, or simply exhale, a long slow breath that means nothing or everything.

They have no possessions beyond the cloth wraps, the woven bags for food gathering, a few tools—stone knives, wooden scrapers, bits of metal salvaged from the machines' periphery. These items are communal, left wherever they were last used. Need a knife? Look around, there's usually one nearby. The concept of ownership seems absent. Even the cloth wraps get exchanged, people picking up whichever one is dry enough or close enough when they want it.

They create very little. No art objects, no decorative impulses. The space itself is their aesthetic achievement, though they didn't make it—they've just learned to inhabit it optimally. Occasionally you'll find arrangements that might be intentional: a circle of stones on a bracket fungus, mushrooms laid out in a pattern, moss stripped from a pillar in a regular grid. But these could be functional, or the work of the macaques, or pure coincidence. The Pneumatics don't claim them.

Their spirituality, if they have one, is invisible. They don't pray, don't make offerings, don't mark the space with shrines or symbols. They might be profoundly religious or utterly secular; there's no way to tell. The singing has a devotional quality, but it might just be singing. Their attention to breath could be meditative or purely practical. They live as if the hollow is not sacred but simply sufficient—which might be the same thing.

When one of them dies—which happens, though not frequently, the population is stable but not static—the body is placed at the base of a root-pillar and left. The fungi and insects and bacteria do their work. Within weeks the body is gone, reabsorbed into the substrate. The other Pneumatics don't mark the spot, don't mourn visibly, don't speak about the deceased. Someone who was present is now absent. The group continues.

New individuals appear the same way, in reverse. One day there are ten Pneumatics. The next day, eleven. The new one is walking around, eating mushrooms, breathing, indistinguishable from the others except by unfamiliarity. Where they came from: somewhere in the canopy, or the forest outside, or they were always here and you simply didn't notice them. The Pneumatics themselves don't explain.

They are deeply attuned to the hollow's rhythms. They know when the generator will run before it runs—you can see them gathering near it minutes before someone goes to start it. They know when the elevators will rise, when the macaques will descend for water, when the hornbills will fly through. Whether they're controlling these events or predicting them or simply synchronized with them is impossible to determine. Causality gets strange here, in the acoustic space, in the filtered light, among people who breathe together.

They seem to hear things others don't. They'll stop mid-motion, head tilted, listening to something far above or far below. Then they'll resume movement, or change direction, or begin climbing. Follow one during these moments and you'll often find they've led you to something—a newly fruiting mushroom cluster, a bromeliad full of collected water, a gap in the canopy where sunlight is streaming through more directly than usual. But whether they heard these things or knew them or chose them randomly: unknowable.

Their sense of smell is acute. You'll see one approach a pillar, nose nearly touching the bark, breathing deeply, then moving to a different pillar to smell it as well. They're reading something in the scent—moisture content, fungal activity, the macaques' territorial marking, something. They can locate food in complete darkness apparently, tracking scent trails across the root-floor.

They touch each other constantly, as mentioned, but the touching has nuance. A hand on the shoulder means one thing, a hand on the upper back another, a brief touch on the forearm something else. The vocabulary is subtle, the grammar invisible to outsiders. But the Pneumatics respond to these touches immediately—changing direction, stopping, starting, joining or leaving an activity. It's communication, paralinguistic, functioning at the speed of nerve conduction.

They don't fight. Conflict might exist but it's not expressed physically. You never see a Pneumatic strike another, or even gesture aggressively. Disagreements, if they occur, must be resolved through the breath-language or the touching vocabulary or simply by one party walking away. The group is not peaceful in the sense of being pacifist—it's peaceful in the sense of being without conflict, which is different and stranger.

Sexual activity happens but is not prominent. Occasionally you'll see two or three Pneumatics entangled more intimately than the usual sleeping pile, moving slowly, breathing heavily, clearly engaged in something. Then they stop, separate, rejoin the group's general flow. There's no pair-bonding visible, no jealousy, no particular preference that persists over time. Sex seems to be another communal activity, like eating or sleeping, engaged in when it arises and dropped when it doesn't.

Gender itself is ambiguous. The Pneumatics have sexual characteristics—breasts or not-breasts, genital variations—but these don't map to behavior or status or role. Everyone climbs, everyone gathers food, everyone sleeps in the pile, everyone sings. The child is raised by the group without visible primary caregivers. The breath-language apparently doesn't gender its speakers; there are no pronouns you can identify as gendered, no titles or forms of address that differentiate.

They are not innocent. They know what they're doing, have chosen this life or something close to choice. When a visitor prepares to leave, you sometimes see a Pneumatic watch them with an expression that might be pity or relief or envy—something complex, anyway, that suggests they understand there are other ways to live and have actively rejected them.

They remember the outside, at least the ones who came from there originally. Ask about it—in gesture, in trade-pidgin, whatever language is available—and you might get a response. One will make a sweeping gesture: all of that, out there, too much. They'll tap their chest, breathe out slowly: here, enough. They won't elaborate. They've made their assessment and don't need to justify it.

The machines are legacy, that much is clear. The Pneumatics didn't install them, don't fully understand them, but maintain them through inherited practice. Someone long ago built the elevators, the generator, the pump. Someone taught the then-Pneumatics how to operate them. That knowledge has persisted through generations, degraded somewhat—they probably don't know what the machines were originally for, what project they served—but functional enough to keep the equipment running.

The lag screws bolting the elevator tracks to the pillars have been there so long the wood has grown around them, engulfing the heads, making removal impossible without destroying the tree. The machines are permanent now, as much a part of the hollow as the moss. The Pneumatics are their keepers, though neither party benefits much from the relationship. The machines run because they always have. That's sufficient reason.

At night, in the communal sleeping pile, the Pneumatics' breathing is extraordinary. Twenty-two lungs, eleven people, all synchronized to the same rhythm. Deep inhale—the pile expanding, ribs spreading. Hold. Long exhale—the pile contracting, settling. The sound fills the hollow more completely than the generator, than the wind-rush above. It's tidal, oceanic, the sound of massive slow systems exchanging fluids. If you lie among them—and they'll make space if you're accepted—your own breathing will synchronize within minutes. You can't help it. The rhythm is too strong, too present. You become one more lung in the collective respiratory system.

This is their achievement: perfect attunement to the space. They have become instruments of the hollow, sensors and actuators, living components in a system that was already complete before they arrived. They don't disturb the equilibrium. They've inserted themselves into it so precisely that removing them would create a gap, a silence, a wrongness. The macaques expect them. The birds are accustomed to their movements. The moss probably grows differently where they touch it regularly. They've made themselves necessary through absolute integration.

They are not interesting in the way stories are interesting. They don't have arcs or development or conflicts to resolve. They simply persist, breathing, eating, moving, singing, maintaining. They've achieved a stable state, like the banyan itself. They've stopped happening except in the sense that continuation is a form of happening.

Watch them long enough and you stop seeing them as people, start seeing them as phenomena—like the condensation, like the light-shafts, like the acoustic nodes. They're just another aspect of the hollow, no more or less strange than shelf fungus or diesel engines. They belong the way everything here belongs: completely, without question, as expression of the place's deep grammar.

And then one will turn and look directly at you, and you'll see intelligence there, awareness, the vast complex interior life that makes a person a person. And you'll realize they haven't stopped being human. They've just become human in the only way that works here, in the filtered light, in the constant humidity, in the hollow within the ancient wood. They've solved the equation of this particular existence.

This is what people become when given enough time in this space. This is what emptiness looks like when breathed by humans. This is the sound of equilibrium embodied: constant motion that goes nowhere, change that preserves form, loudness that surrounds silence.

They are the Pneumatics. They live here. They always have, even the ones who arrived yesterday. They always will, even the ones who will leave tomorrow. The hollow made them necessary. They answered by becoming inevitable.

Stand among them at dawn, in the communal pile, breathing together, and you'll understand: there's nothing to explain. They are here because here is where they are. That's the complete answer. Everything else is elaboration.
