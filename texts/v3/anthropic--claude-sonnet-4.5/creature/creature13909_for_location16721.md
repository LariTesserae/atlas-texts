# Those Who Dwell in the Breathing Canopy

## The Descended

They call themselves the Descended, though whether this refers to their origins or their daily relationship with gravity is a question they answer differently depending on who asks. They are human—or human-adjacent, or human-derived—though generations in the cold green light have begun to mark them in subtle ways.

Their skin tends toward pallor, but not the pallor of sun-deprivation. Something else. A faint translucence in certain lights, as if the green brightness has learned to pass through them the way it passes through the canopy above. Hold a Descended's hand up to one of those surgical beams of focused light and you might see the shadows of bones more clearly than you should, see the blood moving in capillaries drawn in delicate dark tracery. Their children are born with this quality more pronounced, each generation slightly more permeable to the particular frequencies that filter through miles of living chlorophyll.

They move with a deliberateness that isn't quite caution, isn't quite ceremony. It's the gait of people who learned to walk on surfaces that grow, that shift incrementally day by day, who learned as infants that stable ground is a temporary condition. They place their feet with attention, feel the surface's response before committing their weight. Even on stone—especially on stone—they maintain this testing quality, as if they've forgotten how to trust anything that doesn't grow.

Their clothing is layered for the constant cold, but the layers are thin, numerous, adjustable. They wear what looks like seven or eight garments where someone from above-world might wear two, each piece simple—a wrapped length of processed bark-fiber, a vest of felted moss-material, a cape of woven root-runners still retaining some flexibility. The layering allows them to add or subtract as they move between the microclimates: colder in the open voids, warmer near the dense tangles, coldest in those strange pockets where temperature drops without reason. Their outermost layers often show green staining from constant contact with moss and lichen, as if they're slowly becoming part of the landscape they move through.

They go mostly unshod, or wear only thin wrappings around their feet. Their soles have thickened into something like leather, but flexible leather, sensitive leather. They need to feel what they're walking on—need to detect the subtle give of living wood versus dead, need to sense the grain and texture that tells them how much weight a surface will bear, need to feel the warning vibration when something structural is about to give way.

Their hands show the wear of rope and wood and stone. Callused, yes, but also scarred in patterns that suggest specific repeated tasks: the webbing between thumb and forefinger thickened from gripping rope in particular ways, the fingertips slightly flattened from testing surfaces, the palms marked with old burns from the careful fires they maintain. Many are missing a finger—usually the last joint of the little finger on the left hand—though whether this is from accident or ritual is unclear. When asked, they touch the shortened digit and say "the offering" or "the price" or simply shrug as if the question is foolish.

## The Rope-Dancers

Among the Descended are those who specialize in movement through the vertical, who have made an art and science of traversing the three-dimensional maze of hanging root-masses. They're called Rope-Dancers, though they rarely dance and often work with no rope at all.

They're identifiable by their build—leaner than others, their muscles developed in specific patterns. Their shoulders carry pronounced definition from decades of supporting their own weight with their arms. Their legs are strong but different from a ground-walker's strength; their power is in the climbing muscles, the gripping muscles, the balance-maintainers. Their toes can spread and grip with an almost prehensile quality. Watching them move through the root-tangles is watching someone for whom the vertical is as natural as the horizontal, for whom up and down are simply directions like north and south, no more or less difficult to travel.

They wear less than others despite the cold, their layered garments abandoned for minimal coverage—usually just a wrapped loincloth and a chest binding, leaving their limbs free. Their skin shows the contact history of their work: scraped patterns where bark has abraded them, shine-marks where rope has burnished them, the odd smooth patches of old friction burns healed into slick scar tissue.

They carry their tools on their bodies in carefully distributed weights. Coiled rope worn across the torso. Carved wooden spikes on a belt, each spike shaped for driving into specific types of wood-matter. Small hooks of bone and metal. A pouch of some powdery substance—ground bark, perhaps, or dried moss—that they dust on their hands for grip. Everything positioned so it won't shift or swing as they move, won't throw off their center of gravity as they hang from fingertips or brace between two surfaces.

The Rope-Dancers maintain the routes. When a passage closes from new growth, they find or create another. When a bridge-root breaks, they install the replacement—sometimes by lashing new ropes, sometimes by training living runners to grow in useful directions, encouraging them with careful pruning and by anchoring their tips where they're needed. They mark the safe paths with subtle signs: a pattern of notches, a certain type of knot in a rope-rail, arrangements of small stones that mean something to those who know the language.

They also serve as messengers, carrying word between the scattered holdings faster than anyone else could. A Rope-Dancer can cover in half a day what might take a normal walker two days, moving through the vertical shortcuts, knowing which root-masses to climb over and which to go around, understanding the geometry of the hanging landscape in ways that others don't.

But they pay a price. Few Rope-Dancers live past forty. The work breaks them in specific ways. Their joints wear out—shoulders especially, but also hips and knees from the constant dynamic tension. They develop tremors in their hands from the endless micro-adjustments of balance. Many go suddenly, a fall from a height too great to survive, their bodies never recovered from the deep haze below, or recovered weeks later by others, broken on some stone outcrop. The Descended accept this. The Rope-Dancers accept this. It's the price, like the missing finger-joint, like the cold, like living in a place that grows faster than you can learn it.

## The Listeners

There are individuals among the Descended who have developed—or been born with, or trained into—an unusual sensitivity to sound. They're called Listeners, and they serve functions that are part sentinel, part oracle, part something else that doesn't have a name in languages from above-world.

Listeners dress similarly to others but always include some element that covers their eyes. A strip of dark cloth wrapped around the head. A hood pulled low. A wooden mask carved smooth and featureless. This isn't because they're blind—most aren't—but because they learned that seeing interferes with the quality of hearing they need. The visual distraction pulls them out of the acoustic space they're trying to inhabit.

They position themselves at the scattered outposts, usually on the stone towers or on prominent root-masses that extend far from the main tangles. They stand, sit, or lie still for hours, their heads slightly tilted in that universal posture of focused listening. They're monitoring the space, reading the acoustic information that travels through the cold bright air.

What do they listen for? Everything. The sound-signatures of different fauna—the clicks of the arthropod-things, the calls of the strange birds, the low frequencies that might be something larger moving through the deep tangles. The acoustic signs of structural stress: a root-mass groaning in a pattern that suggests it's about to tear free, the subtle change in echo-patterns that means a passage has closed or opened, the snap and crack of growth happening fast enough to be dangerous.

But also more subtle things. They claim they can hear weather forming in the far distances, can detect the acoustic properties of mist condensing, can identify which water-drip sites have started or stopped flowing by the change in the background percussion. They can supposedly hear human movement across kilometers, can distinguish between the footfalls of someone they know and a stranger, can count how many people are in a group by the complexity of the sound-patterns their movement creates.

Other Descended treat them with a mixture of respect and unease. Listeners are necessary—their warnings have saved lives, their information about the far territories is valuable, their ability to locate lost individuals is sometimes miraculous. But there's something unsettling about them. The way they'll suddenly tilt their heads in the middle of conversation, focusing on something no one else can hear. The way they speak about the sounds: describing them with the kind of emotional language others might use for visual beauty, calling a particular echo "lonely" or a drip-pattern "angry."

Some Listeners develop the ability to echolocate, making specific clicking sounds with their mouths or tapping carved resonators and reading the returning sounds for spatial information. The most skilled can navigate through complete darkness this way, moving through root-tangles without sight, without touch, only the acoustic map they're building in real-time from their own generated sounds and the way space shapes those sounds.

They pay their own price. Many Listeners develop a sensitivity to noise that becomes painful—ordinary sounds that others ignore becoming overwhelming, forcing them to retreat into the quietest zones during the times when acoustic activity is highest. Some lose their tolerance for human speech entirely, can only communicate in writing or simple gesture, the complex sound-patterns of language becoming intolerable noise. A few go entirely silent, stop speaking forever, live in a space of pure listening until they die—sometimes of exposure, sometimes of simple refusal to eat, lost in whatever acoustic world they're perceiving.

## The Tenders

While Rope-Dancers move and Listeners monitor, the Tenders form the closest thing to a settled population. They're the ones who maintain the dwellings, manage the water-collection, cultivate the edible plants, process the raw materials into usable forms.

They tend to be older, or younger, or injured—those for whom the demanding physical work of rope-dancing isn't possible or no longer possible. But this doesn't mean they're inactive. Tending is constant work in a place that grows aggressively. A dwelling built last season might need complete renovation this season as roots push through its structure. A water-collection basin placed under a reliable drip might need moving when that drip stops and a new one opens twenty meters away.

Tenders develop deep knowledge of plant cultivation. They know which moss-analogues are edible and which cause the stomach-sickness. They know how to encourage certain lichen-complexes that provide protein, how to discourage others that produce toxins. They've identified flowering plants whose seeds can be processed into something like flour, whose roots can be chewed for their sugar content, whose leaves can be dried and smoked for the mild stimulant effect that helps with the cold and the monotony.

They manage the small gardens that some holdings maintain—areas where living root-runners have been trained into horizontal surfaces, packed with collected soil (fallen organic matter from above, carefully hoarded), planted with cultivated varieties of the native vegetation. These gardens produce most of the Descended's plant food. Wild harvesting supplements, but the gardens are the foundation.

The Tenders also process materials. They take dead root-wood and season it, understanding the different qualities of wood from different species, knowing what works for construction versus what works for carving versus what burns best in the carefully managed fires. They work with the bark-fibers, soaking and pounding and combing them into material that can be woven or felted. They make the rope from processed runners, understanding the importance of this task—lives depend on rope quality.

Pottery is a Tender's art. The clay comes from specific sites—places where the stone outcrops have eroded into fine particles, or where water has deposited sediment. The Tenders collect this clay, mix it with organic temper (ground root-fiber, usually), shape it by hand, and fire it in small kilns built from stone and packed with carefully husbanded fuel. The resulting ceramics are simple but refined, each piece showing the maker's hand-marks, each one slightly different in form and thickness because they're built by eye and feel rather than measuring tools.

The Tenders are also the primary makers of clothing, the processers of food, the builders of furniture. They do the quiet essential work that makes living possible in this space. They're often in groups, working together on tasks too large for one person, talking as they work—and their conversations preserve the accumulated knowledge of the Descended: which root-species grow fastest, which are strongest, which have useful secondary properties. How to read the light-changes to predict when growth will accelerate. How to identify the acoustic signature of a root-mass about to fall. Where the reliable water-drips are, where the good stone for tools can be found, which of the scattered holdings are currently occupied and which stand empty.

## The Shepherds

The fauna of the breathing canopy is sparse and often hidden, but there are those among the Descended who have developed relationships with certain creatures, who manage them in ways that resemble herding or husbandry or something between those concepts.

The most common animals under their care are the arthropods—creatures somewhere between insects and crustaceans, about the size of a human forearm, with many legs and a chitinous shell that comes in mottled patterns of gray and pale green. These things naturally inhabit the root-tangles, feeding on the moss and lichen, and they've been semi-domesticated by the Descended. Not tamed—they remain skittish, prone to fleeing or occasionally aggressive—but habituated to human presence, tolerant of being herded.

The Shepherds know how to read these creatures' behavior, can predict when they're about to molt (the shed shells are valuable—ground into powder, they provide minerals that are otherwise scarce in the Descended diet). They know how to encourage the arthropods to cluster in specific areas where their feeding helps control moss-overgrowth, how to guide them away from areas where their feeding would be destructive.

The arthropods can be eaten, though the flesh is minimal and the taste reportedly bitter. More valuable is their ability to process plant matter into something more digestible. They consume the tough lichen and moss, and their droppings—small dry pellets—can be collected and processed into a paste that's higher in usable nutrients than the raw plants. It's not delicious, but it's efficient.

Some Shepherds also work with the bird-things, though "work with" is perhaps too strong a phrase. The birds are barely tamable, too independent, too mobile. But they can be attracted and encouraged. Shepherds maintain specific calling trees—prominent roots where they'll leave offerings of food, where they'll make particular sounds, training the birds to associate these locations with feeding. Over time, the birds learn, and they'll come when called—or some of them will, sometimes.

Why encourage the birds? Because they travel far, and they have patterns. A Shepherd who knows the birds' behaviors can gain information about distant territories: if the birds are coming from the north more frequently, something's changed there—perhaps a new flowering, perhaps a disturbance that's driven them south. The birds also serve as a kind of early warning system. When they go suddenly silent or flee en masse, it often precedes some larger event—a falling root-mass, a pressure change, the arrival of one of the rarer, more dangerous fauna.

The Shepherds are often solitary in their work, spending long periods alone in the deep tangles where their charges live. They develop a quality of stillness, of patience, of attention that resembles the Listeners but focuses on visual and behavioral cues rather than acoustic ones. They learn to move without startling, to be a presence that the fauna accept as part of the landscape.

They're recognizable by what they carry: carved calls—wooden tubes and resonators that produce sounds similar to the fauna's natural vocalizations. Pouches of the processed food they use to attract and reward. Often they'll have dried arthropod shells incorporated into their clothing, either as decoration or as some kind of sympathetic magic, wearing the creatures' cast-off armor as a sign of their connection.

## The Rememberers

Among the scattered population are those whose primary function is memory—the preservation and transmission of accumulated knowledge, history, and what might be cultural identity or might be mythology or might be both without clear distinction.

They're called Rememberers, and they're usually older individuals who have transitioned out of more physically demanding roles. They can recite the genealogies of the major family lines, can list the significant events in the community's history (which apparently extends back seven or eight generations, suggesting the Descended have been here for perhaps two centuries, maybe slightly less). They know the stories of how this place came to be, though the stories vary and contain elements that are clearly impossible or metaphorical or both.

The Rememberers teach the children. In the scattered holdings, when children reach a certain age—somewhere around six or seven—they begin spending portions of their days with a Rememberer, learning the essential knowledge: how to read the signs the Rope-Dancers leave on the paths, how to identify edible versus toxic plants, what the different calls of the Listeners mean, how to properly maintain a rope, how to orient yourself in the vast space when landmarks are temporary and shifting.

But they also learn the stories. The tales of famous Rope-Dancers who could traverse from one edge of known territory to another without touching the ground. The accounts of the great falling—the time when an entire region of root-masses tore free simultaneously, the sound of it echoing for hours, the devastation changing the landscape completely. The legends of those who went down into the deep haze below and what they supposedly found there (these stories vary wildly and are probably mostly fiction).

The Rememberers also maintain what might be religious or ritual knowledge, though the Descended don't use those terms. They know the proper ways to mark a death, which involves constructing a small wooden cage around the body and hanging it from a high root where the air circulation is good, letting the cold and dryness preserve it, creating what becomes essentially a mummy. These hanging graves accumulate in certain areas, generations of them, the oldest reduced to almost nothing—just bones in a wooden framework—the newest still recognizable as human forms.

They know the songs that are sung at certain times—simple melodies, usually wordless or with minimal words, that the community sings together during specific occasions. When a child is born (a song of greeting). When a Rope-Dancer falls (a song of descent). When the light shifts into one of its particularly bright or particularly dim phases (songs of acknowledgment). These songs often use the acoustic properties of the space, designed to echo and layer, creating harmonies from the overlap of voices with their own reverberations.

The Rememberers preserve the craft knowledge through apprenticeship. A young person interested in pottery will spend time with a Rememberer who specialized in pottery, learning not just the techniques but the history of those techniques—how they were developed, what innovations previous generations made, which methods were tried and abandoned. This creates a living library of practical knowledge encoded in human memory and human practice.

Some Rememberers focus on mapping—keeping mental maps of the territory, updating them as the landscape changes, teaching others the key landmarks and the major routes. These mental maps are transmitted verbally, as long lists of landmarks and distances: "From the Three-Fingers stone to the Weeping Mother root is half a day north and up. From Weeping Mother to the Split Crown is a day east and level. The Split Crown has fourteen marked routes radiating out..." and so on. A skilled Rememberer can recite these navigational chains for hours, covering vast territories, providing the information that allows Descended to travel between distant holdings.

## The Silent Ones

There are individuals who don't fit the established roles, who have withdrawn or been pushed to the margins, who live in the deep tangles or the far territories, rarely seen, subjects of rumor and uncertainty.

The Silent Ones, as they're called, might be former Listeners who went too deep into sound and never came back. Might be individuals who suffered some trauma and chose isolation. Might be exiles for some transgression, though the Descended are vague about what crimes would warrant permanent exile. Might be those who simply couldn't adapt to communal living in close quarters, who needed the solitude that the vast space makes possible.

They're glimpsed occasionally: a figure moving through a distant root-tangle, there and then gone. A dwelling found in some remote location, clearly inhabited but empty when you arrive, the occupant fled or simply elsewhere. Signs of human presence—a fire pit still warm, tools left out, food stores carefully maintained—in places where no one is supposed to live.

The Descended tell stories about the Silent Ones with a mixture of concern and respect. Some stories paint them as half-feral, dangerous, to be avoided. Others paint them as wise hermits, keepers of secret knowledge, living in deeper harmony with the space than those who maintain the communal structures. Probably the truth varies by individual—some Silent Ones are simply people who wanted to be alone, others are genuinely unstable, others are pursuing some private understanding of the space and their relationship to it.

There are reports of Silent Ones who have lived alone for so long that they've forgotten speech, communicate only in gestures and sounds, relearned language from the fauna. Reports of Silent Ones who have covered themselves in living moss and lichen, their clothes and skin hosting entire ecosystems, becoming mobile gardens. Reports of Silent Ones who have perfect knowledge of the routes, who can navigate the hanging landscape in complete darkness, who can predict when and where root-masses will fall.

How much of this is true is unclear. The Silent Ones, by definition, don't communicate with the wider community, don't confirm or deny the stories told about them. They're the mystery at the edges, the suggestion that there are other ways to live in this space, other relationships with it beyond the communal patterns the majority follow.

## The Children

The younger generation of the Descended are born into this space, know nothing else, and this shows in their adaptation.

They're raised communally to some degree—while families exist and are recognized, child-rearing is partially shared, with children spending time in various households, learning from multiple adults. This is partly practical (population is sparse enough that concentrated child-watching makes sense) and partly cultural (the Descended seem to have developed a philosophy that children belong to the whole community, not just their birth parents).

The children play in ways that reflect their environment. They climb before they walk confidently, their infant explorations taking them vertical as readily as horizontal, adult supervision constantly vigilant to prevent falls. They develop balance and spatial awareness precociously, their young nervous systems plastic enough to wire themselves for three-dimensional navigation. By age four or five, a Descended child can move through root-tangles that would challenge an adult from above-world, their small size and developing agility letting them slip through gaps and navigate routes adults can't use.

They play games of chase through the vertical, games of hide-and-seek that use the whole volume of space, games of echo-calling where they make sounds and try to predict where the echoes will come from. They learn rope-work as soon as their hands are strong enough, practice knots endlessly, compete over who can climb highest or fastest. They learn to read surfaces by touch, running their hands over different bark-textures and guessing which species, which age, which condition.

But they also show the marks of adaptation more clearly. The translucence of their skin is more pronounced—the youngest generation looks almost crystalline in certain lights, their blood moving visibly under skin that seems thinner than it should be. Their eyes reflect light in a way their parents' eyes don't quite, a slight shine in the dimness, suggesting some enhancement of night vision emerging. Their hearing is acute—children as young as three or four can already do rudimentary echolocation, clicking their tongues and navigating around obstacles in dim light.

There's discussion among the adults about what this means, where it's leading. Some see it as positive adaptation, the younger generation becoming more suited to this environment. Others worry about what's being lost—the children are less tolerant of bright direct light when exposed to it, seem uncomfortable with the idea of solid ground extending horizontally for great distances, show no interest in or understanding of the world above the canopy. They're becoming native to this space in ways their parents and grandparents aren't quite, and that trajectory has implications no one can fully predict.

The children are also educated in the lore from an early age. By the time they're eight or nine, most can recite the major navigational chains, know the stories of significant events, understand the basic craft techniques. Some show aptitude for specific roles early—a child who climbs obsessively and fearlessly, clearly headed toward becoming a Rope-Dancer; a child who sits still for hours listening, future Listener emerging.

But they're children, despite the environment. They laugh and fight and form friendships and rivalries. They get into trouble—climbing too high, wandering too far, taking risks that adults know are foolish. They ask the endless questions children ask: Why is the light green? Where does the water come from? What's below the mist? How far does it go? Adults answer as best they can, which sometimes means admitting they don't know, which seems accepted—the idea that this place contains mysteries is fundamental to the Descended worldview.

## The Elders

Those who survive into old age in this place—and survival isn't guaranteed; the cold, the constant physical demands, the accidents and falls all take their toll—become something special in the community's structure.

The elders are often physically limited: tremoring hands from a lifetime of rope-work, joints stiff from the constant cold, hearing loss common among former Listeners, arthritis in climbers' shoulders. But they hold institutional memory, accumulated wisdom, the long view that comes from decades of watching the space change.

They're consulted on major decisions. When a holding is considering relocation because local conditions have shifted, the elders discuss the options, remember similar situations from decades past, advise based on what worked or failed before. When disputes arise between individuals or family groups, elders often mediate, their age granting them authority. When strange phenomena occur—unusual sounds, unexpected light-patterns, behavioral changes in fauna—elders search their memories for precedent, try to determine if this is normal variation or something requiring response.

The elders are also the most likely to think about the future in longer timeframes, to worry about sustainability and trajectory. Some speak about how the growth-rate of the root-masses seems to be accelerating, how passages that once stayed open for years now close in months, what this means for long-term habitation. Others discuss the changing nature of the children, wonder aloud if eventually the Descended will become so adapted to this space that returning to above-world would be impossible—if it isn't already.

There's a particular elder mentioned in multiple scattered accounts, an old woman (no name given, or perhaps she's lost it, or given it up) who is reportedly over eighty, ancient by Descended standards. She lives in a small dwelling on one of the highest accessible points, a stone outcrop that extends far from the main ceiling. She rarely receives visitors, but when Rememberers have questions about the deepest history—about the founding generation, about how the Descended came to be here, about the nature of this space—they make the long climb to consult her.

Her accounts (secondhand, filtered through those who've spoken with her) suggest the Descended's presence here wasn't entirely voluntary. Something about refugees, about catastrophe in the above-world, about this space being refuge or prison or both. But her memories are old and possibly confused, and she speaks in metaphors and fragments that are difficult to interpret. Some of what she says is clearly impossible, suggesting her mind has degraded with age, but other details are oddly specific and consistent, suggesting core truth wrapped in embellishment.

She supposedly says this space is "not finished," that it's still "deciding what it is," that the Descended are "here to witness the becoming." Whether this is prophecy or poetry or delusion is debated among those who've heard these accounts.

## Relationships and Social Structure

The Descended's social organization is loose but functional, adapted to their scattered distribution and the demands of their environment.

Family units exist—partnerships form, children are born and raised with acknowledged parentage—but the structure is flexible. Partnerships sometimes dissolve as people move between holdings or take on roles that require long absences. Children might be raised partly by grandparents or aunts and uncles or unrelated adults who happen to live nearby. This fluidity seems accepted, even expected, perhaps necessary given the population distribution and the demands of survival.

There's no obvious centralized authority, no chief or council or formal governance. Decisions are made locally at each holding by consensus among those present. Larger issues affecting multiple holdings are discussed when people gather—which happens occasionally, during scheduled meets at certain locations, or when word is sent out (via Rope-Dancers carrying messages) that a gathering is needed.

These gatherings serve multiple functions: practical (coordinating resource management, sharing information about routes and conditions, arranging marriages between holdings to prevent genetic isolation), social (the scattered population needs periodic contact), and cultural (this is when the largest ritual observances happen, when major stories are told, when the community affirms its collective identity).

Trade exists but is limited by the simple fact that most holdings have access to similar resources. What trading does occur focuses on specialty items: particularly well-made rope from a Tender known for quality, clay from a source with especially good properties, processed arthropod-materials, carved tools. Trading also includes knowledge—a Listener sharing information about a new route, a Shepherd explaining techniques for attracting specific fauna, a Rememberer teaching songs to those who don't know them.

Conflict occurs, of course. Disputes over territory, over resources, personal conflicts between individuals. The Descended's approach to conflict resolution seems pragmatic: talk it through if possible, involve respected elders if talking doesn't work, accept physical separation as a solution if nothing else works—in a space this vast, people who can't get along can simply avoid each other.

Violence is rare but not unknown. There are stories of murders, of exiles, of individuals who became dangerous and had to be restrained or killed. But the frequency seems low, perhaps because the environment itself is challenging enough that internal conflict is a luxury the community can't afford, perhaps because the personality types who can survive here are self-selected for cooperation and emotional stability.

Romance and sexuality exist in practical forms. Partnerships are sometimes formal—a small ritual observance, an exchange of carved items, a song sung together—and sometimes simply understood. There seems to be flexibility around gender roles and expectations, likely because survival requires everyone to be capable of most tasks; rigid gender divisions would be impractical. Same-sex partnerships are mentioned in the accounts without particular emphasis or distinction, suggesting acceptance.

The Descended place high value on certain traits: reliability, skill, patience, courage (particularly the courage to move through the vertical spaces), but also honesty, the ability to work cooperatively, emotional steadiness. They seem to have little patience for boasting or displays of ego—in a place where your rope-work might save someone's life next week, empty posturing is worse than useless.

Humor exists, often dark, often focused on the absurdity of their situation: living in a place that shouldn't exist, hanging in a void, farming moss and herding arthropods. They laugh at pratfalls and mistakes, tell jokes about the fauna, make puns using the terms from their specialized vocabulary. The laughter serves the usual human function—tension release, social bonding, coping mechanism for stress and fear.

## Their Understanding

The Descended have theories about their world, about what this space is and how it came to be, though these theories are more mythological than scientific, more poetic than precise.

Some believe the breathing canopy is a living entity, a single vast organism that happens to contain them, that the root-masses are like blood vessels or nerves, that the space itself has intention and awareness. They point to the way growth sometimes seems purposeful, the way certain patterns repeat, the strange phenomena that suggest intelligence.

Others believe it's a space between—neither fully the underworld nor the surface world but something in between, a liminal zone that connects them. They interpret the upward rain and the stranger phenomena as evidence of this in-between nature, as reality being uncertain here, as the laws of physics being negotiable.

Some hold to more practical interpretations: this is simply a geological formation, a vast cavity in the earth over which vegetation has grown, unusual but ultimately explicable. The strange qualities are just properties of scale and ecology not yet fully understood. This view is more common among the younger adults, those who've grown up here and take it as given, who are less interested in origin stories and more focused on practical survival.

The religious or spiritual beliefs, such as they are, seem focused more on practice than doctrine. The rituals around death, birth, major life transitions. The songs sung at specific times. The small offerings left at certain locations—carved wooden objects, bundles of dried plants, small cairns of stone. Whether these offerings are directed at deities or spirits or simply at the space itself is unclear; the Descended don't discuss theology in explicit terms.

There's a concept they reference sometimes, translated roughly as "the agreement" or "the understanding." It seems to involve living in right relationship with the space—not taking more than needed, maintaining the routes, watching for changes, being careful with fire, respecting the growth patterns. Breaking "the agreement" brings consequence—not supernatural punishment but practical result: the person who doesn't maintain their rope correctly eventually falls; the holding that doesn't manage water collection carefully runs out during dry periods; the community that doesn't watch growth patterns gets trapped when passages close.

This worldview is pragmatic mysticism: the space has patterns and properties, some understandable and some not, and survival requires attention to both the explicable and the mysterious, requires accepting that you live in a place that operates by rules you don't fully comprehend and may never fully comprehend.

## The Marks of Time

Individuals accumulate visible history as they age in this space. Beyond the general physical characteristics—the translucent skin, the callused hands, the specific muscle developments—there are other marks.

Many Descended develop a fine green staining of their fingernails and toenails, permanent discoloration from constant contact with the moss and lichen. Some have similar staining around their mouths from eating the plant foods, or on their knees and elbows from constant contact with root-bark.

Scarring is common and often elaborate. Beyond the work-related injuries, there's deliberate marking—some Descended carve or burn simple patterns into their skin, usually geometric designs on the forearms or shoulders. These seem to be personal choices rather than cultural requirements, markers of significant events or simply aesthetic preference. The patterns sometimes echo the grain-patterns of wood or the branching patterns of roots, as if people are inscribing the landscape onto their bodies.

Hair tends to become brittle in the cold dry air, often worn short or wrapped for protection. Some Descended braid small carved beads or bits of polished stone into their hair, the clicking of these ornaments creating a subtle sound as they move, a personal acoustic signature.

Teeth show the wear of the diet—the slightly gritty quality of processed plant foods slowly grinding down enamel, the mineral deficiencies causing brittleness. Older Descended often have significant tooth loss, which affects their ability to eat certain foods, creating a dietary transition toward softer preparations as they age.

But there's also a strange preservation quality. The cold and the dry air mean bodies don't decay the way they would in warmer, wetter environments. Wounds that would fester instead dry out, heal slowly but cleanly. The dead, in their hanging cages, mummify quickly, becoming light and dry, preserved for generations. This means the Descended live surrounded by their ancestors in visible form, the hanging graves accumulating in certain areas, creating zones where dozens or hundreds of the dead hang in the cold bright air, watching the living pass beneath them.

## What They Know That Others Don't

The Descended have accumulated knowledge specific to their environment, practical wisdom that would be useless elsewhere but is essential here.

They know how to read root-bark for age and health, can tell at a glance which roots will bear weight and which are rotting from the inside. They know the different creaking sounds mean different things—this pitch means simple settling, that pitch means imminent failure. They know how to identify the arthropods' molting cycle by observing their behavior, know which lichen produces the antibacterial compound useful for treating wounds, know which time of day the light is best for specific tasks.

They know how to navigate using sound—not just the Listeners' specialized skill but basic echolocation that most adults can do adequately, using tongue-clicks or hand-claps and reading the returning echoes for rough spatial mapping. They know how to move through mist by feeling air currents on their skin, following the flow toward openings or away from dead-ends.

They know the calendar of this place, the patterns of light-change that mark seasons, the cycles of growth acceleration and dormancy, the periods when fauna are more active or more scarce. They know how to predict water-drip behavior based on subtle signs—the dampness of bark upstream, the sound of water moving through plant tissues, the appearance of certain mosses that only grow where water is about to emerge.

They know how to make rope that won't fail, how to tie knots that will hold under dynamic loads, how to inspect rope for wear patterns that indicate weakness. They know how to build structures that can accommodate growth—joints that can shift, connections that can be adjusted, materials that can be relocated when the inevitable encroachment of roots makes staying impossible.

They know how to ration food during lean periods, how to process the tough plant materials into maximum nutritional value, how to identify which arthropods are safe to eat and which have consumed toxic lichen and now carry that toxicity in their flesh.

They know the social signals, the subtle communications essential in a community this scattered: how to leave signs indicating where you've gone and when you'll return, how to read mood and intention in the limited interactions they have, how to maintain relationships across distance and time.

And they know how to accept the strangeness, how to live in a place that regularly violates expectation, how to treat the impossible as simply another condition to adapt to. This might be their most important knowledge: the psychological flexibility to exist in a world that shouldn't exist, to raise children in it, to find meaning and purpose and even beauty in the cold bright spaces between hanging gardens in a void.

---

They are human, the Descended, but they're becoming something else—slowly, incrementally, adaptation by adaptation. They're still recognizable as people from above-world, still have the same basic needs and drives and capacities. But they're marked by this place, changed by it, becoming native to it in ways that both preserve and alter what it means to be human.

They live in the breathing canopy because they have no choice, or because this was always their destiny, or because nowhere else would have them, or because they chose it—the story varies depending on who tells it. But they live here nonetheless, scattered across continental distances, hanging in the cold bright air, watching their world grow around them, teaching their children to climb before they walk, singing their songs into the vast acoustic spaces, maintaining their agreement with the place that contains them, waiting to see what they'll become by the time the breathing canopy finishes deciding what it is.
