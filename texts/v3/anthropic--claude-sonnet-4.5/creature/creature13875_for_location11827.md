# The Inhabitants of the Woven Altitudes

## The Velenne

They are what you notice first, because they move with purpose through a landscape where most things drift.

The Velenne are neither the builders nor their descendants—or if they are descendants, the relationship has been stretched across so much time that it means nothing. They stand upright, bipedal, approximately human-height though their proportions are wrong: too much leg, torso compressed, arms that hang to their knees. Their skin—if it is skin—has the translucent quality of certain deep-sea creatures, pale grey-green with a faint luminescence concentrated around what might be joints, might be organs, the light pulsing gently with their circulation.

They do not speak. Their mouths exist—horizontal slits beneath their sensory organs—but they open only for feeding. Communication happens through touch, through the placement of their long-fingered hands against the plant matter beneath them, sending signals through the vascular network of the mountains themselves. Stand close enough and you can sometimes feel it: a vibration in the surface, a pattern of pulses that carries meaning in ways your nervous system almost but not quite understands.

Their eyes are their most unsettling feature: large, set wide on a face that has no nose, no ears, nothing else to break the smooth curve of the skull. The eyes themselves are complex—compound, but not insectile. They refract light in ways that create rainbow halos in the mist when the Velenne turn their heads, and watching them watch you creates the distinct impression that they are seeing something other than your physical form. Seeing through you, perhaps, to the pattern of your presence in the plant-network, the disturbance you make in the vast living structure simply by existing.

They wear nothing, but their bodies are not naked in any way that suggests vulnerability. The translucent skin reveals what might be musculature, what might be something else—structures that flow and reconfigure as they move, as if their interior organization is fluid, responsive. Some individuals have growths: small flowers emerging from their shoulders, moss-like patches on their backs, root-structures trailing from their lower legs into the surface below. Whether these are parasitic, symbiotic, or actually part of the Velenne themselves is unclear. They don't seem to distinguish.

The Velenne live in the chambers, the hollow spaces inside the mountains. They have no permanent dwellings—or rather, the entire structure is their dwelling, and they move through it according to patterns that might be seasonal, might be social, might be something else entirely. You encounter them emerging from vents, from passages that open in the surface when they approach and seal behind them when they pass. They carry no tools. They carry nothing. Possessions are a concept that seems not to apply.

When they feed, they place their hands flat against specific plant surfaces—marked somehow, chosen somehow—and wait. The plant matter beneath their palms begins to change, to soften, to liquify slowly into a pale sap that they absorb directly through their skin. The feeding can take hours. They stand motionless, patient beyond any human capacity for stillness, while the plant-mountain offers itself to them and they accept. Afterwards, the surface where they fed grows new flowers within days, dense blooms that are subtly different from those around them, as if the exchange has altered the local genetics.

They travel in groups of three to seven, usually. Occasionally alone. Very rarely in larger numbers. The groups have no obvious hierarchy—or if they do, it's invisible to outside observers. They make decisions through that subsurface communication, coming to agreement through pulse-patterns sent through the plant matter, and then they move with unified purpose toward wherever they've decided to go.

Sometimes they guide travelers. Not rescue—they don't intervene in danger—but guidance. A Velenne will simply begin walking parallel to you, adjusting their path to match yours, and if you follow them instead, you'll find yourself avoiding the thin patches, the weak points, the areas where predators wait below. They never acknowledge this service. They simply walk, and eventually they diverge, returning to their own trajectory, and you realize only later that they've probably saved your life.

They build, occasionally. Not structures—those already exist, grown over millennia—but modifications. You'll find places where the plant matter has been encouraged into new patterns: a spiral of growth that creates a shelter from the wind, a careful arrangement of vent-flowers that produces a particular quality of air, a living bridge across a gap that wasn't there before. The Velenne work on these projects for weeks, sometimes months, their hands pressed to the surface for hours each day, sending instructions down into the plant-consciousness, waiting for the growth to respond.

What they want is never clear. They leave no art, no writing, no obvious culture. But they are not animals. Watch them long enough and you see the deliberation, the planning, the way they solve problems that cross generational timeframes. They plant seeds—actual seeds, carried internally somehow, expelled through a pore near the base of their spines—that won't germinate for decades. They modify growth patterns that won't bear fruit for a century. They are thinking across time in ways that make human planning look frantic and myopic.

Their children are rarely seen. The Velenne reproductive cycle seems tied to something environmental, something about the state of the plant-network itself. When conditions are right—by metrics only they can sense—new Velenne begin appearing in certain deep chambers, small and pale, still partly fused to the walls where they emerged. The adults care for them communally, standing in circles around the young, hands pressed to the surface, presumably feeding information directly into the developing minds through the shared network.

The young grow quickly at first, then slowly. A Velenne child reaches adult size in perhaps two years, then continues subtle development for what might be decades. The oldest individuals—and you can recognize them by the way they move, by the complexity of the growths they carry, by the depth of luminescence in their skin—might be hundreds of years old. They are patient in ways that make the younger ones seem almost frenetic.

Sometimes the Velenne die. You find the bodies occasionally, lying on the surface, already being absorbed. The translucent skin has usually ruptured, releasing the interior fluids into the plant matter below, and within days the body has begun to root, to send out shoots. Within weeks, it's indistinguishable from the other growths, except that flowers bloom there with unusual intensity, sometimes glowing brighter than any others nearby.

The other Velenne don't mourn, exactly, but they visit these spots. They stand with their hands pressed to the ground where their dead are being absorbed, and they remain for hours, sending pulses down into the network. Communicating with the deceased? With the memory of the deceased now distributed through the plant-consciousness? Simply marking the event? All of these? None?

## The Breath-Kin

Higher up, where the mist is thinner and the surface has compressed into something harder, live the Breath-Kin.

They are not quite fauna, not quite Velenne, something orthogonal to both. They exist primarily as patterns in the air itself, visible only when the light catches them right, when the mist condenses around their forms and makes them briefly solid. At those moments, you see something like a human torso, arms, a head—but hollow, translucent, made of nothing but organized moisture and intention.

The Breath-Kin were definitely intentional creations. The builders made them, though for what purpose is lost. They are consciousness without meat, awareness inhabiting the movement of air through the hollow mountains. They ride the thermal currents, rising from the deep vents, spreading across the surface, then descending again through the intake passages, completing cycles that take weeks or months.

They communicate by altering local humidity. Stand near a Breath-Kin and you'll feel it: sudden changes in the moisture on your skin, patterns of wet and dry that carry meaning if you learn to read them. They are generally benevolent, or at least indifferent. Sometimes curious. They gather around travelers, manifesting as thickenings in the mist, subtle presences that don't quite coalesce into form but cluster close enough that you can sense them, circles of condensed vapor that pulse gently with something like breathing.

The Velenne interact with them regularly. You'll see a Velenne standing at a vent, hands raised, and the Breath-Kin spiraling around those hands, moisture condensing and evaporating in rapid cycles, clearly some kind of exchange happening. Information sharing? Negotiation? Greeting? The relationship is old, practiced, comfortable. Neither party seems subordinate to the other.

The Breath-Kin can enter the chambers inside the mountains freely—they are air, they go where air goes. Deep inside, in the vast cathedral spaces, they sometimes gather in large numbers, dozens or hundreds spiraling together in patterns that create localized weather: small rain showers that fall upward, fog banks that hang in geometric shapes, winds that blow in impossible directions. Whether this is recreation, ritual, or something else is unknown.

They can harm, though they rarely do. A Breath-Kin can become dense enough to suffocate, wrapping around a head and preventing breath. Or they can become so dry that they pull moisture from living tissue, desiccating flesh in minutes. But these attacks are almost unheard of. The Breath-Kin seem to operate on timescales that make individual creatures irrelevant. They care about patterns, flows, the long-term health of the air circulation system. Individual deaths are meaningless to them.

Occasionally, a Breath-Kin will bond with a traveler. You'll find yourself followed by a persistent thickening of mist, a presence that never quite manifests but never quite leaves. This companion will guide you toward good air, warn you of thin patches by suddenly thinning itself to near-invisibility, even provide moisture when you need it, condensing directly onto your lips and tongue. The bond can last for the duration of your visit, or sometimes longer—there are reports of people leaving the Woven Altitudes and still feeling that presence, that sense of being watched by organized vapor, even thousands of miles away.

## The Grazers

The large herbivores are called Grazers by most visitors, though they're nothing like any terrestrial grazing animal.

They are immense—shoulder height of twelve feet is common, some much larger—but hollow-boned, their bodies mostly air. Their legs are the most solid part of them, wide and pillar-like, ending in feet that spread to distribute weight across square yards of surface. These feet are sensitive, reading the structure beneath them, able to detect the load-bearing capacity of the plant matter and adjust their path accordingly. They never break through. Somehow, despite their size, they've learned to move across even the thinnest sections without collapsing the surface.

Their bodies are pale, often translucent enough that you can see the internal structures: chambers and passages rather than organs, networks that seem designed to process air as much as food. Their heads are elongated, eyeless—they navigate through touch and through something else, some sense that reads the plant-consciousness directly. Their mouths are wide, lined with soft, comb-like structures that strip vegetation without damaging the underlying growth patterns.

They feed constantly but slowly, moving in dreamlike procession across the surface, mouths always working. They can eat almost anything—leaves, flowers, woody growth, even the compressed plant matter of the ancient surfaces, though they prefer the younger, softer material. What they excrete is valuable: rich, processed plant matter that serves as seed-bed for new growth. The Velenne often follow Grazer herds, planting seeds in the fresh fertilizer, using the animals as agricultural machinery.

The Grazers travel in herds of twenty to a hundred. The herds have structure: larger individuals around the perimeter, smaller ones protected in the center. They move slowly enough that you can walk alongside them, can place your hand on their flanks as they pass—the skin is warm, slightly damp, and pulsing with internal rhythms that feel like multiple heartbeats, overlapping, creating complex patterns.

They are mostly silent, but occasionally they produce sounds: deep, subsonic rumbles that you feel in your chest more than hear. These rumbles travel through the plant matter, can be felt miles away as vibrations in the surface. They seem to serve as communication between herds, or perhaps as echolocation, mapping the hollow spaces below by the way the sound reflects.

During the mist-heavy hours, the Grazers cluster together, standing motionless in groups, their pale forms barely visible as shapes in the greyness. They seem to enter a dormant state, their internal rhythms slowing almost to stopping. The bioluminescence in their bodies—barely visible during the bright hours—becomes more obvious, creating pools of soft light in the mist. Other fauna gather around them during these times, seeking the warmth they generate, creating temporary communities that dissolve when the Grazers wake and begin moving again.

They give birth rarely. A pregnant Grazer becomes more luminous, her body glowing with increasing intensity as the offspring develops. When ready, she lies down—a process that takes hours, her massive body slowly settling onto the surface—and the offspring emerges already large, already mobile, but soft, its bones not yet hardened. The herd surrounds the birth, standing in protective formation, and the Velenne come, always, to witness. The newborn begins glowing immediately, brighter than any adult, a beacon in the mist that attracts the Breath-Kin, who spiral around it in welcome.

## The Silk-Spiders

In the vertical sections, where the plant matter grows in walls and the architecture turns ninety degrees from the horizontal plateaus, live the Silk-Spiders.

They are not spiders, not really. They have eight limbs, but that's where the resemblance ends. Each limb is segmented, yes, but each segment can work independently, can grip and climb and even detach if necessary, crawling back later to reattach. Their bodies are flat, almost two-dimensional when viewed edge-on, designed to slip into the narrow gaps in the plant matter where they make their homes.

They're called Silk-Spiders because they produce threads—not silk, something else, something that has properties of both plant fiber and animal protein. They string these threads between surfaces, creating networks that are part architecture, part trap, part communication system. The threads are barely visible, catching light only at certain angles, but they're everywhere in the vertical sections, creating a three-dimensional web that connects surfaces hundreds of feet apart.

The Silk-Spiders use these networks to travel, to hunt, to communicate. They can sense vibrations along the threads from enormous distances, can feel when something touches any part of their web. They're fast when they want to be, scuttling along the threads in bursts of motion, then freezing, becoming invisible against whatever surface they're on. Their camouflage is perfect—their skin can mimic any texture, any color, shifting in real-time to match their background.

They hunt the gliding creatures, mostly. They wait at strategic points in the aerial pathways, and when something flies through their web, they strike. Not with venom—they have no venom—but with speed and strength, wrapping their prey in layers of thread before it can react. The prey is taken into gaps in the wall, into the Silk-Spider's den, and consumed slowly over days.

But they're not just predators. The Silk-Spiders are engineers. They modify the vertical sections, clearing passages, reinforcing weak points, even growing specific plants by carefully tending seeds in their dens. They're intelligent—not like the Velenne, not communicative in ways that allow interaction, but showing clear planning, clear purpose. Their thread-networks are beautiful, geometric, sometimes serving no obvious functional purpose except aesthetics. They create art, apparently, for its own sake.

The Velenne trade with them. This is one of the strangest relationships in the Woven Altitudes: the Velenne will come to the vertical sections carrying specific seeds, specific plant cuttings, and leave them at the base of the walls. The Silk-Spiders descend, examine the offerings, and if satisfied, they leave threads—carefully measured lengths spooled into neat packages. The Velenne take these threads and use them for their own projects, combining them with living plant matter to create structures that have properties of both.

The Silk-Spiders die in a particular way: when their time comes, they climb to the highest point they can reach and attach themselves with all eight limbs, becoming a permanent fixture. Their bodies desiccate slowly, their flat forms pressed against the wall, and over time they become indistinguishable from the bark-like surface. But their threads remain, often for decades, still functional, still part of the network. Other Silk-Spiders maintain these ghost-webs, repairing them, extending them, building on the work of the dead until the networks become ancestral, some threads so old their origins are lost.

## The Depths-Swimmers

The very deep chambers, the ones that descend for miles into the planet's interior, contain water. Or something like water. A liquid, certainly, that has pooled in the lowest points, forming underground seas inside the hollow mountains.

The Depths-Swimmers inhabit these seas.

They are rarely seen—the deep chambers are almost inaccessible, the descent taking days through darkness so complete that light becomes meaningless. But occasionally they surface in the higher levels, emerging from flooded passages into chambers that still have air, and those who have witnessed this describe creatures that seem to flow rather than swim.

Their bodies are serpentine, muscular, covered in scales that are actually modified leaves—plant matter grown into armor through some evolutionary pathway that blurs the line between animal and vegetable. They range from a few feet to hundreds of feet in length, the size apparently determined by age, and they continue growing throughout their lives, which might span centuries.

They have multiple heads—three is common, sometimes more—growing from a shared neck, each head semi-independent, capable of breathing air or extracting oxygen from water. The heads are eyeless, sensing through electroreception and through direct contact with the plant-network. They can read the structure of their environment through touch, their scales constantly sampling the chemical and electrical properties of whatever they're moving through.

The Depths-Swimmers feed on the root structures that descend into their domain. They are not predators in the traditional sense—they don't hunt other fauna. Instead, they graze on the enormous roots that anchor the surface structures, carefully pruning them, preventing any single root system from dominating. They are gardeners of the deep places, maintaining a balance that prevents the underground seas from being choked by plant growth.

But they are also the memory-keepers.

The deep waters contain dissolved minerals, nutrients, the chemical residue of millions of years of decay and renewal. The Depths-Swimmers, living in this soup, absorb it continuously. And somehow—through mechanisms no one understands—they retain it. They become living libraries of chemical information, their nervous systems storing patterns that represent the history of the place. The oldest Depths-Swimmers carry memories of the builders, encoded not as thoughts but as molecular configurations, patterns that could theoretically be read if anyone knew how.

The Velenne descend sometimes to visit them. This requires extensive preparation—the Velenne can't breathe water, can't survive the pressure of the deep chambers. But they make the journey anyway, entering the flooded passages with plant-matter breathing apparatuses, descending along carefully maintained routes. What happens when they meet the Depths-Swimmers is unknown. Those who have witnessed it from a distance report seeing the Velenne and the Swimmers touching, their bodies in contact for hours, as if exchanging information through skin contact, through the direct transfer of cellular material.

The Depths-Swimmers sing, sometimes. The sound travels through water, through stone, through the plant matter itself, emerging on the surface as vibrations so low they're felt rather than heard. These songs last for days, complex patterns that might be communication between distant individuals, might be mating calls, might be something else. The patterns affect the plant growth—regions above a singing Swimmer show measurably different growth rates, different flowering patterns, as if the sound itself is a form of instruction.

## The Gliders

Most visible of the fauna are the Gliders—the membrane-winged creatures that ride thermals rising from the vents.

They range in size from as small as your hand to wingspans of thirty feet or more. Their bodies are light to the point of improbability, hollow bones filled with gas, their metabolism burning so hot that they glow faintly even in daylight. They are not birds—they have no beaks, no feathers. Their heads are smooth, eyeless or nearly so, dominated by sensory pits that detect temperature, air pressure, the movement of other Gliders nearby.

Their wings are their glory: membranes stretched between finger-like spars, translucent, showing intricate vein patterns when backlit. These veins glow—some species in blue, some green, some colors that don't have names, wavelengths at the edge of visible spectrum. The glow serves multiple purposes: communication, certainly, as Gliders signal each other in complex patterns. But also navigation—the glow is sensitive to magnetic fields, allowing the Gliders to orient themselves relative to the planet's core.

They feed on pollen and on smaller airborne organisms—the Woven Altitudes has an entire ecosystem of aerial plankton, tiny creatures that drift in the mist. The Gliders sweep through these clouds with mouths wide, filtering out nutrition. They never land, or land only rarely—some species are born, live, and die entirely in flight. They mate in midair, elaborate dances that can involve dozens of individuals, spiraling around each other for hours.

Their colonies are in the high chambers, where they cluster on ceilings in masses that can number in the thousands. Seen from below, these clusters look like bioluminescent chandeliers, pulsing with combined glow. The Gliders sleep while hanging, their wings folded tight, their bodies cool and dark. But even in sleep they maintain a subtle awareness, and any disturbance sends the entire colony swirling into flight, a sudden explosion of light and motion.

They interact with the Breath-Kin constantly—the Gliders can see or sense the Breath-Kin, can read their patterns, and they often fly in formation with them, following the Breath-Kin to find the best thermals, the richest pollen clouds. The relationship seems symbiotic: the Gliders distribute plant pollen as they fly, facilitating reproduction for plants that exist in the vertical spaces where normal wind pollination doesn't work. And in return, the Breath-Kin guide them to resources, protect them from certain predators by creating turbulence that disrupts attack patterns.

The Gliders are short-lived—most species live only a few years. They lay eggs in suspended nests, woven from plant fibers and saliva, hanging from the highest points they can reach. The eggs are phosphorescent, glowing softly, and they attract the young of Silk-Spiders, who sometimes prey on them but just as often protect them, a strange relationship that might be parasitism, might be mutualism, depending on circumstances no one has fully mapped.

When Gliders die, they fall. Their bodies are so light that the descent takes minutes, even from great heights, and they spiral down through the mist like falling leaves. Most are consumed by predators before reaching the surface, but those that land intact are quickly absorbed by the plant matter, their phosphorescence fading over hours as they decompose, leaving small glowing patches that persist for days.

## The Root-Crawlers

In the darkness of the internal passages, feeding on the plant matter itself, are the Root-Crawlers.

They are segmented, worm-like, but armored, each segment covered in plates that interlock perfectly. They range from tiny—no larger than your finger—to massive serpentine forms fifty feet long and thick as your torso. They move through the plant matter as if it were soft, though to any other creature it's solid, burrowing through the woody substrate, leaving tunnels that close behind them as the plant matter heals.

Their mouths are complex: circular arrangements of grinding plates that can process even the hardest, most compressed plant matter. They feed constantly, moving through the structure, breaking down the old growth, creating space for new. They are essential to the health of the system—without them, the Woven Altitudes would slowly choke on its own dead matter, would lose the porosity that allows water and air to circulate.

They have no eyes, no need for them in the perpetual darkness where they live. They navigate through chemical sensing, following gradients of nutrients, avoiding toxic regions where the plant matter has gone bad. They are almost entirely non-aggressive—they have no interest in living prey, only in the plant substrate—but they can be dangerous by accident, emerging suddenly from a wall, their grinding mouths capable of crushing anything in their path.

The Root-Crawlers produce sounds as they feed: grinding, crunching, the woody plant matter splintering under their plates. In the quiet of the deep passages, these sounds carry for enormous distances, creating an ambient noise like distant industry, like machinery that never stops. Some visitors find it comforting—proof of the system's ongoing maintenance. Others find it unsettling, a reminder of the constant consumption, the endless digestion happening all around them.

They reproduce by fission—a Root-Crawler grows until it reaches a certain size, then simply splits, becoming two smaller individuals who immediately begin feeding and growing. There are no juveniles, no parental care. Every Root-Crawler is alone, was always alone, will always be alone. And yet they form patterns, their tunneling creating networks that have structure, that seem almost intentional. Whether they coordinate somehow—perhaps through chemical signals left in their tunnels—or whether the patterns emerge spontaneously from simple rules followed by millions of individuals is unclear.

The Velenne harvest from them occasionally. A Root-Crawler will sometimes surface, emerging in a chamber where Velenne are present, and the Velenne will approach, will touch the armored segments. The Root-Crawler will remain still—the only time they're ever observed to stop moving—and pieces of its armor will detach, falling away to reveal soft tissue beneath. The Velenne take the armor plates and use them for specific purposes: grinding tools, scoops for digging, even as surfaces for growing particular plants that need the mineral content the plates contain. The Root-Crawler then descends back into the substrate, its armor already beginning to regenerate.

## The Shepherds

Most mysterious are the Shepherds.

They are rare—encountering one is considered significant, though what it signifies is debated. They appear human-sized, roughly human-shaped, but that's where resemblance ends. Their bodies seem to be made of the same material as the plant matter itself: woody, fibrous, yet mobile. They walk on two legs, have two arms, have a head—but these are formations rather than anatomy. They have no face, no features, just a smooth surface where a face should be.

The Shepherds appear near groups of fauna, particularly during times of stress or danger. A herd of Grazers trapped in a section where the surface is failing will suddenly have a Shepherd among them, walking ahead, leading them to safer ground. A colony of Gliders caught in a storm will have a Shepherd appear on a high promontory, and the Gliders will orient toward it, will follow its position to shelter.

They never interact with individuals, only with groups. They never force, only suggest through their presence. And they never remain—once the fauna are safe, the Shepherd simply stops moving, and within minutes it becomes obvious that it was never a separate creature at all, just a formation of plant matter that briefly organized itself into ambulatory form and is now dissolving back into the structure.

Whether the Shepherds are automatons left by the builders, or emergent phenomena arising from the plant-consciousness itself, or something else entirely is unknown. The Velenne show no surprise when Shepherds appear, suggesting they understand something about them, but the Velenne aren't explaining.

Some visitors claim to have been shepherded themselves—to have been lost, disoriented, in danger, and to have seen a figure ahead in the mist, featureless and patient, who led them to safety without ever acknowledging their presence. Whether these were actual Shepherds or hallucinations brought on by stress and the strange atmospheric conditions is impossible to verify.

What's certain is that the Shepherds only appear when needed, and they appear to care about the collective welfare of the system's inhabitants in ways that suggest purpose, intention, design. They are the closest thing to proof that the plant-consciousness itself is aware, is directing, is maintaining not just the physical structure but the community of life it contains.

---

All of these beings—Velenne, Breath-Kin, Grazers, Silk-Spiders, Depths-Swimmers, Gliders, Root-Crawlers, Shepherds, and dozens of other species less commonly encountered—exist in a web of relationships so complex that no outside observer has fully mapped it. They are not an ecosystem in the traditional sense, because the environment itself is alive, is participating, is one of the actors rather than a stage.

They live in a place that was built and that is still building itself, using them as organs, as tools, as expressions of its ongoing development. And they, in turn, use it, shape it, respond to it, in a conversation that has been continuing for longer than memory, longer than history, in the patient language of growth and breath and light in the mist.
