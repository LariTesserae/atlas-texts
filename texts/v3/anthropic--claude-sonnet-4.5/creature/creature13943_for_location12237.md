# Those Who Dwell in the Greenhouse Courts

## The Wren People

They are the primary presence here, though to call them "primary" suggests hierarchy where there is only distribution. The Wren People—this is not what they call themselves, but what you might call them, watching—are small-bodied and numerous, moving through the courts with an attention that seems to include everything simultaneously. They are darker on their backs, paler beneath, built for both the vertical stone faces and the horizontal perches. Their size allows them to inhabit the scale of the courts fully: what is a wall to a human is a landscape to them, full of hunting grounds, nesting prospects, territorial boundaries invisible to larger eyes.

They live in the incomplete architecture as though it were complete. Where excavation left a hole, they see a cavity perfect for nesting. Where tool marks scored the limestone in parallel lines, they find perches at exact intervals. Where stone was removed to create a niche and then abandoned, they discover a sheltered feeding station, a place to crack seeds against hard surfaces while protected from above. The incompletion that makes the courts feel unfinished to human perception is, to them, a surfeit of opportunity—edges to grip, crevices to explore, depths to nest in.

Their days follow rhythms tied to insect emergence and light quality. Dawn brings them out in waves of activity, each individual—and there are many individuals, though you'd need time to recognize their territories, their particular movement patterns—focused on feeding with an intensity that seems to vibrate in the air. They move in quick bursts: perch, scan, strike at prey, return. Their calls are varied and frequent, communications about territory, about threats, about the simple fact of presence. As morning brightens, their activity often subsides slightly, becomes more diffuse. They're still hunting, still moving, but less frantically, more efficiently.

What's remarkable is their distribution across the courts. They don't flock—these aren't colonial nesters—but they also aren't solitary in the strict sense. Instead, they maintain a kind of dispersed community, each pair or individual occupying a section of a court or several adjacent courts, the boundaries negotiated through call-and-response, through quick chases when lines are crossed, through a constant low-level awareness of who is where. The result is that wherever you stand in the courts, you are within the territory of some member of the Wren People, and also near the edges where territories meet. The space is thoroughly divided, completely inhabited, yet never crowded.

They nest in the most unexpected places. A gap between two limestone blocks that looks like it leads nowhere may contain a carefully constructed cup of plant fibers, moss, spider silk. A deeper excavation, something that might have been intended as a storage chamber but was never finished, its back wall still rough from tools—they nest on a ledge there, where water never reaches but where morning light enters at an angle that warms the stone. Some nest in the fig tree, in the one court where it grows from that waist-high niche, building among the roots that cascade down the wall. Others nest in the thin trees, in forks where branches divide, though these sites are less preferred—they seem to favor the stone, the permanence of it, the thermal properties.

Their relationship with the lizards is one of coexistence with occasional tension. The lizards compete for some of the same insect prey, occupy some of the same sun-warmed stone faces. But the Wren People are more active, more aggressive in defending space, and the lizards generally yield, moving to less contested areas when a bird approaches. There's no real conflict, just a constant negotiation of space and resource. Sometimes you see them near each other—a bird on one section of wall, a lizard on another, both basking, both hunting—and the proximity suggests that the territorial boundaries are drawn finely, known to both parties, respected.

The Wren People respond to human presence with wariness but not panic. When someone enters a court, the nearest birds go still, watching. If the person moves slowly, doesn't approach nests, the birds resume their activities, though with continued awareness. If the approach is direct, they retreat—not fleeing from the court entirely, but moving to its far edges, to higher positions, maintaining distance. They've calibrated their fear response to the actual threat level: the residents who live in the buildings around the courts are familiar, their patterns known, their lack of predatory interest established. A stranger provokes more caution. Children provoke the most alarm, because children's movements are unpredictable, their intentions unclear.

Some of the Wren People have lived in these courts for their entire lives, hatched here, nested here, will die here. Others are more recent arrivals, perhaps displaced from elsewhere in the settlement, finding in these incomplete excavations exactly what they needed—abundant food, protected nesting sites, water sources, the right temperature range. The population turns over constantly at the scale of years: birds die, new birds hatch, territories are contested and reassigned. But the presence of the Wren People as a collective feels permanent, as established as the stone itself.

They know the courts in ways you cannot. They know which crevices harbor the largest spiders. They know where water collects most reliably after rain. They know which ferns host the best populations of small insects. They know the pattern of light through every season, which walls warm first, which stay cool. They know the sound of every regular footstep—which human passes through at dawn, which children play in which court in afternoon. They know the other birds, the ones who aren't Wren People, and which ones represent competition, which ones are harmless, which ones are threats to eggs or nestlings.

Their vocalizations create much of the soundscape. Individually, each call is small, barely louder than a whisper. But collectively, with many individuals distributed throughout the courts, the effect is of a constant gentle conversation, a presence that makes the spaces feel inhabited even when you see no movement. The calls vary by context: sharp chips when alarmed, longer trills during territory defense, soft churrs between mates near nests. There are seasonal variations—more singing during breeding periods, more alarm calls when nestlings are vulnerable. But year-round, the voice of the Wren People is the voice of the courts themselves, the sound that tells you these spaces are alive.

## The Still-Watchers

They are fewer, larger, slower. You might call them lizards, but that word doesn't capture their particular quality of presence. The Still-Watchers—because that's what they do, predominantly, is watch while remaining still—are elongated, built for the limestone's texture. Their coloring matches the stone so precisely that you often don't see them until they move, and then they freeze again and disappear. They're not camouflaged in the sense of trying to hide; they're simply of the stone, part of its pattern, its continuation into the realm of the animate.

They're solitary in practice but occupy the same spaces, so you encounter them regularly once you learn to see them. They favor the vertical faces, the places where morning or afternoon sun strikes stone and warms it. They orient themselves to maximize warmth absorption, bodies pressed flat against limestone, legs splayed, heads slightly raised, eyes open and unblinking. In this posture, they can remain motionless for hours. You might think them asleep or dead, but they're intensely aware, processing everything in their field of vision, waiting for the movement that signals food.

Their size varies—some are as long as your forearm, others barely the length of your hand. The larger ones are older, having survived years in these courts, growing incrementally, becoming more skilled at their particular mode of existence. The smaller ones are this year's hatchlings or last year's juveniles, still learning the best basking spots, the most productive hunting positions, the boundaries that keep them safe from the territorial displays of larger individuals.

The Still-Watchers eat insects primarily, but they're opportunistic. A butterfly that lands too close, a beetle crossing the stone, a moth that emerges from a crevice—they strike with shocking speed after their long stillness, the movement almost too fast to follow, and then they're motionless again, processing their prey with mechanical efficiency, swallowing, resuming their watch. They also take spiders, pulling them from webs in the tool marks, from the gaps between stones. Occasionally, you see them eating plant matter—flower petals, soft leaf tips—though whether this is intentional or accidental, consumed while catching an insect on vegetation, is unclear.

Their relationship with temperature governs everything. In early morning, when the stone is cool, they're sluggish, barely moving, oriented toward wherever dawn light first touches. As the sun rises and stone warms, they become more active, though "active" for them is relative—they're never frenetic like the birds, never busy. They hunt, they move between basking sites, they occasionally engage in territorial encounters with others of their kind—these are mostly display, pushups and head-bobs, rarely escalating to actual combat. By midday in warm seasons, they may retreat to shade, to the cooler depths of courts, to north-facing walls. Late afternoon, they return to hunt during the second insect peak, to bask in evening warmth.

They're long-lived by the standards of small creatures. An individual you see today might still be here years hence, larger, more deeply colored, more skilled at being still, more efficient at striking. This gives them a kind of continuity that the birds, with their faster life cycles, don't have. The Still-Watchers are the long memory of the courts, the ones who have witnessed seasons accumulate, who have seen plants grow from seedlings to established trees, who have watched human patterns shift and settle.

When they move between basking sites, they're surprisingly quick, darting across floors, up walls, their feet finding purchase on stone that looks smooth to human touch but offers them abundant grip. They can run across ceilings, along narrow ledges, up vertical faces. But these bursts of movement are brief, purposeful, and followed by more stillness. They don't patrol like the birds, don't constantly forage. They position themselves and wait, trusting that prey will come to them, that the courts' abundance will deliver food within striking range.

Some of the Still-Watchers have specific home ranges—a particular court, a particular wall—where you can reliably find them day after day. Others seem to range more widely, appearing in different courts, though likely following a pattern you simply haven't learned to recognize. They don't nest in the way birds do; instead, the females lay eggs in gaps in rubble piles, in deep crevices in the stone, places where temperature remains stable and threats are minimal. The eggs are small, leathery, easily overlooked. The hatchlings emerge already miniature versions of adults, already capable of hunting tiny prey, already knowing how to be still.

They shed their skins periodically, leaving translucent fragments in crevices, caught on rough stone, ghostly echoes of themselves. These shed skins accumulate slowly, breaking down gradually, becoming part of the organic matter that feeds the courts' soil-building process. The act of shedding seems to be done in private, in the deepest crevices, the most protected spots. You rarely witness it. Mostly you find the evidence afterward—a perfect casting of head and body, empty eye scales, discarded.

The Still-Watchers are preyed upon occasionally by the larger birds—not the Wren People, who are too small, but other residents of the courts, the ones discussed below. This predation is rare enough that it doesn't govern their behavior, but common enough that it happens. You might find a half-eaten body in a corner, or see a bird carrying one away. The risk is part of their existence, built into their population dynamics. Enough survive to maintain the population; some are lost. The balance holds.

They drink from the water that collects in floor depressions, lapping at it with quick tongue movements, or they absorb moisture from the damp stone, pressing their bodies against walls where groundwater seeps through. They don't need much—they're efficient with water, designed for climates where it's not abundant. The courts' moderate humidity probably makes their lives easier than they might be elsewhere in the settlement.

What you notice after watching them for a while is their quality of presence. They're not decorative or incidental. They're fundamental to the courts' functioning, part of the insect control, part of the food web, part of the visual texture. They make the stone feel alive, because at any moment, what looks like a pattern in the limestone might suddenly move. They teach you to look more carefully, to notice subtle differences in color and texture, to see the courts not as empty spaces with some plants but as thoroughly inhabited volumes where life exists at every scale.

## The Insect Nations

To say "the insects" as though they're one thing is to fundamentally misunderstand what lives here. There are nations upon nations, ecologies within ecologies, layers of coexistence and predation and pollination that make the courts possible. Without them, the birds would starve, the lizards would leave, the plants would fail to reproduce. They're the foundation, and they exist in overwhelming diversity and abundance.

The builders come first to mind: ants, multiple species, each with different strategies. Some have nests in the rubble piles, large colonies with complex tunnels through the accumulated limestone chunks, workers constantly moving across the court floors in chemical trails, carrying food back—seeds, dead insects, plant matter. Their trails cross each other, sometimes leading to border skirmishes between colonies, brief chaotic encounters that resolve quickly. Other ants nest in the stone walls themselves, finding natural cavities or excavating soft mortar between blocks. These are smaller colonies, more discreet, but no less industrious. You see them on the walls, tending aphids on the ferns, protecting their sugar-sources, creating vertical farms in the courts' many dimensions.

Then there are solitary builders: wasps, many kinds, some building mud nests in protected corners, under ledges, in the incomplete niches. Each cell of the nest contains a paralyzed spider or caterpillar, an egg laid beside it, provision for the larva that will hatch. Other wasps patrol the vegetation, hunting, their movements purposeful and methodical. They're beautiful if you look closely—metallic blues, iridescent greens, black and yellow warnings. They're also dangerous if you interfere with nests, but mostly they ignore humans, focused on their hunting, their building, their reproduction.

The pollinators are everywhere during flowering periods. Small bees—not hive-makers but solitary nesters, each female excavating her own tunnel in soft stone or finding a hollow stem—work the flowers that bloom on the vines climbing the walls. They're fuzzy, efficient, moving from blossom to blossom with focused intensity. Butterflies are less systematic but more visible, their flights adding color to the air: yellows and whites and occasional blues, depending on species, on season. They're drawn to the sun-patches, to the flowers, to the damp stone after rain where they sometimes gather to drink minerals from the wet limestone. Moths are their nocturnal counterparts, less often seen but present, evident in the morning by the scales they leave behind on spider webs, by their presence in bird droppings, by the flowers that open at dusk and are pollinated by night.

The decomposers work in the leaf litter, in the rubble, in every accumulated pocket of organic matter. Beetles of many kinds, from tiny ones that could hide under a fingernail to larger ones that crunch loudly when walking across dry leaves. They're breaking down plant matter, recycling nutrients, slowly building soil. Some are specialized, eating only certain kinds of leaves, certain fungi. Others are generalists, processing whatever organic matter they encounter. Their larvae—grubs of various kinds—work through the deeper layers of leaf litter, through rotting wood when a tree branch falls, invisible most of the time but essential.

Spiders might be their own nation, or many nations. They're everywhere, once you learn to see them. Web-builders string their traps between walls, across corners, in the spaces between fern fronds. The webs range from barely visible gossamer to substantial structures that catch the morning light, jeweled with dew. Each web represents a hunting strategy, a microhabitat chosen for its insect traffic patterns. The spiders sit in web centers or hide nearby, waiting for vibrations that signal caught prey. Other spiders don't build webs but hunt actively—jumping spiders on the stone faces, wolf spiders on the floors, ambush predators in the flowers. They're eating vast quantities of insects, controlling populations, while also providing food for birds and lizards. Their populations are dense but mostly invisible—you have to look deliberately to realize how many are present.

The fliers create much of the courts' atmosphere. Beyond the butterflies and bees, there are countless flies—not just pest species but predators, pollinators, decomposers. There are midges that swarm in sun-shafts, creating columns of mating flight that look like smoke. There are delicate damselflies near the water pools, their bodies metallic, their flight precise. There are true bugs, plant-feeders with piercing mouthparts, moving across leaves and stems. There are lacewings, with wings like fine green glass, predatory as larvae but nectar-feeding as adults. The air is rarely empty—there's always something flying, something catching light, something being pursued by a bird or a dragonfly.

Speaking of dragonflies: they're present but not abundant, because the water sources are marginal, temporary. But you see them occasionally, patrolling the courts, hunting on the wing, their flight impossibly agile. They're eating mosquitoes, midges, anything they can catch. They're also indicators—their presence suggests the courts' water sources, while limited, are sufficient for their larvae to develop in the longer-lasting pools, the ones that don't fully evaporate between rains.

The night shift is a different population. Moths emerge from daytime hiding places, drawn to flowers, to fermenting fruit when figs ripen and fall. Crickets and katydids sing from vegetation, from stone crevices, their calls creating a completely different soundscape than the day's bird voices. They're eating plants, eating each other, being eaten by hunting spiders and bats—yes, bats visit sometimes, swooping through the courts from above, though they nest elsewhere in the settlement. The night insects are responding to temperature, to darkness, to the reduced bird predation, to the flowers that evolved for night pollination.

There are also the parasites and parasitoids, the ones whose lives depend on other insects. Tiny wasps laying eggs in aphids, in caterpillars, in the larvae of other wasps. Flies doing the same. These relationships create population controls, preventing any one species from overwhelming the system. The caterpillar you see on a leaf might be doomed, already hosting wasp larvae that will consume it from inside. The population dynamics are brutal at this scale, but they create stability.

The seasonal pulses are dramatic for insects in ways they aren't for the vertebrates. A warm period after rain might bring massive emergences—termites sprouting wings and swarming, beetles emerging from the soil, moths hatching in waves. These events draw congregations of birds, of lizards, everyone eating frantically while abundance lasts. Then it subsides, populations crash, the system returns to baseline. But baseline is still rich, still abundant, still sufficient to support all the predators that depend on insects.

What's remarkable is the specialization. Each species has carved out its niche—this beetle eats only the seeds of that particular plant, this wasp parasitizes only that particular spider, this moth pollinates only those flowers that bloom at night. The courts contain multitudes of these specific relationships, ecological threads woven into a fabric that seems stable but is constantly adjusting, constantly responding to conditions, to season, to the other species sharing the space.

The insects also create physical structures that modify the courts. Termites build galleries inside fallen wood, processing it, returning it to soil faster than it would decay otherwise. Leaf-cutter ants (if they're present—and in these conditions, they might be) harvest leaf fragments, carrying them to underground gardens where they cultivate fungus. Caterpillars build silk tents in tree branches, creating protected spaces for their development. Spiders' webs accumulate dust and pollen, creating surfaces that themselves become habitats, textures in the air.

And they die in enormous numbers, constantly. Their bodies fall to the court floors, are carried away by ants, are eaten by scavenger beetles, decompose into the leaf litter. This mortality is part of their strategy—reproduce quickly, in large numbers, accept massive losses, trust that some will survive. The courts' fertility depends partly on this constant rain of insect bodies, nitrogen and minerals being cycled back into the system, feeding the soil-building process.

If you sit quietly in a court and simply watch at insect scale, the activity is overwhelming. There's always something moving—a fly landing and taking off, an ant carrying a seed, a spider repositioning in its web, a beetle climbing a stem, a butterfly nectaring, a wasp hunting. The seemingly still air is full of flight paths, of invisible chemical signals, of sounds outside human hearing range. The apparently solid stone is riddled with insect tunnels, insect eggs, insect larvae developing in darkness. The plants are covered with insect herbivores, insect predators hunting them, insects visiting flowers, insects laid eggs on leaves.

The Insect Nations are the engine that drives the courts, the energy base that allows everything else to exist. They convert plant matter to animal protein, making the sun's energy available to higher trophic levels. They pollinate the flowers, ensuring plant reproduction. They build the soil, decomposing matter that would otherwise accumulate. They're food for birds and lizards and spiders. They're builders, modifying the physical structure of the space. And they're numerous beyond counting—any attempt to census them would find thousands of individuals, hundreds of species, relationships of staggering complexity.

## The Moss-Mind

This is not a creature in the conventional sense but a presence so pervasive, so fundamental to the courts' character, that it must be understood as a dweller. The Moss-Mind is the collective of moss species—and there are many, requiring close examination to differentiate—that inhabits the north-facing cuts, the perpetually damp crevices, the bases of walls where water runs during rain. It's called a "mind" not because it thinks but because it seems to know things, to respond to conditions with strategies that feel intelligent, to create effects that alter the courts fundamentally.

The moss is ancient in type even if these particular individuals are recent. It grows slowly, spreading millimeter by millimeter, establishing itself with rhizoids that grip the limestone, that secrete mild acids that slowly dissolve the stone's surface, creating the finest layer of soil that the moss itself occupies. This is how it begins the transformation of raw stone into living substrate. Where moss grows, conditions change: the stone stays damper because the moss holds moisture, which allows more moss to grow, which holds more moisture. It's a positive feedback loop, but one that stabilizes rather than running away, because the moss also requires the stone, requires the drainage that prevents it from staying too wet, requires the minerals the limestone provides.

In the deepest, dampest corners of certain courts, the moss creates landscapes in miniature—hummocks and valleys, different species succeeding each other as moisture gradients shift across centimeters. There are cushion-forming mosses, dense and springy to touch, bright green when wet, darker when dry. There are feathery mosses that look like tiny forests, each individual plant a branching structure, the colony creating a texture like green smoke against the stone. There are mosses that cling so tightly to the rock they look painted on, adding just a dark green stain, barely there but still alive, still photosynthesizing.

The Moss-Mind holds water. After rain, when the courts' stone floors have pools sitting in the excavation depressions, the moss is saturated, swollen, rich with stored moisture. It releases this slowly over days, creating persistent humidity, providing drinking opportunities for insects, maintaining dampness that allows other plants' seeds to germinate in nearby soil pockets. During drier periods, it desiccates, curls in on itself, goes dormant, waiting. When the next rain comes, it swells again within hours, resumes growing, resumes its slow acidic work on the limestone.

It's a habitat. Tiny invertebrates live within moss colonies—springtails, mites, minute spiders, creatures visible only with close inspection. They're grazing on the moss itself, on the algae that grows among moss strands, on each other. The moss-dwelling fauna is its own complete ecosystem, rarely interacting with the larger world of the courts but present, numerous, carrying out their microscopic dramas of predation and reproduction within the green. Birds sometimes harvest moss for nesting material, pulling at it, carrying away beakfuls of the softest species, leaving patches disturbed but recovering.

The Moss-Mind's distribution maps the courts' hydrology. By noticing where moss grows densest, you can read where water moves—down this wall during rain, pooling in that corner, seeping from the stone itself here. It's an indicator, making invisible water visible through its presence. The margins of moss colonies mark the edges between consistently damp and occasionally dry, between suitable and unsuitable conditions. Over time, as climate varies, as the stone's permeability changes with weathering, the moss advances or retreats, always tracking the moisture.

Some species are specialized to the peculiar conditions of the courts. They require the limestone's alkalinity, the particular mineral content of the water that seeps through it. They couldn't grow on granite or sandstone, couldn't survive in more acidic conditions. They're here because the courts are what they are—limestone, excavated, in this particular climate. Other species are generalists, would grow anywhere sufficiently damp and shaded. But all are bound by the same needs: moisture, light (though not too much), stable substrate, appropriate temperature.

The moss also traps things. Dust settles on it and is held, slowly being incorporated into the organic matter at the moss colony's base. Pollen lands and is kept. Seeds sometimes germinate in moss, sending roots down through it to the stone beneath or the thin soil nearby. The moss creates surfaces that intercept what falls from above, preventing it from simply washing away during rain, giving it time to settle, to become part of the courts' accumulating richness.

In certain lights—early morning, late evening, when the sun's angle sends light skimming across walls—the moss glows. Its green becomes luminous, seems to radiate rather than reflect light. This effect is purely optical, a function of chlorophyll and cell structure and light angle, but it creates moments when the courts feel inhabited by something that generates its own light, something almost magical. The stone's paleness behind the moss intensifies this effect, creating contrast that makes the green impossibly vivid.

The Moss-Mind grows year-round because the temperature never freezes, never gets hot enough to kill it. There's no winter dormancy, just the cycling between wetter and drier periods, between active growth and patient waiting. This means its influence is constant, its slow work on the stone never stopping for long, its presence always modifying the courts' conditions.

Where moss grows thickest, especially at the bases of walls, soil accumulates fastest. The combination of moss holding organic matter, moss bodies dying and decomposing, moisture supporting decomposer organisms—this creates visible build-up over years. The transition from bare stone to moss-covered stone to moss with soil pockets to ferns establishing in that soil is visible as a sequence across different areas of the courts, a timeline of the courts' ongoing transformation.

The Moss-Mind is silent, still, easy to overlook when birds are calling and lizards are darting. But it's patient, persistent, and ultimately more transformative than the animals. The animals live on the courts' surfaces, use them, depend on them. The moss is changing the courts themselves, particle by particle, creating the conditions for succession, for the next stages of growth. Give it centuries, and the moss will have turned stone to soil, prepared the ground for larger plants, fundamentally altered what the courts are. It's already begun.

## The Seed-Carriers

These are the small birds that differ from the Wren People in focus and behavior. The Seed-Carriers—again, a descriptive name, not theirs—are adapted for eating seeds and fruit rather than insects, though they're opportunistic enough to take insects when abundant. They're slightly larger than the Wren People, with thicker beaks, with plumage that tends toward browns and grays and subtle patterns rather than the Wren People's more complex markings.

They're less territorial than the Wren People, more willing to form loose aggregations, especially when a resource is abundant. When the fig tree fruits, when seed-heads ripen on the grasses that grow in soil pockets, the Seed-Carriers gather in shifting groups, feeding together, moving between food sources, communicating with soft contact calls that keep the group loosely coordinated.

Their ecological role is obvious in their name. They eat seeds, process them through their guts (which in many cases improves germination), and deposit them throughout the courts in their droppings. This is how many of the courts' plants arrived—carried in from elsewhere in the settlement, from the surrounding landscape, even from distant places if the birds range widely. The courts' vegetation is, in part, a map of where the Seed-Carriers have been, what they've eaten, where they've perched.

They prefer certain perching sites, which receive most of the deposited seeds: horizontal edges at mid-height on the walls, the thin trees' branches, specific stones in the rubble piles that offer good vantage points. Beneath these preferred perches, dense concentrations of seedlings often emerge, creating visible hot-spots of germination. Most seedlings die—the conditions aren't right, or competition is too intense—but some survive, establishing new plants, adding to the courts' diversity.

The Seed-Carriers' relationship with the fig tree is particularly important. They eat the ripe figs enthusiastically, competing for them, sometimes squabbling briefly at the tree, then dispersing to perch and digest. They're the ones carrying fig genetics around—not just within the courts but potentially to other parts of the settlement, though any figs that germinate in the courts would be especially significant, creating more of this species that's so well-suited to rooting in stone crevices.

They nest less obviously than the Wren People, building in denser vegetation, in the trees' canopies rather than in stone crevices. Their nests are more substantial cups of plant material, mud-bound, carefully constructed. They're more secretive about nest locations, less aggressive in defending them through vocalization and more likely to rely on concealment. You find their nests usually after the breeding season, when leaves fall or when you notice the structure by chance.

Their populations fluctuate with food availability more than the Wren People's do. A year when many plants produce seeds, when the fig tree fruits heavily, might see more Seed-Carriers successfully raising multiple broods, their population increasing. A year when seed production is low might see them ranging more widely, spending less time in the courts, their breeding less successful. The Wren People, eating insects that are always present, show more stable populations.

The Seed-Carriers are diurnal in the same way as other birds, but their activity pattern is somewhat different. They feed most intensively in morning and late afternoon, with a midday lull when they rest in shade, preen, socialize quietly. This might be related to when seeds are most available, or simply to temperature—the feeding style requires less constant movement than insect hunting, allowing for longer rest periods.

Their interactions with humans are less wary than the Wren People's, perhaps because they're less tied to specific territories, more willing to explore, more adaptable. They sometimes perch near humans, especially if the person is still, watching to see if the human's presence disturbs insects or otherwise creates feeding opportunities. They're curious in a measured way, aware but not frightened.

Younger Seed-Carriers, birds from this year's nests, are distinguishable by less crisp plumage, more tentative behavior, tendency to follow older birds and copy their feeding choices. They're learning which seeds are worth eating, which plants produce the best fruit, where the reliable food sources are. This learning period means they explore more, try more foods, potentially discover new plants that older birds have overlooked.

The Seed-Carriers also eat the fern spores occasionally, or perhaps the insects that are attracted to ferns, but you see them perched on fern fronds frequently, examining them, sometimes causing the fronds to bounce and shed their spores in clouds. Whether this is intentional, whether they're actually consuming spores, or whether they're simply using ferns as perches while hunting nearby insects is unclear.

Their droppings, being seed-rich, have different appearance and composition than insectivorous birds' droppings. They're often visibly full of seed coats, of partially digested plant matter, of recognizable fruit fragments. This makes them effective as fertilizer in addition to being seed dispersal—they're delivering both seeds and nutrients to the same location, improving the chances that the deposited seeds will germinate successfully.

## The Night-Singer

You rarely see this one, but you know it lives here. The Night-Singer is a single individual—or perhaps a pair, though they're solitary nesters—a larger bird than the Wren People or Seed-Carriers, predatory, with eyes adapted for low light and flight feathers modified for silent movement. During day, it roosts in the darkest corners of the deepest courts, in thick vegetation, motionless, invisible unless you know exactly where to look. At dusk, it emerges.

The song begins at twilight, a haunting series of notes that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, echoing in the courts' stone chambers, impossible to localize precisely. It's territorial announcement, advertisement for a mate, declaration of presence. The song is part of the courts' identity—residents of the surrounding buildings know this sound, associate it with evening, with the transition from day's activity to night's different rhythms.

The Night-Singer hunts the Still-Watchers sometimes, the Wren People occasionally (especially nestlings, if it can locate nests), but primarily hunts the larger insects, the moths, the big beetles that emerge after dark, the crickets. It swoops silently through the courts, perches on prominent stones or branches, watches, listens, then strikes. Its predation pressure is light—it's just one bird, needing modest food—but it adds a selection pressure, culling the less wary, keeping prey populations alert.

Its presence changes the courts at night. The diurnal birds, the ones active during day, roost carefully, choosing protected sites where the Night-Singer can't easily reach them. They go silent as dusk approaches, stopping their calls, settling into stillness. The nocturnal insects emerge more cautiously, aware somehow—through evolutionary experience encoded in behavior—that night brings aerial predators, that the open spaces between vegetation and stone are dangerous.

The Night-Singer is territorial but its territory includes multiple courts, maybe all of them in this particular district. It patrols, following a route that varies but covers its range, checking on food sources, ensuring no competitors have moved in. Other birds of its species are excluded—you don't see them, don't hear additional songs, which suggests the Night-Singer is successfully maintaining its claim on this space.

During breeding season, the song changes—becomes more frequent, more elaborate, sometimes answered by a mate though you can't distinguish whether there are two birds or one singing twice. If there's a nest, it's hidden in one of the deepest excavations, in thick ivy or fern growth on a stone ledge, built there during night when no one's watching. The young, when they hatch, are fed on night-hunted prey, grow in darkness, fledge eventually to find their own territories elsewhere in the settlement.

The Night-Singer's droppings, found occasionally in morning on prominent perches, are different from the other birds'—full of insect exoskeletons, sometimes small bones if it's killed a vertebrate, sometimes the pellets of indigestible matter regurgitated rather than passed. These remains tell what it's been eating, providing evidence of the night's hunts.

In moonlight, on those nights when the moon is bright enough to send light down into the courts, you might glimpse the Night-Singer in flight—a shadow crossing the sky-opening, wings spread, tail fanned for maneuvering, gone before you can register details. It's adapted to use whatever light exists, however minimal, its eyes collecting photons, its hearing acute enough to hunt in complete darkness if necessary.

The Night-Singer's voice is how most residents know it. The actual bird is a rumor, a shadow, occasional evidence but rarely confirmed sightings. It doesn't want to be seen, has learned over its life that daylight brings the attention of birds that mob predators, that humans get curious, that visibility is dangerous. So it hides during day, emerges at twilight, hunts at night, and is known primarily by its territorial song, by the absence of that song if you're in a part of the settlement where it doesn't live, by the particular quality it gives to evening in the Greenhouse Courts.

## The Root-Makers

These are the trees, the figs especially but also the other species that have established themselves in the soil pockets, in the deep crevices, in the places where conditions allow rooting despite the predominance of stone. To call them dwellers is to recognize that they're not just present but active participants in the courts' life, organisms with strategies, with responses, with effects that ripple through the ecosystem.

The fig growing from that waist-high niche is perhaps the most dramatic. Its roots cascade down the wall in a pattern that looks like water frozen mid-flow, gripping the stone, finding and exploiting every small crack, every imperfection. The tree is both autonomous and parasitic—not in the biological sense, but parasitic on the excavation itself, using the human-carved cavity as if it were a natural fissure, as if the space were made for it. The roots have probably reached soil somewhere—behind the stone face, in accumulated material at the wall's base—but the visible roots are in air, covered with thin bark, alive despite their exposure.

This fig produces fruit in cycles, the crop ripening over weeks, attracting Seed-Carriers, attracting insects, attracting attention from all frugivores. The fruit is small, dark when ripe, sweet. It doesn't all ripen at once, which means there's an extended period when something is always ready to eat, which means sustained bird presence, which means sustained seed dispersal, which means more figs potentially germinating elsewhere in the courts. This species of fig doesn't require the specialized wasps that some figs depend on—it's more generalist, easier to establish, which is why it succeeded here.

The other trees, the ones growing from soil pockets in corners, are different species, probably multiple species, each establishing where its particular seed happened to land, where conditions happened to be right. They're thin-trunked, reaching upward for light, their canopies spreading once they clear the walls' tops, finding sun above the courts' excavated depths. Their roots, invisible beneath soil and leaf litter, are binding that soil, preventing it from washing away, contributing their own leaf fall to the organic matter, slowly building more soil through their growth and death and decay of their parts.

These trees have grown quickly—they're pioneers, early successional species, the kind that colonize disturbed ground, that grow fast and reproduce young. They're not the massive long-lived trees of mature forests but rather the opportunists, the ones that get in first. Their wood is softer, their lifespans measured in decades rather than centuries. But they're perfectly suited to these courts, to this stage of the courts' development. They provide structure for other plants—vines use their trunks as support, epiphytes might establish on their bark in the humidity—and they provide canopy, shade, perching sites for birds, nest sites.

The Root-Makers are responding to the courts' conditions constantly. Their roots grow toward moisture, finding the seeps in stone walls, finding the pockets where water collects. Their canopies orient toward light, branches reaching toward the sky-openings, leaves positioning to maximize photosynthesis while minimizing water loss. In windier periods, they flex, their trunks bending, then straightening, the movement strengthening their wood, ensuring they can withstand storms. In drier periods, some drop leaves, reduce their water demands, then flush new growth when rain returns.

They flower and fruit according to species-specific patterns, some responding to day length, some to temperature, some to rainfall. The flowering brings pollinators, the fruiting brings Seed-Carriers, and both events pulse energy through the ecosystem, creating temporary abundances that multiple species exploit. The trees' reproductive success is tied to the courts' fauna—they need pollinators, need seed dispersers, need the complex community that's established here.

The Root-Makers also modify microclimates. Beneath their canopies, temperatures are moderated, humidity is higher, wind is reduced. This creates conditions where shade-tolerant plants can establish, where moisture-loving mosses thrive, where certain insects breed. The gradient from sunny open stone to deep tree-shaded corner represents a range of microclimates within single courts, which creates habitat diversity, which supports more species.

When branches fall—in storms, from age, from disease—they become substrate for decomposition, for insects, for fungi. The wood slowly rots, returning nutrients to the soil, creating habitat for beetles and their larvae, for termites if they're present. The fallen branches become nurse logs in miniature, places where fern spores germinate, where moss grows thicker, where new soil accumulates.

The Root-Makers are long-term presence in a way most of the fauna isn't. An individual tree might be here for your entire life, growing continuously, changing the courts incrementally but significantly. The fig might eventually split the wall it's rooted in, its expanding roots exerting pressure, widening cracks, fundamentally altering the stone structure. The soil-rooted trees might grow tall enough that their canopies merge, creating continuous cover over multiple courts, changing light patterns, changing what can grow below.

## The Fern-Folk

The ferns are numerous enough, diverse enough, and consequential enough to warrant recognition as dwellers. They're not mobile, not conscious, but they're inhabitants nonetheless, organisms with preferences and strategies, with effects on the courts that make them more than merely decorative.

They grow in clumps and clusters on the horizontal ledges, in the vertical crevices, anywhere that accumulates enough organic matter to provide rooting substrate. Each clump is a colony of fronds emerging from rhizomes, underground stems that spread slowly, sending up new fronds, expanding the colony incrementally. The fronds themselves are the visible part, the photosynthetic organs, elegant and divided, catching light, moving slightly in air currents.

Different species have colonized different niches. Some prefer deep shade, growing in the darkest corners, their fronds deep green, efficient at capturing low light levels. Others prefer brighter conditions, growing where light comes down directly from above, their fronds paler, more finely divided. Some are drought-tolerant, able to curl their fronds during dry periods, surviving on minimal moisture. Others require constant dampness, growing only where the Moss-Mind keeps stone wet, where seeps provide reliable water.

The Fern-Folk reproduce by spores, which creates interesting patterns. Spores are produced on the undersides of fronds in structures that look like neat rows of brown dots or lines. When mature, these release spores in clouds of fine dust, invisible individually but visible collectively when disturbed. The spores disperse on air currents, landing throughout the courts, landing beyond the courts in the wider settlement. Most die—they land in unsuitable locations, fail to germinate, are eaten. But some land in perfect spots: damp stone, shaded, with thin film of soil or organic matter, and they germinate, beginning the slow process of growth toward becoming new fern colonies.

This means the Fern-Folk are constantly trying to colonize new spaces. You see tiny ferns, just two or three fronds, in crevices where no fern was before. Most of these pioneers fail, but some succeed, establishing, growing, eventually producing their own spores, continuing the cycle. The courts' fern distribution is constantly shifting at the margins, new colonies appearing, old ones dying if conditions change, the overall pattern stable but the details always in flux.

The Fern-Folk provide habitat. Insects hide among the fronds, sheltered from predators, protected from sun. Spiders build webs between fern fronds and stone walls, using the structure. Birds nest behind fern clumps sometimes, the fronds concealing the nest, providing protection. The fern roots bind whatever soil they're growing in, preventing erosion, building structure. And the fronds, when they die, fall and decompose, adding to the organic matter, contributing to soil building.

Some ferns are epiphytic or semi-epiphytic, their roots not reaching soil but instead taking moisture and nutrients from air and rain, from organic matter trapped in bark crevices if growing on trees, from the stone itself if growing on walls. These are especially adapted to the courts' conditions, able to survive on minimal resources, opportunistic in their establishment.

The Fern-Folk's presence makes the courts feel lush despite the stone. Their green softens the limestone's hard edges, their texture contrasts with the stone's smoothness, their growth pattern creates visual rhythm. They're also indicators of moisture levels—where ferns grow densely, conditions are favorable, moisture is reliable. Where ferns are sparse or absent, conditions are harsher, drier, less conducive to plant growth.

They're ancient in lineage, ferns being among the oldest plant forms, yet these particular individuals are young, recent colonizers, their establishment here measured in years or decades. But they carry the genetic memory of hundreds of millions of years of fern existence, adaptations evolved over deep time, strategies that allow them to exploit the peculiar niches that places like the Greenhouse Courts provide.

## The Ground-Walkers

These are mammals, small ones, rarely seen but present. The Ground-Walkers are probably rodents of some kind—mice or rats or something similar—nocturnal, cautious, living in the gaps and crevices, emerging at night to forage. Their presence is known more through evidence than sighting: droppings in corners, seeds stored in hidden caches, gnaw marks on plant stems, trails through leaf litter visible in morning before day's activity obscures them.

They're eating seeds primarily, some insects, some plant material, whatever's available. They're competitors with the Seed-Carriers for seeds, but they hunt at different times, have different strategies. The birds are day-active, mobile, eating and dispersing. The Ground-Walkers are night-active, territorial, eating and storing, creating hidden larders in stone crevices, in protected nooks, saving food against scarcity.

Their populations are probably small—the courts don't offer abundant resources for mammals, and predation pressure exists. The Night-Singer takes them occasionally. Perhaps there are snakes, though you haven't seen evidence. Perhaps there are mammalian predators from outside the courts, visiting at night, hunting. The Ground-Walkers' behavior suggests constant wariness: they move quickly, stay near cover, freeze at unfamiliar sounds, are gone instantly if threatened.

They nest in the deepest crevices, in rubble piles, building nests of plant fibers and stolen feathers and moss, creating protected spaces where they raise young. The nests are hidden, defended, probably reused across generations if not discovered and destroyed. Population turnover is fast—these are short-lived creatures, reproducing quickly, with young dispersing to find their own territories, some staying if space allows, others leaving the courts entirely.

Their effect on the ecosystem is subtle but real. They're seed predators, eating seeds that might otherwise germinate, affecting plant populations. But they're also inadvertent seed dispersers—seeds they cache and forget, seeds they carry to new locations, seeds that pass through their guts and are deposited elsewhere. They're prey, supporting the Night-Singer and potentially other predators. They're ecosystem engineers in small ways, their burrowing and nesting creating structures other creatures use, their food caches supporting insects and fungi.

The Ground-Walkers are one of the few mammals in this account because the courts, being small, being urban, being structured as they are, don't support large mammal populations. No deer browse here, no foxes hunt, no badgers dig. The scale is too small, the resources too limited, the human presence too close. But these small mammals fit, finding in the courts a workable habitat, a place to live if carefully, if cautiously, if staying hidden.

## The Slug-Kin

In the dampest corners, on the wettest nights, slugs emerge. The Slug-Kin are gastropods, mollusks without shells, soft-bodied, vulnerable to desiccation, active only when moisture is reliable. They're nocturnal, hiding during day in the deepest crevices, under leaf litter, in any protected damp spot, emerging after dusk to feed.

They eat detritus primarily—decomposing leaves, rotting wood, fungi, algae on stones. They're recyclers, breaking down organic matter, processing it, returning nutrients to the system in their excrement. They're also occasionally herbivorous, eating living plant tissue, leaving characteristic holes in leaves, slime trails on stems. The damage is usually minor—the courts' plants are vigorous enough to tolerate some slug predation—but it's visible, part of the pattern of consumption and growth that defines the ecosystem.

The Slug-Kin move slowly, their muscular foot rippling beneath them, secreting mucus that they slide on, leaving trails that glisten in morning light before drying to thin silvery lines. These trails are everywhere in the dampest courts, on walls, across floors, on leaves, evidence of night's activity, patterns of slug movement across the landscape.

They vary in size and species. Some are small, barely as long as a finger joint, living in moss colonies, in leaf litter, eating microscopic algae and detritus. Others are larger, hand-length, bold enough to cross open stone, capable of eating substantial amounts of plant matter. Colors vary: some are plain brown or gray, cryptic, easily overlooked. Others are patterned, spotted or striped, perhaps warning coloration suggesting unpalatability, though who would eat them is unclear—perhaps the Night-Singer, perhaps visiting mammals, perhaps nothing in these courts.

They reproduce by laying eggs, small gelatinous clusters hidden in moist soil, in leaf litter, in protected crevices. The eggs develop slowly, hatch into miniature slugs, which grow gradually, reaching maturity over months. Some species are hermaphroditic, each individual possessing both sexes, which allows any two slugs to mate, which increases reproductive flexibility in populations that are sparse and scattered.

The Slug-Kin's moisture requirements tie them to the same areas where the Moss-Mind grows thickest, where ferns are most abundant, where water is most reliable. They're indicators of microclimate, their presence suggesting conditions that other moisture-loving organisms need. In drier courts, in the brighter, more exposed areas, you won't find them.

Predation pressure on them is probably light—they're mostly active when the diurnal predators are inactive, and their chemical defenses (many slugs are distasteful) protect them somewhat. But they're still vulnerable, still consumed occasionally, still part of food webs that extend beyond what's immediately visible.

## The Web-Weavers (revisited in detail)

Earlier, spiders were mentioned as part of the Insect Nations discussion, but they deserve deeper attention. The Web-Weavers are the orb-weavers specifically, the ones building the classic wheel-shaped webs, and they're the most visible of the spider community, the ones whose architecture catches light, whose presence is unavoidable.

Each orb-web represents a significant investment of energy and silk. The spider builds usually at night, starting with a bridge line thrown across a gap—between wall and fern, between two branches, across a corner—then constructing the radial lines that support the structure, then spiraling out from center, laying down the sticky capture spiral that will trap flying insects. By dawn, the web is complete, and the spider waits, usually at the web's center or hidden nearby with a signal line leading to the web, monitoring for vibrations that mean caught prey.

The webs are positioned strategically. The spiders know—through evolutionary trial and error encoded in instinct, through individual learning—where insects fly, where flight paths concentrate. They build in the spaces between vegetation and stone, where insects must navigate, where the probability of interception is highest. Some build high, catching the insects that fly over the courts. Others build low, catching the ones that emerge from leaf litter. The courts become three-dimensional traps, webs at all heights, all orientations, collectively catching a significant proportion of the flying insect population.

Each Web-Weaver is solitary, territorial about its web site, but also part of a community of web-builders distributed throughout the courts. They're mostly females—male orb-weavers are smaller, less obvious, often living on the edges of females' webs, waiting for mating opportunities, or wandering in search of females. The females are the builders, the hunters, the long-term residents of web sites that might be productive enough to maintain for weeks or even the whole season.

The webs don't last. Morning dew weighs them down, making them visible but also stressing the silk. Wind tears them. Trapped insects struggling can damage spiral threads. Birds flying through destroy them, sometimes deliberately harvesting the silk for nest construction. By afternoon, many webs are damaged, and by evening, the spider often consumes the remaining silk (recycling the proteins) and begins construction anew.

The Web-Weavers catch insects in extraordinary numbers. Each web, while active, might catch a dozen insects per day, sometimes more during emergence events, sometimes fewer during quiet periods. Multiply that by the number of webs—and there are many—and the collective impact on insect populations is substantial. The Web-Weavers are controlling herbivorous insects, predatory insects, pollinators, everything. They're a major selective pressure, favoring insects that fly at different times, that detect webs and avoid them, that are too large or too small for effective capture.

The spiders themselves are prey. The Wren People take them from webs, plucking them off, eating them or feeding them to nestlings. The Night-Singer might take them at dusk, when they're beginning construction, vulnerable. The Web-Weavers' population is constantly being replenished by reproduction, by young spiders ballooning in from elsewhere, by adults recolonizing after local population crashes.

The silk itself is a resource. Birds harvest it, carrying it away in beaks, using it to bind nest materials, to create structure. Insects sometimes steal trapped prey from webs, taking advantage of the spider's hunting without the cost of web-building. Other spider species live as kleptoparasites, building tiny webs attached to the orb-webs, stealing small insects that the orb-weaver misses or ignores.

## The Stone-Eaters

These are the most overlooked dwellers because they're microscopic and slow-moving, but they're fundamental. The Stone-Eaters are the communities of bacteria, algae, and fungi that live on and in the limestone's surface, organisms that derive energy and nutrients from the stone itself, from airborne particles, from rain that washes minerals and organic matter across rock faces.

The bacteria are pioneers, colonizing bare stone soon after it's cut, forming biofilms—thin layers of cells embedded in self-secreted slime—that protect them from desiccation, from UV radiation, from the harsh conditions of exposed rock. These biofilms are barely visible, perhaps a slight discoloration of the stone, a sense of texture that's not quite roughness. But they're alive, metabolizing, growing, preparing the way for other organisms.

The algae are photosynthetic, turning sunlight and carbon dioxide into organic matter, creating food where none existed before. They're visible as greenish stains on north-facing walls, in areas that stay damp, in conditions where they can survive. Some are single-celled, others are filamentous, forming mats. They're eaten by grazers—tiny insects, snails, slugs—which brings their productivity into the larger food web.

The fungi are decomposers and symbionts, breaking down organic matter, forming partnerships with plant roots (mycorrhizae) that help the plants access nutrients and water. Fungal hyphae—thread-like structures—permeate the soil, the leaf litter, the rotting wood, creating networks that move nutrients through the ecosystem. Some fungi are visible as mushrooms during the right season, fruiting bodies that emerge, release spores, then wither. Others are never visible, their entire existence underground or within substrates, known only by their effects.

The Stone-Eaters are collectively creating soil. Their acids dissolve the limestone slightly, releasing minerals. Their bodies, when they die, add organic matter. They trap dust, pollen, dead insects, holding it against the stone until it accumulates into the thinnest layer that might, eventually, become actual soil. This is happening slowly, but it's happening everywhere, every stone face in the courts hosting these microscopic communities, every surface being transformed particle by particle.

They also alter the stone's physical properties. Biofilms can make stone surfaces less permeable, changing how water moves. Fungal hyphae can penetrate along grain boundaries, slowly fragmenting the stone. The combined effect of all these organisms is weathering—the conversion of rock to sediment, the breaking down of the durable into the mobile, the preparation of mineral substrate for biological use.

The Stone-Eaters are responding to moisture, to light, to temperature, to nutrient availability, but at scales and speeds that are difficult to perceive. A bacterial population might double overnight. A fungal network might grow centimeters in a week. Algae might bloom after rain, turning stone surfaces visibly greener, then fade during dry periods. These are dynamics as real as bird migrations or insect emergences, but invisible without microscopy, without chemical analysis, without long-term observation.

They're the base of the base, the foundation beneath the foundation. Without them, the courts would be bare stone, sterile, unable to support the plants that feed the insects that feed the birds. With them, the courts are fertile, productive, capable of supporting complexity.

## The Snails

Distinct from their shell-less slug relatives, the snails are present but not abundant, carrying their spiral homes, scraping algae and biofilm from stone surfaces with their radulae—tongue-like organs covered in tiny teeth. They're nocturnal and crepuscular, most active when moisture is high, retreating into their shells during dry periods, sealing the opening with a temporary membrane to prevent water loss.

They're varied in size and species, from tiny ones no larger than a grain of rice to others approaching thumb-size. Their shells are beautiful if examined closely: spirals with mathematical precision, colors ranging from pure white to brown to banded patterns, surfaces smooth or sculptured. Each shell is the snail's history, grown incrementally from the apex, each whorl representing a period of growth.

The snails lay eggs in clutches, small spheres of jelly containing developing embryos, hidden in moist soil, under leaves, in protected crevices. The hatchlings emerge as miniature snails, already bearing tiny shells, already capable of feeding and moving. Growth is slow—it might take a year or more to reach adult size—but snails can be relatively long-lived by invertebrate standards, surviving several years if conditions are favorable.

They're eating the same resources as the Slug-Kin—algae, lichens, decaying plant matter, occasionally living plant tissue—but their shells protect them, allow them to venture into slightly less humid areas, to tolerate conditions that would desiccate unprotected slugs. The shells also make them more visible, less cryptic, but also better protected against some predators.

They're prey for the Night-Singer potentially, though their shells offer protection. Certain beetles can eat small snails, crunching through shells. But mostly they're left alone, their population controlled more by resources and moisture than by predation.

Their movement is similar to slugs—slow, muscular, slime-trailing—but they can seal their shells and wait out unfavorable conditions, which gives them flexibility. During the driest periods, you might find them attached to sheltered walls, shells sealed, dormant, waiting for rain. When moisture returns, they emerge, resume feeding, resume moving, resume their slow work of grazing the stone-eating microorganisms.

## The Transients

Finally, there are those who don't live in the courts permanently but pass through, visit, use the courts as part of larger territories. These transients are harder to characterize because they're diverse and their presence is irregular, but they're part of the courts' community nonetheless.

Bats, mentioned briefly before, hunt through the courts at night, swooping down from above, catching insects in flight or gleaning them from vegetation. They roost elsewhere—in buildings, in caves, in tree hollows—but they hunt here, drawn by the courts' insect abundance. Their presence is brief, measured in minutes per night, but their impact is real, additional predation pressure on night-flying insects.

Butterflies and moths from elsewhere in the settlement visit the courts' flowers, supplementing the resident pollinators. Some might complete their whole life cycle here, but others are just passing through, nectaring, then moving on. The result is genetic mixing, pollen transfer from outside, seeds arriving from distant parents, maintaining genetic diversity in the courts' plant populations.

Larger birds—not the residents but visitors from elsewhere—occasionally appear. Perhaps a hawk perches in a tree, scanning for prey, not finding much in these small spaces, moving on. Perhaps a heron wanders in from the harbor, looking at the water pools, finding them too shallow, leaving. Perhaps migratory species pause briefly during passage, resting, feeding, then continuing their journeys.

Cats from the settlement might enter occasionally, hunting the Ground-Walkers, stalking birds, usually unsuccessfully in these stone mazes where prey has many escape routes. Their visits are disruptive but brief, and the courts' fauna has learned to recognize threat, to respond appropriately, to resume normal activity once the cat leaves.

Humans are transients too, except for the residents in the surrounding buildings who might be considered part-time dwellers. Most humans pass through, walking from one part of the settlement to another via the connecting passages, or entering deliberately to experience the courts, to sit quietly, to escape the settlement's busier areas. Human impact is light—some trampling of vegetation in the most-used courts, occasional introduction of exotic seeds on shoes, the stress of presence on nesting birds—but not destructive, not transformative.

Wind is a transient in its way, entering from above, swirling through the courts, carrying seeds and spores and insects, stirring the air, then leaving. Rain is a transient, arriving in storms, filling the floor depressions, soaking the stone, then departing as the water seeps away or evaporates. These non-living transients are still part of what makes the courts what they are, forces that visit, affect, move on.

---

The Greenhouse Courts are inhabited thoroughly, at every scale, at every hour. From the Stone-Eaters transforming mineral to soil at microscopic scale, through the insects and spiders and gastropods, up to the birds and small mammals, each dweller is both individual and part of a community, both independent and interdependent. They didn't plan to create an ecosystem in these incomplete excavations, but they did, each species responding to conditions, to opportunities, to each other, generating complexity from the simple starting point of carved stone and arriving water and settling seeds.

The human intention that began the courts' excavation has been superseded by natural process, but not entirely—the shape of the spaces, the quality of the stone, the position within the settlement, these human-created conditions structure everything that followed. The dwellers are living in human-made habitat, but they've made it their own, claimed it, transformed it, made it wild despite its urban setting, made it complete despite its architectural incompleteness.

And the succession continues. The dwellers are changing the courts constantly: soil building, plants establishing, structures forming, relationships evolving. What lives there now is not what will live there in decades, in centuries. The Stone-Eaters and the Moss-Mind and the Root-Makers are preparing the ground for whatever comes next, slowly but relentlessly converting stone to soil, open space to canopy, incomplete excavation to complete ecosystem. The current inhabitants are the pioneers, the early colonizers, the ones adapted for raw conditions. They're making possible the later arrivals, the more specialized species, the deeper complexity that develops as ecosystems mature.

Human Details

The residents in the buildings surrounding the courts are also dwellers, in a sense—or perhaps more accurately, they're witnesses, observers, neighbors to the nonhuman community that's established itself in these incomplete spaces their ancestors began but never finished.

They hear the Night-Singer every evening, know when it's absent, notice when a second voice suggests a mate has arrived or a young bird is establishing territory. They recognize individual Wren People by their territories, by their particular calls, by their nesting sites used year after year. They notice when the fig fruits, when the butterflies are abundant, when the Still-Watchers are basking in unusual numbers. They've learned the courts' rhythms without necessarily studying them formally, simply through daily proximity, through windows that look into these spaces, through passages walked regularly.

Some residents maintain the tentative human presence: the person who set stepping stones across a flooded court, making passage easier without disrupting the water's ecology; the one who placed that large ceramic pot, perhaps thinking to collect rainwater, perhaps just offering a form to the space; the children who play carefully in certain courts, who've learned which birds nest where, which lizards are most tolerant of approach, which courts are too densely vegetated or too actively used by birds to make good play spaces.

Others maintain careful distance, treating the courts as sacred or at least as separate, closing their doors that open onto them, watching from windows but not entering, allowing the space to be wild, to be other, to be claimed by the birds and plants and insects. This restraint is itself a form of habitation—the choice not to domesticate, not to impose order, not to finish what was started is an active relationship, a way of dwelling alongside rather than within.

There may be disagreements among residents about the courts—some might want them cleaned up, completed, made into formal gardens; others might value their wildness, their incompleteness, their quality of being neither architecture nor nature but something between. These tensions are probably low-level, informal, negotiated through conversation and compromise and gradual consensus rather than through formal decisions. The result is the current state: minimal intervention, maximum tolerance, a passive permission for the nonhuman dwellers to do what they will.

The residents provide resources without intending to: crumbs from kitchen windows that feed ants and birds, water dripping from laundry lines, materials that blow in from the settlement's everyday life. They also provide disturbance: footsteps, voices, cooking smells, occasional construction work on buildings. The courts' dwellers have adapted to this background of human presence, learned to ignore what's not threatening, to respond only to direct approaches, to exist alongside people in a state of mutual awareness and general tolerance.

Some residents might leave offerings—seeds scattered deliberately, water left in containers during dry periods, nest boxes installed in sheltered spots. Others might be more extractive—harvesting figs when they ripen, collecting moss or ferns for personal use, occasionally catching insects for children to observe before releasing. The balance tips neither toward complete separation nor toward full integration, but occupies a middle ground where humans and nonhumans share space while maintaining distinct lives.

In the broader settlement, the Greenhouse Courts have a reputation, a quality of being known. People mention them when giving directions, visit them intentionally for their atmosphere, bring guests to show them something unusual, somewhere that feels separate from the settlement's ordinary spaces. The courts function as a commons of sorts, not owned or controlled by any single person or group, maintained through collective restraint rather than collective action, valuable precisely because they're left incomplete, left wild, left to the dwellers who claimed them.
