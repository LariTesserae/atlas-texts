# What Lives in the Condensery Plains

The **Tenders** are what remain of people here.

They don't call themselves that—they don't call themselves much of anything anymore. The word comes from old records found in the archived buildings, technical manuals that refer to "system tenders" and "water tenders," and it stuck to them the way the dampness sticks to everything else. 

They number perhaps three hundred across the visible expanse, though no one counts anymore and the number fluctuates in ways that don't quite make sense. Someone will be gone for months, their station unmanned but somehow the machinery still maintained, and then they'll be back, or someone who looks like them will be back, moving through the same routines. Or a new person will appear, already knowing the schedules and the codes, as if they'd always been there and you'd simply failed to notice.

Each Tender has their territory—not in the sense of ownership, but in the sense of a heartbeat has a heart. A section of the plains where they know every valve, every channel junction, every sound the machinery makes when it's running correctly and every deviation that signals malfunction. These territories overlap, bleed into each other, are abandoned and reoccupied according to patterns that might be systematic or might be intuitive or might be something else entirely.

They're tall, most of them, or they were once. The years here have bent them into conformity with the landscape—shoulders curved forward against the perpetual damp, heads tilted in a permanent listening posture, spines adapted to the slight crouch needed to move through the low doorways of the older structures. They move with a peculiar efficiency, no wasted motion, their paths through the machinery as optimized as the water channels themselves.

Their clothing has become uniform not by design but by convergence. Rubberized canvas in grey-green, the same color as everything else here, patched and repatched until it's hard to tell what's original fabric and what's repair. High boots, always. Gloves with the fingertips worn through, because some work requires touch, requires feeling the temperature of a pipe or the vibration of a pump housing. Hoods that can be pulled up during the misting phases, though many don't bother, have stopped caring whether they're wet or dry since they're always some degree of both.

Their faces are hard to see, hard to remember. Not because they hide them, but because the low light and the moisture in the air blur features, and because there's a sameness that develops—the same squint against the damp, the same set to the mouth, the same distant focus in the eyes as if they're always listening to something just beyond normal hearing range. Young or old becomes hard to determine. Gender becomes secondary, unemphasized, irrelevant to function.

They speak, but infrequently.

Their language has degraded into technical shorthand, or perhaps evolved into something more efficient. "Basin three showing turbid" instead of "the water in the third collection basin appears cloudy." "Western array needs scraping" instead of any longer formulation. Sometimes just sounds—a particular whistle that means the pressure's wrong in a section, a clicking noise made with the tongue that signals all's well, a grunt that indicates something's attention is required but it's not urgent.

When they do speak at length, which happens rarely and usually only in the communal spaces during shift changes, their voices are strange. Modulated oddly, with rhythms that match the machinery, pauses where a pump cycle completes or a gate hammers shut. They've learned to speak in the gaps of the sound, to make themselves heard without raising their voices, threading words through the mechanical cacophony like water through a complex channel system.

They can communicate across surprising distances this way, from one structure to another, using the acoustics of the flat landscape and the sound-reflective properties of the slakecrete. A message sent from the Old Basin can be heard at Whiner's Corner, not as an echo but as something carried and amplified by the machinery itself, passed through the system like a signal through a network.

The communal spaces are few and cheerless.

A central mess hall exists somewhere near the geographic center of the visible plains—a large slakecrete structure with tables and benches built into the floor, a kitchen area with equipment that processes the algae paste into something marginally edible, water dispensers running off the same system that feeds everything else. The Tenders gather here in overlapping shifts, never all at once, eating quickly and mechanically. The food is protein-rich and tasteless, supplemented by what must be supply shipments from elsewhere, though no one sees the shipments arrive. Canned goods appear in the storage rooms. Bags of grain. Occasionally, improbably, fresh vegetables that get consumed immediately, treasured for their wrongness here, their reminder that other places grow things in soil under sunlight.

There are dormitories, though many Tenders sleep in their stations, in small rooms attached to the processing plants or the pump houses. Narrow cots with thin mattresses that never fully dry. Blankets that smell of mildew. The sound here is loudest, the machinery right on the other side of thin walls, and the Tenders sleep curled around it, synchronized to its rhythms, their breathing and heartbeats entraining to the pulse of the pumps.

Some of the older Tenders—the ones who've been here longest—don't seem to sleep at all in any normal sense. They enter a state of maintenance, sitting or standing in their stations with their eyes half-open, aware but resting, their bodies conserving energy while their attention stays diffused across their territory, ready to respond to any change in the system. They can stay like this for hours, motionless except for the slight movements that match the vibration coming up through the floor, and when they rouse it's instant, complete, already moving toward whatever problem has manifested.

The work itself is constant but not frantic.

A Tender moves through their territory on a route that might take hours or a full shift, checking pressure gauges, clearing intake screens, adjusting valves to balance flow between channels. They carry tools—wrenches, scrapers, measuring devices—on belts or in bags, and their hands are always busy, touching, testing, correcting. A gate that's sticking gets tapped with a hammer in just the right spot. A pipe showing stress cracks gets wrapped with sealant tape, a temporary fix that might last years. An algae buildup on a condensation net gets scraped away before it can reduce collection efficiency.

They know their machinery intimately, individually. This pump runs slightly hot and needs extra monitoring during the condensation phase. That valve tends to stick when the flow rate drops below a certain threshold. This section of channel has a slow leak that's been there for decades, unfixable without tearing up half an acre of slakecrete, so instead you just account for the loss in your calculations.

They make adjustments constantly, tiny optimizations, keeping the system balanced. The Condensery Plains operates in a state of continuous marginal functionality—nothing works perfectly, but everything works well enough, maintained at the edge of breakdown by constant attention. The Tenders are what keep it from tipping over into failure, their labor invisible except in the continued operation of the machinery, their skill expressed as the absence of catastrophe.

The relationship between Tenders is hard to characterize.

They're not friends, not in any conventional sense. Friendship implies a social dimension that's atrophied here, replaced by something more functional. They're colleagues, partners in the work, each dependent on the others maintaining their sections so the whole system continues. When Tenders meet, there's a brief exchange—status updates mostly, information about flow rates or equipment states, sometimes a request for assistance with a repair that requires two people.

But there's also something deeper, harder to articulate. A recognition, maybe. A shared understanding that they've all been changed by this place, worn down and rebuilt like the landscape itself, until they're more similar to each other than to whoever they were before they came here. They look at each other and see mirrors, not of appearance but of function, of purpose, of the particular way the Condensery Plains has shaped them.

Romance, sex, family—these things persist in attenuated forms or don't persist at all. Some Tenders pair off, share sleeping spaces, find comfort in proximity. But the partnerships are quiet, practical, based more on compatible territories and work schedules than on passion. Children are rare to nonexistent here—whether because of something in the environment, or because the Tenders have collectively decided this isn't a place for children, or because whatever process brings new Tenders to the plains doesn't involve birth in any normal sense.

The older Tenders—the ones who seem to have been here for decades, who move through the machinery like ghosts, who can predict failures days before they happen—don't talk about their pasts. Maybe they've forgotten. Maybe the information has been worn away by the repetition of days, the cycles of condensation and clearing and misting grinding memory down to nothing. Maybe they came from somewhere else once, but that somewhere has become irrelevant, replaced entirely by here, by now, by the endless present tense of maintenance.

The new Tenders, when they appear, are already adapted.

They arrive knowing things they shouldn't know, moving through the machinery with confidence that suggests they've been trained, but no one remembers training them. They fit into gaps in the coverage, taking over territories that have been neglected or abandoned, and within weeks they're indistinguishable from the others—same posture, same efficiency, same distant listening expression.

Where they come from is a question that doesn't get asked. Maybe they come up from the deeper structures, levels below what's visible, promotion from some training ground no one talks about. Maybe they come from outside, though the idea of outside has become theoretical here, a concept rather than a place you could actually reach. Maybe they've always been here, just unnoticed until the system needed them to become visible.

What's certain is that the Tenders replenish themselves somehow. The population stays roughly constant despite the attrition of age and accident. Someone will be missing, their station silent, and then someone else will be there, maintaining the same equipment, sleeping in the same room, walking the same routes.

The hierarchy, if it exists, is invisible.

There are no supervisors, no managers, no one giving orders. The work proceeds according to the system's needs, and the Tenders respond, coordinating without coordination, each knowing their part because the machinery tells them what's required. The sound carries information—you can hear when a section is struggling, when flow rates are wrong, when intervention is needed—and the Tenders react, converging on problems without any obvious communication, working together until the system stabilizes, then dispersing back to their territories.

Some Tenders seem to have broader knowledge, deeper understanding. They're the ones other Tenders go to with unusual problems, situations that don't fit the standard procedures. They might be elders, though age is hard to determine. They might be specialists, though specialization implies a deliberate structure that's not visible. They're recognized somehow, respected, consulted, but they don't command. They advise, suggest, demonstrate, then return to their own work.

There are gatherings, occasionally, in the deep night of the darker grey periods.

In the Cathedral—that vast empty structure whose walls rise to arch overhead, enclosing a space that must have had some ceremonial purpose once—Tenders collect in numbers larger than you see elsewhere. Not all of them, perhaps not even most, but dozens, standing in the darkness lit only by their sparse work lights and the faint phosphorescence of the not-moss growing in cracks.

They stand in silence, listening.

To what? The machinery, certainly. The sound of the system running through its cycles. But maybe to something else, something in or under or behind the mechanical noise. The harmonics and beats, the moments when different sections synchronize briefly before drifting apart. The tones that sound almost like language. The vibrations coming up through the stone from the deep pumps, pulling ancient water from the substrate and mixing it with captured moisture from the air.

This isn't worship, exactly. The Tenders don't pray, don't perform rituals in any religious sense. But there's something ceremonial about it nonetheless, a collective acknowledgment of the system that contains them, that gives their lives shape and purpose. A moment of pure listening, of opening themselves to the information the machinery provides, of aligning themselves more completely with its function.

It lasts perhaps an hour, perhaps longer. Then, without signal or discussion, the Tenders disperse, returning to their territories, their work, the endless maintenance.

Some Tenders change in ways that seem to go beyond normal human adaptation.

The oldest ones, the ones who've been here so long they seem to be part of the infrastructure, show signs of convergence with the machinery itself. Their skin takes on a grey-green patina similar to the slakecrete, particularly on their hands and faces, the parts most exposed. They develop a sensitivity to vibration that's almost supernatural, able to detect minute changes in the system from floors away, through walls, their bodies resonating with the pumps and channels like tuning forks.

Their speech becomes even more abbreviated, until sometimes they don't speak at all, just emit sounds that are halfway between human vocalization and mechanical noise—whistles that match the pitch of steam vents, clicking that echoes the gates opening and closing. Other Tenders understand them perfectly. To an outsider, they sound like they're becoming part of the soundscape, their voices absorbed into the general cacophony.

They move differently too, with a precision that seems almost mechanical, their actions efficient to the point of being uncanny. No hesitation, no wasted motion, their hands knowing exactly where every tool is, every adjustment needed, as if the information is coming from outside their conscious thought, provided directly by the system itself.

Some spend less and less time in human spaces, preferring to stay in their stations, surrounded by the machinery, or walking their territories in endless circuits, never stopping, perpetually checking and adjusting. They become fixtures of the landscape like the condensation nets and the pumps, expected and unremarkable, their presence marked more by the smooth functioning of their section than by any direct encounter.

And eventually, they're not there anymore.

Not dead, necessarily—there are no bodies, no funerals, no marker of ending. Just absent, their territory empty for a time until a new Tender appears to fill it. Maybe they've moved to a different section. Maybe they've gone down into the deeper levels, if those exist. Maybe they've finally merged completely with the system, their consciousness distributed across the machinery they tended for so long, their awareness become something else, something that exists as pattern rather than person.

The other Tenders don't mourn these disappearances. Don't celebrate them either. They're noted, adjusted to, incorporated into the ongoing operation. Someone's gone, someone else is here, the work continues. That's the extent of the ceremony.

There are others in the Condensery Plains, though they're not Tenders.

The **Scribes** are what you might call them, though they have no official designation. There are perhaps a dozen of them, occupying small structures scattered across the plains, usually near the older ruins where the carved channels and empty niches suggest the place once had different purposes.

They're here to record, though what they're recording and for whom is unclear.

They keep logs—massive volumes bound in water-resistant material, filled with hand-written notes in ink that doesn't run when damp. Dates, times, water levels, flow rates, weather patterns, sounds heard, anomalies observed. They've been keeping these records for as long as anyone remembers, volume after volume, stored in rooms that are nothing but shelves, archives of data about the system's operation.

Who reads these records? Perhaps other Scribes, checking current conditions against historical patterns. Perhaps no one—maybe the act of recording is the purpose itself, preserving information against some future need that may never arrive. The Scribes treat the work with solemnity regardless, spending their shifts writing in careful hands, noting everything, creating a documentary shadow of the Condensery Plains that's as extensive as the physical place itself.

They're different from the Tenders—less changed by the landscape, more obviously still human. They speak more normally, wear clothing that's less uniform, maintain a separation between themselves and the machinery. They're here but not of here, observing rather than participating in the system's operation, though their observations have continued for so long they've become part of the structure, expected and relied upon.

The Scribes talk to the Tenders sometimes, asking questions about unusual occurrences, requesting details about repairs or failures. The Tenders answer briefly, impatiently, eager to return to work. The Scribes write it all down, preserving in their logs what the Tenders experience directly and immediately, creating a memory the Tenders themselves don't maintain.

Then there are the **Strangers**.

Not often—months or years between sightings—but occasionally, someone appears in the Condensery Plains who is neither Tender nor Scribe, who moves through the machinery with the hesitation of unfamiliarity, who still flinches at the sound, whose clothes are wrong for this place, too dry, too colorful, too much a reminder of elsewhere.

The Tenders notice them but rarely acknowledge them directly. A Stranger will walk through a section, staring at the condensation nets or the processing plants or the channels, trying to understand what they're seeing, and the Tenders will work around them, neither helping nor hindering, simply continuing their maintenance as if the Stranger is just another obstacle to navigate around like a pillar or a junction.

Some Strangers ask questions. The Tenders sometimes answer, in their abbreviated way, but the answers don't help much. "Collects water." "Processes algae." "Runs the system." These explanations don't satisfy because they don't address the real question, which is why, why all this, why here, why continue, and those questions the Tenders either can't or won't answer, maybe because they don't know, maybe because the questions don't make sense from inside the system.

Most Strangers leave fairly quickly. The sound is intolerable if you're not adapted to it, the dampness oppressive, the flat landscape disorienting in its sameness. They retreat to whatever edge they came from, back to places where the sun is visible and the ground is dry and silence is possible.

But some stay.

They find an empty station or are assigned one by some invisible authority. They're given tools, pointed toward a section of the machinery, and they begin to learn. Within weeks, the changes start—posture, speech, attention. Within months, they're indistinguishable from the other Tenders, and if you asked them why they stayed, they'd probably look at you with that distant, listening expression and say something like "the system needed maintenance" or simply not answer at all, already turning back to their work.

This is how the Tenders replenish themselves, maybe. Strangers arrive, some stay, they're transformed by the place, and the population remains constant. A slow exchange between the Condensery Plains and wherever else is, people flowing in, something else flowing out, though what that something else is—whether the Strangers who leave are still themselves or whether they've been changed too—isn't clear.

And there are the things that might not be alive but aren't quite not-alive either.

The **wets**, drifting in the channels, pulsing slowly as they feed on whatever microscopic content the water carries. They have no eyes, no brain that's visible, no structure that suggests consciousness, and yet they navigate the channel system with what looks like purpose, avoiding the intake screens when they can, clustering in certain areas where the water chemistry must suit them, occasionally washing up in places they shouldn't be, stranded in empty channels or inside buildings, drying to paper until the next misting revives them.

The Tenders scrape them off screens, dump them back into outflows, occasionally collect them in jars for reasons that aren't explained. The wets don't respond to humans, show no awareness of being handled, just pulse and drift and feed, existing at some boundary between animal and process, life and mechanism.

And the **sound-things**, those patches of air where the mechanical noise becomes visible, becomes tactile, becomes something that shouldn't exist under physics as usually understood.

They're not alive in any conventional sense. They don't eat or reproduce or die. They appear, drift, dissipate. But they seem to have... if not intention, then directionality. They cluster around the loudest machinery, yes, but also around the oldest structures, the places where the sound has been resonating for longest. They avoid certain areas—the open pools, the newer structures with their different acoustics—and prefer others, creating a kind of invisible map of the landscape based on sound properties.

And very, very rarely, they interact with the Tenders.

Most Tenders walk through them without effect beyond the brief disorientation, the nauseating distortion of sight and sensation. But some Tenders—usually the oldest ones, the ones most changed by the place—seem to perceive something in the sound-things, react to them, sometimes even appear to communicate with them in some fashion that involves standing very still in their presence, listening with complete attention, then moving with sudden certainty to address a problem elsewhere in the system.

Are the sound-things providing information? Are they the system's way of communicating its needs, a sensory mechanism it's evolved to bridge the gap between mechanical operation and human maintenance? Or are the Tenders simply interpreting noise as signal, finding meaning in randomness because they've been here so long they've learned to see patterns everywhere?

No one asks these questions out loud. The Tenders accept the sound-things as part of the environment, neither stranger than anything else here nor particularly noteworthy. They exist. The machinery exists. The Tenders exist. All of them work together to keep the system running, and the details of how that happens are less important than the fact that it does happen.

In the deepest darkness, in the periods of heaviest condensation when the fog is so thick you can barely see the structures around you, the boundary between the living and the mechanical and the strange becomes even less clear.

The Tenders move through the fog like ghosts, their shapes barely visible, their sounds merging with the machinery. The wets pulse in the channels, their translucent bodies catching what little light there is. The sound-things drift through it all, dense concentrations of auditory reality moving through visual obscurity. And underneath everything, the deep pumps rumble, pulling water from stone that remembers oceans, mixing time periods in the collection basins—water that fell as rain last week combining with water that's been locked in limestone for millions of years, all of it processed together, indistinguishable, becoming the substance that keeps the Condensery Plains running and the Tenders alive.

This is what lives here: humans who are becoming something else, creatures that might be animals or might be symptoms, phenomena that blur the line between physics and metaphysics, and underneath it all, the system itself, vast and ancient and self-perpetuating, conscious in the way a body is conscious of its parts, aware through the aggregate of its processes, alive in a manner that has nothing to do with biology and everything to do with continuation.

They exist in mutual dependence—the Tenders need the system to give their lives purpose and structure, the system needs the Tenders to maintain its function, the strange things need both to provide the conditions for their existence. None of them could exist elsewhere. None of them would want to, or would even understand the concept of elsewhere anymore.

They are the Condensery Plains, embodied and mobile, the landscape's distributed consciousness, its hands and eyes and ears, its capacity for self-repair and adaptation.

And they will continue until the moisture stops condensing or the stone finally dissolves completely or the last Tender transforms into something that can no longer hold tools, and maybe even then they'll continue, the system finding new ways to maintain itself, new forms of life or pseudo-life to serve its purposes.

Because the work is never finished. The water must be collected. The machinery must run. The channels must flow.

This is what it means to live in the Condensery Plains: to accept that you are not separate from your environment but continuous with it, to surrender individuality to function, to let the sound and the damp and the endless grey wear you down until you're smooth and integrated and essential.

Until you are, finally and completely, home.
