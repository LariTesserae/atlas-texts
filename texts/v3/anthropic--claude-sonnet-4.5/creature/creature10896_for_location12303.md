# Those Who Dwell in the Stepped Cities

## The People of Stone and Moisture

They are human, but shaped by this place across so many generations that the shaping shows in their bodies and their breath. Not dramatically—they are not a different species—but in the subtle adjustments that accumulate when populations stay put for millennia at altitude, in cold, in thin air.

Their chests are broad, barrel-deep, to house lungs that have learned to extract oxygen from atmosphere that holds less of it. Children born here have this already, inherited, though those who arrive from lower elevations struggle for months, sometimes years, gasping after exertion that the natives endure with steady breathing. Their blood runs thick with red cells, and visitors sometimes notice that cuts bleed darker, more viscous.

They are not tall. Height offers no advantage here; indeed, compactness conserves warmth. Most adults stand between five feet and five and a half, solidly built, dense with muscle from constant physical labor and from simply moving through a terrain of stairs and slopes. Their hands are broad, fingers blunt, adapted to handling cold stone and cold tools. They can work bare-handed in temperatures that would numb lowlanders, though they do wear gloves when the cold deepens—mittens of felted wool, or leather shells lined with fur.

Their faces have a weathered quality regardless of age, skin toughened by wind and dry cold, often reddened across cheeks and nose where blood vessels dilate to prevent frostbite. Eyes are deep-set, protected by strong brow bones, often narrowed from squinting against the glare when sun reflects off wet stone. Eye color runs to darker shades—browns, dark grays, occasionally a gray-blue that mirrors the stone itself. Hair is dark, coarse, worn in practical styles: cropped short, or braided and pinned close to the head, or hidden under wool caps. Beards are common among men who work outdoors, rare among those who work with fine crafts where stray hairs might interfere.

They dress in layers, always: an inner layer of soft wool or leather against skin, middle layers of felted wool or woven cloth, an outer layer of tightly-woven or leather-backed fabric that sheds moisture. The clothing is in the colors of their world—grays, browns, the natural tones of wool and leather, with occasional dyed pieces marking status or occasion. Wealthy individuals or those with ceremonial roles might wear a sash of red-dyed wool, or a vest with geometric patterns in vegetable-dyed threads. But daily wear is uniformly practical, muted, designed to last years and be repaired repeatedly.

Their gait is distinctive: a steady, tireless walking pace that can cover miles of terrace and stair without pause. They place feet carefully, automatically, never looking down because they've learned the surfaces from childhood. Children acquire this sure-footedness young, watching where adults step, learning which stones are stable and which might shift, developing an instinctive sense for the solidity of surfaces.

## The Varieties of Age and Role

The children are everywhere, often moving in loose groups, occupied with tasks appropriate to their years. The youngest ones, still toddling, stay mostly in courtyards under the watch of elders or older siblings, playing with simple toys—carved wooden animals, stones arranged in patterns, bits of leather to practice sewing stitches. By five or six, they're given real work: fetching water, feeding the courtyard animals, carrying messages between buildings, sweeping passages.

They're dressed as miniature adults, in the same layered woolens, though their clothes are patched and repatched, handed down through multiple children. They play, but their play includes practice for adult tasks—building tiny stone structures in courtyard corners, making miniature tools, learning to use slings to throw stones at targets (and occasionally at the cliff-runners, though they rarely hit them at first).

By ten or twelve, children are apprenticed to crafts or to herding or to water-management, beginning the long process of acquiring specialized knowledge. They still have the quickness and energy of youth, but it's being channeled into purposeful learning. They watch their teachers with remarkable intensity, memorizing sequences of movements, asking occasional questions in quiet voices.

Adolescents—thirteen to eighteen or so—are the most physically capable, strong and fast, with developing skill. They take pride in their emerging competence, sometimes competing to see who can carry more, work longer, climb faster on the terrace faces. They're also beginning to form the relationships that might become partnerships. Courtship is subtle here, conducted through glances and small gestures, the offering of a particularly well-made object, a song sung in a way that others recognize as directed at someone specific.

Adults in their prime—twenties through fifties—carry the bulk of the physical labor and the skilled craft work. They've achieved mastery of their specializations, whether that's stone-splitting or weaving or metal-forging or animal husbandry or water-management. They're also raising children, maintaining households, participating in the councils and decision-making processes. Their days are full from before dawn to after dark, yet they have a steadiness about them, an economy of movement that conserves energy across the long years.

They speak with authority on matters within their expertise, but defer to others in their domains. A master stone-worker might be near-silent in a council discussing water-rights, listening to the water-managers debate, but when discussion turns to reinforcing a terrace wall, that same person speaks and others listen.

Partnerships are mostly between a man and a woman, formalized through ceremonies that involve both families and witnessed by the community, but the form varies. Some partnerships are intensely close, the pair inseparable; others are more pragmatic arrangements for raising children and managing a household. Some individuals never partner, living instead in extended family compounds, their labor and identity located in craft or in care of aging parents or in service to communal needs.

The middle-aged—forties and fifties—begin to take on advisory roles, teaching younger crafters, serving on councils more frequently, managing the communal resources. They've seen enough seasons to know the patterns, to remember what happened the last time certain conditions arose. They're repositories of practical knowledge: which stone-seams are reliable, which trade routes become impassable in which seasons, which families have obligations to which others.

Elders—sixties and beyond for those who live that long—are valued for their memory and judgment. Physical labor becomes harder, and they shift to less demanding work: fine craft like carving or intricate weaving, supervision of children, management of household resources. They sit in courtyard doorways on stone benches worn smooth by generations of sitters, watching the activity, occasionally offering comments. Their voices carry weight in disputes because they've seen similar situations before, because they remember outcomes.

The very old—those who reach their seventies or beyond—are rare and respected, treated with a deference that includes ensuring they have warm places to sleep, good food, assistance with movement. They're walking histories, able to recall events from fifty years past, to describe how things were done before certain innovations, to tell stories of ancestors whose lives they overlapped. When they die, their passing is mourned publicly, and particular objects they made or owned are preserved in family shrines.

## The Specialists and Their Knowledge

Among the general population are those who carry specialized knowledge, not separate from the community but woven into it.

The stone-masters understand the rock itself. They can read fracture planes in a glance, know where to place a chisel to split a block cleanly, understand which stones will endure weather and which will spall and crack. They're consulted before any major construction, and they walk the terrace edges periodically, checking for instability, marking dangerous areas with cairns. They work mostly with apprentices, teaching through demonstration, occasionally explaining in technical language that has evolved over generations to describe precisely the qualities of different stones.

The water-finders manage the collection and distribution systems. They know which cisterns feed which channels, where blockages are likely to occur, how to read the weather to predict when particular springs will flow. They carry mental maps of extraordinary complexity, able to navigate through the underground relationships between rainfall and seepage. In disputes over water rights—which happen regularly, given the resource's importance—their word is usually final.

The metal-workers are fewer, their craft requiring resources that must be imported and temperatures that must be carefully controlled. They work bronze mostly, casting or forging tools and ornaments and structural elements. The forge-spaces are hot and bright, dramatic contrasts to the general cold dimness. The workers wear leather aprons and cloth wrappings on their arms, and they develop a particular squint from years of watching molten metal. They're often partially deaf from the hammering, and they communicate partly through gestures, a sign language that has developed in the forge-spaces.

The fiber-workers—mostly women, though not exclusively—transform the wool from the plateau animals into textiles of remarkable warmth and durability. They've developed techniques of felting and weaving that create fabrics with specific properties: wind-resistant outer layers, insulating middle layers, soft inner layers. They work in groups, often in courtyard spaces when weather permits, singing work songs that help coordinate the rhythm of the looms. The songs are old, the words sometimes archaic, referring to things or places no longer present, but the rhythms are perfect for the work.

The herders manage the semi-feral animal populations, a task more like wildlife management than traditional herding. They know individual animals, can identify them by markings and behavior, track lineages, understand the social structures of the herds. They guide the animals toward better grazing, prevent over-concentration in particular courtyards, cull when necessary, and select breeding animals for desired traits. They spend much time outdoors, moving through the cities and across the plateau surface, and they're often accompanied by children learning the work.

The food-preparers have knowledge of preservation and nutrition, crucial for survival. They know fermentation times, drying techniques, how to combine limited ingredients for adequate nutrition, which stored foods to use when to maintain variety and prevent spoilage. They manage communal storage in the stone cellars, rotating stock, monitoring for pests or decay. During festivals, they orchestrate complex meal preparations, coordinating multiple households to create feasts from pooled resources.

The singers and rememberers maintain the oral traditions: the stories of how the cities were founded, the genealogies of important families, the great events—catastrophic terrace collapses, successful defenses against raiders from lower elevations, epidemics survived, innovations discovered. They perform in formal contexts—festivals, funerals, partnership ceremonies—but also informally, telling stories to children, singing work songs, teaching the younger rememberers. Their knowledge is prodigious, stored in memory because writing, while present, is limited to important records and contracts. The rememberers can recite for hours without repetition, their voices rising and falling in patterns that aid memory.

The healers work with the injuries and illnesses that occur. They set broken bones, stitch wounds, treat the respiratory ailments common at altitude, manage the infections that can follow even minor cuts. They know medicinal plants—the few that grow on the plateau, and those that arrive via trade—and prepare infusions, poultices, salves. They also know when they cannot help, and they ease dying when it comes, staying with the afflicted, speaking quietly, ensuring they're not alone.

## The Textures of Relationship

People here are never truly alone. The architecture ensures it, the density of population guarantees it, and the culture has adapted to make proximity bearable and even supportive.

Families are extended and complex. A household might include three generations: grandparents, their adult children with partners, and grandchildren. Spaces are shared, meals are communal, decisions involve consultation. Privacy exists only in small moments—a walk alone on the terrace at dawn, a corner of a workroom where one sits with a particular task.

But within this closeness is a practiced courtesy, a respect for interior states. People recognize when someone needs quiet and grant it without discussion. They read moods and adjust accordingly. A person who sits apart during a communal meal is noted but not questioned unless it persists, becomes a pattern that suggests real trouble.

Friendships form through shared work, through proximity in the passages, through childhood play that evolves into adult companionship. Friends might meet regularly in particular courtyards, sharing the day's events, discussing problems, simply sitting together in comfortable silence. These relationships are steady, enduring, not emotionally dramatic but deeply reliable.

Tensions exist, inevitably. Disagreements over resource use, over boundaries, over obligations that haven't been met. The culture has mechanisms for managing these: mediation by respected elders, formal councils where both sides present their positions, and ultimately the pressure of continued proximity. You cannot avoid your neighbors, so resolution becomes necessary.

Grudges are remembered but usually not acted upon. Someone who feels wronged might be stiff and formal with the offender for years, but they'll still work together when necessary, still participate in the communal tasks. The memory of the wrong is preserved—recorded in the oral histories if significant enough—but life continues.

Love happens in various forms: the passionate attachments of youth, the steady partnerships of adulthood, the deep friendships that can be as important as family bonds, the love of parents for children and children for aging parents. It's expressed through actions more than words—through making something beautiful for someone, through assistance offered without asking, through showing up when needed.

Sexuality is present but not discussed openly. Partnerships are expected to be sexually exclusive, though lapses are sometimes forgiven, especially if handled discreetly. There's some flexibility for those who don't fit standard patterns—people whose desires are for their own gender, or for both, or for none. As long as they contribute to the community, maintain their obligations, they're accepted, though perhaps not entirely understood.

Children are communally valued. A child with no living parents will be absorbed into another household without question. Childless adults often take particular interest in nieces, nephews, or neighbors' children, teaching them crafts, telling them stories, providing additional guidance. The whole community participates in raising children, correcting dangerous behavior, praising good work, ensuring they learn what they need to survive.

## The Rhythms of Daily Being

People wake before dawn, roused by internal clocks attuned to the rhythm of seasons. The first task is always fire—reviving the coals from overnight, adding fuel, bringing warmth back to cold rooms. Then comes water: checking the collection vessels for condensation, fetching from the nearest channel if needed, starting the morning drink—an infusion of herbs, sometimes with milk from the animals.

Breakfast is simple: a porridge of grain, or bread kept from the previous day's baking, perhaps cheese, occasionally dried meat. People eat quickly, already thinking about the day's tasks, which are numerous and specific. The work begins early and continues with brief breaks until the light fades.

Midday brings a longer pause, a meal that's more substantial: stew often, made with preserved meat and root vegetables, thickened with grain, seasoned with herbs. People eat in household groups, or in work groups if they're laboring away from home. The pause allows rest, essential given the altitude and the physical demands of the work.

The afternoon work continues until dusk, when the light begins to fail and tasks requiring visual precision must stop. The evening is for final chores—securing animals, checking on elders, preparing for night—and then for the small pleasures: a better meal if resources allow, conversation, music, craft work that can be done by lamplight, stories told to children before sleep.

Sleep comes early by lowland standards, shortly after full dark in most seasons. People sleep heavily, bodies exhausted from the day's labor, wrapped in wool blankets on platforms covered with furs or felted mats. Couples sleep together for warmth as much as intimacy. Children cluster in shared spaces, their small bodies generating surprising heat.

Some people wake in the middle of the night, briefly, check the fire, listen to ensure all is well, then return to sleep. Night-workers—bakers, those maintaining watch—move through the passages quietly, their lamps small points of light in the darkness.

## The Visitors and the Strangers

Traders arrive periodically from lower elevations, distinctive in their lighter build, their initial breathlessness at altitude, their different clothing styles. They bring goods the plateau cannot produce: grain in bulk, fruits dried or preserved, certain woods, sometimes luxuries like dyed fabrics or metal ingots or spices.

The plateau people receive them with formal courtesy—there are protocols for trade that have evolved over generations, ways of negotiating that minimize conflict. The traders are given shelter in designated buildings near the city edges, fed from communal stores, allowed to rest and acclimate before serious trading begins.

The exchange takes days, sometimes weeks. Trade is not just commercial but social, an opportunity for news, for stories of the lower lands, for the traders to hear plateau news in return. Marriages are sometimes arranged between trader families and plateau families, creating ongoing relationships that smooth future exchanges.

Some traders return year after year, becoming familiar figures, learning the plateau languages, forming friendships. Others come once and never again, finding the cold and altitude too difficult, or deciding the profit doesn't justify the hardship of the journey.

Occasionally, refugees arrive—people fleeing conflicts in the lower lands, seeking safety in the plateau's remoteness and harsh conditions that discourage pursuit. The plateau people assess these refugees carefully. If they seem willing to work, to learn, to integrate, they're allowed to stay, given space in a household that needs labor, taught the necessary skills. If they seem likely to cause trouble, they're given supplies and directed to move on.

Very rarely, scholars or seekers arrive, people pursuing knowledge or spiritual goals, drawn by stories of the plateau's distinctive culture or by religious traditions associated with high places. These are treated with puzzled hospitality. The plateau people share what's requested—demonstrate their crafts, explain their water systems, allow observation of rituals—but they don't understand the fascination. To them, this is simply how life is lived; they don't see it as remarkable.

## Those Who Leave and Those Who Return

Some young people, restless with the confined patterns of plateau life, descend to the lower lands seeking something else—adventure, different work, escape from family obligations or failed relationships. They're allowed to go with mixed feelings: sadness at the loss, understanding of the urge, hope they might return.

Some do return, after months or years, having satisfied their curiosity or failed to find what they sought below. They're received back without judgment, though they must re-earn their place, prove they haven't forgotten the essential knowledge. Often they bring new skills, new ideas, innovations that can be adapted to plateau conditions.

Others never return, and they're mourned in a particular way—not as dead, but as lost to a different world. Their names are remembered, stories are told about them, and if word arrives of their doings below, those stories are incorporated into the family narratives.

Occasionally, someone who left sends gifts back—goods from the lowlands, money earned through trade. These are appreciated, distributed among family members, but there's always an edge of sadness to them, a reminder of absence.

## The Embodied Knowledge

What's most striking about the people here is how much they know that they don't know they know—the vast body of embodied knowledge that operates below conscious thought.

They know stone by feel: can distinguish between types by texture even in darkness, know the sound a chisel should make against different stones, can sense impending fracture by subtle changes in resistance.

They know weather through body-awareness: feel in their joints when storms are coming, smell the change in air before rain, sense the quality of cold that means snow versus clear night.

They know their animals individually: recognize the bleat of a particular creature, notice when its behavior changes in ways that indicate illness or pregnancy or distress, understand the social dynamics of the herds through observation accumulated over lifetimes.

They know the passages of their cities without thinking: navigate in near-darkness through spaces they've walked since childhood, take shortcuts that visitors don't notice, understand the three-dimensional maze at an instinctive level.

They know time without clocks: judge the hour by light quality, by the rhythm of activities around them, by internal patterns developed through years of rising and sleeping according to natural cycles.

This knowledge is transmitted through participation rather than instruction. Children absorb it by being present, by watching, by trying and being corrected gently, by years of immersion in the patterns and practices. By adulthood, it's part of them, as natural as breathing.

## The Oddities and Variations

Within the general patterns are individuals who don't quite fit, whose differences make them notable.

There's the woman in one city who has a gift for predicting stone fractures, who can look at a terrace wall and know where it will fail years before it happens. People bring her to look at structures, trust her judgment even when they can't see what she sees. She's treated with a respect that borders on wariness—her knowledge is useful but uncanny.

There's the man who lives alone by choice, occupying a tiny building at a city's edge, working as a carver of remarkable skill. He speaks rarely, seems uncomfortable around others, but his carvings—small stone animals, geometric patterns of hypnotic complexity—are treasured. People leave food for him, receive carvings in return, respect his need for solitude.

There are twins in one of the cities, identical, who've developed their own language and work in perfect coordination. They're stone-setters, and watching them work is mesmerizing—they move in synchrony, seeming to know without communication where the other will place the next stone. They're valued workers but socially isolated by their intensity of connection to each other.

There's an elder woman who remembers everything, literally—events from seventy years past recalled with perfect clarity, genealogies extending back generations, every story she's ever heard. She's consulted constantly, has become essentially a living archive, though the burden of such memory has made her serious, almost grim.

There are children who show unusual aptitudes—for music, for mathematics, for physical coordination—and these are noted, encouraged, channeled into appropriate apprenticeships. Talent is valued but also expected to serve communal needs. A gifted musician might want to experiment and innovate, but is also expected to perform the traditional songs at ceremonies.

## The Unspoken and the Avoided

Certain topics are not discussed openly, though everyone is aware of them.

Mental instability happens here as elsewhere. Someone who becomes unpredictable, who rages without cause or withdraws into unresponsive silence, is cared for as well as possible—kept safe, prevented from harm—but there's limited understanding of such conditions. They're attributed to spirit affliction or to personal weakness, and they carry shame for the family.

There are deaths that aren't quite accidents—people who walk out onto the plateau in storms and are found frozen, or who fall from terrace edges in circumstances that seem questionable. These are treated as tragedies, mourned publicly, but privately there's understanding that the harshness of life here can become unbearable, that some choose to leave it.

Violence between individuals is rare but happens. When it does, the response is calibrated to the offense. Minor violence—a punch thrown in anger—is handled through mediation and compensation. Serious violence—injury with weapons, assault—results in exile, the perpetrator driven from the city with minimal supplies, forbidden to return. Murder is so rare there's no standard response, but historical cases resulted in execution, the body denied the usual burial rites.

Theft is similarly rare, because in a community where everyone knows everyone, there's nowhere to hide stolen goods. But it happens occasionally, usually items small enough to conceal. The response is public shaming and restitution, with the thief required to repay multiple times the value of what was taken.

There are also the sorrows that are simply borne: children who die in infancy or childhood, stillbirths, women who die in childbirth, young people killed in accidents. These are mourned, honored with ceremonies, and then life continues because it must.

## The Sense of Themselves

The plateau people understand themselves as different from those below—tougher, more capable, adapted to conditions others cannot endure. There's pride in this, though not often expressed directly. It emerges in stories, in the subtle condescension toward lowlanders who struggle with the altitude and cold, in the satisfaction of successful trade deals where lowland goods are acquired for what the plateau people consider reasonable exchange.

They also understand themselves as part of something larger and older than individual lives. The cities predate any living memory, and the expectation is that they'll continue long after. This creates a particular orientation—toward maintenance and transmission rather than innovation, toward stability rather than growth, toward ensuring that what has been learned is not lost.

Yet they're not static or passive. Within the constraints of their environment, they make choices, pursue preferences, create beauty beyond mere function. The textiles have patterns that vary by maker, expressing individual aesthetic sense. The songs, while traditional, are interpreted differently by different singers. The stone carvings that ornament doorways and niches show individual styles.

They find meaning in the work itself, in the mastery of craft, in the raising of children, in the maintenance of relationships, in the rhythms of seasons and ceremonies. They don't tend toward philosophical abstraction—life is too full of immediate physical demands—but they have a developed sense of rightness, of how things should be done, of the obligations people have toward each other and toward the continuity of the community.

They're capable of joy, though it's often quiet: the satisfaction of a task completed well, the pleasure of a good meal shared, the warmth of sitting with friends as evening falls, the beauty of the plateau under clearing skies after rain. And they're capable of sorrow, though it too is often quiet, borne with the same steadiness that characterizes their response to cold and altitude and the unending demands of survival.

They are, in the end, deeply human—shaped by their place but not defined entirely by it, carrying the full range of human capacity within the particular expression that thousands of years in the stepped cities have created.
