# What Lives in the Drowned Scaffolds

The **grip-walkers** are everywhere, the fundamental animal of this place. They range from the size of your thumb to the length of your leg, but they all share the same basic form: four to six limbs ending in broad, adhesive pads; bodies flattened dorsoventrally into teardrop shapes that present minimal resistance to upward flow; skin that's permeable enough to extract oxygen from the water-film they inhabit but tough enough to resist abrasion against stone. Their colors trend toward the stone's palette—gray-green, mottled brown, occasionally a pale cream—but they can darken or lighten somewhat, a limited chromatophory that helps them fade into the rock when larger predators pass.

The smaller grip-walkers, the ones the size of your finger, move in abundance through the kelp-forests and fern-meadows. They're grazers primarily, scraping bacterial mats and algae from stone surfaces with rasping mouthparts that work constantly, audibly, a collective sound like gentle sandpaper. They lay eggs in the crevices, gelatinous masses that cling to the rock and develop quickly—within days the larvae emerge, already equipped with their adhesive pads, already flattened for flow. The mortality rate is enormous; the egg masses are rich protein sources for anything that can reach them, and the larvae that do hatch are small enough to be swept away if they don't find purchase immediately. But the reproductive strategy is simply to overwhelm the loss with numbers. Every suitable crevice holds eggs. Every square foot of stone hosts dozens of young grip-walkers learning to hold on, to move against current, to read the turbulence and find the calmer microhabitats within the flow.

The larger grip-walkers—the ones you first noticed, two or three feet long—are predators. They move with deliberate slowness most of the time, conserving energy, their bodies pressed flat to the stone as they hunt by a combination of vibration-sensing and chemical detection. The water flowing over them carries information: the scent of prey, the pressure-wake of movement, the subtle temperature differences that indicate a warm-blooded creature nearby. When they strike, it's explosive—a sudden release of coiled muscle that launches them across gaps between stones or through thinner water-films. They have teeth, rows of them, sharp and recurved, designed to grip rather than shear. Once they bite, they hold on, and their prey—usually other grip-walkers, occasionally a fish-shape that's ventured too close to the stone—weakens through blood loss and exhaustion. The large grip-walkers are patient. They can maintain a hold for hours if necessary, letting the current and their own locked jaws do the work.

They're not strictly solitary, but they're not social either. You see them tolerate each other's presence at feeding sites—when a particularly rich bloom of algae attracts grazers, multiple large grip-walkers might hunt the same area without conflict. But there's no cooperation, no pack behavior. Each is its own economy, its own closed system of hunting and resting and reproduction. In breeding season—you haven't witnessed this, but the evidence is there in scars and missing limbs—the males fight, ritualized combat that involves showing the throat, displaying size, and if that fails, actual violence. The victor mates; the loser retreats to heal or dies from infection. The females lay their eggs alone, in deep crevices they'll defend briefly before abandoning, their parental investment exhausted.

The **sky-fish** live in the water-columns, the suspended rivers and curtains that arc between pillars. They're not fish in the terrestrial sense—they lack true bones, having instead a cartilaginous skeleton that flexes and compresses—but fish-shaped in the way that fast-moving aquatic predators everywhere converge on the same general body plan. Streamlined. Muscled. Efficient.

The smaller sky-fish, the ones that school, are filter-feeders, though "filter" undersells the complexity. Their mouths are huge relative to body size, opening to form a scoop that catches not just the organic particles suspended in the water-columns but also the smaller arthropods, the larvae, the bacterial mats that slough off the oily films coating certain flows. They swim constantly—rest for a sky-fish means death, means sinking out of the column into thinner water where they can't extract enough oxygen—and they orient always into the flow, holding position or ascending slowly, their bodies working against the current in a way that looks effortless but represents a massive metabolic commitment. They school for efficiency: the fish at the front breaks the current, and those behind draft in the reduced resistance, a cycling arrangement where individuals rotate from front to back in patterns you'd have to watch for hours to fully decode.

Their spawning is coordinated with the turbulence cycles. When the water-architecture reconfigures—when a cable-stream broadens into a curtain, or a curtain splits into spiraling flows—there's a moment of chaos, a brief window where the current becomes unpredictable. The schooling sky-fish use this window. They release eggs and milt simultaneously into the turbulence, letting the chaos mix and scatter the fertilized eggs across vast distances. Most eggs are carried down-current, falling eventually into the kelp-forests below where they settle into the vegetation and develop. The larvae that hatch must immediately swim upward, against flow, ascending through increasingly strong currents until they reach the water-columns where they'll spend their adult lives. It's brutal selective pressure—only the strongest swimmers survive—but it ensures that the sky-fish population maintains the vigor necessary for their demanding lifestyle.

The larger sky-fish, the pike-shapes and bigger, are hunters. They patrol the boundaries between water-columns and thinner air, exploiting the transition zones. Their hunting strategy is to wait, hovering in a column with minimal effort, watching the stone surfaces and kelp-forests nearby through eyes that are disproportionately large, adapted for the filtered greenish light. When they spot a grip-walker or a smaller sky-fish that's strayed from its school, they strike: a burst of speed that carries them out of the column, through air or thin water-film, impact, bite, retreat back to the column with prey secured. They can't survive long outside the thick water—they suffocate in air or thin flow within minutes—so their attacks are brief, calculated, successful or abandoned quickly.

These large sky-fish are solitary and territorial. Each claims a section of a major water-column—perhaps a hundred-foot stretch—and defends it from others of similar size. The defense is mostly display: flaring fins that are normally kept folded tight, opening the mouth to show teeth, patterns of bioluminescent cells along the flanks that flash in sequences you begin to recognize as communication. Actual combat is rare, risky; a damaged sky-fish can't maintain position in the current and will fall to its death. So they settle disputes through demonstration of fitness, and only when two fish are closely matched does it escalate to violence.

Their lifespan appears to be measured in years—you see individuals with scars that look old, healed into thick ridges, suggesting survival over multiple seasons. And you see one, once, that's clearly dying: hanging in the current with visible effort, its movements uncoordinated, its color faded to a sickly pale. It falls eventually, sinking out of the column in a slow spiral, and within seconds smaller opportunists appear, fish-shapes you hadn't noticed before, specializing in carrion. They're faster than the dying fish, more maneuverable, and they begin feeding before it's even fully dead, tearing away chunks of flesh that they swallow whole. By the time the carcass reaches the kelp-forest below, it's mostly bones and cartilage, and those will be scavenged too, by the grip-walkers and the crab-shapes and the detritivores that specialize in breaking down the hard parts.

The **flying rays**—you've decided that's the best name, though they're as unrelated to terrestrial rays as the sky-fish are to terrestrial fish—are something else entirely. They're vertebrates, probably, given their internal structure visible through the thin membrane of their wings, but they've adapted to an aerial lifestyle in an environment where "aerial" is a complex proposition. They don't fly so much as they glide and soar, exploiting the wind currents that surge between pillars, the convection generated by the temperature differentials, the pressure waves that propagate through the air when the water-architecture reconfigures.

Their bodies are almost nonexistent—a thin rod of muscle and bone perhaps two feet long—and the wings extend from this central body in membranes that span three or four feet tip to tip. The membranes are translucent, veined with what might be blood vessels or structural fibers, and they flex constantly, adjusting to micro-changes in the air. The rays' heads are small, with large eyes positioned to look downward primarily, scanning the stone surfaces and water-films for prey.

They feed on the smaller arthropods, the ones that live in the mist-globes and the transition zones where water atomizes into spray. Their hunting method is to glide through or just above these zones, mouth open, trailing specialized filaments from their lower jaw that capture small creatures on contact. The filaments are sticky, coated in mucus, and once prey adheres, the ray curls its jaw inward, drawing the filaments into its mouth where the prey is scraped off and swallowed. It's efficient in terms of energy—the ray expends almost nothing on the capture itself, just the metabolic cost of gliding—but it requires excellent flight control, the ability to maneuver through turbulent air and mist without colliding with stone or water-columns.

You watch one for an extended period, following its flight path between pillars. It rides an updraft created by warm air rising from a spot where geological heat apparently warms the stone, spiraling higher and higher until it's a distant shape against the diffused light above. Then it banks, folds its wings slightly, and begins a long gliding descent, covering lateral distance as it loses altitude, adjusting course to pass through multiple mist-globes in sequence, feeding opportunistically. When it reaches a lower altitude, it finds another updraft and begins climbing again. The pattern repeats, endlessly efficient, a lifestyle based on finding and exploiting the atmospheric energy available in this place.

They roost on the stone surfaces, clinging with small clawed feet, folding their wings tightly against their bodies to minimize exposure to the water-flow. During these rest periods, they're vulnerable; you see a large grip-walker approach one roosting ray, clearly considering an attack. But the ray notices—it has excellent peripheral vision—and launches before the grip-walker can strike, a single powerful downstroke of the wings carrying it into the air, where it's safe, and the grip-walker returns to hunting easier prey.

Their breeding involves the old platforms, the industrial structures. The rays prefer to nest on horizontal surfaces that are above the thickest water-flow, and the platforms, tilted though they are, provide this. You see evidence: the white splash of guano crusting parts of the platforms, the stick-and-kelp constructions that pass for nests. The rays lay eggs—two or three per clutch—and both parents tend them, taking turns sitting and hunting. When the young hatch, they're already equipped with wings, but small, underdeveloped. They spend weeks on the platform, being fed by parents who regurgitate partially digested arthropods, and during this time they're intensely vulnerable. The salamander-grip-walkers patrol beneath the platforms specifically hunting for fallen chicks, for eggs that roll off during turbulence, for any opportunity. Most clutches lose at least one offspring. Some lose all.

The surviving juveniles eventually fledge, launching from the platform in first flights that are clumsy, terrifying, sometimes fatal. You witness one: a young ray at the platform edge, wings spread, hesitating, while a parent circles nearby calling—a sound like metal scraping, surprisingly harsh. The juvenile finally commits, drops into open air, flails, catches a current, stabilizes, and begins its gliding career. The parent joins it, and they circle together for several minutes before separating, the juvenile now committed to the aerial life.

The **mist-dwellers** are the small things, the ones you only notice when you look closely at the mist-globes hanging between pillars. They're arthropods mostly, ranging from microscopic to the size of your fingernail, and they've adapted to a life lived entirely in suspended droplets, in the atomized water that hangs in globe-formations for minutes or hours before dispersing.

There are shrimp-like creatures, transparent, with legs modified into paddles that let them swim through the dense mist as if it were thicker water. They're filter-feeders at this scale, consuming bacteria and algae, and they're present in such numbers that the mist-globes are essentially nutritional substrates, food-sources that the flying rays and certain specialized sky-fish exploit.

There are predators among the mist-dwellers too: larger arthropods with grasping limbs and decent vision, hunting the shrimp-shapes by sight and vibration. These predators look like miniature mantises, their bodies segmented and angular, their raptorial forearms folded until striking. They position themselves at the edges of mist-globes, where clarity is slightly better, and ambush prey that swims past.

And there are polyps, sessile organisms that somehow anchor to the droplets themselves, or perhaps to the organic particles suspended in the mist, creating fixed points in the drifting globes. These polyps extend feathery tentacles that catch even smaller prey—the bacteria, the algae, the larvae of other mist-dwellers—and they reproduce by budding, each polyp eventually splitting to form two that drift slightly apart in the mist's internal currents. When a mist-globe disperses—when the turbulence shifts and the droplets fall or rise or simply spread too thin to maintain coherence—the polyps either die or, if fortunate, land on stone or kelp where they might, possibly, survive long enough to be caught in the formation of a new globe.

The mist-globes themselves have a kind of ecology distinct from the individual organisms within them. Older globes—ones that have persisted for hours—accumulate higher concentrations of life, becoming visible as faintly cloudy masses compared to the clearer, newer globes. These older globes attract the flying rays preferentially; the rays have learned to distinguish rich feeding from poor. And when a globe finally dissipates, it releases a pulse of organic material that falls or rises into the surrounding water and air, a nutrient subsidy for whatever ecosystem lies in that direction.

The **deep-grazers** live in the kelp-forests and fern-meadows, specializing in the lower elevations where the water-flow is most consistent and thick. They're slug-like, soft-bodied, ranging from an inch to nearly a foot in length. Their skin is covered in cilia that beat constantly, creating a mucus layer that protects against abrasion and helps them adhere to surfaces. They move slowly—observing one for ten minutes reveals only a few inches of progress—but relentlessly, grazing paths through the bacterial mats and algae films that cover every surface.

Their mouths are on their undersides, equipped with a radula that scrapes the stone and kelp clean, leaving visible trails: pale scratches on the dark rock, stripped sections on the kelp-trunks where the surface growth has been consumed. They're hermaphroditic, and they reproduce through a careful ritual where two individuals meet, intertwine, exchange gametes, and separate, each then laying eggs in protected crevices. The eggs are enormous relative to body size—a deep-grazer might produce only a dozen eggs in a spawning, but each egg is large enough to see individually, and each contains enough yolk to support a fully developed juvenile that hatches ready to graze immediately.

Their predators are primarily the grip-walkers and certain of the sky-fish that can tolerate brief periods in the thicker, kelp-dense water. But the deep-grazers have defense: when disturbed, they release the mucus layer suddenly, becoming incredibly slippery, nearly impossible to hold. A grip-walker that tries to bite a deep-grazer often finds its mouth full of mucus and an escaping prey item. It's not foolproof—a sufficiently large predator can simply swallow the deep-grazer whole—but it's effective against casual predation.

The deep-grazers also serve an ecological function beyond their own survival: their grazing keeps the bacterial mats from overgrowing the stone and kelp, maintaining the diversity of the surface community. In areas where deep-grazers are absent—perhaps a section where predation has been too heavy, or where the flow is wrong for their lifestyle—the mats grow thick, eventually choking out other growths, and the diversity collapses until the mats themselves die back and the cycle resets.

The **crab-shapes** are not crabs but they fill the same niche: scavengers and opportunistic predators, armored in carapaces of calcium carbonate, moving on multiple legs with a sideways-walking gait that looks identical to terrestrial crabs because that's simply an efficient way to move when you're built low and wide. They're larger than the grip-walkers generally—the biggest are nearly two feet across, leg-tip to leg-tip—and they inhabit the same stone surfaces but focus more on the crevices, the overhangs, the areas where detritus accumulates.

Their claws are asymmetrical: one large and powerful for crushing and defense, one smaller and more precise for manipulating food. They'll eat anything: dead grip-walkers, fallen sky-fish, the eggs of other creatures, the polyps and sessile organisms that grow on the rock, even kelp if other food is scarce. They're particularly abundant near the old platforms, where rust-flakes and metal fragments provide minerals that they apparently need for carapace maintenance. You see them scraping at the rust, consuming it directly, and you notice that their shells often show a reddish tint, especially on the largest, oldest individuals.

They're aggressive to each other and to anything they think they might overpower. You watch two crab-shapes dispute over a section of kelp-forest, claws raised, circling. The smaller backs down, but only after a display that involves drumming the large claw against stone—a sound that carries even over the water-roar, a rapid tapping that must communicate something: size, fitness, willingness to fight. The larger takes the territory, and the smaller relocates to a different pillar, making the leap across a gap by timing its jump with the turbulence cycles, when the water-flow between pillars is at its thinnest.

They're long-lived, apparently. The largest individuals have carapaces covered in growth—small barnacle-analogs, algae, even tiny polyps that have found purchase on the walking shell. These crusted crab-shapes move more slowly, weighted down by their passengers, but they're also left alone by predators. Nothing attacks a two-foot crab-shape with a crushing claw that can break stone.

The **singing-hollows** are not a single organism but a symbiotic system, and you only gradually piece together what you're observing. Inside certain pillars—the ones with extensive solution cavities created by ancient water flow through soluble rock layers—there are colonies of sessile organisms lining the cavity walls. They look like anemones, tentacled and soft-bodied, extending from the stone into the water that fills these internal spaces. The water circulates through the cavities, driven by the pressure differentials, entering through cracks at lower elevations and exiting higher up, and the anemone-things filter feed from this flow.

But they also produce sound. Each individual vibrates at a specific frequency, using internal muscles to flutter a membrane, and when hundreds or thousands of individuals in a single cavity vibrate together, the cavity itself resonates, producing the deep booming you heard earlier. The sound carries through the stone, emerging as vibrations across the pillar's surface.

And living within the anemone-things, you eventually observe, are small fish-shapes—juveniles, perhaps the young of the sky-fish species, seeking shelter in the tentacles like clownfish in terrestrial anemones. The fish are immune to the stinging cells that the anemone-things use to paralyze prey, and in exchange for shelter, the fish bring food: they venture out of the cavity during foraging trips, returning with small arthropods and detritus which they release near the anemone-things' mouths.

The booming seems to serve multiple functions. It's territorial—other singing-hollow colonies respond with their own booming, different frequencies, and over time you could probably map the locations of multiple colonies by their acoustic signatures. It might also be reproductive—the vibrations perhaps help synchronize spawning among the anemone-things, or help disperse gametes through the water-filled cavities. And it definitely affects the local ecosystem: certain creatures are attracted to the booming, perhaps using it as a landmark for navigation, while others avoid it.

When a singing-hollow colony dies—and you find evidence of this: a cavity whose walls are covered in dead, rotting anemone-things, the water inside foul with decay—the cavity goes silent. But the silence doesn't last. Within weeks or months, new larvae settle on the walls, beginning a new colony, and gradually the sound returns, lower at first, increasing as the colony matures.

The **sky-jellies** are rare, or perhaps just rarely visible. You see one over the course of your observation: a translucent, gelatinous organism perhaps three feet in diameter, drifting through the air between water-columns. It's clearly a medusa-form, with trailing tentacles that extend ten feet below its bell, but it's floating in air, not water, somehow maintaining buoyancy through an internal gas bladder or some mechanism you can't immediately determine.

It moves slowly, passively, carried by wind and current, and it feeds by trailing its tentacles through the mist-globes. The tentacles are covered in stinging cells—you watch as one touches a flying ray, and the ray reacts instantly, veering away, clearly having been stung. The tentacles are lethal to small prey: when they pass through a mist-globe, dozens of tiny arthropods stick to them, paralyzed, and the sky-jelly gradually retracts the tentacles toward its mouth, consuming the caught prey.

Its life cycle is mysterious. Does it reproduce here in the air, or does it have a polyp stage anchored to stone somewhere? Do the juveniles develop in the water-columns before transitioning to aerial drifting? You don't observe enough to know, but its presence suggests a whole dimension of the ecology that operates primarily in the air, exploiting the aerial plankton of the mist-globes.

The **bore-worms** you don't see directly, but their effects are everywhere: the holes in the stone, perfectly circular, ranging from the diameter of your finger to the diameter of your fist, drilled into the rock in patterns that suggest systematic boring rather than natural weathering. Inside these holes, if you could look closely enough, you'd find the worms: segmented, bristled, equipped with jaws at their anterior end that are reinforced with metal-infused compounds—iron, perhaps, or manganese—making them hard enough to actually excavate stone.

They're filter-feeders once they've established a burrow, extending feathery gills from the burrow entrance to catch organic particles from the water-flow. But juveniles must bore their own burrows, a process that takes weeks or months and requires consuming the stone-powder they create, digesting what organic material and minerals they can, excreting the rest. It's a slow, patient lifestyle, but once established, a bore-worm can live for years in its burrow, growing, reproducing by releasing gametes into the water-flow during mass spawning events.

Their burrows provide habitat for other creatures: small arthropods, juvenile grip-walkers, even certain fish-shapes use abandoned bore-worm holes as shelters. And when a bore-worm dies, its burrow remains, a permanent modification to the stone that adds to the growing complexity of surface texture. Over centuries, over millennia, the bore-worms and their predecessors have contributed to the erosion of the pillars, their collective boring gradually reducing the stone's mass.

The **kelpwood trees**—you've settled on calling them trees, though they're something more intermediate, something between kelp and wood—are the largest sessile organisms, and their ecology deserves its own examination. They anchor in the deep cracks, sending roots that somehow penetrate meters into the stone, perhaps following solution cavities or creating their own through chemical dissolution. The trunks are thick, two or three feet in diameter at the base, and they're internally structured—you can see this where one has broken, revealing fibrous tissue that's organized into concentric rings, growth layers maybe, or structural support.

The bark is spongy and saturated, covered in symbiotic growth: algae, bacteria, small arthropods that graze these, and grip-walkers that hunt the grazers. The bark itself might be photosynthetic, or it might purely house the photosynthetic symbionts. The relationship is murky, but the result is clear: the trees are nutrient factories, fixing energy from light and minerals from water, growing steadily upward and outward.

Their branches split and split again into the hanging masses of fine tendrils, and these tendrils are covered in tiny polyps—you can see them if you look closely enough, each polyp the size of a pinhead, extended into the water-flow, catching particles. The polyps might be part of the tree's own structure, modified branches perhaps, or they might be a different organism living in symbiosis, providing nutrients to the tree in exchange for substrate and exposure to flow.

Birds—the flying rays—nest in the larger branches, weaving the tendrils together to create platforms. The trees tolerate this, or perhaps benefit from it; the guano from the nests provides nitrogen and phosphorus, nutrients that the trees absorb through their bark. And when a tree dies—they do die, eventually, toppling when their roots lose grip or their trunks weaken from disease—they become substrate for a succession of organisms. The wood, if it is wood, decays slowly, providing habitat for bore-worms, for bacterial colonies, for fungi-analogs that you see as pale, bracket-like growths extending from fallen trunks.

Some trees show signs of great age: trunks thick enough that three people with arms extended couldn't circle them, bark fissured into deep cracks, branch systems that extend forty or fifty feet from the trunk. These ancient trees dominate sections of the pillars, creating local ecosystems in their structure. More creatures live in and on a single old kelpwood tree than on an equivalent area of bare stone.

The **rust-eaters** are a specialized group, organisms—probably bacteria at the base, but you see larger things too: insect-analogs, certain crab-shapes—that have adapted specifically to exploit the industrial remnants. They consume rust, extracting both the iron and whatever organic residue remains in the old machinery. The bacteria form visible blooms on heavily rusted surfaces, orange and red and brown films that spread across the metal in geometric patterns.

The larger rust-eaters actively accelerate decay: they scrape and bite at metal surfaces, consuming flakes, digesting them in guts that apparently contain extremely acidic secretions. You watch a crab-shape working at a bolt for several minutes, its mandibles scraping methodically, collecting rust-powder that it consumes as it works. Over time—decades, centuries—these rust-eaters will completely break down all the metal in this place, reducing platforms and gears and cables to minerals that will be incorporated back into the stone and water.

But for now, the metal structures support their own micro-ecology: rust-eaters, the creatures that prey on rust-eaters, the filter-feeders that use metal surfaces as substrate. The old gear that's still turning is particularly rich; the motion continually exposes new surface to the water, creating fresh rust, and the rust-eaters congregate there, covering the gear so completely that sections of it look organic, alive.

**Parasites** exist here too, though they're harder to observe directly. You see their effects: grip-walkers with distended abdomens, moving sluggishly, eventually rupturing to release small worms that immediately seek new hosts. Sky-fish with patches of discolored scales, areas where something is growing beneath the skin. Flying rays with malformed wings, the membranes thickened and opaque where larvae are developing.

One parasite you witness more directly: a tiny crustacean that attaches to the eyes of larger sky-fish, feeding on the eye tissue, eventually blinding the host. A blinded sky-fish can't hunt effectively and starves, but before dying it serves as a food source for the parasite, which grows to maturity, reproduces, and releases larvae into the water-flow to find new hosts. It's brutal, specific, and apparently common; you see multiple sky-fish with the characteristic eye-parasites, and you see one dead fish, freshly fallen into a kelp-forest, its eye sockets packed with the mature parasites preparing to release their young.

The **night-glow organisms** you haven't observed in action—you're experiencing this place during day—but the evidence is everywhere. Certain arthropods have bioluminescent patches on their carapaces. Some sky-fish have light-producing organs along their flanks. The bore-worm gills glow faintly even in daylight, a pale green that must be spectacular in darkness. And in the deepest crevices, in the shadowed overhangs where direct light never reaches, you see fungi or bacteria—something—growing in mats that have a faint luminescence even now, suggesting that in full darkness they'd be clearly visible.

The bioluminescence serves different purposes depending on species. For some it's communication: the flash-patterns of sky-fish coordinating schooling behavior, or attracting mates, or warning of territory. For others it's hunting: an anglerfish-analog you haven't seen but whose lure you find evidence of in the stomach contents of a dead grip-walker. And for others still it's defense: a startled arthropod flashing bright, briefly, hopefully startling a predator long enough to escape.

At night, this place must transform into a constellation of scattered lights: the bioluminescent organisms creating a visual map of the ecosystem that would tell you, if you knew how to read it, where the major activity was occurring, what was hunting, what was fleeing, what was courting.

**Seasonal breeders** are harder to identify without extended observation, but you see hints. Certain trees have structures that look like seed pods, clusters of bulbs hanging from branches, unopened. These might release seeds or spores during a particular season, perhaps when the turbulence cycles shift into a different pattern. Some grip-walkers are gravid, their abdomens swollen with eggs, but others show no signs of reproductive activity. The sky-fish schools sometimes split, smaller groups breaking off, and this might be spawning behavior beginning.

There must be seasons here, though what drives them is unclear. Tidal cycles, if the water-flow is tidal. Solar cycles, changes in light and warmth. Atmospheric patterns on a larger scale than you can observe. And the life here responds, timing reproduction and migration and dormancy to whatever rhythms govern this place.

**Migrants** might exist: creatures that move between the Drowned Scaffolds and other ecosystems, wherever those might be. The flying rays seem capable of long-distance flight; they could potentially travel to other pillar clusters or even to more traditional land if it exists within their range. Some sky-fish might migrate vertically, moving to different depths seasonally, following food or responding to temperature changes. But you haven't observed direct evidence of migration, just the sense that these populations are connected to something beyond what's immediately visible, that genetic exchange must occur somehow, that colonization of new habitats must happen.

What you're seeing, ultimately, is a mature ecosystem in equilibrium. The predator-prey relationships are balanced; no single species dominates to the point of excluding others. The energy flow—from sunlight and geological heat through photosynthesizers to herbivores to predators—is stable and complex. The nutrient cycling works: decomposers break down the dead, releasing minerals that are taken up by the primary producers, creating a closed loop that can persist indefinitely.

The life here is neither struggling nor paradisial. It simply is: adapted, specialized, successful in the same way any ecosystem that's persisted for long enough becomes successful. The creatures are not aware of the strangeness of their home—the vertical water, the suspended rivers, the ancient eroded stone. They know only their immediate needs: food, shelter, reproduction, survival. And they meet those needs with the same fundamental tools that life everywhere uses: motion, sensing, consumption, growth.

You are watching something that will continue long after you stop watching, something that was here long before you arrived. The individuals will die and be replaced, but the patterns will persist: the grip-walkers on stone, the sky-fish in water-columns, the flying rays riding updrafts, the boom of the singing-hollows echoing through the pillars. This is what lives in the Drowned Scaffolds, and it is enough, and it is abundant.
