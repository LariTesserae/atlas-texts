# Who Lives There

The **swallows** own the vertical world. They are the true inheritors of the quarry's industrial geometry, treating cut stone and crane towers as an improvement on nature's offering—more ledges, more horizontal lines, more nesting opportunities in a landscape that already suited them. There are hundreds of them, perhaps more than a thousand at the colony's peak, their numbers difficult to count because they are always in motion, weaving flight paths through the quarry's infrastructure with a precision that suggests not learned behavior but instinct that has already, in just seventy years, begun to encode the presence of cable and scaffold and cutting shed into something heritable.

They are cliff swallows primarily, with some barn swallow minority, and the two species coexist without obvious territoriality, their nest architectures different enough—mud globes versus mud cups—that competition becomes geometry rather than conflict. The nests cluster in the horizontal galleries the quarrying has exaggerated, each shelf hosting its own dense constellation of mud structures, and the birds return each spring with fidelity not just to the mesa but to specific shelves, specific sections of specific shelves, as though the quarry's terracing has created not one colony but dozens of sub-colonies, each with its own identity known only to swallows.

Their feeding happens in the air over the pools and over the plain below in sweeping circuits that make the sky seem organized, patterned. They take insects on the wing with aerial choreography that looks like joy but is simply the visible form of profound competence. When the diesel generators kick over, when saws bite stone, when cables sing under load, the swallows continue, unbothered, their flight paths adjusting around obstacles with the same smooth inevitability that water flows around stones. They've learned which machinery moves and which doesn't, which sounds precede danger and which are simply noise. They've become industrial birds without ceasing to be wild.

The **ravens** are different—fewer, warier, more calculating in their relationship with human presence. There are perhaps a dozen regular residents, pairs and a few solitary individuals, and they work the margins between wild and industrial with opportunistic intelligence. They're large common ravens, black as shadow, their presence announced by that distinctive deep croak and by the heavy sound of their wingbeats, so different from the swallows' light flickering.

They've learned the rhythms of human activity and wait for them. They know when workers break for meals, know which buildings have food, know the maintenance schedule of various work sites well enough to appear when equipment is being moved and small scavengeable items might be exposed or dropped. They work the pools for fish with surprising patience, walking the shallow edges and striking quick as herons, emerging with gold-orange fish gripped crosswise in heavy beaks.

But they're not tame, not nearly. They maintain distance, watch with that sideways head-tilt that suggests assessment and judgment. Some workers swear the ravens recognize individuals, respond differently to different humans. Whether this is true or projection doesn't particularly matter—the ravens behave as though it's true, which shapes the interaction just as effectively. They call to each other across the quarry's expanse, coordinating, sharing information, their voices carrying across distances that make the individual birds invisible against stone and sky.

They nest on the western cliff face, on ledges inaccessible without technical climbing, and they harass the **eagles**—the pair that nests somewhere in that same vertical geography—with fearless aggression despite the size differential. The eagles are golden eagles, huge and amber-eyed and capable of taking prey as large as hares, but the ravens know they're more maneuverable, quicker in the turns, and they mob any eagle that ventures too close to raven nests with coordinated attacks that drive the larger raptors away through sheer persistent harassment.

The **eagles** themselves are remote presences, more often seen as distant shapes riding thermals than as actual birds with feather-detail visible. They hunt the wire-grass patches for hares and the larger lizards, stooping from altitude with that characteristic controlled fall, binding to prey with yellow talons before flapping heavily back to altitude or to the nest site with their catch. They've been here longer than the quarry—golden eagles are long-lived, mate for life, maintain territories across decades—and they've incorporated the industrial presence into their territory the way the mesa incorporated the talus pile: as simply another feature, neutral until proven otherwise.

They're more visible in winter when they're more active, when they need more calories to maintain body heat and when the absence of swallows makes the airspace less cluttered. Workers see them then perched on crane towers occasionally, using the industrial structures as hunting perches, which seems like either profound adaptation or profound indifference to human categories of wild versus built.

The **kestrels** are everywhere the eagles aren't—smaller, more numerous, more energetic in their hunting. American kestrels, fox-faced and rust-colored, hovering on rapid wingbeats over the wire-grass patches, dropping suddenly on grasshoppers and small lizards. There are perhaps six or eight regular individuals, hunting territories that overlap loosely, nesting in cavities in the cliff face and occasionally in gaps in quarry structures where rock meets metal and space exists. They're noisier than the eagles, more vocal, their high killy-killy-killy calls a frequent soundtrack, and they're unbothered by human proximity, hunting within meters of active work sites, perching on equipment, treating the whole landscape as their hunting ground without much distinction between natural and industrial spaces.

The **plovers**—rust-colored and long-legged, probably mountain plovers though identification is complicated by the fact that no ornithologist has visited specifically to confirm—work the pool margins with focused intensity. There are a dozen or two, their numbers fluctuating seasonally, and they're ground nesters, laying eggs in simple scrapes among the wire-grass, relying on camouflage and stillness rather than inaccessibility. Their presence indicates something about the pools' invertebrate productivity; they're eating constantly, probing mud for larvae and small crustaceans, and their ability to sustain breeding populations suggests the pools support more invisible life than the visible fish and insects immediately reveal.

They're nervous birds, quick to alarm-call and fly when approached, their flight low and direct to another pool, another margin, where they resume feeding immediately. But they return, resettle, adapt. They've nested successfully near the worker settlement, near active quarry sites, in places that should be too disturbed, which suggests either exceptional tolerance or limited options or both.

The **lizards** are the mesa's real abundance, its biomass champion, its most successful vertebrate adaptation to this landscape of heat and stone and limited water. There are species upon species, though identifying them precisely would require capture and close examination that nobody's bothered with: small spiny lizards doing push-up displays on every sun-heated surface, whiptails moving through wire-grass with tail-up alertness, larger fence lizards and what might be chuckwallas in rock crevices, horned lizards—rare but present—in the approach road's lower elevations.

Their density is such that the landscape seems to breathe with them—every stone you turn would reveal one or several, every crack hosts juveniles, every sunny spot by mid-morning supports basking individuals. They've colonized the industrial structures completely, treating metal and concrete as thermally equivalent to natural stone, which it basically is. They bask on cable housings, on equipment covers, on the roofs of work sheds. They hunt insects around sodium lights at night, dozens congregating where moths cluster. They lay eggs in wind-blown dust accumulated in equipment crevices, in gaps between stacked stone blocks, in the talus pile's infinite interstices.

The workers are fond of them in the abstract, unbothered way you're fond of ubiquitous wildlife that doesn't create problems. Nobody names them—there are too many, they're too similar—but everyone notices when the big fence lizard that lives near the tool shed has disappeared, presumably taken by a raven or a kestrel, and everyone notices when the new big one establishes territory there a week later.

The lizards' role in the ecology is fundamental: they convert insect biomass into vertebrate biomass available to birds and mammals, they're mobile enough to distribute nutrients between pool-edges and dry stone, they're abundant enough that predation doesn't threaten their populations. They're what thriving looks like in a constrained environment—not large, not long-lived, but densely present and successfully reproducing and eating constantly and being eaten regularly in a balance that constitutes health.

The **fish** are stranger, more mysterious in their origins and persistence. They're small—six to ten centimeters mostly, with occasional larger individuals twice that—and gold-orange with darker mottling, and they school tightly in the pools' deeper sections when threatened, dispersing to feed in shallows when calm. Species identification is genuinely uncertain; they might be mosquitofish, might be some carp variant, might be descendants of aquarium releases from decades back, might be something else entirely. Nobody's bothered to confirm because it doesn't particularly matter—they're here, they've been here for as long as anyone remembers, they survive the pools' seasonal extremes and reproduce successfully and feed the ravens and herons and whatever else can catch them.

They're tolerant of remarkable conditions: water temperatures that swing from near-freezing winter nights to blood-warm summer afternoons, oxygen levels that must crash during long hot periods between rains, pools that shrink to puddles concentrating dozens of fish into spaces barely large enough to contain them. Yet they persist, and in the monsoon season when pools refill and connect temporarily through overflow, they redistribute, colonize, rebalance their populations across the mesa's aquatic archipelago.

They feed on algae and insect larvae and probably each other's eggs and fry. Workers sometimes see them surface-feeding at dawn and dusk when hatching insects are thick, the pools' surfaces dimpling with dozens of small rises. Their presence means the pools maintain enough productivity through the dry season to sustain vertebrate populations, which suggests microbial and invertebrate communities below the visible surface doing work nobody observes but the fish's survival confirms.

The **hares**—desert cottontails, pale and large-eared—live in the wire-grass patches and among the shrubs, emerging in the cooling hours to feed and to visit the pool edges to drink. They're scarce relative to the lizards and birds, maybe a few dozen across the mesa top, and they're warier, quicker to flight, more aware of their vulnerability. They have multiple predators: eagles from above, coyotes from ground level, and probably great horned owls at night, though the owls are heard more often than seen, their hooting carrying across the mesa in the dark hours.

The hares are quiet prey, contributing little obvious presence beyond their scat and the occasional individual frozen in watchful stillness at dawn. But their presence indicates something about the vegetation's productivity—they're eating constantly, and their survival through the dry season means the wire-grass and shrubs maintain enough nutritional value, enough moisture content, to support mammalian herbivores, which is not a given in this environment.

They den in rock crevices and among the talus pile's gaps, and they've adapted to include quarry structures in their landscape of safety, bolting under equipment, sheltering in gaps between stacked stone blocks. Workers sometimes startle them during early shifts, the hares exploding from cover in that distinctive zigzag flight, clearing three meters in a single bound, vanishing into the landscape's texture with speed that makes you understand why they survive despite being prey for everything larger.

The **coyotes**—if they are coyotes, if they're not dogs, if they're not some hybrid population blending the two—are the mesa's apex mammalian predators, and they're mostly nocturnal presences known by signs rather than sightings. Their scat appears near pools most mornings, full of lizard bones and hare fur and fruit seeds and sometimes fish scales. Their tracks appear in dust around the settlement and along the quarry terraces. Workers hear them occasionally at night: yips and howls echoing off the cliff face, near but directionless, sound bouncing off stone until origin becomes uncertain.

Actual sightings are rare—a shape moving between pools at dusk, eyes reflecting headlights on the access road at night, a glimpse of tawny movement among the wire-grass at dawn before the watcher can focus and confirm. They might be a pair, might be a small family group, might be several individuals with overlapping ranges. They're certainly regular—the sign is too consistent for opportunistic visitors—but they're also successfully avoiding direct encounter, maintaining wildness through distance and nocturnal habits.

They're important ecologically despite their scarcity, keeping hare populations from overgrazing, taking weak or sick lizards and ground-nesting birds, cleaning up carrion, distributing seeds through scat, connecting the mesa's ecology to the broader landscape through their movements down to the plain and back. Workers accept their presence with the same pragmatism they accept the eagles—large predators are part of the landscape, cause no problems, occasionally provide the pleasure of a glimpse into something genuinely wild.

The **water beetles** and other aquatic invertebrates—backswimmers and water boatmen and predaceous diving beetles and several dragonfly and damselfly nymphs—populate the pools in densities that become visible when you look closely, when you watch water surfaces and shallow bottoms long enough to see past the initial impression of empty water. They're eating algae and each other and mosquito larvae (when present) and providing food for fish and for the larvae of the adult insects that feed swallows and kestrels.

The dragonfly and damselfly adults—huge dragonflies with wingspans broader than your hand, delicate damselflies in electric blues and greens—are the pools' most visible invertebrate product, emerging in pulses through the warm season, hunting smaller insects around the pools and vegetation, mating in tandem flights and egg-laying dips to the water surface. Their presence in such numbers, their size and color, gives the pools an improbable beauty, makes them seem more tropical than high desert, as though the water's existence has created not just habitat but genre, shifted the landscape into something other than expected.

The **bats**—species unknown, probably several species including Mexican free-tailed bats and perhaps canyon bats or western small-footed bats—emerge at dusk from roosts in the cliff face and possibly in gaps in quarry structures, flickering shapes against the sunset that disperse to hunt insects over the pools and plain. They're not numerous enough to be dramatic, not the clouds of thousands you'd see at more famous bat sites, but they're present, they're hunting successfully, they're presumably roosting and raising young somewhere in the vertical geography.

Their ecological role parallels the swallows' but in the dark hours: converting insect biomass into vertebrate biomass, eating moths and beetles that concentrate near lights and natural emergence events, pollinating and dispersing seeds if any of them are nectar-feeders (uncertain), providing prey for owls, connecting day and night ecologies through the shift change at dusk and dawn.

The **workers** themselves—twenty or so at any time, rotating on schedules that bring some in for weeks or months, send others down to the city for leave—live here in a way that's neither tourist nor fully inhabitant, neither wild nor fully domestic. They're here for pay, for the work, for reasons of employment rather than love of place, yet they're shaped by the place anyway, by the altitude and heat and isolation and the particular demands of cutting stone at twenty-eight hundred meters.

They're mostly men, mostly middle-aged, mostly experienced in quarry work or heavy equipment operation or industrial maintenance. A few women, mostly in supervisory or technical roles. Ethnically mixed in the way of industrial labor in this region: Anglo and Hispanic and Indigenous, speaking English and Spanish interchangeably, code-switching mid-sentence. They're from the city mostly, from smaller towns in the region, from wherever quarry work pays better than available alternatives.

They know the ravens, recognize individual eagles, notice when the big fence lizard disappears, watch monsoon storms approach across the plain with the attention of people whose work depends on weather. They develop altitude tolerance and heat tolerance and learn to drink more water than seems necessary and learn which shelters provide best shade and which machinery can be safely touched after hours in the sun.

Some love it here in a way they don't articulate or examine. Some endure it for the paycheck. Most fall somewhere between—it's work, it's fine, the isolation is sometimes pleasant and sometimes oppressive, the landscape is impressive until it's boring until it's impressive again. They take photos on phones in the first few weeks, stop taking photos after a month, maybe take a few more when leaving because they realize they'll miss it, or realize they should document it, or realize departure makes everything worth photographing again.

They eat in a communal dining hall, sleep in individual rooms in the barracks-style housing, share common spaces, develop the particular social dynamics of isolated work crews: close friendships and simmering resentments, in-jokes and hierarchies, cooperation born of necessity and conflicts born of proximity. They celebrate birthdays, share supplies, help each other with equipment failures and personal problems and the countless small crises that arise when you're hours from the nearest town and something breaks or hurts or goes wrong.

They respect the site's dangers: the cliff edges, the heavy equipment, the heat, the altitude, the long access road that's treacherous in rain. There have been accidents—a cable failure years ago, a vehicle accident on the access road, heat exhaustion cases, a broken leg from a fall—but fewer than you'd expect given the isolation and the hazards. Safety culture is strong, enforced by regulation and by the simple fact that serious injury here is hours from real medical care, that mistakes have consequences amplified by remoteness.

They leave offerings sometimes, unconsciously or half-consciously: a water dish maintained for the birds near the settlement, food scraps placed where ravens can find them, the planted tree tended by whoever notices it needs water. They've created a small lending library in a common room, shelves of paperbacks left by successive crews, titles ranging from genre fiction to technical manuals to a surprising amount of poetry, books arriving and departing with workers, accumulating the notes and marginalia of dozens of readers.

They're here temporarily, even the long-timers cycling eventually back to the city, to other jobs, to families, to lives that can't be permanently sustained at this elevation and isolation. But while here, they're part of the ecology—not predators or prey but disturbers and providers, creating structures that swallows use, maintaining water sources that hares visit, operating machinery that's become habitat for lizards, cutting stone and in doing so creating new surfaces, new microclimates, new possibilities for whatever lives here to exploit.

The **great horned owls**—heard more than seen, their hooting carrying across the mesa through winter nights—hunt from perches on crane towers and cliff edges, taking hares and ground-roosting birds and probably the occasional coyote pup if dens can be found. Their presence is confirmed by pellets found near regular perches, compact masses of fur and bone that disintegrate to reveal what's being eaten: primarily hare and rodent, occasionally bird.

Daytime roosts are probably in the cliff face, in natural cavities or sheltered ledges, and the owls are secretive enough that even workers who've been here for years haven't seen them clearly, just glimpses of large shapes against stars or silhouettes against the marginally lighter sky at dusk. But they're regular—the hooting is too consistent for wanderers—and they're presumably nesting, presumably raising young somewhere in the vertical geography, presumably sustained by the prey populations the mesa maintains.

**Insects** beyond the obvious dragonflies and water beetles include grasshoppers in the wire-grass, dozens of species clicking away when you walk through, their presence sustaining kestrels and lizards and swallows. Moths in improbable variety congregate at the sodium lights, from tiny micromoths to sphinx moths with wingspans rivaling small birds. Butterflies visit the sparse wildflowers that bloom briefly after rains—sulfurs and blues and painted ladies. Wasps and solitary bees work the same flowers, nest in rock crevices and hollow stems, maintain their own parallel economy of pollination and parasitism and predation.

There are ants—several species, from tiny pavement ants to larger harvester ants—creating networks of trails between colonies and food sources, their presence indicated by small cleared circles around nest entrances in dust and by the trails themselves, visible as lines of activity across bare stone. They're eating seeds primarily, dragging grass seeds and shrub seeds underground to storage chambers, maintaining food reserves that see them through the dry season and winter.

The **vegetation** itself, while not animal, lives here with the same compressed intensity as the mobile creatures. The wire-grass clumps are individual organisms decades old, growing slowly, expanding incrementally, surviving through root systems that penetrate cracks in stone to find water and purchase. The shrubs—creosote bush and saltbush and bursage variants—are similarly long-lived, similarly patient, growing millimeters per year, their wood dense and hard, their leaves small and waxy and adapted to hold every molecule of moisture.

After rains, ephemerals appear: tiny wildflowers that weren't visible as seeds, completing their life cycles in a few weeks, turning patches of bare stone briefly into gardens of yellow and purple and white, feeding the pollinators, producing the seeds the ants harvest, then dying back to wait as seeds again, their timing linked to moisture in patterns established over evolutionary time deeper than human presence, deeper than the quarry, deeper than the most recent geological events that shaped this mesa.

The whole community—swallows and ravens, eagles and kestrels, plovers and lizards, hares and coyotes, fish and water beetles, dragonflies and bats, workers and vegetation—exists in dynamic balance, each element constrained by the others and by the physical reality of altitude and heat and limited water, each element finding its niche in the three-dimensional landscape of stone and air and improbable pools, the industrial presence woven into the ecology now, part of the puzzle pieces that have to fit together for anything to persist here, the quarry and its inhabitants—human and otherwise—making a kind of sense together that neither would make alone, creating between them a place that rings like struck stone when the conditions align, that reflects sodium light into tea-dark water, that exhales heat through long nights and inhales cool air at dawn, that cuts and is cut, that erodes and builds, that lives.
