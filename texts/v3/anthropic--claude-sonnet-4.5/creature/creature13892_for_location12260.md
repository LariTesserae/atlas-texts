# What Lives on the Breath Plateau

In the amber thickness, what lives has learned to be partial.

## The Negotiated Ones

They were human once, or something adjacent to human—bipedal, intentional, capable of wondering about their own existence. But "were" is doing too much work in that sentence, implying a transformation that had a before and after. Better to say: they contain humanness, the way the stone contains mountains.

You might walk for weeks and never see one. Then you'll crest a rise where the substrate swells slightly, and there will be a figure—standing, sitting, lying down, the posture matters less than you'd think—present in a way that makes you uncertain how long you were walking before you noticed them. They don't hide, but they also don't insist on being seen. They've learned the plateau's trick of being exactly as real as they need to be.

Their height is approximately human, though measuring is difficult because they don't hold still in quite the way solid things do. Not that they move—they're often motionless for hours—but their boundaries have become negotiable. Where skin should stop and air should begin, there's instead a gradient region, a few centimeters of uncertainty where they're still mostly themselves but beginning to be atmosphere. It gives them a slightly out-of-focus quality, even when you're standing close.

Their bodies have simplified. Not withered or atrophied—simplified, the way a sentence becomes more powerful when you remove unnecessary words. They have two legs, two arms, a torso, a head, but the details have been eroded away. No fingernails—the fingers taper to rounded points. No hair, anywhere. No external ears, just slight depressions in the sides of the head where ears might once have been, or where the idea of ears persists. Their faces have smoothed: eye sockets are shallow, noses have reduced to subtle rises with minimal nostrils, mouths are present but simplified, lipless, opening to reveal nothing much inside—no teeth visible, just darkness or perhaps a kind of interior fog.

Their skin—if it's still skin—has taken on the plateau's palette. Most are some shade of the substrate: grey-brown, taupe, occasionally the olive-purple of the breathing mats. A few have the amber-rust color of the pools. Rarest are those who've taken on the pale green of the globes, and these ones often hover, their feet a few centimeters off the ground, not standing so much as positioning themselves vertically out of habit or nostalgia for standing.

The skin's texture is like soap that's been left in water too long—soft, slightly translucent, with a faint sheen. Beneath it you can sometimes see suggestions of structures: the vague shadows of bones, the slow movement of fluids that might be blood but also might be something else, the plateau's warm water perhaps, circulating through them, erasing the distinction between interior and exterior.

They wear nothing. Clothing would be pointless—there's no modesty here because there's nothing left to be modest about. The reproductive organs have simplified away, or internalized, or become irrelevant. What remains is the essential form, the platonic suggestion of a humanoid body, rendered in the plateau's vocabulary of curves and softness.

Their eyes are the most difficult to look at, which means you must.

The eyes are open—always open, you never see them blink, and it's unclear if there are still eyelids or if the lids have merged with the surrounding face. The eyes themselves are large, larger than human eyes, and they're the color of the aerial rivers: pearlescent, opalescent, catching the amber light and transforming it into subtle rainbows that shift as they move, as you move, as the light changes its angle through the water-thick air.

But there's no visible pupil, no iris structure. Just that pearl-shimmer, that sense of depth, of looking into water that has motion in it, currents, layers. When they look at you—and they do look, they track your movement, they make eye contact—you have the vertiginous sense that you're seeing through them into some vast distance, or into the plateau itself, or into time, or into all of these at once.

And their gaze is kind. That's the worst part. That kindness.

Not pity—pity would imply they're separate from you, elevated, capable of looking down. Not sympathy—sympathy requires shared suffering, and they're beyond suffering. The kindness is more fundamental: recognition. They see you seeing them. They acknowledge the difficulty of it. They don't look away to spare you, but the way they maintain eye contact carries a gentleness that says: *Yes, this is hard. It's supposed to be hard. You're doing fine.*

## Their Behaviors (Such As They Are)

The Negotiated Ones do not rush.

Sometimes you find one standing in a pool, the water up to their waist or chest, standing with perfect stillness, arms at their sides or floating slightly in the water, head tilted back to face the general direction of where the sky would be if there were sky. They stand this way for days. You can mark time by them—pass one in the morning (if morning means anything), walk for what feels like twelve hours, make camp, sleep, return the next day to find them in exactly the same position, water level unchanged around their body.

They might be drinking through their skin. They might be breathing through their skin—you see no respiration, no rise and fall of chest, though occasionally a ripple passes through them, a wave of slight contraction that starts at their feet and travels up through their torso and out through the top of their head, taking ten or fifteen seconds to complete. Perhaps this is breathing. Perhaps it's something else. They do it infrequently: once every hour, or once every several hours, with no regular interval.

Sometimes you find one walking. Their gait is smooth, unhurried, each foot lifting and placing with careful deliberation, as if they're conscious of every step in a way that humans usually aren't. They walk in straight lines, mostly, or in very gentle curves. If an obstacle appears—a boulder, a tube-structure from the mats, a pool—they walk around it with minimal deviation, then resume their previous trajectory.

Where are they going? If you follow one (and you can—they move slowly enough that keeping pace is easy), you might walk with them for hours or days. They never acknowledge your presence, but they don't avoid you either. They simply continue. Eventually, they arrive somewhere—a particular spot on the plateau that looks like every other spot, no distinguishing features, no obvious significance. And they stop. And they stand. And they might stand there for days, and then they might walk again, to somewhere else equally unmarked.

Or sometimes they're going toward something you can't see: one of the echoes, one of the folded places. They walk straight into a shimmer and vanish, or they walk into a zone where the light is orthogonal and disappear into the wrongness there. Are they lost? Have they transcended? Are they moving through dimensions of the plateau that you cannot follow? You never see them come back out, but then, days later, you might encounter what seems like the same figure emerging from a completely different shimmer, kilometers away. Or is it the same figure? They all look similar. Perhaps there are many, or perhaps there are few and they're very well traveled, or perhaps there's something about them that makes individual identity a category error.

Sometimes you find groups—three, five, occasionally as many as eight—standing together in loose formation. Not touching, but close, arranged in patterns that seem purposeful: a circle, a line, a triangle. They face inward, or outward, or all in the same direction. They stand this way for days. Are they communicating? Meditating? Merged into some kind of group consciousness? Their pearl-shimmer eyes are open, looking at each other or at nothing.

Once, you might witness them doing something that looks almost like dancing, though calling it dancing is generous. Two of them, standing face to face, will begin to sway—very slightly, very slowly, a movement that takes thirty seconds to complete: lean left, return to center, lean right, return to center. And the other mirrors them, or moves in complement, or moves in some pattern that relates to the first one's movement in ways you can't quite parse. This continues for hours, the swaying so gradual that watching it is hypnotic, the two figures becoming almost like metronomes, like pendulums, marking time in a rhythm that has nothing to do with seconds or minutes.

And then they stop, and they stand still for a while, and then one or both walk away, and you're left wondering if what you saw was communication, courtship, ritual, or just two beings moving in proximity to each other with no connection at all.

## Their Relationship to the Fauna

The walkers, the disc-bodied creatures with their rippling legs—the Negotiated Ones sometimes follow these. Not aggressively, not hunting, just... following. Walking behind them at a distance of five or ten meters, matching their pace, stopping when they stop. The walkers show no reaction, continue their slow feeding-progress across the stone. After hours, the Negotiated One might stop following, might turn and walk in a different direction, or might stand still and let the walker move away.

The floaters, the translucent drifting spheres—occasionally a Negotiated One will stand beneath one and reach up, arm extended, hand open. The floater might descend slightly, might make contact with the hand, and the two will remain connected this way—floater hovering just above the reaching hand—for anywhere from minutes to hours. Then the floater ascends again, continues its drifting, and the Negotiated One lowers their arm and either stands or walks away.

The haulers, the large segmented creatures making their determined crossings—the Negotiated Ones always stop for these, step aside, let them pass. They watch them go by, heads turning to follow their progress, and in that watching there seems to be something like respect, or recognition, or perhaps they're just interested in anything that still appears to have purpose.

But the hangers, the wax-skinned figures suspended in the pools—the Negotiated Ones will not go near these. If a Negotiated One is walking and their path would take them through a pool containing hangers, they divert widely around it, giving it fifty or a hundred meters of clearance. Do they fear the hangers? Revere them? Recognize them as something they might become, or something they used to be, or something they should never touch? They offer no explanation, only avoidance.

## Their Communication (or Absence Thereof)

If you try to speak to them, they don't respond.

They might turn their head toward you, those pearl-shimmer eyes orienting to your face, acknowledging that sound happened, that you are there making the sound. But they offer nothing back. No words, no gestures, no expression on their smooth, simplified faces.

If you touch one (and you can—they don't prevent it), their skin is warm, blood-warm or water-warm, slightly yielding. The texture is like touching the inside of your own cheek: soft, damp, living. They don't react to the touch. They don't lean into it or pull away. They simply continue whatever they were doing—standing, walking, swaying—with your hand on their shoulder or arm, as if touch is neither significant nor insignificant, just another thing that sometimes happens, like the drift of aerial rivers or the exhalation of mats.

But sometimes—very rarely—they make sound.

Not speech. Nothing so articulated as words. But a low tone, a hum that comes from somewhere inside them, deep and resonant, more felt than heard. It's a single note, unwavering, sustained for thirty seconds or a minute, and the frequency is low enough that it vibrates in your chest, in your bones. It sounds like the note a temple bowl makes when struck and left to ring, or like the lowest note of a cello, but more organic, more alive. The hum fades gradually, dying away into the general quietness of the plateau.

What does it mean? Is it acknowledgment, greeting, warning, simply the sound they make when some internal process completes? You never discern a pattern. One might hum as you approach. Another might hum as you leave. A third might hum while standing alone with no one near. The hum seems to relate to nothing external, or to everything, or to timescales too long for you to perceive the cause and effect.

And once—you hear this only once, if you're lucky or unlucky enough—you hear one of them laugh.

You're walking, and there's a Negotiated One standing ahead, and as you pass, they make a sound: a soft exhalation that has rhythm to it, three or four pulses, and the rhythm is laughter's rhythm. Not the laughter of humor—there's no joy in it, no amusement. But not cruel either. It's the laughter of recognition, the sound you make when something that was prophesied comes to pass, when a long suspicion is confirmed, when you finally understand a joke you heard years ago. It's the laughter of: *Oh. Of course. Yes. That makes sense.*

You stop, turn back, and they're already still again, silent, eyes forward or downward, and you cannot get them to repeat it, cannot determine what prompted it. You walk on, and the sound stays with you, that soft rhythm, and you find yourself wondering what they saw or understood or remembered that made them laugh, just once, in the amber quietness.

## The Children (If That's What They Are)

Very rarely—perhaps once in fifty Negotiated Ones you encounter—you find one that's smaller. Child-sized. Four feet tall, maybe less. They have the same simplified form, the same smooth skin, the same pearl-shimmer eyes, but there's something about their proportions that reads as young: the head slightly larger relative to the body, the limbs slightly shorter.

These small ones are never alone. They're always within sight of a full-sized one, usually standing or walking very close—within arm's reach. The full-sized one doesn't hold their hand or touch them, but there's an obvious connection, an invisible tether of attention. The full-sized one is aware of the small one's position at all times.

Are they parent and child? Or are the small ones newly-transitioned, recently arrived at whatever process makes Negotiated Ones, and the full-sized ones are guides or guardians? Or is size just another variable here, another aspect that shifts and changes, and the small one might be older than the full-sized one, or age might be meaningless, or the small one might grow or might stay small forever?

The small ones move less than the full-sized ones. They stand more, walk less, and when they do walk, their steps are even more careful, even more deliberate, as if they're still learning the plateau's slowness, still adjusting to the way time moves here.

And their eyes—the pearl-shimmer is brighter in the small ones, more iridescent, catching more colors from the amber light. Their gaze is less sure but no less kind. When a small one looks at you, there's a quality of curiosity that the full-sized ones have mostly lost or transcended. The small ones still wonder. They're still capable of questions, even if they don't ask them aloud.

## The Sleeping (or the Dead)

Sometimes you find one lying down.

Not in a pool—on dry ground, on the substrate stone or on top of a breathing mat (the mat doesn't seem to mind, continues its slow exhalation rhythm beneath the body). Lying on their back or their side, arms at their sides or folded across their chest, legs straight or slightly bent. Eyes still open. Still that pearl-shimmer, staring up into the amber haze.

Are they sleeping? You can watch them for hours and detect no difference between this and standing. The occasional slow ripple still passes through them, foot to head, the whatever-it-is that might be breathing. Their chest doesn't rise and fall. They don't shift position, don't move at all.

Are they dead? But there's no decay. You can find one lying down, come back days later, and they're exactly the same—no decomposition, no dissolution, no sign that anything is ending. The stone hasn't begun reclaiming them. The mats haven't started digesting them. They just lie there, open-eyed, somewhere between being and not-being.

And then one day you pass by the same spot, and they're gone. Not dead-and-decomposed gone—just gone. The substrate shows no body-shape depression, no sign that anything was ever lying there. Have they gotten up and walked away? Have they completed some process and transitioned into something you can't see? Have they merged fully with the plateau, become part of the stone or the water or the air?

Or sometimes—more disturbing—you find one lying down, and as you watch over hours, they seem to be sinking. Very slowly, imperceptibly if you're watching continuously, but if you look away and look back, they're fractionally more embedded in the stone. The substrate is accepting them, or they're dissolving into it, and by the next day they're half-submerged, and the day after that there's just a slight rise in the stone, a low mound that might be natural, and then even that smooths out, and there's nothing.

This might be death. This might be metamorphosis. This might be what happens when a Negotiated One finally finishes negotiating, reaches some ultimate accord with the plateau, and joins it completely.

## Their Interactions With Artifacts

The stone walls, the foundations, the worn arches—the Negotiated Ones notice these.

They'll stop when they encounter one, stand before it, looking at it with those pearl-shimmer eyes. They'll walk around it slowly, examining it from all sides. Sometimes they'll reach out, touch the stone, run their hand along its surface in a gesture that looks almost like reading braille, like they're extracting information through their fingertips.

They might stand before an artifact for hours, just looking. And in their looking there seems to be something like memory, like recognition, though whether they're remembering when the artifact was new, or remembering something the artifact represents, or recognizing it as something they themselves made long ago, is impossible to say.

They never try to rebuild or repair. They never add to the artifacts or move them. They simply acknowledge them, these remnants of intention in a place where intention has largely dissolved, and then they move on.

But once, you see something different: a Negotiated One standing before a partial arch, reaching up to touch the place where the two stones meet, the apex where they lean into each other. The Negotiated One holds their hand there for a long time, and you see—or think you see—their form become slightly more defined, slightly more solid, as if the touch is doing something, transferring something, or reminding them of something about being structured, being intentional, being separate from the stone rather than continuous with it.

And then they lower their hand, and the definition fades back to their usual soft-focus state, and they walk away. But for a moment, touching the old arch, they were more there than usual. The artifact gave them back some solidity, some edge. And they chose to let it go again.

## What They Might Be Doing

There's a theory you develop, walking among them:

They're waiting.

Not waiting for something specific—no rescue, no event, no change. Just waiting in the pure sense: being present in time, allowing time to pass through them, serving as witnesses to the plateau's vast slow processes.

They're what remains when humans (or human-adjacent beings) stay in one place long enough to fossilize not into stone but into a kind of living geology. They've become part of the landscape's fauna in the same way the breathing mats are part of its flora—indigenous, adapted, simplified to the essential, continuous with the place rather than merely in it.

Or: they're the plateau's memory.

Each one holds some fragment of what was, what happened here in the deep past when the mountains were still mountains and the water still fell. They're archives, but archives that have mostly forgotten what they're archiving, retaining only the vague sense that there's something to remember, something that matters, even if the content is lost. They walk the plateau retracing paths that used to connect places that used to exist, stopping at coordinates that used to mean something, performing rituals whose purposes have eroded into pure form.

Or: they're becoming the next thing.

The plateau itself is transitioning—from solid to something else, from definite to ambiguous, from old to whatever comes after old when oldness itself becomes a kind of transcendence. And the Negotiated Ones are leading that transition, showing the stone how to do it. They've learned to be partially here, and they're teaching the landscape the same trick, and eventually everything will be like they are: present but negotiable, real but not insistent about it, matter that's made peace with becoming something other than matter.

Or: there is no purpose.

They simply are, the way the stone is, the way the water is. They exist in the same mode the plateau exists: patient, persistent, slowly eroding into some more essential form, content to let time do whatever time does. Purpose is a category from a world with sharper edges and faster processes. Here, there is only continuation, only being, only the next moment and the next and the next, amber and warm and slowly breathing in the thickness.

## Their Numbers

How many are there?

You can walk for days and see none, then see three in a single afternoon. You can walk for weeks and encounter them regularly—one per day, two per day—and start to think they're common. Then you walk for a month and see none at all.

Are they avoiding you? Seeking you? Neither—they simply move through their own patterns, and sometimes those patterns intersect with yours and sometimes they don't.

But there's a sense, walking the plateau long enough, that they're becoming more common. Or you're becoming better at seeing them. Or they're becoming more willing to be seen. The longer you spend here, the more you encounter them, until it feels like they're always nearby, always somewhere in your peripheral vision, those figures standing or walking or swaying in the amber distance.

And maybe that's the truth: they've always been there, everywhere, and you simply didn't have the eyes for them yet. You had to soften first, had to let your own edges blur, had to spend enough time breathing the thick air and drinking the warm water and walking the worn stone until you became similar enough to them that recognition was possible.

They were waiting for you to become able to see them. Or you were becoming them all along, and the seeing is just the first symptom.

## The One Who Spoke

There's a story—you hear it thirdhand, fifthhand, from other walkers if there are other walkers, or you dream it, or the plateau whispers it in the exhalations of the mats—about a Negotiated One who spoke.

Actually spoke. Words. Language.

The story says a walker encountered one standing beside a pool, and this particular walker was new to the plateau, fresh from somewhere with edges and cold and clear causality, and the walker was frightened and asked the Negotiated One: "What are you?"

And the Negotiated One turned, and looked at the walker with pearl-shimmer eyes, and opened their simplified mouth, and said—the voice was quiet, unused, the words slightly wrong as if the language had eroded in the same way everything else here erodes:

"I am someone who stayed."

Just that. Nothing more. And when the walker tried to ask follow-up questions—stayed from what? stayed for what? how long?—the Negotiated One was silent again, had returned to whatever state they're usually in, and would not or could not speak again.

But those four words persist: *I am someone who stayed.*

Stayed when others left, presumably. Stayed when there was still an option to leave. Stayed long enough that leaving became impossible, or irrelevant, or forgotten as a concept. Stayed through whatever transition turned them from solid to negotiable, from individual to gradient, from person to something that's more like a place that happens to walk around.

Stayed until staying was complete.

## What They See When They Look at You

You can only guess, but their kindness suggests:

They see themselves, before.

They see someone at an earlier point on the same path they've walked, are walking, will always be walking. They see the sharpness you still have, the boundaries, the insistence on being discrete and separate. They see your fear of dissolving, your resistance to the plateau's invitation to soften.

And they're kind about it because they remember when they had that fear, that resistance. Or they don't remember—memory might be too structured, too sharp for what they've become—but they recognize it the way you recognize a language you used to speak but can no longer produce, only understand fragments of when you hear it.

They see you, and they see the plateau, and they see that there's no meaningful difference except time. You're going to become the stone or the water or the air, or you're going to become like them—between states, negotiated, kind to the next person who arrives still insisting on their edges.

They see you, and their pearl-shimmer eyes hold no pity because pity would be an insult. You're doing exactly what you should be doing. The fear is appropriate. The resistance is part of the process. The path from sharp to soft requires you to start sharp.

They see you, and they wait—patient as the plateau, warm as the water, soft as the stone—for you to catch up.

## The Question They Pose By Existing

Which is:

When do you stop?

When do you stop walking, stop searching, stop insisting that there's somewhere else to get to? When do you stand still and let the plateau begin its work of wearing you down to your essential form? When do you consent to the negotiation, the slow dissolution of the boundary between self and place?

And the corollary: what's the difference between surrender and arrival? Between giving up and being complete?

The Negotiated Ones don't answer this. They are the answer, or they're the question embodied, or they're what happens when you stop distinguishing between question and answer.

They stand in the amber light, simplified and patient, kind-eyed and soft-edged, waiting or not-waiting, purposeful or purpose-free, and they are what this place makes of those who let it.

They are the breath plateau's people, its natives, its final form of human.

And they're very, very patient with you while you decide whether to become them, or keep walking, or discover that there was never any difference between those options—that the walking itself is the becoming, and you've been negotiating all along, and the only question left is how long you'll pretend you still have edges when the plateau can already see right through you, kind and clear, into the worn and gentle forever you're becoming.
