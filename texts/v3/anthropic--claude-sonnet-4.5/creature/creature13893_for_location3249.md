# What Lives in the Hydraulic Hollows

The Hollows have inhabitants, though the word feels too substantial for what actually occurs here. Life in this place is marginal, opportunistic, adapted not through generations of selection but through the desperate flexibility of organisms finding themselves in a space that should not support them but somehow does.

## The Moss Kingdoms

Begin with the moss, because the moss is foundational. It is not one species but seven, perhaps eight, possibly more—identification is complicated by the environment, by the way the cold and minerals and intermittent light force morphological changes that blur taxonomic boundaries.

The most common is what you might call *iron moss*, though it has no proper name because no one has properly studied it. It grows in the rust zones, on the horizontal surfaces of steel beams where water runs continuously. The moss itself is a deep brown-green, nearly black, and when you examine it closely you see that its structure incorporates rust particles, binds them somehow, uses them. The iron concentration should be toxic, but this moss has made accommodation. It grows in patches that spread slowly—you can see growth rings if you scrape carefully, concentric zones suggesting years of expansion at perhaps a millimeter per month. Under magnification (if you had magnification, if you cared to look that closely) you would see that the rhizoids penetrate not into soil but into rust scale, chemically dissolving and binding the oxidized metal into something like substrate.

In the coldest sections, there is *frost moss*. Paler, almost gray-green, growing in mats rather than patches. It survives temperatures that would kill its relatives through a strategy of extreme patience—metabolizing so slowly that it barely counts as growth, entering dormancy when water freezes in its cells, reviving when conditions allow. During the coldest periods, it becomes brittle, can be crumbled between fingers. During warmer cycles, it softens, dampens, and if you watch (you would have to watch for hours) you might see it very slowly expand, absorbing water like a sponge given time-lapse agency.

There is a moss that grows only in spray zones, where water atomizes against concrete or steel. It forms vertical streaks, dark green lines running down from impact points. This one is most like surface mosses, least adapted to the specific conditions of the Hollows, and you suspect it was introduced recently—seeds or spores blown in from above, finding in the spray zones enough moisture and just barely enough light to persist. It has not spread far. It seems barely viable. But it has not died either, and that counts for something here.

In the standing pools, where water collects in depressions and remains for weeks or months, there is something that might be moss or might be algae or might be both in some symbiotic confusion. It forms underwater mats, pale green and slippery, producing oxygen in tiny bubbles that rise and pop at the surface. This growth is important—it is the base of a food web, such as it is. The small things that move in the water feed on it.

The moss does not cover surfaces the way it would in a damp forest. There are no carpets of it, no generous spreads. Instead, it appears as isolated patches, tactical deployments in microclimates where conditions permit. A handful here on the underside of a beam. A cluster there where a pipe drips consistently. Three separate patches on a single platform, meters apart, each responding to slightly different moisture regimes. The distribution maps the water, shows you where the environment is just barely hospitable enough for photosynthesis and respiration to exceed the metabolic cost of survival.

## The Aquatic Smallness

The water channels host life, though "host" suggests more hospitality than the Hollows provide.

There are crustaceans. Small ones, translucent, resembling freshwater shrimp but not quite matching any species you know. They are perhaps two centimeters long, with too many legs, with eyes on short stalks that seem functional but might not be, given how little light reaches the water. They cluster in the slower sections of flow, in the eddies behind structural elements, feeding on particulate matter that settles out of the current. When disturbed, they scatter with quick backward flicks, disappearing into the turbidity.

You see them most clearly during low-flow periods when the water slows and clears slightly. They become visible then as small mobile punctuation marks against the concrete, dozens of them in a meter-long stretch of channel, then none for a hundred meters, then another cluster. The distribution is patchy, tied to local food availability, to places where the flow deposits organic matter or where algae grows on submerged surfaces.

There are larvae. Multiple types, difficult to distinguish. Some are clearly aquatic insects in their immature stage—mayfly nymphs, perhaps, or caddisfly larvae in cases constructed from rust particles and concrete grit. Others are less identifiable, worm-like things that might mature into flies or midges or something else. They occupy the substrate at the bottom of channels, visible only when water is very shallow or very clear, moving slowly through the sediment, processing it, extracting whatever nutrition can be extracted.

In the standing pools, there are beetles. Diving beetles, you think, though they are small and dark and difficult to observe. They surface occasionally to replenish air supplies, then descend again, hunting through the underwater vegetation, feeding on smaller invertebrates. You have found their shed exoskeletons on platform edges, translucent husks that suggest growth, reproduction, lifecycle completion.

And there are worms. In the sediment, in the organic matter that accumulates in channel bends, there are oligochaetes—relatives of earthworms but adapted to aquatic life. They are pale, almost white, and they process the sediment continuously, aerating it, breaking down organic material, converting it into forms that other organisms can use. You rarely see them directly, but you see their effects: the worked-over appearance of sediment in certain areas, the small castings they leave on the surface.

The fish are surprising. You did not expect fish, but there are fish.

They are small, none longer than your finger, and they are colorless—not white but truly without pigment, translucent enough that you can see their skeletons, the dark shadow of their organs. They are blind, their eyes reduced to functionless spots beneath the skin. They navigate by lateral line, sensing pressure waves, vibrations, the movement of water around obstacles.

You have seen them in the deeper pools, in sections where the channels widen into basins. They move slowly, conserving energy, hanging nearly motionless in the current, then darting suddenly to intercept some prey item too small for you to see. They feed on the crustaceans, on the larvae, on anything organic that the water carries.

How did they get here? They are cave fish, obviously adapted to subterranean life, and that adaptation takes thousands of generations. But the Hollows are not thousands of generations old—they are decades old, a century at most. Either these fish arrived already adapted, introduced somehow from natural cave systems, or they are surface fish whose development has been so thoroughly disrupted by the environment that they appear cave-adapted without actually being so. Their translucency might be lack of pigmentation caused by absence of light rather than evolutionary loss. Their blindness might be developmental failure rather than genetic certainty.

Or time works differently than you think. Or the Hollows are older than they appear. Or the connections to other systems, other caves, run deeper than you know.

The fish are silent, as fish are. They add nothing to the soundscape. But their presence adds something else—a sense that the Hollows are not merely infrastructure hosting incidental life, but have become, however improbably, a functioning ecosystem.

## The Arthropod Majority

Insects dominate, numerically if not by biomass. This is expected. Insects always find a way.

There are flies. Multiple species, though you are not entomologist enough to separate them. They cluster near the moss patches, presumably feeding on the organic matter that the moss traps, or on the moss itself, or on each other. They are small, dark, and undramatic. Their larvae develop in the damp detritus that accumulates in corners, processing it, breaking it down.

There are springtails. You find them when you look closely at the moss, at the standing water, at any surface with organic matter. They are tiny, barely visible without magnification, but they are everywhere in those microhabitats. They jump when disturbed, vanishing too quickly to track. They feed on fungi, on bacteria, on organic particles, and they are themselves food for larger predators.

There are beetles beyond the diving beetles. Ground beetles, rove beetles, things you cannot identify. They hunt through the rust dust, patrol the concrete platforms, investigating every crack and shadow. Some are predatory, feeding on springtails and fly larvae. Others are detritivores, processing dead organic matter, converting it into frass that bacteria and fungi can further break down.

There are spiders. Of course there are spiders.

Small ones, mostly. Sheet-web weavers that construct their traps in the angles between structural members, in the protected spaces under catwalks. Their webs are visible when moisture beads on them, turning them into delicate nets of captured droplets. The spiders themselves are reclusive, hiding in silk retreats, emerging only when vibrations indicate captured prey.

The prey is mostly flies, mostly springtails, mostly other tiny arthropods. The spiders are not abundant—you might find one web every ten meters in suitable habitat, less often in marginal areas. But they are persistent. In this cold, their metabolism slows. They can wait weeks between meals. Their silk is proteinaceous, recyclable—they eat old webs and spin new ones from the reclaimed material.

There are larger spiders, rarely seen. You have found them occasionally on the platforms, moving with deliberate speed across open concrete. These are hunting spiders, wolf spiders perhaps, though the species is uncertain. They do not build webs. They patrol, they hunt actively, feeding on anything they can overcome. What they eat in the Hollows is unclear—the small arthropods seem insufficient for their size. Perhaps they prey on each other. Perhaps they eat the crustaceans when they can catch them near water. Perhaps they starve slowly, surviving through metabolic thrift.

There are mites. You need magnification to see them properly, but once you look, they are everywhere. In the moss, hundreds of them, grazing on fungal hyphae or preying on springtails. On the spiders themselves, parasitic species riding along, feeding on their hosts' hemolymph. In the water, water mites, tiny red dots swimming jerkily, hunting through the aquatic vegetation.

And there are millipedes. You find them in the damper sections, moving slowly over concrete, processing organic matter, producing waste pellets that bacteria colonize. They are small, cryptic, and you suspect you are missing most of them, seeing only the individuals that happen to cross your path.

The insect sounds are mostly inaudible under the water noise. But in the quieter sections, you can hear them if you listen: the buzz of flies, high-pitched and brief. The clicking of beetles' mandibles. Once, something that might have been a cricket's chirp, though you saw no cricket and the sound did not repeat. The absence of crickets is notable—you expected them, expected their adaptability to bring them into the Hollows, but either they have not arrived or they have arrived and failed to establish.

## The Aerial Visitors

Birds should not be here, but birds are here.

You see them rarely, brief glimpses in the distance, silhouettes against the working lights. They are small, probably starlings or sparrows or something similar, surface birds that have found access to the Hollows and have chosen, for reasons of their own, to venture in.

They do not live here. You find no nests, no eggs, no sustained evidence of residence. But they visit, flying through the galleries, landing on catwalks, investigating. They drink from the standing pools. They pick at surfaces, feeding on flies or spiders or moss-dwelling invertebrates. They perch on the machinery, calling occasionally—sounds that echo strangely in the industrial space, birdsong made alien by architecture.

Why they come is unclear. Perhaps the Hollows offer shelter, refuge from surface weather or predators. Perhaps they have learned that food exists here, that the invertebrate communities are sufficient to reward the journey into darkness. Perhaps they are simply curious, exploratory, driven by whatever drives birds to investigate new spaces.

Their droppings mark their presence. You find them on certain platforms, white splashes and dark solids, accumulating over time. The droppings seed the concrete with nutrients, with bacteria, with undigested seeds that will never germinate in this lightless cold. They contribute to the organic matter budget, feeding the decomposers, closing loops.

And there is at least one resident bird species. You have heard it more than seen it—a call that echoes through the galleries, a whistled phrase that repeats. When you finally glimpse it, it is small and dark, moving along a beam with the posture of a dipper or a wren. It hunts the arthropods, picking spiders from their webs, plucking beetles from rust accumulations. It is comfortable here, navigates the three-dimensional space with confidence. It has nested somewhere, you're certain, though you have not found the nest. It has made the Hollows home.

## The Rat-Things

The tracks you find are troubling because they suggest something larger, something mammalian, something that should require more resources than the Hollows seem to offer.

Four toes on the front feet, five on the back. Claws evident in the prints. Roughly rat-sized, perhaps slightly larger. The prints appear in the rust dust, in the damp sediment near water, on surfaces that take impressions. They are most common in the areas with more organic matter, near the cold-zone habitation site, in sections where debris has accumulated.

You see the creatures rarely, and never well. Movement in peripheral vision. A shape on a distant gantry, low and swift. Once, clearly, a silhouette in a light pool—rat-sized, certainly, but the proportions were wrong, the tail too short or absent, the body too elongated. It paused for perhaps two seconds, then flowed into shadow and was gone.

What are they? Rats seem most likely—rats are adaptable, persistent, capable of surviving in marginal environments. But rats require food, require organic matter in quantities that the Hollows barely provide. Unless they are eating things you cannot account for. Unless they are leaving the Hollows regularly to forage above. Unless the food web is more complex than you understand, with resources being imported from elsewhere through mechanisms you have not identified.

Or they are not rats. They might be weasels or stoats, small mustelids hunting the sparse prey. They might be something that was introduced deliberately, that was living here during the operational phase and that persists now, adapted to the changed conditions. They might be something else entirely, something whose presence in the Hollows is anomalous, unexplained.

You find their scat occasionally. Small dark pellets, dry and odorless, usually in protected corners. The scat contains chitin fragments—beetle exoskeletons, spider legs—and sometimes fish bones, scales, evidence of successful hunting in the pools. The volume of scat suggests a small population, perhaps a handful of individuals across the entire system. They are rare, these creatures. But they are apex predators here, such as that title means anything in an environment this marginal.

They make sounds, rarely. Scratching, certainly, the sound of claws on metal. Once, a vocalization—a soft chittering, almost birdlike, lasting a few seconds before silence. The sounds are startling when they occur, biological intrusions into the mechanical soundscape.

## The Cat-Sized Thing

Once, you saw it. Only once, and from a distance, but once is enough to know that something larger moves through the Hollows.

It was crossing a gantry perhaps a hundred meters away, silhouetted against a working light. Cat-sized is the right description—the size of a small cat, moving with a cat's fluid precision. But the shape was not quite right. The body was too elongated, the limbs too short, or perhaps it was just the angle, the distance, the way backlight distorts proportion.

It moved deliberately, navigating the steel structure with complete confidence, placing feet carefully on narrow beams, never hesitating. It crossed from one side to the other in perhaps five seconds, then descended out of sight down a vertical support.

You did not see it again. You have looked, have returned to that location, have waited. But it does not appear on schedule. It moves through the Hollows according to its own rhythms, its own territories, and those territories are large enough or its density low enough that encounters are rare events.

What does it eat? The rat-things, perhaps—it is large enough to prey on them. The birds, if it can catch them. The fish, though fishing would require techniques and patience. Perhaps it scavenges. Perhaps it leaves the Hollows regularly, hunting above, returning only for shelter or water or reasons of its own.

Or perhaps it eats things you do not know about. Perhaps there are resources in the Hollows that you have not identified, food sources that support a predator of this size. Perhaps the ecosystem is deeper than you understand, with more complexity hidden in the shadows and the water and the spaces you have not explored.

Its presence changes the Hollows. Knowing that something that large moves through this space, you become more cautious, more alert. You listen differently, watching for movement, for signs. You have found no tracks definitive enough to confirm its size or species. You have found no scat, no prey remains, no territorial marks. It exists in the Hollows like a rumor, like a possibility, seen once and then absent, leaving you uncertain whether it was real or whether distance and light and expectation conspired to create meaning from random movement.

## The Fungal Margins

In the dampest sections, in the corners where water and organic matter accumulate, there are fungi.

Not mushrooms, mostly. The cold inhibits fruiting bodies, keeps most fungi in their mycelial phase. But if you scrape at the organic matter, at the wood fragments and paper scraps and indeterminate debris, you find the mycelium—white threads, delicate networks, breaking down complex organics, converting them into forms other organisms can use.

The mycelium is extensive in places, forming mats beneath the surface layer of detritus. It smells faintly of earth, of decay, of the transformative chemistry of decomposition. It is doing the essential work, the work that allows the ecosystem to function at all—taking the dead material, the waste, the indigestible, and converting it into nutrients that moss can absorb, that bacteria can process, that can cycle back into life.

There are occasional fruiting bodies. Small ones, delicate, appearing and disappearing rapidly. Tiny mushrooms with caps no larger than your smallest fingernail, erupting from moss clumps, lasting a few days before collapsing into slime. What disperses their spores in this nearly windless environment is unclear. Perhaps water splash. Perhaps the arthropods, carrying spores on their bodies. Perhaps the rare air movements are sufficient.

And there are fungal colonizers on surfaces. Black molds on damp concrete, spreading in dendritic patterns. Orange crusts on wood, breaking down cellulose. White fuzz on submerged materials, aquatic fungi processing whatever organic matter the water provides.

The fungi are not dramatic. They do not call attention to themselves. But they are essential infrastructure, decomposer communities that underpin everything else. Without them, the system would choke on its own waste, on the dead moss and insect corpses and organic inputs. With them, matter cycles, nutrients return, and the marginal ecosystem maintains its marginal function.

## The Microbial Foundation

You cannot see them without equipment, but you know they are there. The bacteria, the archaea, the protists—the microscopic majority that does most of the actual biological work in the Hollows.

They form biofilms on every submerged surface. They colonize the water, processing dissolved organics, nitrifying ammonia, reducing sulfates, doing the chemical transformations that keep the water marginally hospitable. They live in the sediments, in the moss, in the digestive systems of every larger organism, essential symbionts and decomposers.

The water would be lifeless without them. The smell would be different—sharper, more chemical, less processed. The moss would starve, unable to access nutrients in usable forms. The small crustaceans would find nothing to eat, or would poison themselves on their own waste.

The bacteria are why the Hollows work at all as an ecosystem. They are the foundation, the chemical engineers, the recyclers. They are why organic matter that falls into this system can be converted into life rather than simply accumulating as death.

You see their effects even if you cannot see them directly. The clarification of water as it flows through certain sections, biofilm communities filtering and processing. The breakdown of organic matter in the detritus. The smell of healthy decay rather than septic stagnation. The survival of everything larger, everything more complex, everything that depends on the microbial work happening constantly, invisibly, in every damp surface and water volume.

## The Trees That Should Not Be

In three locations, there are trees. Or tree-like growths. Or something that has organized itself into an approximate tree morphology.

They are small, none taller than two meters. Their trunks are strange—textured like fungal fruit bodies, rough and fibrous but not quite bark, not quite wood. When you touch them, they are cool and damp and slightly yielding, as if they are not entirely solid, as if their structure incorporates water in unusual ways.

The leaves are dark, nearly black, and minimal. During what might be their active season, each tree carries perhaps twenty leaves, small and leathery. During dormancy, they drop these and stand bare, looking dead but not dying.

They grow from debris-choked corners, from places where rust and concrete dust and organic matter have accumulated over years into something approximately like soil. Their roots, visible at the surface, are pale and thick, more fungal than plant-like in appearance. They spread through the substrate, breaking it down, extracting nutrients, possibly forming mycorrhizal associations with the fungal networks.

What species they are is uncertain. They might be willows or alders, species adapted to damp conditions, whose seeds made it into the Hollows and germinated despite the impossible conditions. Their strange appearance might be the result of etiolation, of nutrient deficiency, of developmental responses to cold and darkness and mineral-heavy water.

Or they might be something else. Something that was already adapted to subterranean life, whose seeds arrived from deeper systems. Something that has been here much longer than seems possible, that predates the industrial construction, that was buried and has now found its way back toward surface.

They do not photosynthesize, not significantly. There is not enough light. They might be mycoheterotrophic, drawing energy from fungal partners, parasitizing the decomposer networks. Or they might be metabolizing at rates so low that the minimal light is sufficient, that they grow millimeters per year, that their persistence is a form of suspended animation rather than active life.

Around the trees, the ecosystem is slightly richer. More moss grows nearby. The arthropod density is higher. The fungi are more abundant. Whether the trees create these conditions or simply mark locations where conditions are better is unclear. But their presence concentrates life, creates nodes of slightly elevated biological activity in the otherwise sparse environment.

Birds perch in their branches occasionally. The rat-things visit them. You have found their tracks circling the trunks, investigating. The trees are landmarks, reference points, places that feel significant despite their strangeness or perhaps because of it.

## The Microclimates of the Cold Zone

In the coldest section, where frost coats the metal and the temperature drops below freezing, the life changes character.

The moss here is frost-tolerant, metabolizing when it can, dormant when it must. It grows even more slowly than elsewhere, but it grows, accumulating bulk over what must be years of patient persistence.

The arthropods are different. Fewer species, but some uniquely adapted. There are springtails that survive freezing, that contain cryoprotectants in their hemolymph, that can be encased in ice and revive when thawed. There are mites specialized for extreme cold, moving slowly when active, entering cryptobiosis when temperatures drop too far.

And there is the evidence of human habitation—old, but not as old as the industrial abandonment. Someone lived here for a time, in the coldest place, for reasons unknown. They left debris: food containers, fragments of insulation, the fire rings where they burned scavenged materials for warmth.

Who they were, why they chose this location, where they went—these questions have no answers. But they contributed to the ecosystem. Their waste, their discarded organics, their fire ash—these added nutrients, seeded the area with surface materials, with organic complexity that the Hollows otherwise lack.

The moss grows denser around the old habitation. The fungi are more diverse. The arthropod communities are richer. Humans, even temporarily, even decades ago, changed the biology of this place. Their presence echoes in the distribution of life, in the species composition, in the organic matter budget.

And possibly they explain some of the larger fauna. Did they bring rats with them, inadvertently or deliberately? Did they feed birds, creating associations that persist after their departure? Did the cat-sized thing arrive with them, a pet gone feral, now adapted to independent life in the Hollows?

Or is the human presence coincidental, unconnected to the biology? Perhaps people came because the ecosystem was already richer here, because the cold zone offered something that other sections did not. Perhaps the causation runs the other direction.

## The Biofilms of the Effervescent Channel

In the channel where the water produces bubbles, where some dissolved gas comes out of solution, there are unusual biofilms.

They coat the concrete in thick, slimy layers—centimeters deep in places, colors ranging from white to pink to dark green. They smell different from other biofilms, more sulfurous, more chemical.

These are microbial communities doing unusual chemistry. Possibly sulfur-oxidizing bacteria, possibly iron-oxidizers, possibly methanotrophs feeding on dissolved methane if that is what the bubbles are. They are primary producers in a sense, generating energy from chemical gradients rather than from light, building organic matter from carbon dioxide and dissolved minerals.

The crustaceans graze on these biofilms. So do certain larvae. The biofilms are food, a resource that is locally abundant in a system where abundance is otherwise rare. The effervescent channel supports higher animal densities than comparable sections, its microbial productivity creating a localized food surplus.

The fish congregate here. You see them more often in this channel than elsewhere, attracted by the prey density, by the oxygen-rich water, by whatever qualities the bubbling creates. They school loosely, if three or four fish can be called a school, hanging in the current, feeding opportunistically.

The biofilms grow rapidly, visibly. You can return to a location after a day and see that the coverage has expanded, that the thickness has increased. And periodically, sections slough off, sheet-like masses of microbial tissue that the current carries downstream, exporting the productivity to other parts of the system, providing food for filter-feeders and detritivores in locations far removed from the source.

## The Seasonal Absence

Something is missing. The Hollows should support bats, and there are no bats.

The environment seems suitable—sheltered, stable temperature, permanent darkness, water available, insects present. Bats should find this space, should roost here, should hunt the flying insects through the galleries.

But there are no bats. You have watched at the access points during what should be active periods. You have listened for echolocation calls. You have looked for guano, for roost stains, for any sign of presence. There is nothing.

Why? Perhaps the access points are too difficult, too vertical or too flooded for bats to enter. Perhaps the temperature is wrong—too cold for the species in this region, below their operational minimum. Perhaps the insect density is insufficient, unable to support bats' high metabolic demands. Perhaps bats have not yet discovered the Hollows, and given enough time they will arrive.

Or perhaps something about the acoustics, about the way sound behaves in this space, makes echolocation unreliable. Perhaps bats enter and become disoriented, unable to navigate, and leave quickly or not at all. Perhaps there is an ultrasonic component to the machinery sounds, something that interferes with echolocation, that makes the Hollows unusable for bats despite their apparent suitability.

The absence feels significant. Bats would change the ecosystem substantially—increasing predation pressure on flying insects, adding guano to the nutrient budget, creating new habitat complexity at the roosting sites. Their absence leaves a niche unfilled, a role unoccupied, and the ecosystem is different because of it.

## The Pattern of Persistence

What lives here lives precariously. Every organism is at the margin of viability, surviving through tolerance rather than thriving, persisting rather than flourishing.

The moss grows but does not spread aggressively. The arthropods maintain populations but do not boom. The fish exist in small numbers, replacing losses but not increasing. The larger animals are rare individuals, not breeding populations, and it is unclear whether they reproduce here or whether each individual is an immigrant that will eventually die without replacement.

This is not an ecosystem in equilibrium. It is an ecosystem in the early stages of assembly, still forming, still sorting out which species can make it and which cannot. Or it is an ecosystem that will never reach equilibrium, that exists in perpetual marginal status, unable to develop beyond this minimal complexity.

The energy budget is constrained. Primary production from photosynthesis is minimal—the moss, the aquatic algae, collectively producing organic matter at rates barely sufficient to support the rest of the system. Chemosynthetic production in the biofilms adds to this but does not fundamentally change the calculation. The Hollows are energy-poor, and everything that lives here reflects that poverty.

But life persists. Against the cold, the darkness, the mineral-heavy water, the absence of soil, the scarcity of nutrients—against all of it, life persists. The moss finds purchase. The arthropods find food. The fish navigate darkness. The fungi decompose. The bacteria cycle nutrients.

The Hollows are not dead space. They are not sterile infrastructure. They have been colonized, inhabited, converted into habitat. The conversion is incomplete, the habitat is marginal, but it exists. Life, as it always does, has found a way.

And the system is changing, slowly. More organic matter accumulates each year. More moss establishes. More arthropods arrive. The trees grow, millimeter by millimeter. The biofilms thicken. The ecosystem is assembling itself, building complexity from minimal inputs, creating something from almost nothing.

In another decade, another century, the Hollows might be recognizable as a functioning ecosystem. Or they might remain as they are, marginal and sparse. Or the infrastructure might fail—the lights might die completely, the water might find new paths, the temperatures might shift—and the life that has established might be extinguished, or might adapt again, might find new strategies for persistence.

What lives in the Hollows lives uncertainly, tentatively, making accommodation with an environment that was never meant to support it. But it lives. That is the essential fact. Despite everything, it lives.
