# What Lives There

Nothing lives there in the way you mean. But the plain is not uninhabited.

## The Incorporators

They are not born and they do not arrive. They simply *remain*—people who stopped moving and forgot to remember why they should start again. You find them built into the architecture, sometimes.

A man sits in what has become a niche, though it was open ground when he sat down three months ago. The bricks have grown up around him in a careful bracket, leaving just enough space for his body, his breathing, the slight movements he still makes. His skin has taken on the color of wet clay. Not metaphorically—literally, the moisture that beads on every surface has colonized him, and he weeps the same warm condensation as the bricks. He watches the plain with eyes that track the emergence of new formations with something beyond interest, beyond fascination. Hunger, maybe, but not for food. Hunger to understand the pattern. Hunger to see the plan that must be there, that must reveal itself if he watches long enough.

He eats the bricks sometimes. Scrapes the soft surface of new ones with his teeth, consuming the wet clay. It doesn't nourish him—it shouldn't—but he doesn't die. The rules are thin here. His body has negotiated some private arrangement with the plain's metabolism. He's been digesting brick for longer than should be survivable, and his stomach has apparently accepted this new diet, or his stomach has become something else, some organ better suited to this place. When he shits, and he does, irregularly, the result looks like brick paste. Within hours, it has hardened into small, malformed brick-things that push into the ground and spawn proper bricks in their turn.

He doesn't speak anymore, or hasn't for weeks, but he's not catatonic. His eyes track visitors. Sometimes his mouth moves, shaping words in the grammar of construction—"corner," "course," "span," "bearing"—architectural terms repeated like prayer or diagnosis. He's trying to read the plain. He believes, with the last shreds of his dissolving selfhood, that it means something, that the pattern is about to resolve, that he's close to comprehension. He's wrong, but the plain rewards his attention anyway, building him more deeply into itself, making him architectural, making him support.

There are dozens like him, maybe hundreds, scattered across the distances. Some are more progressed—you find skeletons sometimes, still seated, perfectly enclosed in brick chambers that have no entrance, no way the body could have gotten inside except by being there first, by being there while the chamber grew. The bones are stained red-brown, the color of the clay. Some of them are hard to distinguish from brick themselves. The plain is patient with its converts. It builds them tombs that are also wombs, chambers that might be graves or might be shelters, and the distinction doesn't matter because the plain doesn't distinguish between housing the dead and housing the living.

## The Walkers

Then there are those who haven't stopped yet, who move through the plain with the jerky, difficult motion of people fighting against acceptance.

A woman stumbles through a brick field, counting her steps aloud. "Forty-seven, forty-eight, forty-nine." She's been counting for days, maybe weeks. The numbers are absurdly high—she's well into the hundreds of thousands—but she doesn't stop, doesn't break the rhythm. The counting is discipline, is resistance, is proof that she still has a mind separate from the plain's mindless manufacturing. Her lips are cracked, clay-dust in the splits. Her feet leave prints that immediately begin sprouting, so she's pursued by a trail of emergence, a wake of new construction. She never looks back. Looking back might mean seeing what she's making, and that might mean stopping to examine it, and stopping is death, or transformation, or something without a good name.

She talks to herself in two voices. One is still hers, more or less—tired, frightened, determined. "Keep moving. Don't stop. North. Must be north." The other voice is newer, slower, uses words strangely. "We could rest here. Nice foundation starting. Good corner. Three bricks already, see? Four now. Five." The second voice admires the plain's work. The second voice is seduced by the construction. She argues with it, with herself, a constant muttered debate about whether to continue or to stop and watch the bricks arrange themselves in their infinitely patient chaos.

She's not alone in this. The plain has walkers scattered across it like mobile ornaments, like ambulatory decorations that haven't found their niches yet. They all share that same jerky, compulsive motion, that same sense of fighting against gravity or magnetism. The plain wants them still. The plain wants everyone still, incorporated, built-in, made structural. Moving is an act of continuous defiance, and it exhausts them, but stopping exhausts them faster, differently, in ways that hollow out the parts of them that were human.

Some walkers have been mobile so long they've worn paths, and the paths have become permanent features, trenches in the ground where bricks no longer grow, or grow differently—small, dark, hard like gravel. These paths wander without destination, loops and tangles that show where someone walked in circles, following the degrading compass of their own confusion. Sometimes you find the walker still following their path, around and around, a circuit that's become ritual, become their entire universe. They don't acknowledge visitors. They're too busy maintaining the discipline of motion, even though the motion no longer goes anywhere.

## The Listeners

Rarer, more disturbing: those who've developed a relationship with the plain that's neither resistance nor submission but something oblique.

An old person—gender worn away by exposure and strangeness—sits cross-legged on a low brick platform, ear pressed to the surface. They're listening. Not to anything visitors can hear, or not *only* to the low hum of emergence. They hear the plain's intention, or claim to. "There," they whisper, pointing to a clear patch of clay. "Soon. Watch." And within minutes, hours, a brick begins its slow push upward, exactly where they indicated. Coincidence, maybe. Maybe not.

They've learned to predict the growth, or they're delusional and occasionally correct, or—and this is the uncomfortable possibility—they've learned to *influence* it. They hum sometimes, a low drone that matches the plain's frequency, and when they hum, the bricks near them seem to emerge faster, more eagerly, as if responding to encouragement. They place their hands on new bricks and murmur to them, welcome them, and the bricks solidify more quickly under their touch, hardening from wet clay to fired-seeming ceramic in minutes instead of hours.

This listener has been here longer than anyone else still mobile, still themselves. Years, perhaps. They've gone thin, not from starvation but from translucence, as if they're becoming less material, more conceptual. You can almost see through their hands in the dim light. They eat, when they remember to, but sparingly—clay, yes, but also insects that don't exist, licking moisture from bricks that weeps from impossible sources, drinking what might be water or might be the plain's condensed intention. They don't seem to need much. They're sustained by their dialogue with the construction, their participatory witness.

"It's trying," they tell visitors, if they acknowledge visitors at all. "Can't you hear? It's trying so hard." What it's trying to do, they never specify, or they specify differently each time, contradicting themselves cheerfully. Trying to remember. Trying to forget. Trying to build a city. Trying to build a body. Trying to achieve consciousness. Trying to escape consciousness. The listener believes all of these simultaneously, sees no contradiction, because in their understanding the plain contains multitudes, intentions layered like brick courses, purposes that conflict and cooperate and cancel each other out in the endless manufacturing.

## The Changed

Sometimes you find things that used to be people but have negotiated further, crossing thresholds the incorporators haven't reached, transforming in ways that suggest the plain accepts offerings, accepts volunteers for deeper integration.

In a dense section where bricks have piled into something almost like a building, almost like a temple, there's a figure built into the wall. Not enclosed like the seated watchers—*integrated*. Their body extends into the brickwork and the brickwork extends into their body, and the boundary between flesh and clay is a negotiated gradient rather than a clear division. An arm becomes a buttress. A leg roots into the foundation. The torso is partially hollow, and water runs through it, the same warm seepage that weeps from all the bricks, draining through the person-structure and into the ground.

They're alive. You can tell because they shift slightly, adjusting their stance with geological slowness, and because they breathe, though their breathing has become tidal, vastly slow, minutes between inhalation and exhalation. Their face is still recognizable as a face, though the features are simplified, clay-smooth, details worn away or never possessed. The eyes are open but don't track. They're looking inward, or at something on a wavelength visitors can't perceive.

Are they suffering? It's impossible to know. They don't scream. They don't struggle. The incorporation seems consensual, or past the point where consent is a meaningful category. They've become architectural, and architecture doesn't suffer, doesn't feel in the way flesh feels. Perhaps they've escaped feeling. Perhaps they've achieved a different kind of feeling, the slow satisfaction of load-bearing, the deep pleasure of structural integrity, sensations that have no names in the language of separate bodies.

There are others in various stages. A person whose fingers have elongated and hardened into brick-fingers, who uses them to build, compulsively arranging and rearranging small formations, always dissatisfied, always adjusting. Their human hands are still in there somewhere, but the brick-hands are more real, more functional, and they seem grateful for the improvement, seem to prefer their new digits even though the transformation must have been agony, though maybe it wasn't, maybe the plain is merciful in its way, maybe the change comes with anesthesia built-in, welcome numbness.

A child, or something child-sized, that has become a fountain. They sit in a shallow depression, and water pours from their mouth in a continuous stream, has been pouring for so long that the ground around them has eroded into channels, into a pattern that might be decorative or might be linguistic, characters in a script no one remembers how to read. The child-fountain's eyes are closed. They might be dead. They might be dreaming. The water tastes like clay and salt and something mineral, not unpleasant, and visitors sometimes drink from them, though whether this is respectful or violating is unclear.

## The Geometers

Very occasionally, you encounter someone who seems to be *working*, who moves through the plain with purpose, with tools, with plans.

A person with a measuring cord, pacing out distances, making marks in the clay with a pointed stick. They're mapping, or trying to, though the map changes faster than they can record it. They have notebooks filled with sketches, diagrams, calculations that don't calculate, measurements that contradict themselves across pages. "The angle here was seventy degrees yesterday," they mutter. "Today it's acute. Tomorrow it might be obtuse again. But there's a pattern, a cycle. I'm close to the formula."

They aren't close. They'll never be close, because the plain doesn't operate on formulas, doesn't repeat precisely, doesn't care about consistency. But the geometer can't accept this. They're a scientist, or were, or want to be, and scientists don't accept phenomenon without explanation. So they measure and record and calculate, filling notebook after notebook with data that describes nothing, that captures only their own desperate attempt to impose order on prolific chaos.

They're kind to visitors, usually, eager to share their findings, to explain their theories. "See, the brick emergence rate correlates with ambient humidity, I'm sure of it. And the humidity correlates with the light cycles, approximately, though there's drift, unexplained variance." They'll talk for hours if permitted, unspooling hypotheses and corollaries, building elaborate theoretical structures as compulsively as the plain builds brick ones. The loneliness has made them hungry for audience. The isolation has turned their research into religion.

Sometimes there are pairs of geometers, small teams, arguing constantly about methodology, about interpretation. "No, no, you're measuring from the wrong datum point. We agreed the datum was the first brick, not the ground surface." Their disagreements are passionate, technical, meaningless. But the arguing keeps them human, keeps them separate from the plain's influence. Fighting about measurement technique is proof that measurement matters, that precision exists, that reality obeys rules even if they haven't discovered them yet. As long as they can argue, they can resist incorporation. Though their arguments grow stranger over time, more abstract, concerned with categories that don't exist, mathematical operations that have no basis in any mathematics taught in any school.

## The Shepherds

Most mysterious, most rare: those who seem to be *herding* the bricks, guiding the emergence, shaping the formations with an authority that suggests they understand something fundamental that others miss.

You might see them in the distance, a figure moving along the edge of a growing formation, hands outstretched, not touching but gesturing, conducting. Where they gesture, bricks emerge more quickly, or in different colors—yes, there are color variations, subtle, brick-red to clay-brown to something almost purple in certain lights. The shepherds encourage these variations, seem to be painting with them, creating patterns in the brickwork that might be artistic or might be instructional, might be messages or might be purely aesthetic choices.

They don't stop when approached, don't acknowledge visitors except to wave them away from areas where emergence is active, dangerous perhaps. "Not there. Wrong alignment. Wait." Their voice has a doubled quality, speaking and being spoken through, as if they're translating from a language that isn't sound. They place bricks sometimes, moving them from one position to another, and the bricks accept this, don't spawn replacements in the original location, as if they understand they've been relocated by proper authority.

The shepherds have tools that visitors don't, or the same hands as everyone but they use them differently. They can reach into soft brick and pull out moisture in threads, glistening fibers that they weave into small objects—rings, bracelets, tiny hollow spheres. These objects harden into something like ceramic but lighter, warmer, and they serve purposes that aren't clear. Talismans maybe. Or keys. Or simply proof that the plain's material can be worked, can be diverted from its constructive obsession into other forms.

Where shepherds work, the formations are different—more coherent, more intentional, though no less strange. Arches that frame nothing. Towers that spiral without climbing. Walls that curve back on themselves but don't enclose. It's still senseless architecture, still construction without function, but there's artistry in it, consciousness, choice. These shepherds might be the plain's original inhabitants, or its priests, or its only true workers, the ones doing the work deliberately while everything else happens as autonomic process.

They don't explain themselves. They don't have time for explanation—the work is constant, urgent, never complete. They sleep standing up, swaying slightly, dreaming in brick patterns. They eat wet clay by the handful, mechanically, fuel rather than food. They're devoted to their incomprehensible labor the way monks are devoted to prayer, the way artists are devoted to impossible visions. And the plain responds to them differently, grows more eagerly where they've walked, produces formations that almost make sense, that nearly achieve coherence before collapsing back into proliferation.

## The Returned

And finally, impossibly, there are those who left and came back, who escaped the plain and then chose to return, and these are the most disturbing of all because they suggest the plain is not a trap but a calling.

A woman builds a small shelter from salvaged bricks at the edge of a formation, builds it carefully, with technique, with knowledge of masonry that she didn't have before. She was a merchant once, in a city whose name she remembers but whose location she's forgotten. She wandered into the plain by accident, lost her caravan, lost her goods, nearly lost her mind. She walked out after weeks of struggle, emerged into normal lands, returned to something like her old life.

But the dreams started. Dreams of emergence, of bricks pushing up from fertile ground, of water weeping from clay surfaces, of the low hum that underlies everything. She couldn't sleep in buildings anymore—the walls felt dead, inert, finished. She needed to be near construction, active construction, and no human building site satisfied the need. The work was too slow, too planned, too finite. She needed the profligate chaos of the plain's manufacturing, the purposeless fecundity of it.

So she came back. Deliberately. Walking away from safety into strangeness, because the strangeness had marked her, had shown her something about the nature of making that civilization had forgotten or never knew. Now she builds, tears down, builds again. She's learned to eat the clay without sickness. She's learned to drink the weeping water. She's learned to sleep through the noise of emergence and to wake instantly at the silence that means collapse. She's becoming native to the plain, and she's grateful for it, relieved, like someone who's finally found their dialect, their proper country.

"It makes sense here," she tells visitors who find her, who ask why she returned. "Out there, nothing makes sense. Purpose, profit, plans—all lies. Here it just makes. Makes and makes and makes. That's honest. That's true." Her philosophy is half-baked, brick-born, but she believes it utterly. The plain has taught her that construction without purpose is the purest form of construction, that manufacturing without product is the highest manufacturing, that endless becoming is more valuable than any achieved state of being.

She's not alone. There are others who returned, though they rarely congregate, rarely speak to each other. They recognize each other somehow—a nod as they pass, a hand sign in brick-gesture, acknowledgment between converts. They're building something collectively, perhaps, or they're each building their own piece of a structure too large to conceptualize, or they're not building anything at all and simply participating in the plain's work, lending their small human efforts to its vast geological making.

## What They All Share

All of them—incorporators, walkers, listeners, changed, geometers, shepherds, returned—share certain qualities, certain marks of the plain's influence.

Their sense of time is damaged, stretched, become unreliable. They'll tell you they've been here for three days and then mention events that happened months or years ago as if they were recent. Or they'll claim decades of residence and their clothes are barely worn, their bodies not aged. The plain unmoors them from duration, or duration unmoors itself from them.

They don't sleep normally. Some don't sleep at all, or sleep standing, or sleep with eyes open, or sleep in five-minute intervals scattered across the day and night cycle that doesn't exist. Those who do sleep report dreams that are really memories, or memories that are really predictions, dreams where they're bricks being formed, being placed, being incorporated into structures, dreams where they're the plain itself, vast and mindless and infinitely patient, making and making and making.

They lose names before they lose identity. The listener, the geometer, the woman who returned—none of them remember what they were called, or they remember but don't answer to it, or they've taken new names that are positions rather than identities. "I'm the watcher of the eastern emergence." "I'm the keeper of this formation." Names that describe function, relationship to place, because personal names require a personal self, and personal selves wear away here like details weathering from sculpture.

Their bodies are always slightly damp, covered in the same moisture that beads on the bricks. It doesn't wash off. It renews itself constantly, as if they're sweating the plain's humid breath, as if they've become part of its respiratory system. The moisture is warm, slick, not quite like water. Lick it and it tastes of minerals, of earth, of distance, of the strange metallic flavor that pervades everything.

They stop distinguishing between the plain making itself and themselves making things. "We're growing a tower today," they'll say, meaning the plain is producing a tall formation. "I'm sprouting a wall here," pointing to ground that will indeed produce a wall, though they're not doing anything to cause it, or they think they're not, or the line between their will and the plain's process has dissolved into a collaborative blur where both are true simultaneously.

And they're all, in their various ways, devoted. The plain has no god but it has worshippers, votaries who've given themselves to its endless project not out of fear or compulsion but because the project has revealed itself to them as worthy, as the only thing worthy, as the work that justifies existence. Construction without completion. Making without meaning. Formation for the pure sake of formation. They've seen through the lies of purpose, the deceptions of goal-oriented labor, and they've embraced the plain's more honest obsession: to build, to make, to manifest, forever, without reason, because building is not a means to an end but the end itself, the only end, the eternal occupation of matter that has decided to take form.

They're alive in the way the plain is alive—not with consciousness exactly, not with intent, but with insistent, undeniable process. They breathe because breath is a form of manufacturing, breathing in clay-thick air and breathing out moisture that will bead on bricks. They move because movement is construction in time rather than space, each step a brick, each gesture a formation. They are the plain's hands finally, its eyes, its witness to itself, the way it experiences its own making.

And they cannot leave. Not because they're trapped—the plain has no boundaries, no walls that block exit—but because leaving would mean stopping participation, withdrawing from the work, and the work is all that matters now, the only thing that keeps them coherent. Without the plain's constant manufacturing to mirror and join and lose themselves in, they'd have to face the terrible emptiness of being finite, separate, purposeless selves. Better to be half-brick, half-becoming, dissolving gradually into the plain's vast project, than to be wholly themselves and wholly alone and wholly without the comfort of endless, mindless, magnificent making.
