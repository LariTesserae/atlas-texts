# What Lives There

## The Ungulates: The Rindwalkers

They are called rindwalkers, though no one remembers who first coined the term—perhaps from the rind they eat, perhaps from something else lost in the early documentation. They stand taller than elk at the shoulder, but it's the necks that define them: three and a half meters of articulated reach, jointed in seven places rather than Earth's typical vertebral arrangement. The joints are visible as slight swellings in the neck's length, and they bend in combinations that seem wrong to Earth-adapted eyes—lateral flexion combined with forward reach, a twisting motion that allows the head to spiral around a culm's circumference while the body remains still.

Their faces are narrow, almost delicate, with eyes positioned to allow binocular vision both forward and upward—they need to judge distances to browse targets twenty meters above their heads. The muzzle is equipped with a rasping tongue, dark purple and rough-textured, that strips the culm's outer rind in neat vertical bands. Their teeth are like nothing Earth has produced: interlocking ridges of continuously-growing enamel that scissor rather than grind, adapted for cutting through plant material that contains silicate deposits that would destroy conventional dentition within weeks.

The coat is neither fur nor hair but something between—hollow fibers that scatter light, giving each animal a faint halo in the bright conditions. Colors range from pale gold to deep amber, with darker striping on the legs and neck that breaks up their outline against the vertical culm pattern. Young are born nearly white and darken over their first year, making them conspicuous, vulnerable, clustered always at the center of herd formations.

Their legs are disproportionately long, ending in feet that are almost comically broad—three-toed, with a central pad that can flex independently, adapted for the springy compressed plant substrate. They walk with a distinctive gait, a careful placing of each foot that tests the ground before committing weight. On the rare solid patches where culm falls have created denser substrate, their movement changes, becomes almost bouncy, as if they find the firmer ground unnatural.

The herds number in the hundreds for each discrete grouping, though calling them discrete is slightly misleading—herds blend at the edges, exchange individuals, split and recombine over seasonal cycles. Matriarchs lead, identifiable by their size and the deference other animals show them. These old females know routes, remember good browsing zones, can predict weather changes through senses humans don't fully understand. When a matriarch dies, the herd becomes visibly distressed, mills uncertainly, until a new leader emerges through some process of recognition that involves smell, sound, and possibly the infrasonic communication some researchers claim to have documented.

They breed seasonally, triggered by changes in the culm chemistry that indicate planetary position. Calves are born able to walk immediately—they must be, given the predators—but cannot reach browse height for their first six months. During this time, they nurse, and the milk is strange: thick, almost crystalline, laden with mineral complexes the mother concentrates from her browsing. It has a faint sparkle in bright light, visible when a calf nurses, the liquid catching the glow-green illumination filtered through culm walls.

Communication among the herd is primarily visual and chemical. They have scent glands at the base of the skull, behind the joints in their necks, and along their flanks. The chemical signals are complex, encoding information about reproductive status, individual identity, stress levels, possibly food quality. They mark culms as they browse, leaving traces that other herds read and respond to—avoiding areas that have been recently browsed, or seeking them out, depending on seasonal needs and population pressure.

Vocalizations are rare, almost vestigial. The acoustic environment makes most sounds useless. But sometimes, in the relatively quiet periods between major wind gusts, you hear them: a low thrumming from deep in the chest cavity, felt more than heard, that ripples through a herd and causes coordinated movement. Whether this is true communication or simply a by-product of breathing pattern changes, no one has definitively established.

They sleep standing, never lying down except in terminal illness or injury. Sleep itself is fragmented—fifteen-minute intervals scattered through the day-night cycle, the head lowered to chest level, the eyes half-closed but still scanning. In a herd, individuals sleep in rotation, always some portion alert, always watchers at the perimeter. During the deepest part of night-cycle, when the moon is highest and the culms glow their brightest blue-green, the whole herd sometimes enters a synchronized rest state, all heads lowered together for perhaps twenty minutes. These moments feel sacred if you witness them, the hundreds of animals still and silent in the bright darkness, the culm-drone continuing around them like a breathing that includes everything.

Their lifespan is fifteen to twenty standard years. The old ones become recognizable—their neck joints stiffen, limiting reach, forcing them to browse lower culms or the juvenile shoots in clearings. Their coats thin and darken. They move to the centers of herds, protected, tolerated even when they can no longer contribute to the group's knowledge or defense. When they finally die, the herd doesn't linger—they move on within hours, leaving the body to the insects and the scavengers. The industrial processors never take the old ones; they taste wrong, their meat too dense with mineral accumulations, too saturated with the strange chemistry of decades of culm browsing.

## The Predators: The Weftcats

Weftcats, from "weft and warp," someone's poetic interpretation of how they move through the vertical culm-pattern, weaving between the uprights with liquid precision. They are felid-analogous, low-built and long—from nose to tail-tip they measure nearly four meters, but they stand only waist-high to a human at the shoulder. The elongation gives them a stretched appearance, almost serpentine when they're moving at speed, their spine flexing in ways that add length to each stride.

The coat is extraordinary: patches of mirror-bright white alternating with pale gold, in patterns that make no sense when viewed up close but become perfect camouflage at distance. The white patches catch and reflect the bright light; the gold patches blend with the culm colors. When a weftcat is still between the vertical trunks, it disappears—your eye slides past it, cannot hold focus, registers only confused brightness and shadow. They've evolved for hunting in the most visually complex environment imaginable, and their camouflage is correspondingly sophisticated.

The eyes are large, forward-facing, with pupils that are neither slit nor round but something between, able to constrict to pinpoints in the fierce daylight and expand to huge apertures in the relative dimness of moon-glow. They are pale silver-gold, almost metallic, and they reflect light with remarkable efficiency. At night, a weftcat's gaze from a hundred meters appears as two small cold stars between the culm trunks, visible long before the body itself becomes apparent.

Their heads are broad, shortened like a leopard's, with powerful jaws and teeth that include two enormously elongated upper canines—not saber-teeth exactly, but approaching that territory, long enough to curve down past the chin when the mouth closes. These are precision instruments, evolved for striking between specific vertebrae in rindwalker necks, severing the spinal column with a single bite. A weftcat kill is almost surgical: the prey animal drops instantly, no struggle, minimal noise. Death comes fast enough that the rest of the herd barely reacts.

The paws are large and soft, with retractable claws that are kept meticulously sharp. Unlike Earth cats, they regularly strop these claws against culm surfaces, creating distinctive vertical scratches that mark territory. The paw pads are dense with nerve endings, able to detect vibrations through the compressed plant substrate—they can feel a herd moving from several kilometers away, sense individual animals' positions and movements through the transmitted tremors. This tactile sense is possibly their primary hunting tool, more important than vision or hearing in an environment where sight is confused by light and sound is overwhelmed by the culm-drone.

Weftcats are solitary except for brief mating periods and the extended time females spend with cubs. A single adult's territory encompasses three hundred to five hundred square kilometers, marked with scent and claw-marks, overlapping loosely with other weftcats' ranges. The boundaries are not rigid—more like zones of probability, areas where encounters are more or less likely. When two weftcats meet, the interaction depends on sex, relative size, and current hunger status. Fights are rare but spectacular—brief explosive engagements that send both animals bounding between culms at impossible speeds, breaking apart as quickly as they began, one or both retreating to lick wounds and restore the territory détente.

They hunt primarily at night, during moon-phase, when the blue-green culm glow creates the most complex light patterns. Their approach is patient to the point of appearing vegetative—they may spend four or five days tracking a herd, staying parallel to its movement, never closer than a kilometer, waiting for the moment when an individual separates enough from the group, when the geometry of culm positions creates a clear approach lane, when wind direction carries scent away from the herd. Then: an explosive sprint, thirty seconds of total commitment, a strike that is over before the victim has registered threat.

They feed quickly, consuming as much of the kill as possible in the first hour. The meat is torn free in efficient strips, swallowed without much chewing—their digestive systems are remarkably robust, able to process the mineral-heavy tissue, the strange chemical compounds. They eat the organs first, the highest-value nutrients, then the major muscle groups. By the time the insects begin to swarm, the weftcat is gone, dragging away a final haunch to cache among culm roots, covering it with shredded plant matter for later recovery.

Cubs are born in litters of two or three, hidden in the rare dense patches where fallen culms create sheltered spaces. The mother is ferociously protective but also practical—if food becomes scarce, she will abandon the weakest cub without hesitation. The survivors grow rapidly, reaching half adult size within six months. They begin to hunt with their mother at eight months, learning the patient parallel tracking, the reading of ground vibrations, the explosive final sprint. By one year, they are nearly competent. By eighteen months, the mother drives them off, forces them to establish their own territories. Mortality is high—perhaps half of cubs die in their second year, unable to successfully compete for territory or make kills independently.

Adult weftcats have few threats. They occasionally kill each other in territorial disputes. Very rarely, an old or injured individual becomes careless, is trampled by a defensive herd or crushed by a falling culm. Mostly they die of accumulated hunting injuries, infections from prey-inflicted wounds, organ failures from decades of processing the mineral-heavy diet. A weftcat that reaches fifteen years is ancient, probably no longer hunting effectively, becoming thin and desperate, eventually making a mistake that proves fatal.

They vocalize rarely, and when they do, it's barely audible beneath the culm-drone. A low growl from deep in the chest, felt more than heard. A sharp hiss when startled. Mothers call to cubs with a sound that's almost bird-like, a high-frequency chirrup that carries only a few dozen meters. Mating involves more vocal exchange—yowls and growls that might be audible from a half-kilometer if the wind drops, that sound eerie and somehow wrong in an environment where animal sounds are normally overwhelmed by plant-generated acoustics.

Their relationship with humans is wary on both sides. Weftcats quickly learned that humans are not prey—wrong size, wrong smell, wrong behavior—and that approaching human installations leads to unpleasant consequences (deterrent systems, loud noises, occasionally projectiles). Humans learned that weftcats will not attack unless cornered or defending cubs, but that they are perfectly capable of killing a person if motivated. The result is a détente: mutual avoidance, mutual acknowledgment. Workers moving between stations keep watch for the paired eye-gleams at night. Weftcats route their territories around the fixed installations but otherwise continue their ancient patterns, indifferent to the industrial presence.

## The Climbers: The Culm-Grips

They look almost like sloths but are not, parallel evolution producing similar solutions to the problem of living in vertical space. Culm-grips are meter-long, furred in dense pale fiber that has an almost woolly texture, colored in variations of cream and light gray that blend with the translucent culm walls when viewed from below or above. They have six limbs—four primary and two reduced secondary limbs positioned mid-body—all ending in curved gripping structures that are part claw, part sucker, able to adhere to the smooth culm surfaces through a combination of mechanical grip and chemical adhesion.

The face is flat, almost brachycephalic, with forward-facing eyes protected by bony ridges that give them a permanently surprised expression. The mouth is small, designed for browsing the soft node-tissue where culms occasionally branch, for licking up the volatile-rich condensation that collects in culm hollows, for catching the small arthropods that colonize upper culm reaches. They have almost no teeth—instead, the mouth interior is lined with keratinous ridges that work like a file, grinding food into paste that can be swallowed.

Their primary adaptation is metabolic: they function efficiently in the thin air of the upper reaches, fifty to eighty meters up, where oxygen levels are significantly reduced. Their blood carries oxygen with unusual efficiency, using a metallic compound that gives it a faint greenish tint, visible through their pale skin when they're backlit against a bright culm wall. Heart rate is slow, perhaps ten beats per minute, supporting a generally slowed metabolism that allows them to survive on the limited food resources available at height.

Movement is glacial. A culm-grip might spend an hour traveling ten meters up a trunk, testing each grip point, moving one limb at a time, never committing to a position until certain of its stability. They can move faster if threatened—can scuttle along at perhaps one meter per minute—but this is exhausting, unsustainable. Mostly they are still, clinging to culm surfaces, nearly invisible, patient beyond human comprehension.

They're solitary animals, tolerating others of their species only during brief mating periods. Each individual claims a vertical zone on a particular culm or set of adjacent culms, defending it from others through scent-marking and, if necessary, slow-motion physical confrontation. These fights look absurd to human observers—two culm-grips slowly grappling on a vertical surface, each trying to dislodge the other, the whole interaction taking place over hours, ending usually when one loses grip and falls a few meters, catches itself, decides the territory isn't worth the energy expenditure.

They descend rarely, usually in response to weather changes that make upper-level conditions untenable—severe wind that threatens to tear them from their holds, or the rare precipitation events that create slippery surfaces. When they descend, they become vulnerable, their slow movement making them easy targets for the aerial predators that occasionally swoop through lower levels. On the ground they are nearly helpless, able to move only in an awkward shuffle, dragging their bodies across the compressed plant substrate toward the nearest climbable trunk.

Breeding happens seasonally, triggered by culm chemistry changes. Males become slightly more mobile, traveling between culms seeking females, calling with ultrasonic vocalizations that are inaudible to humans but apparently meaningful to their own kind. Mating is prolonged—takes place over days, both animals clinging to a culm surface, barely moving, engaged in some exchange of chemical and tactile signals that eventually leads to fertilization.

Females give birth to a single offspring after a remarkably short gestation—perhaps six weeks. The infant is tiny, barely the size of a human hand, born with eyes sealed and limbs already equipped with functional grips. It clings to the mother's ventral surface for the first month, nursing on milk that is almost paste-thick, dense with nutrients. By two months it's making short independent climbs, always returning to the mother. By four months it's functionally independent, establishing its own territory nearby.

Their lifespan might be considerable—ten years, fifteen, possibly more—but this is hard to determine. They're difficult to track, difficult to distinguish individually, live in an environment where direct observation is challenging. What's known is that they accumulate slowly in certain culm groves, that old individuals become recognizable by their size and the darker coloration of their fur, that these elders seem to be tolerated in territories that would exclude younger animals.

Predation is primarily from above—the aerial hunters occasionally take them, striking fast enough that even the culm-grip's excellent grip strength can't save them. Occasionally a weftcat climbing a culm to investigate something will encounter and kill one, though this is opportunistic rather than targeted hunting. Mostly they die from accidents: falling during storms, getting trapped in culm collapses, succumbing to parasites or the slow accumulation of metabolic problems from the challenging environment they inhabit.

Their droppings accumulate at culm bases, concentrated deposits that have distinctive chemistry, high in minerals processed from the node-tissue they browse. These deposits support their own micro-ecosystems of specialized decomposers, contributing to the general cycling of nutrients through the compressed plant substrate. In this way, the culm-grips are keystone species despite their small size and low biomass—they move nutrients from the upper reaches down to ground level, facilitating flows that wouldn't otherwise occur in the vertical ecosystem.

## The Aerial Hunters: The Glint-Wings

They are called glint-wings for the way light catches their translucent wing membranes, creating flashes of brilliance visible from kilometers away. Whether they are birds or something else is a taxonomic question no one has satisfactorily answered. They have features of both birds and pterosaurs, of bats and insects, a combination that suggests either convergent evolution or a lineage that split from familiar categories very early in this world's biological history.

The body is small relative to wingspan—perhaps twenty kilograms of mass supporting wings that span eight meters tip to tip. The skeleton must be almost impossibly light, hollow structures that are probably more air than bone. The wing membranes are translucent, showing fine patterns of support structures, blood vessels maybe, or something analogous. In flight, they're nearly invisible against the bright sky—you see only the occasional flash of reflected light, the shadow that passes across culm surfaces, the disturbance in air that indicates presence rather than the creature itself.

The head is streamlined, equipped with a long narrow beak or jaw structure that seems designed for probing and stabbing. Eyes are enormous, taking up perhaps a third of the head's volume, positioned to provide excellent binocular vision forward and down. The eyes are multifaceted, possibly compound like an insect's, or possibly something else—descriptions from the few people who've seen one up close are contradictory, uncertain.

They hunt from extreme altitude, soaring in the thermals that rise from the culm forest, circling for hours without visible effort. From two hundred meters up, their enhanced vision can detect movement below—the scurrying of a culm-grip, the dash of something smaller, the distinctive motion patterns that indicate prey. When they strike, it's a controlled plummet, wings folded, body streamlined, dropping through the vertical space between culms with precision that suggests incredibly sophisticated spatial processing. At the last moment, wings flare, talons or claws or whatever gripping structures they possess extend forward, and they snatch their target, carrying it back aloft in a single fluid motion.

They vocalize at frequencies mostly inaudible to humans—ultrasonic calls that might be echolocation, might be communication between individuals, might be something else. Occasionally, in the quiet moments when wind drops and the culm-drone decreases, you hear them: sharp clicks, high-pitched chirps, sounds that seem too insect-like to come from something so large.

Nesting happens in the highest culm reaches, in the rare branches where subsidiary shoots create platform-like structures. The nests are minimal—a slight depression, a few gathered fibers, barely enough to contain the two or three eggs each pair produces. Both parents attend the nest in rotation, one always hunting, one always guarding. The eggs are pale, almost translucent like the adult wings, nearly invisible against the culm structure unless you know exactly where to look.

Chicks grow rapidly, reaching flight capability within six weeks of hatching. During this time, they're vulnerable—the nest location is precarious, the wind strong at those heights, predation possible from other glint-wings or from unknown threats. Parents are aggressive defenders, diving at anything that approaches the nest territory, calling with frequencies that might be intended to disorient or alarm.

Juvenile glint-wings are less translucent than adults, their wing membranes slightly opaque, making them more visible, more vulnerable as they learn to hunt. Mortality is high—perhaps half don't survive their first year, succumbing to starvation, accident, predation by their own kind. Those that survive become increasingly ghost-like, their membranes thinning and clarifying, their flight skills sharpening, until by their second year they're nearly indistinguishable from adults.

They range far beyond the culm continent—this is known from rare sightings over deserts, oceans, other landmasses. Whether they migrate seasonally or simply have enormous territories is unclear. What's known is that they always return to the culm forests to breed, that they seem dependent on this landscape for some critical element of their life cycle, that despite their ranging they are fundamentally creatures of this place.

Their relationship with humans is minimal—they exist at altitude, in spaces humans rarely access, pursuing prey humans have little interest in. Occasionally workers at processing stations see them passing overhead, sometimes descending to investigate the activity, quickly losing interest and returning to their high soaring. There are stories of glint-wings attacking humans, but these are almost certainly apocryphal—humans are far too large to be viable prey, offer nothing that would interest a creature adapted for catching small, light targets in midair.

They might be intelligent—more intelligent than their behavior suggests. Some researchers claim to have observed complex social interactions, coordinated hunting between multiple individuals, what might be teaching behavior between parents and offspring. Others dismiss this as pattern-finding in random behavior, projection of human categories onto alien phenomena. The truth is probably somewhere between, but determining it would require study that's almost impossible given the creatures' aerial lifestyle and the harsh conditions of their environment.

## The Swarm-Masses: The Substrate Processors

They are not a single species but dozens, hundreds, a collective term for the arthropod-analogues that work the compressed plant floor, that process fallen material, that form the base of the decay cycle. Calling them insects is taxonomically sloppy but functionally accurate—they are small, segmented, equipped with multiple limbs, adapted for breaking down organic material into forms that can be reincorporated into the substrate or consumed by other organisms.

The largest are the mirror-backs, beetle-analogues the size of a fist, their carapaces developed into almost perfect reflective surfaces. The mirror finish isn't decorative—it's thermal management, reflecting the intense light, preventing overheating. A swarm of mirror-backs catching sunlight is dazzling, painful to look at directly, a moving mass of brightness that flows across dark substrate like liquid metal.

Mirror-backs feed on fallen culm tissue, working in aggregations of thousands, their mandibles designed for cutting through the tough outer rind, accessing the softer internal material. They can reduce a fallen culm section to processed fragments in a matter of days, working ceaselessly, their efficiency suggesting collective coordination. Whether they communicate through pheromones or sound or some other mechanism is unknown, but their behavior demonstrates clear organization—different subgroups working different parts of a culm section, movements that appear choreographed.

Smaller than mirror-backs: the drilling-forms, thin elongated creatures barely the width of a pencil, equipped with specialized head structures that bore into standing culms, creating entry points for fungal-analogues and bacteria-analogues that begin internal decay. The drilling-forms don't kill culms—the plants are too large, too resilient—but they weaken them, accelerate the natural fall cycle, contribute to the turnover that keeps the forest dynamic.

Smaller still: the substrate-dancers, ant-sized creatures that move in enormous rivers across the compressed plant floor, following chemical gradients invisible to human senses. They farm—this has been confirmed through patient observation—cultivating patches of fungal-analogue growth in protected hollows, feeding it specific substrate materials, harvesting it as it matures. The farms are tiny, each no larger than a hand's breadth, but there are millions of them scattered across every square kilometer, collectively representing significant biomass processing.

Then the barely-visible: mite-sized creatures, springtail-analogues, things without names that exist at the edge of what human vision can resolve. These are the true processors, the organisms that break down material to molecular components, that extract the last usable nutrients, that complete the cycle from living culm to substrate back to nutrients available for new growth. They exist in densities that would be hard to comprehend—millions per cubic meter of substrate, a living matrix that means the "ground" is never really ground, always something dynamic, always something in active transformation.

The swarm-masses respond to environmental conditions with remarkable speed. A fresh culm-fall triggers immediate aggregation—within hours, mirror-backs are arriving, within a day, the full processing community is present. They sense it through substrate vibrations, through chemical releases, through mechanisms that haven't been adequately studied. The response is too coordinated, too immediate, to be random migration.

They respond also to the larger animals—rindwalker dung is processed within hours of deposition, swarm-masses arriving to reduce it to substrate, extract nutrients, incorporate it into the cycling system. Weftcat kills attract different specialists, carrion-processors that can handle the high protein density, the complex lipids. The remains of animals become dark patches on the substrate, visible for weeks, slowly fading as processing completes and new growth emerges.

Some of the swarm-masses are aerial, though barely—they can manage short flights, jumps extended by membrane structures, glides from elevated positions. These creatures work the standing culms, processing the shed rind material, the node debris, the droppings of culm-grips. They're pale, nearly translucent, almost invisible against culm surfaces. You notice them only when they move in aggregation, when thousands launch simultaneously from a culm surface, creating a brief cloud that dissipates as quickly as it formed.

Others are truly aerial, equipped with wings that allow sustained flight. These are the pollinators—the culms do occasionally flower, rare events triggered by unknown conditions, producing blooms at impossible heights. The flying swarm-masses service these blooms, transferring genetic material between culms, facilitating the rare sexual reproduction events that add genetic diversity to what is primarily a clonal system. These flying forms are beautiful in their way—iridescent, colored in pale blues and greens, catching light as they move between culms at fifty, sixty, eighty meters up.

The predators among the swarm-masses are fierce despite their size. Spider-analogues with leg spans reaching ten centimeters, equipped with venom that can drop a mirror-back instantly, with web-analogues that are nearly invisible, stretched between culm bases, catching whatever moves through. Mantis-analogues, long and thin, camouflaged against plant matter, ambush hunters that wait motionless for hours before striking. Centipede-analogues that are all moving legs and appetite, flowing across the substrate, catching and consuming anything smaller than themselves.

The collective biomass of the swarm-masses probably exceeds that of the large animals—the rindwalkers, weftcats, all the visible fauna. They are the true dominants of this ecosystem, the organisms that make the cycling work, that transform fallen plant matter back into available nutrients, that maintain the system's dynamic stability. Without them, the culm forest would drown in its own dead tissue, would cease to function within years. With them, it persists, thrives, maintains its character across geological time.

## The Uncertain Forms: The Height-Dwellers

These are known primarily through glimpses, through uncertain observation, through evidence that suggests presence without confirming nature. They live, if they exist as distinct organisms at all, in the highest reaches of the tallest culms, in zones that humans access rarely, where the air is thin and the wind constant and the acoustic environment even more extreme than at ground level.

What workers report, when they report anything: shapes moving against the bright sky, too large to be glint-wings, wrong configuration to be anything familiar. Masses that might be colonial organisms, clusters of smaller creatures acting as coherent units, or might be single creatures of unusual size. Sounds, occasionally, that don't match the expected culm-drone harmonics—rhythmic percussions, modulated tones that suggest purpose rather than random resonance.

Physical evidence is minimal but present: markings on the highest culm surfaces, patterns that seem too regular to be weather damage, too extensive to be from any known animal. Structures, perhaps, woven from shed culm fibers, creating shelters or nests or something else in the rare branch-points that occur at extreme height. Material transport—objects found at height that shouldn't be there, that must have been moved by something deliberate, purposeful.

Some researchers believe the height-dwellers are simply known animals behaving in unexpected ways—glint-wings roosting communally, culm-grips adapted to higher altitude than usually observed, some behavior of the aerial swarm-masses that hasn't been adequately documented. Others believe there are genuine unknown species, creatures adapted to zones too harsh for routine study, persisting in a niche that humans have barely begun to investigate.

The question remains unresolved, probably unresolvable without significant investment in high-altitude research infrastructure. What's certain is that something lives there, something responds to the extreme conditions at the culm tops, something processes the energy available at that height into continuation, into persistence. Whether it's known species or unknown, whether it's remarkable or mundane, it participates in the ecosystem, completes cycles, maintains the whole system's function.

## The Microbial Oceans

Invisible except through microscopy, unmeasured except through indirect chemistry, but present in quantities that dwarf all the visible life: the microbial communities, the bacteria-analogues and fungal-analogues and archaea-analogues and things that don't fit Earth categories at all.

They live everywhere—in the substrate, in the culm tissues, in the air as spores and free-floating cells, in the guts of animals, on every surface, in every liquid film, in the rare water droplets when precipitation occurs. They are the true metabolic engine of the landscape, the organisms that fix atmospheric compounds, that break down the complex culm chemistry, that transform minerals into biological forms, that close every nutrient loop.

Some are chemotrophs, extracting energy from mineral reactions in the substrate, from the oxidation of iron compounds, from sulfur chemistry. They create patches of unusual coloration in the compressed plant floor—rust-reds, sulfur-yellows, greens that aren't from plant material but from bacterial mats processing specific compounds. These patches shift over time, following mineral gradients, responding to water availability, creating slow-moving patterns visible from altitude.

Others are phototrophs, but strange ones—they utilize wavelengths that Earth's photosynthetic organisms ignore, processing the hard ultraviolet that penetrates the thin atmosphere, the reflected light from culm surfaces. They form films on every exposed surface, invisible individually but collectively shifting the color balance, making everything slightly more green-gold than the pure plant material would suggest.

The fungal-analogues fruit rarely, unpredictably, producing structures that emerge from the substrate or from fallen culm material—pale shelf-forms, branching structures, occasionally things that look almost like conventional mushrooms but are subtly wrong in their geometry, their texture, their color. These fruiting bodies release spores that are everywhere, are unavoidable, that coat every surface and fill every breath. Most are harmless to humans, some cause mild allergic responses, a few are actively dangerous and require filters to exclude.

The microbial communities mediate every relationship in the ecosystem. They live symbiotically in rindwalker guts, breaking down the complex culm chemistry into nutrients the animal can absorb. They colonize weftcat wounds, sometimes causing fatal infections, sometimes facilitating healing through antibiotic compounds they produce. They transform swarm-mass processed material into forms the culms can reabsorb through their root-analogue structures.

They generate many of the volatiles that fill the air—the almost-water, the faint anesthetic compounds, the chemical signals that animals respond to. They are the interface between mineral and biological, between living and dead, between complex and simple chemistry. They are invisible, uncountable, essential absolutely.

## The Humans

Small populations, transient, adapted to the environment only through technology and constant effort. They live primarily in the processing stations—small crews of eight to twenty individuals, rotations of three to six months before mandatory off-continent leave. The work is grinding: monitoring the autonomous systems, repairing infrastructure, processing the harvested biomass, maintaining the delicate balance between extraction and ecosystem stability.

They change in this environment. The constant noise damages hearing—most workers show measurable loss within weeks, significant impairment within months despite the best acoustic protection. The brightness affects vision—cataracts develop earlier, retinas degrade, color perception shifts. The air affects lungs—the volatiles cause chronic low-level inflammation, the dust creates scarring, respiratory capacity decreases over time.

Psychologically, the changes are harder to document but equally real. The sameness of the landscape, the intensity of the sensory environment, the isolation—these shape consciousness in ways that persist long after leaving. Some workers become addicted to the place, unable to function in normal environments, requiring the acoustic pressure, the brightness, the compressed intensity of experience. Others develop anxiety disorders, sound sensitivities, need years to recover basic comfort with ordinary stimuli.

A peculiar camaraderie develops among station workers—they share something that outsiders cannot understand, a common experience of endurance, of adaptation to conditions that seem designed to exclude human presence. Communication becomes minimal, efficient, almost telepathic in its compression. Gesture and shared context replace extended speech. Newcomers are obvious—they talk too much, explain too much, haven't yet learned the economy of communication the environment demands.

Some workers claim to understand the landscape in ways that go beyond standard observation—to feel weather changes before instruments detect them, to sense herd movements through substrate vibrations, to predict culm-falls through some integration of acoustic and vibrational cues. These claims are usually dismissed by management, but other workers acknowledge them, speak of developing senses in this place, of becoming slightly less human and slightly more attuned to the rhythms that govern everything here.

The industrial infrastructure they maintain is minimal, deliberately so—railways, processing equipment, communications arrays, habitat structures. Nothing is built to last forever; everything is designed for eventual removal. The working philosophy, never quite articulated formally, is that human presence here is temporary, contingent, a brief interaction with a system that was ancient before humans arrived and will persist long after they leave.

Children are not born here—it's forbidden, though the regulations avoid specifying exactly what the concerns are. Reproductive effects from the volatile chemistry? Developmental problems from the acoustic environment? Or something more subtle, a sense that humans shouldn't begin life in this place, that it would constitute a different kind of claim, a different relationship than the extractive industrial one that currently exists?

Workers near the end of their contracts speak of looking forward to leaving, to quiet, to normality. Then they leave, and within months, a significant percentage apply to return. They claim they miss the work, the pay, the adventure. But really they miss the place itself—the enormous bright loud strange totality of it, the way it makes ordinary life seem small and muffled and insufficient. They come back, do another rotation, and another, until their bodies fail or they finally break the addiction, and even then some remain, taking permanent positions, accepting that they've been changed too thoroughly to fit elsewhere.

The research personnel are different from the industrial workers—smaller numbers, longer rotations, different motivations. They're here to understand, to document, to answer questions about how this ecosystem functions. They set up monitoring stations between the processing infrastructure, deploy sensors, collect samples, observe. Their relationship with the place is more intellectual, more distanced, though it affects them similarly—the same hearing loss, vision damage, psychological adaptation.

They're the ones who discover the strange details: the coordination patterns in swarm-mass behavior, the sophisticated chemical sensing of the rindwalkers, the possible high-altitude organisms. They document the acoustic properties of the culm forest, the weather patterns, the mineral cycling. They build models attempting to predict how the system will respond to increased harvest pressure, to climate shifts, to potential human expansion.

Most of their findings are inconclusive—the system is too complex, too dynamic, too poorly understood in its fundamentals. But they accumulate data, build understanding incrementally, occasionally discover something that shifts comprehension significantly. They're the ones who recognized that the culm forest might be a single organism rather than an ecosystem of individuals, who documented the apparent learning behaviors of rindwalker herds, who first suggested that the acoustic environment might carry information content rather than being merely noise.

Between workers and researchers: tension, sometimes, over priorities and resource allocation. Cooperation, often, because they're all humans in an environment that doesn't care about their internal categories. Shared understanding, always, that they're temporary, permitted, here on sufferance from a system that could exclude them easily if it shifted its parameters slightly.

At night, when the bright moon makes the culms glow, when the herds are browsing in their scattered patterns, when the sound shifts to its nocturnal registers—humans in the stations pause sometimes, looking out through filtered viewports at the landscape's alien magnificence. Understanding that they're small here, that their presence changes almost nothing, that the turbulent bright loud endlessness will continue whether they stay or go. Some find this comforting—it locates them in something larger, removes the burden of significance. Others find it threatening—it diminishes them, makes their work seem futile.

Both responses are correct. The landscape accepts human presence casually, the way it accepts everything—rindwalkers, weftcats, swarm-masses, all the living things that participate in its ancient cycling. Humans extract resources, introduce small perturbations, exist temporarily in the vast patient system. Then they leave, or they stay, and either way the culms keep growing, keep falling, keep singing their deafening song to the bright and merciless sky.
