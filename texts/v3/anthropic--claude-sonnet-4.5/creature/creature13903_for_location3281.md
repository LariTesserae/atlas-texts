# The Inhabitants of the Counting Vaults

The people here move with economy. Not the efficiency of health, but the conservation of those who have learned that everything—energy, water, space, breath—exists in quantities that must be managed. They walk close to walls, keeping to the cooler surfaces in the upper tiers, to the drier paths in the lower ones. Their hands trail along brick as they move, reading the texture for navigation in dim light, for the pleasure of touch, for the unconscious reassurance that the walls are still there, still holding.

There are perhaps forty of them throughout the seven tiers, though the number is uncertain. Some are transient in ways that have nothing to do with arrival or departure—they simply become less visible, spending weeks in the deeper levels or in isolated chambers, then reappearing to collect water, to trade for food from the cultivation boxes, to repair something that needs repairing. You cannot quite census them. They resist enumeration not through evasion but through the fluidity of their presence.

They are thin, most of them, in the way of people whose diet is sufficient but monotonous. The flesh sits close to the bone. Faces are angular, eyes set in hollows that might be shadow or might be the skull showing through. But this is not starvation—it's adaptation. Their bodies have found equilibrium with what the vaults provide: the herbs and mushrooms, the cane shoots that can be eaten when young, whatever preserved goods exist in the storage chambers, whatever trade or scavenging brings from outside, if there is an outside anymore.

Their skin shows the environment's influence. In the upper tiers, where some light penetrates, a few retain something like natural coloring, though muted, grayed. Deeper down, those who rarely ascend have skin gone pale and slightly damp-looking, reflecting the humidity, the darkness. But everyone shows the salt traces—fine white lines where sweat has evaporated repeatedly on forearms, along collarbones, behind knees. The salt from the weeping brick, the salt from their own bodies, creating a kind of archive of moisture on their skin.

They wear layers, but layers that have lost their integrity as separate garments. A shirt under a vest under a canvas coat, but the boundaries have blurred—the shirt is sewn to the vest at the shoulder where a tear was repaired, the coat has been cut and reconfigured so many times it no longer resembles its original form. Clothes here are continuous projects, persistently mended, modified, combined. Patches on patches. Fabric replaced piece by piece until the original garment is a kind of conceptual core around which accumulated repairs have built a new thing entirely.

Boots are complex assemblages. Leather uppers—from what animal, who can say—attached to soles of layered material: rubber, more leather, sometimes wood, sometimes the hard industrial belting salvaged from the old machinery. They're fitted to individual feet through long accommodation, breaking in over years until the boot is a precise map of the wearer's gait, their weight distribution, the particular way they navigate brick stairs and metal gratings. Some people wrap their boots in canvas strips for extra protection in the lower levels where the heat makes leather brittle. The wrappings become permanent, integrated into the boot structure, replaced strip by strip as they wear through.

Children exist but are not numerous—you see perhaps four or five, ranging from an infant carried against someone's chest in a sling of stained canvas, to a girl of perhaps ten who tends the cisterns on the third tier with the focused attention of someone performing a task they've done a thousand times. The children are quiet, not from oppression but from environment. Voices don't carry here; there's no point in shouting. They learn early to move carefully in the dense architecture, where a careless turn can crack your head on a low pipe, where the wrong step can plunge your foot through a rusted grating.

The girl at the cisterns—her name might be Talitha, or that might be a mishearing of something else someone called her once—has arms thin as the canes in the flooded section. She dips a ceramic cup into the cistern, examines the water in the insufficient light, pours it into a filtering vessel lined with sand and charcoal. The filtering is probably unnecessary—the water is already clean enough—but the ritual persists, the careful attention to process. She works barefoot despite the occasional sharp debris on the brick floor, feet so calloused they're effectively shod in their own hardened skin.

An older person—gender ambiguous under the layers, age impossible to determine with precision, somewhere between fifty and ancient—manages the cultivation boxes in the second tier. Their hands are the most active thing about them, constantly touching the soil, the leaves, pinching off dead growth, redistributing water from a small copper watering can whose spout has been repaired so many times it's more solder than original metal. They speak occasionally to the plants, not words exactly, more a kind of humming punctuated with consonants. The plants don't respond, but they continue growing, gray-green and tenacious.

This person has made a dwelling in one corner of the second tier—not a room, exactly, more a claim on space. A sleeping mat of woven cane, remarkably skillful work, the only piece of furniture that shows aesthetic intention rather than pure utility. A wooden box containing seeds, labeled in that indecipherable script that appears on the oldest bricks. A water vessel, ceramic, with a hairline crack that's been sealed with pine resin. A lamp—oil, glass chimney slightly smoked—that burns with unusual steadiness, suggesting they've found or maintained a source of clean fuel.

They sleep sitting up, back against brick, breathing slow and deep. Their dreams, if they dream, leave no trace on their face, which in rest looks like worn wood, grainy and patient. When they wake—and they wake at odd hours, unlinked to any visible cycle—they immediately check the cultivation boxes, as if the plants might have needed something in the hours of sleep, as if neglect could creep in through inattention.

On the fifth tier, someone is performing maintenance on the old pumps, though the pumps haven't worked in living memory. This is a younger person, perhaps thirty, perhaps younger—heat and darkness make age difficult to parse. They have a collection of tools spread on a canvas cloth: wrenches of various sizes, hammers, cold chisels, a pry bar, wooden dowels for reasons not immediately apparent. They're disassembling a valve, carefully, placing each component in sequence on the cloth so they can remember the order for reassembly.

Why? The pump doesn't work. The valve serves no function. But they work with complete focus, handling each piece of corroded metal with care, using a wire brush to clean the threads, examining the sealing surfaces. Perhaps they believe the pump can be restored. Perhaps they're simply maintaining their relationship with the machinery, keeping alive the knowledge of how these things fit together. Perhaps—and this seems most likely—the distinction between functional repair and ritual maintenance has ceased to matter. The act of tending is the point.

They work in near-darkness, supplemented by a single electric bulb that glows orange and uncertain. When the light dims, as it periodically does, they continue working by touch, fingers reading the metal surfaces, finding the bolt heads, the alignment pins, the threaded connections. Their hands are scarred—white lines across the backs, crescents where knuckles have split and healed, the blunt, squared fingertips of someone who shapes their world through direct physical engagement.

In the flooded section, among the canes, someone has made a kind of hermitage. You rarely see them—they move through the dense stalks with minimal disturbance, harvesting the edible shoots, collecting the standing water in small quantities, existing in that pocket of improbable moisture. When you do glimpse them, they're always in motion, never still, as if stillness in that damp heat would mean surrender to it.

They've built a platform above the water level, salvaged planks laid across brick supports, just large enough to sleep on. No possessions are visible except a cutting tool—could be a knife, could be a machete, something for trimming the canes—and a collection of small carved objects. Not the elaborate carved bricks in the wall alcove—these are smaller, simpler. Pieces of wood shaped into forms that might be abstract or might be representational if you knew what they were meant to represent. They line the platform's edge like a fence of tiny monuments.

This person—call them the Cane-Walker—is perhaps the strangest of the inhabitants, not in appearance but in the completeness of their adaptation. They seem to have merged with their environment, become part of the water-and-plant ecosystem of the flooded section. Their skin has a greenish undertone, probably algae growth in the constant damp. Their hair is long, matted into locks with organic material—plant fibers, probably some of the moss from the walls. They smell of standing water and green growth, not unpleasant, just profoundly non-human.

Yet they're not feral. When they emerge to trade—a bundle of cane shoots for a measure of oil, or seeds, or metal scraps—they interact with clear purpose, even etiquette. They place their trade goods carefully. They wait for the counter-offer. They nod acceptance or shake their head for decline. Then they gather their acquired items and disappear back into the canes, where the stalks close behind them and you can't tell where they've gone.

Someone maintains the lights—the electric lights, the uncertain grid that powers them intermittently. This electrician moves through all seven tiers, following the cloth-wrapped wires where they run along ceilings and down walls, tracing connections, replacing bulbs, tightening connections that have worked loose. They carry a bag of tools and supplies: wire, solder and a heating element that they warm at one of the heat sources in the lower levels, replacement bulbs hoarded from some stash, ceramic insulators.

The electrician is methodical, working through the system section by section, not trying to fix everything at once but maintaining what can be maintained. They've inherited this role—you can tell by the way they work, following patterns established by previous electricians, using techniques that predate personal invention. When they splice wire, they do it in a specific way: strip the insulation, twist the conductors, wrap with a thin strip of canvas, coat with a paste made from... something, some insulating compound whose recipe is knowledge held in practice rather than written down.

They speak rarely and then only about their work. "This junction needs attention." "The circuit to the fourth tier east will fail within the month." Pronouncements delivered to whoever is nearby, whether that person has any responsibility for electrical systems or not. The electrician seems to view the entire settlement as collectively responsible for the lights, and their reports as information the community needs, regardless of whether anyone can act on it.

Their dwelling is in the third tier, near a major junction box—a metal cabinet mounted on the wall, filled with knife switches and fuses made from strips of metal calibrated to melt at specific currents. They sleep next to this box, perhaps so they can hear if something goes wrong, if a switch arcs, if the load shifts in ways that indicate problems developing. Or perhaps they simply like being near the heart of their system, the way the gardener sleeps near the cultivation boxes.

Someone keeps bees, though you'd never know it except by accident. Discovering them feels like finding a secret room—you're moving through a less-traveled section of the fourth tier, somewhere between the storage chambers and an abandoned dwelling, and you hear it: a low, vibrating hum, organic and sustained. Following the sound leads you to a chamber sealed with canvas, and behind the canvas, in the darkness, the hives.

They're built from salvaged material—wooden boxes, ceramic pipes sealed at one end, even sections of the old machinery repurposed into insect habitations. The bees move between the hives and some source you never identify—perhaps the herbs in the cultivation boxes, perhaps the fungus, perhaps they've found something in the ventilation shafts, some flower or seep or resource that exists outside the vault's normal provisions.

The beekeeper is protective of their operation, not hostile but firm. When they find you near the canvas curtain, they move between you and the hives without aggression, just positioning their body as a barrier, making clear this space is not for casual visitors. They're willing to trade honey—thick, dark, with an unusual mineral aftertaste from whatever the bees have been feeding on—but the trade happens at the entrance. You don't go inside. The hives remain private, a pocket of managed life within the already-managed environment.

The beekeeper's hands are covered in small stings, white marks where venom has been injected and inflammation has subsided, leaving just scar tissue. They don't seem to mind or even notice. Perhaps they've built up immunity. Perhaps the stings are just part of the cost of maintaining the relationship with the bees, a price paid in small increments of pain for the sweetness the bees provide.

Two people work together on construction projects—not building new structures, but rebuilding, reinforcing, managing the slow failure of the existing infrastructure. One is tall, the other short, and they've developed a seamless collaboration based on this difference. The tall one reaches high placements, handles the upper portions of walls, deals with ceiling-mounted pipes. The short one works the lower sections, crawls into spaces too confined for their partner, handles detail work at ground level.

They communicate in abbreviated form—a gesture indicates "hand me that tool," a grunt means "hold this steady," a specific whistle signals "I'm about to do something that might cause debris to fall, move." They've been working together long enough that their collaborative vocabulary is completely functional for them and completely opaque to outsiders.

Their work is careful and slow. They might spend an entire day on a single section of wall, removing damaged brick, preparing new brick, mixing mortar, placing each course with attention to level and bond pattern. They waste nothing—broken brick is saved for fill, dust is collected for mixing into mortar, even the smallest scraps of material are hoarded against future need.

They dwell separately, on different tiers, but work together daily, meeting at whatever site currently needs attention. One carries the tools; the other carries the materials. At the work site, they spread everything out with neat precision, creating temporary order in the space, then pack it all up when the light fails or the work reaches a stopping point. Their partnership is the closest thing the settlement has to formal organization—not authority, but reliable structure, the knowledge that when something breaks, these two will eventually arrive to fix it.

Someone tends the deeper machines—not the electrician, who handles the lights, but someone whose domain is the mechanical realm, the gears and shafts and the great mysterious installations in the seventh tier. You rarely see this person; they're usually below, down where the heat makes prolonged presence difficult. But evidence of their work appears: oil on a bearing that was dry, debris cleared from around a mechanism, adjustments made to something that was binding.

When you do encounter them, usually on a staircase as they ascend from the depths or descend into them, they're covered in oil and metal dust, face streaked with sweat, breathing hard from the heat. They carry tools you don't recognize—specialized wrenches for fittings that don't exist in the upper levels, gauges for measuring pressures or temperatures or clearances. They nod as they pass but don't stop to talk, driven by whatever maintenance schedule or mechanical urgency governs their work.

Their body shows the cost of the deep maintenance—burns on their forearms from contact with hot metal, calluses on their palms that are thick as leather, a permanent squint from working in near-darkness, reading mechanisms by feel and the dim glow of lamp light. They're probably younger than they look; the work ages people, adds apparent years through accumulated small damages and constant exposure to heat.

Someone—and this is never the same person twice, or rather it's whoever currently holds the responsibility, which rotates according to principles unclear—descends daily to check the deep water source. Below the seventh tier, accessible through a narrow shaft that requires descending a fixed ladder of iron rungs, is the primary seep, the place where water enters the vault system in the greatest quantity. Someone must verify it's still flowing, that the collection basin hasn't failed, that the feed pipes to the upper cisterns remain clear.

This task is undesirable—the descent is hot, claustrophobic, potentially dangerous if the ladder has corroded, if the shaft has partially collapsed, if any of a dozen small failures have occurred. But the water is too important for the check to be skipped. So it rotates, each person taking their turn, descending into the heat and dark, verifying the flow, then ascending with relief visible in their movements, in the way they emerge from the shaft opening with the urgency of someone returning from drowning.

Whoever has just completed the descent drinks heavily, pouring cistern water over their head, their neck, their arms, replacing the fluid the heat stole. They sit for a while, breathing, recovering, before returning to their normal routine. No one congratulates them. No one acknowledges the task. It's simply done, one day's completion in an endless series of days requiring completion.

An elder—genuinely old, showing age not just in the face but in the body's structure, the curve of the spine, the way the joints seem loose and uncertain—sits in the second tier, usually near the ventilation shaft where light and air circulation are best. They do small tasks: sorting seeds, mending canvas, untangling wire that's been stored in careless coils. They work slowly, with frequent pauses, but constantly, as if stopping completely would mean not starting again.

People bring them things—problems that need solving, decisions that need making. Not formally, not like appealing to authority, more like consulting a resource. "These two routes for the new pipe, which would you choose?" "This brick pattern, or that one?" The elder responds minimally: a gesture indicating preference, a few words explaining why, sometimes just a long look at the options followed by a nod toward one.

They seem to hold accumulated knowledge, or at least accumulated experience—they've been here long enough to have seen problems recur, solutions succeed or fail, patterns play out across years. But they don't teach proactively, don't volunteer information. They respond to questions, offer what they know when asked, then return to their sorting, their mending, their slow, continuous, minor work.

At night—or what passes for night, determined by the dimming of lights, by the collective agreement that work should pause—people gather in loose, informal groupings. Not communal, not the whole settlement together, but small clusters. Three people sitting on the stairs between the second and third tiers, sharing food, eating in comfortable silence. Two people in one of the less-occupied chambers, talking quietly, conversation too low to follow, punctuated by long pauses. Someone lying on their sleeping mat, not sleeping but resting, staring at the brick ceiling, thinking whatever thoughts persist in the hour before sleep.

The infant cries sometimes—a thin, persistent sound that carries through the vaults despite the sound-dampening brick. Whoever is carrying them (the relationship is unclear—parent, relative, communal caretaker?) paces while humming, the same tune the gardener uses with the plants, or maybe a different tune, hard to tell. The crying eventually stops, leaving the silence deeper than before.

Dreams happen in the vaults, though people rarely speak of them. But you can see the evidence in the occasional sleeper who moves restlessly, or speaks fragmentary words to the darkness, or wakes suddenly and sits for a while, orienting themselves, remembering where they are. What they dream about—whether it's the vaults, or memories of elsewhere, or visions of neither—remains private, held in the space between sleep and waking, unexpressed.

The dead are handled with pragmatic care. When someone dies—and it happens, not frequently but inevitably—the body is wrapped in canvas, carried to the seventh tier, and placed in a specific chamber that has become the repository. The heat down there accelerates dessication; bodies dry rather than rot. No ceremony is visible, no words spoken over the wrapped form. But people go down afterward, occasionally, and sit near the repository chamber for a while, not inside it but nearby, perhaps remembering, perhaps simply acknowledging.

The repository holds perhaps a dozen bodies in various states of preservation, stacked with care against the far wall, each wrapped bundle slightly different in size, in the details of how the canvas was secured. They're neither hidden nor displayed, just... stored, the way everything in the vaults is stored, assigned to appropriate space, managed with the same practical attention given to tools, materials, food.

No one speaks of leaving, of escape, of the surface. Either it's impossible, or it's possible but undesirable, or it's simply not relevant. The vaults are the world. The seven tiers contain all available space. Problems are solved within the constraints of brick and heat and limited water. Life continues in the incremental way of the drips filling cisterns, the plants growing leaf by leaf, the maintenance work proceeding section by section, the machine in the seventh tier advancing click by click through whatever process it performs.

Some people are more present than others—more visible, more interactive, more engaged with the communal aspects of survival. Others are peripheral, almost ghostly, noticed mainly by their effects: a chamber suddenly cleaner, a tool returned to a different location, food taken from the common stores, evidence of presence without the presence itself.

A few seem to serve no obvious function, perform no essential task, but persist nonetheless. Someone who sits in the flooded section, not the Cane-Walker but someone else, just sits at the water's edge for hours, watching the canes, watching the water, engaged in observation that has no apparent purpose. They accept food when it's offered, return to their sitting. Are they damaged? Are they meditating? Are they performing some subtle task that requires sustained attention? No one interferes, and they continue their watching, day after day, year after year perhaps.

Someone else makes marks—not writing, not pictures, just marks. Scratches on brick using a piece of metal, creating patterns of lines, grids, spirals, geometric forms that might mean something or might be pure expression. They work in the less-traveled areas, where their marks won't be disturbed or built over. The marks accumulate, walls covered with patient scratching, the designs growing more complex, or perhaps just more dense, more layered, harder to parse as the old marks are overlaid with new ones.

The children, when they play—which is rare but does occur—play quietly. Games of patterns: arranging small objects in sequences. Games of navigation: moving through the dense architecture without touching the floor, stepping only on pipes and structural elements. Games of light: catching the moving patches of illumination from the ventilation shafts, standing in them, following them as they migrate across surfaces. Their play has the quality of rehearsal, practicing the skills and attention that adult life in the vaults requires.

Talitha, the girl at the cisterns, sometimes traces the water channels with her fingers, following pipes from source to destination, learning the system's topology through touch and repetition. This is play but also education, the way she'll eventually know without thinking where every pipe goes, what every valve controls, how to reroute flow when something fails. She's inheriting the role of water-tender, learning through years of proximity and practice, though no one has explicitly assigned this to her.

The young person working on the pumps occasionally pauses in their maintenance to simply rest a hand on the metal, the way someone might touch a sleeping animal, checking its breathing. The pump is inert, hasn't moved in decades, but the gesture suggests relationship, connection, as if the boundary between the person and the machine they tend has become permeable, as if they're both part of the same system, differently alive but mutually dependent.

Relationships exist between people—alliances, friendships, possibly romance, though the distinctions are hard to read. Two people who share sleeping space in adjacent chambers, who eat together, who work cooperatively on tasks. Are they partners? Siblings? Simply neighbors who've developed comfortable cohabitation? The vaults don't seem to impose clear social structures; people associate based on proximity, compatibility, shared work. The categories from outside—if there is an outside—don't apply or have been eroded by the peculiar pressures of the environment.

Conflict is minimal, at least visibly. Resources are scarce but sufficient; everyone has access to water, to food, to space for dwelling. Disputes, when they occur, are handled quietly, resolved through conversation or mediation by the elder or simply allowed to fade as people avoid each other in the complex warren of chambers and corridors. The density of the architecture actually facilitates peace—there's room to separate, to claim different territories, to minimize contact when contact would create friction.

Trust is operational rather than emotional. You trust that the person checking the water flow will check it, that the electrician will maintain the lights, that the construction pair will repair walls before they fail. Not because you know these people intimately, but because the alternative—failure of trust, breakdown of the maintenance routines—would mean collective disaster. So trust exists as practical necessity, and perhaps over years it deepens into something more, but the foundation is always the shared dependence on the environment and each other's competent attention to it.

Language is sparse, functional, supplemented by gesture and shared context. Conversations are short. Long explanations are rare because everyone understands the constraints, the environment, the basic facts of survival here. When someone says "The drip in the fourth east has slowed," that sentence contains everything needed: location, problem, implied urgency. No elaboration required. The listener nods, makes a note—mental or physical—to address it or to ensure someone else does.

Some words seem to have shifted meaning, or acquired new meanings specific to the vaults. "Heat" doesn't just mean temperature but a quality of space, of time—"I was down in the heat" means the lower levels but also implies the altered consciousness that comes from prolonged exposure, the way the oppressive warmth makes thinking different, slower, more visceral. "Clear water" means not just transparency but the strange too-clear water in the old cisterns, water that behaves oddly, that might not be entirely water as understood outside.

Numbers persist in odd ways. The tiers are numbered, one through seven, but within each tier, chambers and sections have designations that don't follow obvious logic: "Third-twelve," "Fifth-seven-east," "Second-A-lower." Perhaps these are remnants of the original vault system's organizational scheme. Perhaps they've been assigned by inhabitants according to private logics. The numbers on the oldest bricks—those indecipherable numerals—might relate, or might be coincidence, ancient markings that have nothing to do with current navigation.

People age here, but aging is complicated by the environment. The heat might accelerate some processes, slow others. The limited sunlight affects vitamin synthesis, bone density, circadian rhythms. Some inhabitants are clearly old; others are unreadable, could be young and prematurely worn or old and remarkably preserved. The baseline for human aging, if there ever was one, has been altered by decades or generations in the vaults, until what you're seeing is no longer standard human aging but something adapted, specific to this place.

Illness occurs but is managed quietly. Someone coughing in the night. Someone moving more slowly than usual, favoring a side, working through pain. Someone simply absent from their usual routines for days, then returning, thinner, weaker, but recovered enough to resume participation. What they do for medicine is unclear—perhaps the herbs serve medicinal as well as culinary purposes. Perhaps they simply endure, letting the body heal itself when it can, accepting death when it can't.

Madness, if it exists, is indistinguishable from adaptation. The person who sits watching the water—are they mad, or enlightened, or simply engaged in a task that requires years of observation to complete? The person who speaks to the machines, narrating their maintenance work in constant whispered monologue—is this pathology or method, breakdown or technique? In the vaults, where everyone has adapted in strange ways to strange circumstances, the line between sanity and its absence is difficult to locate.

Memory is unevenly distributed. The elder remembers decades, holds patterns and precedents, but even they don't claim to remember the beginning, the origin of the settlement, why people first came to the vaults or if they've always been here. Younger inhabitants have memory of nothing else—the vaults are their baseline, their entire experiential universe. The middle generations have the most ambiguous relationship to memory: they might remember something else, or they might have inherited stories that feel like memory, or they might have constructed false memories from fragments and imagination, creating pasts that feel real but might not be.

No one writes things down, or almost no one. The marks on the wall are not writing. The labels on the seed box are in that old script no one can read. The electrician occasionally marks junction boxes with chalk, but the marks are symbols, not words—circle-with-line, cross-within-square, patterns that encode information about circuits and loads in a visual language specific to electrical systems.

But someone, somewhere, must be writing, because occasionally you find scraps—pieces of canvas or thin sheet metal with scratched text, hidden in corners, tucked into the gaps in brick walls. The script is crude but legible, and it says things like "the third tier east needs brick" or "check the deep source, flow seems diminished" or just "day 847." Practical notes, or a calendar someone is keeping, or messages meant for future discoverers. The writer doesn't identify themselves. The notes accumulate in secret, a hidden archive of observations.

Perhaps the writer is the person who placed the carved bricks inside the wall. Perhaps it's the elder, recording what they know before death erases it. Perhaps it's one of the children, learning letters from some source, practicing in private. Or perhaps it's everyone, taking turns adding to the hidden archive, creating a collective record that exists in fragments, distributed throughout the vaults, waiting for someone to gather and collate the pieces into coherent history.

But probably not. Probably no one will gather them. Probably they'll remain where they are, tucked into their hiding places, slowly accumulating moisture and salt, degrading back into illegibility, erasing themselves at the same slow rate they're created. The vaults hold their information in more durable forms: in the worn paths on the brick floors, in the soot patterns on the ceilings above the lamps, in the calluses on people's hands, in the adapted shapes of their bodies, in the communal knowledge of which pipes go where and how to coax water from reluctant drips.

That knowledge lives in practice, in the bodies and actions of the inhabitants. When the gardener dies, will someone else know how to tend the cultivation boxes? Probably. Not through explicit teaching but through observation, through working alongside, through the gradual absorption that comes from proximity. Or maybe the boxes will die, and food will become scarcer, and the population will adjust downward, and that knowledge will be lost until someone rediscovers it or invents it anew.

The same with the electrician, the beekeeper, the deep-machine tender. Their knowledge is embodied, demonstrated, available to anyone willing to watch and learn. But it's not preserved in any form that could survive the death of the practitioner. The vaults have no library, no school, no institution of memory. What persists, persists through use. What stops being used is forgotten, absorbed back into the general mystery of the place.

And so the inhabitants continue, forty or so people in seven tiers, maintaining the water systems, tending the sparse gardens, keeping the lights working when they work, managing the heat, repairing the endless small failures of the infrastructure. They're neither happy nor unhappy—those categories seem too simple for the quality of existence here. They're engaged, attentive, adapted, alive in a place that requires constant attention to continue permitting life.

They move through their days with the rhythm of water dripping into cisterns, regular and patient. They sleep when tired, eat when hungry, work when work needs doing. They inhabit the dense complexity of the vaults the way the fungi inhabit the brick, the way the canes inhabit the water, as organisms that have found their niche and persist within it, neither thriving nor failing, just continuing, filling the available space with the fact of their presence, their breath, their small, repetitive actions that maintain the fragile equilibrium between the human settlement and the deeper, older, slowly operating systems of the Counting Vaults themselves.
