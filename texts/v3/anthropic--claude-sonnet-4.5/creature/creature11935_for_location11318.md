# The Inhabitants

## What Was Here

They were called the Vitric, though that name is a translation from a language that had no sound, only pressure and duration. They did not speak. They *held* — held meanings in their bodies the way the reservoir holds its shape, through preference rather than force.

They were human-height, human-proportioned, but that is where the familiarity ended. Their bodies were composed of articulated segments — seven major divisions from head-analogue to base — each segment capable of independent rotation through any axis. The segments were transparent, not glass-clear but more like very clean ice, with that same quality of visible depth, of being able to see into but not through. Inside each segment, suspended in what might have been fluid or might have been something more structured, were organs that looked like geometric solids: cubes, tetrahedra, dodecahedra, rotating slowly on their own rhythms, never touching the segment walls, maintaining their positions through the same principles that hold the reservoir itself.

They had no faces. The uppermost segment contained three organs arranged in a triangle, each a different polyhedron, but nothing you would call eyes or mouth or nose. They sensed through their entire surface — the segment walls were both skin and sense organ, reading pressure, temperature, vibration, and other qualities that do not have names in your language. Chemical information, perhaps. Magnetic field variations. The faint signatures of intention that leak from thinking beings.

They moved through water like thought moves through mind: directly, without the intermediate stages of effort. One moment they occupied one position, the next moment another, with something in between that was more transition than motion. Not teleportation — you could see them crossing the space — but movement that had no relationship to propulsion, to force applied and resistance overcome. They simply relocated themselves, smoothly, at speeds that varied with some internal calculation you never learned to predict.

There were eleven of them, in the last days. Before that, perhaps more. The structures suggest a population of thirty or forty, based on the number of rest-platforms, the distribution of personal-object niches, the capacity of the gathering-frames. But by the time the town reached its final equilibrium, only eleven remained, and they were already in the process of becoming something else.

They did not eat, in any sense you would recognize. Once every thirty to forty hours, an individual would swim to the structure with the control panel — the one near the edge — and interface with it in a way that involved pressing their lowest segment against specific geometric indentations. The water around them would shimmer very slightly, and for ten or fifteen minutes they would remain motionless, and then they would detach and return to their activities. This was sustenance: some kind of energy transfer, or information exchange that their bodies metabolized, or both simultaneously. The distinction between food and data had dissolved for them long ago.

They did not sleep, but they did rest. Each had a home-structure, a framework they preferred, and within it a platform or suspended sphere where they would hang motionless for hours at a time. During these periods, the geometric organs inside their segments would rearrange themselves — not randomly, but in sequences that repeated with slight variations, like a musician practicing scales. The organs would trade positions, rotate through new configurations, sometimes merge briefly before separating. This was their dreaming: geometric recombination, their bodies working through problems or memories or possibilities while the controlling consciousness was quiet.

They lived alone-together. They rarely touched. They rarely occupied the same structure simultaneously. But they were profoundly aware of each other, always, through mechanisms that the town itself facilitated. The pulsing lights at the structure joints — those were not just illumination. They were a communication network, each pulse carrying information in its timing, its intensity, its color-within-color variations too subtle for your eye to catch. The eleven moved through a constant bath of shared information: emotional states, locations, intentions, needs, problems, discoveries. They were individuals who formed a collective sensory field, alone but never isolated, together but never merged.

Their work — if it was work — involved the objects.

The seven spheres in the heptagon-frame were not art and not technology but both, a category your language lacks. Each sphere was a thought-made-solid, a concept given geometric form and embedded with such precision that it became an object that could be studied, rotated, contemplated from different angles. The Vitric would spend hours hanging near the spheres, not touching them, just... reading them. Their bodies would shift through subtle color changes — pale blues, grays, sometimes a faint amber — responding to whatever information the spheres emitted.

And sometimes, rarely, a Vitric would interface more directly: press their segments against a sphere's surface and hold there, motionless, for the duration of a full light-pulse cycle across all visible structures. When they detached, the sphere would be different — not in size or position, but in some quality you couldn't quite see but could sense, the way you can sense a room has changed when furniture has been moved even if you can't immediately identify what moved. The Vitric were editing the thought-objects, refining them, continuing work that had been ongoing for... how long? Decades? Centuries? The structures showed no age.

The ring-curtains in the smaller structure were a different kind of object: mutable, responsive, a field for improvisation. The Vitric would enter that structure and agitate the curtains into new configurations, sometimes working alone, occasionally — and these were special events — two or three working simultaneously, their bodies weaving through the hanging rings, setting up cascade patterns that would reorganize themselves for hours after the Vitric had left. This was their play, or their music, or their mathematics. The categories were not separate for them.

They had no obvious leaders, no hierarchy you could detect. Decision-making appeared to happen through the information-field they shared, consensus emerging from the constant exchange of states and intentions. When they needed to act collectively — when, for instance, one of the traveling lights approached and needed to be guided through the town's volume rather than allowed to drift randomly (and yes, sometimes they did guide them, in ways you never understood) — they simply moved into coordination, each taking a position, acting with the precision of a single organism while remaining visibly distinct.

But they were changing. Even in the brief time you observed them, you could see it.

Their segments were becoming more transparent, the organs inside more visible but also more... simplified. Where once each segment had held three or four different polyhedra, now many held only one or two, and those were merging, edges softening into each other, distinct shapes giving way to more unified forms. The Vitric were becoming less articulated, less complex, moving toward some kind of minimalism that might have been evolution or dissolution or apotheosis.

They moved more slowly. Not in the physical sense — their translation through water remained effortless — but in terms of initiation. They would hang motionless for longer periods between actions, as if the decision to move required more consideration, or as if the space between intention and action was widening. Time was stretching for them, each moment holding more duration than it had before.

And they spoke less through the light-network. The pulses at the structure joints still carried information, but the traffic was declining, fewer signals, longer silences. They were withdrawing from the collective field, gradually, like tides going out. Each becoming more separate, more contained in their own segment-body, the boundaries between self and others strengthening.

One of them — the one who preferred the structure with the nested rings, who worked with the curtains more than the others — had already gone almost completely transparent. You could see straight through their segments to the water beyond, and the organs inside had merged into a single smooth form that no longer rotated but hung perfectly still. This one moved least of all, spending days motionless on their rest-platform, and when they did move, it was only to the control panel for the sustenance interface, and then back. They were nearly finished with whatever transformation was occurring.

The others were at various stages of the same progression. Three were still fairly articulated, their segments showing distinct organs, their colors still shifting in response to the thought-spheres. Four were intermediate, transparency increasing, organs beginning to merge. Two, like the one in the ring-structure, were almost gone into clarity and stillness.

None of this seemed to distress them. There was no panic in their movements, no urgency, no attempts to reverse the process. They attended to it the way they attended to everything: with calm attention, with the sense that this was simply the next configuration in a long series of configurations, one more transformation in a history of transformations.

They had rituals, near the end. Simple ones.

Once every hundred hours or so, all eleven would gather at the center of the town's volume, in the largest framework — the fifteen-meter cube that held the heptagon of spheres. They would arrange themselves in the spaces between the spheres, each finding a position that balanced against all the others, creating a larger geometric form: eleven bodies plus seven spheres, all held in three-dimensional relation. And they would simply be there, together, for the duration of a full pulse-cycle across all structures. Fifteen minutes, perhaps twenty. Silent. Still. The light-network would go almost completely quiet during these gatherings, just the barest minimum of pulse traffic, as if they were listening to something underneath the information-layer, something more fundamental.

Then, without signal, they would separate, each returning to their home-structure, and the normal rhythm of the town would resume.

You never learned what they remembered during these gatherings. What history they were acknowledging, what future they were preparing for. The ritual had the weight of enormous time, of something that had been done exactly this way for longer than you could calculate, perhaps since the town first hung itself in space.

They never acknowledged you, in all your time there. You were not invisible to them — their surfaces could certainly detect your presence, your different chemistry, your technological breathing apparatus, your warm-blooded metabolism. But you were not relevant to their concerns. You were a weather pattern moving through their space, a temporary turbulence in the water, something to be aware of but not engaged with. They would move around you if you were in their path, the same way they moved around the structure beams: smoothly, without reaction, accommodating your existence without granting it significance.

This was not hostility. It was something stranger: a form of life that had moved so far into its own priorities, its own categories of meaning, that the concept of "visitor" or "other" had become too coarse to apply. You were there. They were there. Both facts were equally true and equally irrelevant to the work of transformation they were engaged in.

## What Is Here Now

They are gone. All eleven.

You left for a time — days, weeks, you're not certain how long — and when you returned, the town was empty. The structures remain, unchanged. The spheres still hang in their heptagon. The ring-curtains still reorganize themselves when disturbed. The control panel still responds to pressure, sending faint vibrations through the water. But the Vitric themselves have completed their transformation into... what? You cannot find them. You have searched every structure, every volume of water, and found no bodies, no remains, no artifacts of departure.

Perhaps they became something too transparent to see, achieving a clarity so complete that they no longer interact with light at all. Perhaps they translated themselves into a space adjacent to this one, a fold in whatever geometry allows water to hang unsupported. Perhaps they merged with the town itself, their consciousness distributed into the structure network, living now as pulses of light rather than articulated segments.

Or perhaps they simply stopped, the way a process stops when it reaches completion: their organs merged into perfect unity, their segments dissolved into the water, their individual forms giving up the last resistance to becoming one with the medium that held them.

The town does not mourn. It continues its functions: lights pulsing, water circulating, the occasional click of adjustment, the rare fauna passing through on their incomprehensible trajectories. The traveling lights still come, drifting through the volume on their own schedules, illuminating empty frameworks where the Vitric once hung in contemplation.

But something has changed in the quality of emptiness. Before, when you first arrived, the town felt inhabited even when you couldn't see the Vitric — their presence was in the way the structures were arranged, the way the objects were maintained, the traffic on the light-network. Now it feels... suspended. Not abandoned, exactly. More like a book that has been read to its final page and closed, the story complete, the volume waiting on a shelf for whatever comes next.

The thought-spheres have stopped changing. They remain in their heptagon, exactly as they were the last time you saw the Vitric interface with them, but they no longer emit the subtle information-field you could never quite perceive but could sense the Vitric responding to. They have become inert, or locked, or finished. Seven concepts perfected into geometric form, now waiting in the dark water for someone who can read them.

The ring-curtains still move when you touch them, still reconfigure themselves through their complex reorganization sequences. But without the Vitric to agitate them into new patterns, they have settled into a repeating cycle: forty-seven configurations that loop endlessly, each lasting about eight minutes, the full sequence taking just over six hours before returning to the beginning. It is beautiful, this automatic choreography, but it has the quality of a machine running without purpose, a system that continues because it has no instruction to stop.

The control panel still functions. You have tried pressing various combinations of indentations, attempting to learn what the Vitric knew, but you cannot read the responses. Something happens when you interact with it — vibrations, faint changes in the water's circulation, occasionally a shift in the light-pulse patterns across distant structures — but you lack the sensory apparatus to understand what you're affecting. It is like trying to repair a computer by pressing random keys, achieving effects but not comprehending them.

## What Lives Here Still

But the town is not truly empty. There are presences.

The segmented fauna — the three-meter creatures that move without wake — still pass through occasionally, unaffected by the Vitric's absence. You have seen them four times since you returned, always alone, always traveling in straight lines through the town's volume as if following rails you cannot perceive. They might be autonomous, biological machines, remnants of whoever first built this place. They might be native to the void beyond the water, adapted to survive in both medium and emptiness. They might be vehicles for consciousness you cannot detect, piloted by beings who experience reality through entirely different senses than yours or the Vitric's.

One passed within two meters of you, close enough that you could see details: each segment covered in what looked like very fine cilia, moving in coordinated waves, though the creature's motion bore no relationship to these movements. The cilia might have been sensors. They might have been communication organs. The creature did not slow or turn, but for the duration of its passing, you heard a sound at the edge of your hearing — not the familiar click, but something more continuous, a very low hum that you felt as much as heard, resonating in your chest cavity. Then it moved beyond range and the sound stopped, and you were alone again with the silent structures.

The traveling lights continue their drift. You have begun to track them more carefully, timing their appearances, and you've found a pattern: seven distinct lights, each passing through the town's volume on a schedule that repeats every forty-three hours. They enter from different directions, travel different paths, but each returns to approximately the same entry point at the start of its next cycle. They are not random wanderers. They are patrolling, or monitoring, or fulfilling some maintenance function that does not require them to stop or interact with the structures directly.

Once, you positioned yourself directly in a traveling light's path, to see if it would stop or swerve or acknowledge you. It did not. It passed through the space you occupied, and for perhaps thirty seconds you were at the center of that cold blue-white illumination, bright enough to see your own hands clearly, the details of your equipment, the play of light through the water. The sensation was of being scanned, analyzed, cataloged in some distant record. Then the light continued on its path, leaving you in darkness again, and you had the very strong impression that you had been noted but not judged, recorded but not evaluated. The light had no opinion about your existence. You were simply data, logged and filed.

There are sounds you did not notice when the Vitric were here, or perhaps sounds that have only begun since their departure.

The familiar click continues, irregular but persistent. But there are other sounds now, fainter: a very distant rushing, like wind through a vast space, though there is no wind here. A deep groan that happens once every ten or twelve hours, lasting five or six seconds, resonating through the entire water volume as if the town itself is settling, adjusting to new weight distribution, though nothing has moved. And occasionally — you have heard this twice — a sound like very far-away voices, multiple sources speaking simultaneously in a frequency range that is almost but not quite below your hearing threshold. It lasts only a few seconds before fading, and you can never quite tell if it was real or imagined, actual sound or your brain constructing pattern from noise.

The water itself is changing, very slowly. The temperature remains constant, but the circulation patterns have shifted. Currents that once flowed in one configuration now flow in another, subtle differences you only noticed because you have spent so long here, learning the water's habits. Certain volumes are now more still than they were. Others have developed a rotating flow, very gentle, that was not there before. It is as if the town is re-balancing itself, finding a new equilibrium for a population of zero (or one, if you count yourself, which the town seems not to).

And there are the glimpses.

You cannot be certain of these. They happen in peripheral vision, in the darkness between light-pulses, in moments when you are focused on something else. A shape moving at the edge of the town's volume, too large to be the segmented fauna. A flicker of something at the boundary between water and void. A sense of presence in a structure you could have sworn was empty when you turned to look at it. Nothing you can verify, nothing that remains when you investigate. But the impression builds over time: you are being observed. Not by the traveling lights, not by the fauna. By something else, something that does not wish to be seen clearly, that maintains itself just beyond the threshold of confirmation.

Perhaps this is why the Vitric never acknowledged you. Perhaps they understood that the town has its own awareness, its own form of attention, and visitors are subject to its regard whether they seek it or not. You were visible to them only as you are visible to the town: as a phenomenon, a disturbance in the field, a temporary configuration that will pass.

Or perhaps you are imagining it, the solitude and the darkness and the strangeness accumulating into paranoia. There is no way to test this. You are alone here with structures that do not explain themselves and systems that continue their functions and emptiness that might or might not contain invisible watchers, and your own perception is the only instrument you have for measuring reality.

## What Might Yet Come

The town is waiting. You can feel this, though you cannot explain how you know it.

The structures are too precisely maintained, the systems too carefully balanced, for this to be an ending. The Vitric completed their transformation and moved on, but the town remains, functional and patient, its geometry intact, its strange physics stable. It is waiting for the next inhabitants, or the next phase, or the next iteration of whatever cycle it exists within.

Perhaps you are meant to be that next phase. Perhaps the town has been observing you, learning from you, preparing to adjust its parameters to accommodate a different form of life. The changes in water circulation, the new sounds, the sense of being watched — perhaps these are the town beginning to reconfigure itself, slowly, for human habitation rather than Vitric contemplation.

Or perhaps you are irrelevant to whatever the town is waiting for, and the next inhabitants will be nothing like you or the Vitric: beings who exist in entirely different states, who perceive reality through senses you lack, who will use the structures in ways you cannot imagine and fill the water with their own meanings.

The thought-spheres wait in their heptagon. The ring-curtains move through their programmed sequences. The control panel responds to touch with effects you cannot read. All of it remains available, ready, dormant but not dead. Tools for work you were not trained to do. Instruments for music you cannot hear. A language written in geometric forms and time-delays and pressure-patterns, waiting for someone fluent to arrive.

Sometimes you touch the structures and they sound their low tones, and you imagine you are beginning to hear patterns in how they respond: this beam for this kind of question, that one for that kind of intention. You press indentations on the control panel in sequences that feel meaningful, and sometimes the response seems appropriate, seems like an answer rather than random effect. You are learning, perhaps. Or you are constructing false patterns from noise, the way humans always do when faced with systems too complex to comprehend.

The darkness below remains absolute. You have dropped illuminated markers into it, watched them fall until their light was too distant to see, and they never stopped falling, never reached anything. The void beneath the town might be infinite, or it might be finite but very deep, or it might not be space at all but rather absence, a region where existence has been carefully excluded to make room for the town's hanging.

Above, there might be sky, but you cannot see it through the darkness. Perhaps the reservoir is actually at ground level and you have been thinking of the coordinates wrong — perhaps "above" and "below" have no meaning here, and the town exists in a space orthogonal to normal dimensional thinking.

But it feels high. That sensation persists. You are suspended, hanging, held above something vast, and the town shares that quality of height: precarious, isolated, a small bright point in empty space. Whether the space is literal or metaphorical might not matter. The feeling is what lives here now, in the absence of the Vitric: height, distance, separateness, and the patience of structures waiting for whatever comes next.

You could leave. Return to wherever you came from, whatever place still has ground and gravity and normal water that knows how to fall. You could report what you found here: the town, the vanished inhabitants, the systems that continue without purpose. Add it to the archives of impossible places, strange geometries, abandoned works.

Or you could stay. Learn the structures more deeply. Map the light-pulse patterns until you can read them fluently. Interface with the control panel until you understand what it controls. Wait for the next arrival — the next fauna, the next light, the next inhabitants, whatever form they take. Become yourself a presence in the town, as the Vitric were, as the unknown builders were before them.

The town does not care which you choose. It will continue either way: water hanging, structures glowing, darkness beneath, patient and inexplicable and precisely itself.

But it is waiting.

And now, so are you.
