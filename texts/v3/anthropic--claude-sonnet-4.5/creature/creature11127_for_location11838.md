# What Lives in the Tapping Floors

They are called the Pavers, though they call themselves nothing in any language you would recognize. They have sounds they make to one another—a series of clicks and sustained tones produced somewhere deep in their thoraxes—but these are not names. They are more like addresses, locational data, descriptions of which crew you work with, which section you are extending, how many generations your lineage has been shaping stone.

## The Pavers

A Paver stands about four feet tall when its body is at rest, but they rarely adopt a standard posture. Their slate-gray skin is not quite skin—under close observation, you see it has a fine grain, like stone that has been polished and oiled. It is slightly porous. The moisture that condenses on them beads differently than it would on mammal skin, forming geometric patterns before being absorbed. This absorption is functional. They drink through their skin as much as through any orifice.

The body is segmented, not dramatically, but enough that you can see it when they move. Three major sections: a lower body housing the primary locomotion limbs, a middle section where the manipulation arms emerge, and an upper section containing the sensory organs and whatever passes for a brain. The segments are connected by flexible areas that look almost like leather but are the same color as the rest of them.

Most Pavers have six limbs total. Four are anchored to the lower body segment—thick, weight-bearing limbs that end in broad, flat pads. These pads have a texture like fine sandpaper and grip the damp stone with perfect security. You never see a Paver slip. When they walk, they move with a gait that seems slow until you realize how much ground they're covering—a steady, rhythmic pace that can be maintained for hours. The joints in these limbs bend backwards and forwards, and also laterally in ways that let them navigate the topologically strange floor sections without any sign of disorientation.

The other two limbs are the builders' arms, emerging from the middle body segment. These are longer, more slender, with four joints between shoulder and hand. The hands—and they are hands, functionally if not morphologically—have seven fingers arranged in a radial pattern. Each finger is long, about the length of your own hand, and has three joints. The fingertips are broad and flat, with a slightly textured surface that can sense minute variations in stone texture. The fingers can move independently or in concert, creating grips and manipulation patterns of extraordinary precision.

A Paver working a chisel holds it in a three-finger grip while two other fingers steady the stone and two more serve as guides, sensing the grain and structure. They switch finger configurations without thought, the way you might shift your grip on a pen.

Their heads—if that's the word—are streamlined, slightly triangular when viewed from the front, with the point downward. The sensory organs are not eyes as you understand them. There are pits, six of them, arranged in two rows across the upper portion of the head. These pits are deep, lined with something that glistens in ember-light. They sense vibration, heat, perhaps even the minute echoes of sound bouncing off surfaces. A Paver in total darkness navigates perfectly. They are built for this environment.

Below the sensory pits is a horizontal opening that serves multiple functions. It is mouth, nose, and sound-production organ combined. You can see it flex and dilate as they work—opening to take in air, closing to redirect it through the internal chambers that produce their clicking speech. When they work hard, hauling stones or hammering, this opening gapes wider and you can see inside: not teeth, but ridged plates that might serve to grind food, and behind those, the flutter of something like gills or baffles directing air.

The exhalations have that characteristic flutter you hear—a double pulse at the end of each breath, caused by those internal structures. It marks them immediately as other.

They do not have sexual dimorphism that you can detect. If there are differences between individual Pavers, they are subtle—slight variations in size, in the exact configuration of their manipulation hands, in the tone of their vocalizations. You suspect that reproduction, if that's what they do, happens in ways you have not observed. Perhaps in the lower levels. Perhaps in chambers you have not found.

Their intelligence is obvious and alien. They solve engineering problems with approaches that would never occur to a human mind. You watch a crew working to place a particularly large stone piece—several hundred pounds of shaped rock. They do not use levers in the way you expect. Instead, they arrange smaller stones as a kind of ball bearing system, rolling the large piece on top of them, constantly moving stones from back to front, creating a traveling surface. It's slower than a lever, but it requires less peak force and gives them continuous control.

When they encounter a problem, they gather. Three or four Pavers will convene around the difficulty—a stone that won't fit, a section of floor that's settling wrong. They don't speak, or if they do, it's in frequencies you can't hear. They touch the problem area, fingers moving over surfaces, sensing. Then they disperse and resume work, but the work is different now. The solution has been shared, consensus reached, all without visible leadership or argument.

They possess memory that spans vast time. You see it in how they work around the old failures, the zones where ancient mistakes were incorporated rather than corrected. They remember the decision to leave that cracked stone in place—not individually, perhaps, but culturally, institutionally. The knowledge persists across whatever generations have elapsed.

Their culture, if that's the word, is entirely functional. Everything they do serves the construction. They make tools and use them until they wear out. They maintain the ember-bowls because light, even dim light, makes certain tasks easier. They create the mineral paste because it bonds stone permanently. They manage the water because—because of something. The water is important. Perhaps it's part of their biology. Perhaps it's part of the floor-making process in ways you haven't grasped.

You never see them eat, but they must. The most likely explanation is the fungoid cakes they burn for light. Before burning, perhaps these are food. You occasionally see a Paver with crumbs of the material on their hands, and once you observed one holding a cake close to their mouth-opening, though it moved away before you could see what happened next.

Or perhaps they eat the mineral paste, or drink something from the basins, or feed on the worm-things or the pale crustaceans. Their biology is a black box. They take in energy somehow. They expend it in endless work. The mechanism is hidden.

They do not sleep in any way you recognize, but the work rhythms suggest rest periods. The lulls in activity—perhaps then they enter a dormant state, standing still in place, or crouched on the stone platforms that might be furniture. Recovery happening while they remain outwardly alert, ready to resume work when the rest period ends.

You see young ones sometimes, though young is a guess. They are smaller—perhaps three feet tall—and their slate skin is slightly lighter, more gray than black. They follow the adults, watching, mimicking the motions. You see a small Paver with a copper chisel, tapping at a stone, its strikes far less precise than the adult beside it. The adult does not correct it with words or gestures. But it slows its own work, makes its motions more pronounced, demonstrative. The young one watches and adjusts. Learning by observation, by instinct, by something passed in those inaudible communications.

The young ones also do the carrying. When stones need to be moved, when the sledges need to be pulled, it's often the smaller Pavers who do it. Not as punishment—there's no sense of hierarchy or dominance—but as function. They are the right size for this work, and the work teaches them the weight of stone, the friction of surfaces, the layout of the expanding floors.

There are variations among them. Rarely, you see a Paver with eight limbs instead of six—an extra pair of manipulation arms. These individuals seem to be the tool-makers, the specialists. They work at the forges, hammering copper, shaping chisels and scrapers. The extra arms let them hold the work, manipulate the hammer, adjust the piece in the coals, and operate the bellows all simultaneously. They are living production systems.

Even more rarely, you see Pavers that are larger—five feet tall, more heavily built, their lower limbs thicker, their middle sections more robust. These are the haulers, the ones who pull the sledges loaded with stones, who carry the timber-like fungal stalks that serve as rollers and levers. They are not unintelligent—they work with the same precision as the others—but their bodies are optimized for force rather than fine manipulation.

This variation suggests purpose-built biology, or perhaps just the natural variation that appears in any population exploited by function. The ones good at hauling haul. The ones good at fine work shape stones. The ones who can manage multiple tools simultaneously make the tools.

It implies—though this is speculation—that the Pavers breed selectively, or are shaped by their environment across generations, or perhaps modify themselves in some way. The variation is too functional to be random.

Their relationship to the space is complete. They are not living *in* the Tapping Floors; they *are* the Tapping Floors, in the sense that the place is their total expression. Every stone placed represents hours of decision, effort, precision. The floors extend because the Pavers extend them. The construction proceeds because proceeding is what they do.

There is no separation between their being and their function. A human can be a mason and also be other things—a parent, a believer, a person with hobbies. A Paver is only ever a Paver. The work is not what they do. It is what they are.

You never see them express anything like emotion. No frustration when a stone cracks. No satisfaction when a section is completed. They work, and the work continues, and they are neutral about it in a way that is almost disturbing. It suggests either profound enlightenment or profound absence. They are content or they are empty, and you cannot tell which.

But they are not machines. Machines do not problem-solve the way they do. Machines do not teach their young. Machines do not gather in silent conference to decide on solutions. There is something happening inside those streamlined heads, some form of experience or processing that constitutes consciousness, even if it is consciousness utterly unlike your own.

They notice you, certainly. When you first walk through their work site, several will pause, turn those pit-organs toward you, assess. Then they return to work. You are catalogued: not stone, not tool, not Paver, not threat, not relevant. You are part of the environment now, like the dripping water or the flying things above.

If you stand too close to their work, they navigate around you. If you touch a stone they're working on, they wait until you move. There is no hostility, but also no accommodation beyond the minimal. You are an obstacle that will presumably remove itself.

Once, you picked up one of their copper chisels—just to examine it, to feel the weight. The nearest Paver stopped working. It turned fully toward you and remained motionless. Not threatening, but focused. You put the chisel down and stepped back. The Paver waited another moment, perhaps ensuring you understood, then returned the tool to its proper place and resumed work. The message was clear: Do not interfere.

Their tolerance of your presence seems to have limits, boundaries. Observation is acceptable. Interference is not. You are permitted to walk the floors, to watch, to exist among them. But the work is theirs, and you will not touch it.

## The Drawn Ones

In the empty sections, the long-finished areas where Pavers no longer work, other things have made their homes.

The segmented worm-creatures are everywhere here. In the ember-light, you see them as pale streaks against the dark stone, each one about as thick as your thumb, ten to eighteen inches long. They are segmented like earthworms but more rigid, with a distinct front and back. The front has a darker region—perhaps sensory organs, perhaps just pigmentation—and below that, a circular mouth lined with tiny rasping structures.

They feed on the film. The mineral paste between the stones grows something—bacteria, algae, some microorganism that exists in the perpetual damp darkness. The film is invisible to you, but clearly nutritious to the worms. They move slowly across the pavement, their mouths working at the stone surface, scraping off whatever grows there.

When you disturb one, it contracts instantly, pulling itself into a tight coil. From the segments along its back, a clear droplet appears—secreted from pores you cannot see. The droplet has a sharp metallic smell, like iron and vinegar. It is probably defensive, perhaps toxic or irritating to predators. You do not touch it.

The worms are hermaphroditic or asexual. You sometimes see two of them intertwined, motionless for hours. Perhaps this is mating, or perhaps they are simply sharing chemical information, or perhaps they are doing something else entirely. When they separate, they resume feeding. Later—days later, if your sense of time here is accurate—you find tiny worms in the same area, barely visible, thread-thin. They grow.

Their population is substantial. In some empty zones, there are hundreds in a small area, creating a living carpet across the stone. The sound of their movement is like whispers, like cloth dragged over a rough surface. The sound of their feeding is a faint scritch-scritch-scritch, thousands of tiny mouths rasping.

They are eaten by the crustacean things.

These are harder to see because they match the stone so perfectly. They are low, wide, about the size of your hand, with ten or twelve legs depending on the individual. The legs are thin, multi-jointed, and move in rippling waves that propel the creature in sudden bursts of speed.

The body is segmented and plated, covered in a carapace that has the same texture as worked stone—finely granular, slightly reflective when damp. Coloration is dark gray to black with subtle variations, patterns that might be natural or might serve as camouflage against the varied pavement.

They have sensory organs on short stalks, two of them, positioned at the front of the body. These organs are photosensitive—you see the creatures react to your light if you carry one of the ember-bowls, moving away from it, preferring the deeper darkness.

At the front, below the eye-stalks, are mandibles. Complex structures that fold and unfold, lined with serrated edges. You watch one catch a worm. The worm was moving across the stone, oblivious. The crustacean-thing was motionless ten feet away, waiting. Then, in a burst: legs rippling, body covering the distance in two seconds, mandibles extending and closing around the worm's midsection. The worm coiled and secreted its droplet, but the mandibles were already cutting through the segments. The crustacean-thing held the worm and fed, pulling the soft interior out of the segmented shell, leaving the empty husk behind.

They are solitary hunters, but they are not territorial. You see multiple crustacean-things in the same zone, hunting independently, passing each other without interaction. When prey is scarce, they become more mobile, ranging across greater distances. When prey is abundant, they hunt locally, expending minimal energy.

They molt. You find the shed carapaces sometimes—perfect hollow copies of the creatures, translucent, left behind on the stone. The creature that molted is somewhere nearby, hidden in a crack or under a stone platform, its new carapace still soft and vulnerable. It will remain hidden for a day or more, waiting for the new shell to harden.

They also eat each other, opportunistically. A freshly molted individual is food if discovered. The husks of their own kind are food. The pale crustaceans are not sentimental.

And there are things that eat them.

You see these more rarely, but their presence is obvious from the remains. Carapaces cracked open, the interior cleanly removed. Legs scattered across the stone. Something with significant force and precision is hunting the hunters.

Once, you saw it. Or part of it.

You were standing near an ember-bowl in one of the empty zones, listening to the usual soundscape, when you heard a new sound above—a leathery snap, rapid, moving closer. You looked up and caught a glimpse in the light before it passed back into darkness: wings, enormous, each one perhaps six feet across, membranes stretched between struts of something rigid and dark. A body beneath them, compact and muscular, with a long neck extended forward and a head you couldn't see clearly, just an impression of angularity, of a mouth built for grasping.

It descended fast, struck something on the floor twenty feet from you—you heard the impact, a heavy thud and a scraping of legs on stone. Then the wing-beat again, labored, and it was airborne, carrying something. You saw a crustacean-thing clutched in talons or claws, legs still moving, and then the flier was gone into the upper darkness.

Total duration: perhaps four seconds.

These are the patrol creatures, the fliers. You hear them often, their wing-beats echoing across the vast open spaces, but you see them rarely. They live in the darkness above, roosting on surfaces you cannot see—the distant ceiling, if there is one, or perhaps on the tops of the tallest pillars in the deeper sections.

They are carnivores, hunting anything that moves on the floors. The crustacean-things are prey. The worms are prey, though possibly too small to be worth the effort. The Pavers are not prey—either they are too large, or the fliers have learned not to hunt them. You might be prey, though none have attacked you. Perhaps you are too unfamiliar, too risky.

The fliers breed somewhere in the upper dark. You sometimes hear sounds that might be juveniles—higher-pitched calls, less regular wing-beats. You once saw a pair flying together, one slightly smaller than the other, moving in coordination. Mating flight, or parent and offspring, or cooperation for some other purpose.

Their droppings fall to the floors below. You find the splatter marks—white, crystalline deposits that accumulate on the stones. The worms avoid these areas, suggesting the droppings are toxic or simply inhospitable to the film they eat. The Pavers clean them occasionally, scraping them off when they interfere with work, but in the empty zones, the deposits remain and grow into small mounds over time.

The fliers are part of the ecosystem here, the top predators in a simple food chain: film feeds worms, worms feed crustaceans, crustaceans feed fliers. Each level is sparse—populations kept in check by limited resources and predation. It is a stable system, ancient, self-regulating.

## The Sessile Ones

In the pools, where water sits undisturbed for long periods, other life appears.

The translucent sacs grow attached to the stone at the bottom or sides of the basins. They are approximately fist-sized, though some grow larger. The outer membrane is clear enough that you can see inside: structures, maybe organs, suspended in fluid. They pulse slowly, rhythmically, maybe once every thirty seconds. The pulsing moves fluid through them—you can see it if you watch closely, the internal structures contracting and relaxing.

They are filtering something from the water. The water in basins with many sacs is clearer than water in empty basins. What they remove—minerals, microorganisms, dissolved organics—you cannot say. What they do with it, whether they eat it or concentrate it or transform it, is unknown.

They reproduce by budding. A sac develops a small protrusion that swells over days until it separates, a new individual, drifting until it contacts stone and attaches. The new sac is small but already pulsing, already functional.

Occasionally a sac dies—stops pulsing, clouds internally, and eventually decays. The membrane ruptures and the contents disperse into the water. Other sacs nearby absorb the nutrients. The system recycles itself.

The Pavers do not remove them. In fact, the presence of sacs in a basin seems to be acceptable, maybe desirable. Perhaps the sacs purify the water in some way that benefits the paste-making or the drinking or some other function. Perhaps they are inadvertently cultivated, encouraged by the Paver management of water systems.

The worms sometimes enter the pools and eat the film that grows on submerged stone surfaces. The crustacean-things do not enter water—their bodies are adapted for land. But there are smaller arthropods, water-dwelling versions the size of your fingernail, that scuttle through the pools eating detritus, dead worms, the occasional ruptured sac.

The water ecosystem is simpler, more contained. But it connects to the land system through the worms, which travel between wet and damp-dry zones, and through the Pavers, who manage the water's movement and therefore shape which pools exist and which dry up.

## The Deep Fauna

In the lower levels—the floors you can glimpse through cracks, or the sections where you've descended via ramps or spiral paths—there are larger things.

You've seen them from a distance: creatures built like the Pavers but scaled up, perhaps seven feet tall, massively built through the lower body and middle section. They have only four limbs—two for locomotion, two for manipulation—and they move slowly, ploddingly, pulling sledges loaded with massive stones or carrying bundles of material on their broad backs.

They might be a different caste of Paver, grown or bred for hauling. Or they might be a different species entirely, domesticated or co-evolved or simply tolerated for their usefulness. They do not have the intelligence of the regular Pavers—they do not problem-solve or confer. They follow. A regular Paver will guide one, touching it, directing it toward the sledge or the load. The large one obeys.

Their skin is thicker, more heavily textured, almost bark-like in places. They have fewer sensory pits, suggesting diminished perception. Their mouth-opening is larger, simpler. They breathe heavily, constantly, the sound like a slow bellows.

You have not seen them eat, but in the lower levels, there are areas that smell organic, fungal. Large growth structures—stalks and caps, pale and fibrous—grow from the stone in cultivated patches. The large Pavers are often near these patches. Perhaps they eat the fungus. Perhaps they harvest it for others. The fungus is certainly the source of the dried cakes burned in the ember-bowls.

There are also crawling things in the deep levels, much larger than the worms above. You saw one briefly: a segmented creature as thick as your leg, ten feet long, moving through a water channel with slow, sinuous motions. Its segments were plated, armored, and it had many small legs along its length, too many to count. It was pale but not white—a gray-cream color, adapted for low light rather than total darkness.

It might be a scavenger, eating the detritus that washes down through the levels. Or it might eat the smaller worms, or the fungus, or something else entirely. It ignored you. You did not approach it.

In the deepest areas—where the stone is older, more worn, where moisture collects in greater quantities—you hear sounds that suggest even larger fauna. Splashing, heavy movement through water. Bellowing vocalizations that echo up through the levels, low-frequency, felt more than heard. You have not gone deep enough to see what makes those sounds.

Perhaps there are creatures that live entirely in the flooded lower levels, adapted for aquatic life in the cold, mineral-rich water. Perhaps they are part of the Paver system, serving some function, or perhaps they are wild, remnants of something older that has found refuge in the depths.

## The Micro-scale

At the smallest scale, invisible to you without close examination, there is an entire kingdom.

The film on the stones is alive. Bacteria, archaea, algae-analogues—something that can metabolize minerals and moisture and the trace organics in the air or water. The film is what the worms eat, and it is what drives the bottom of the food web.

In the mineral paste, there are also microorganisms. You see this when paste is freshly mixed: it bubbles slightly, ferments, changes consistency over hours. The microbes are breaking down the organic binder, transforming it, making it sticky and then hard. The Pavers are not just mixing minerals—they are cultivating a microbial process that creates the paste's unique properties.

In the pools, the water is alive. If you let a sample sit in the light, it clouds, thickens. Things are growing. Perhaps they are related to the sessile sacs, or perhaps they are independent organisms. The water is an ecosystem in itself, microscopic and vast.

The Pavers understand this at some level. They manage the moisture, control where water goes, maintain the ember-bowls whose smoke (if there is smoke) might carry spores or heat or something that affects the microbial communities. The entire constructed environment is not just stone—it is a matrix, an engineered habitat for visible and invisible life that all serves the ultimate purpose of extending the floors.

## What Is Not Seen

There are presences suggested but not confirmed.

The voices you hear—syllables almost like words, conversational tones in the middle distance—do not belong to the Pavers or any fauna you've identified. They might be acoustic illusions, sounds created by the interaction of many work noises and the strange echoes of this place. Or they might be something else, something that exists here but remains unseen, communicating just outside your range of comprehension.

The shadows that fall wrong suggest light sources you cannot identify. The darkness that varies in texture might contain entities that are native to it, that exist more comfortably in the absence of light than in its presence.

The zones where space behaves oddly—where distances are wrong, where the topology twists—might be artifacts of the construction, or they might be inhabited by things that use geometry differently, that navigate dimensions you do not perceive.

You sometimes feel watched in ways that cannot be attributed to the Pavers. A sense of attention from above, or below, or from the darkness itself. It might be paranoia, the natural human response to an utterly alien environment. Or it might be accurate. Something might live here that is aware of you, that observes, that has not chosen to make itself known.

The Pavers themselves might have depths you haven't fathomed. Their silent conferences might involve communication through channels—pheromonal, electromagnetic, acoustic frequencies outside your hearing—that convey information you cannot intercept. They might have culture, history, religion, art, all encoded in forms invisible to you.

The construction itself might be more than physical. The patterns in the stone placement, the specific geometry of the spirals and channels, the arrangement of the ember-bowls—these might encode information, might serve purposes that are not structural but symbolic or computational or magical, if magic is the word for technology you cannot parse.

## What They Are

The Pavers and their associated organisms are a system. They are not a civilization in the human sense—there are no cities, no apparent hierarchy, no visible governance. But they are also not merely animals. They build, plan, teach, remember, solve, create.

They are what lives in a place that is entirely constructed, entirely purposeful, and entirely other. They are adapted to it, or they made it, or both happened simultaneously over timescales you cannot conceive. The floors extend because the Pavers extend them, and the Pavers exist because the floors require extending.

It is a closed circle, a self-perpetuating system. The purpose is immanent—the work is its own justification. The floors extend. The construction continues. The Pavers shape stone and place it and move on to shape more stone.

You walk among them and you are peripheral. You are not part of the circle. You cannot be. You do not tap the stone or breathe the cold damp air as nourishment or navigate by echolocation in perfect darkness. You are from a place with soil and sky and the assumption that structures should reach upward rather than spreading eternally outward and downward.

The Pavers are not hostile to you because hostility requires seeing you as a threat or a competitor. You are neither. You are barely relevant. You are a curiosity that will eventually leave or die, and in either case, the work will continue.

The tapping will continue.

The floors will extend.

The patience, inhuman and absolute, will endure.
