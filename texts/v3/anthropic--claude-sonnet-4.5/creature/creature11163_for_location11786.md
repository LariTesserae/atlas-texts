# Who Lives on the Porcelain Terrace

## The Glaze-Tenders

They are not many—perhaps two dozen at most times, though the number fluctuates in ways that follow no discernible pattern. Three might labor on the western slopes while five work the valley platforms, then days later those same platforms stand empty while seven figures move across the highest peaks. They do not live here in the way settlers live in a place, but neither are they visitors. They are in constant motion, following the water.

Each one dresses in undyed linen that was once white but has taken on the mineral taint of the seeping water—a pale gray-blue that makes them difficult to distinguish from the ceramic at distances. The fabric hangs loose, tied at waist and wrists, practical for work that requires crawling across wet slopes and reaching into tight seams. Their clothes darken where moisture soaks through, which is everywhere, constantly. They are always damp. They smell of clay and alkaline water.

Their hands tell their story best. Fingers worn smooth at the tips from constant contact with glazed surfaces, palms callused in patterns that match the edges of tiles, nails kept ruthlessly short and stained with mineral deposits that no amount of washing removes. These are hands that have touched millions of tiles, that know ceramic by feel, that can detect the hairline crack or failing grout while moving at speed across a surface. They move on hands and feet equally—the wet tiles demand it—and their palms have grown as tough as their soles.

They do not speak much, and when they do, their voices are quiet, almost apologetic, as if sound itself is an intrusion here. What conversation occurs is technical: observations about water flow, assessments of settling patterns, discussions of which seams need attention. They have a vocabulary for things that have no names elsewhere—seventeen words for different qualities of seepage, a classification system for the sounds tiles make when shifting, terms for the way light reveals stress patterns in glazes.

Their faces are weathered by reflected light more than by direct sun—the glare from millions of tiles creates a unique kind of exposure that etches fine lines around eyes and across cheekbones. They squint constantly, even in dimness. Many have eyes that seem to have faded, their irises bleached toward gray or pale blue regardless of what color they might have been originally. They are sun-marked people made moon-pale by perpetual reflection.

Each one carries tools, though calling them tools seems excessive for their simplicity. Thin spatulas made of horn for clearing blocked seams. Soft cloths for wiping surfaces, though why surfaces need wiping when water films them constantly is unclear—perhaps it's ritual, perhaps there's a distinction between clean-wet and dirty-wet invisible to outsiders. Small hammers with ceramic heads for testing tile integrity through sound. Vials and bottles for collecting water samples from different areas, though what they do with these samples, where they analyze them, remains mysterious.

They work without apparent direction. There is no overseer, no schedule visible to observation, no master plan consulted. Yet their work is coordinated in ways that suggest deep communication through means other than speech. When one begins excavating a failed section of tiles, others appear within hours to assist. When water flow changes in one valley, tenders from distant slopes arrive to trace the new patterns. They seem to feel the landscape's needs through their constant contact with it, receiving information through their abraded fingertips and wet-socked feet.

Sleep happens in the roofless structures, though calling it sleep might be inaccurate. They lie on the tiled floors—no bedding, no comfort beyond what the hard surface provides—and enter a state that seems closer to hibernation than rest. Their breathing slows dramatically. Their body temperature drops. They become almost indistinguishable from the ceramic around them, pale and still and cool. This happens at irregular intervals, not tied to day-night cycles. They work until they stop, lie down where they stop, remain motionless for hours or sometimes days, then wake and resume work as if no time has passed.

Food is rare and strange. They eat tiles.

Not the tiles of the landscape—they would never damage the construction—but small pieces they carry in cloth pouches at their waists. These are unfired clay, still soft, mixed with something that gives them a gray-green color and a texture like soft cheese. The tenders pinch off pieces and chew slowly, their faces blank, processing this substance that is somewhere between food and medicine and sacrament. It sustains them. How it sustains them is unclear, but they eat nothing else, drink only the seeping water caught in their palms or licked from tiles.

Their origins are not discussed, perhaps not remembered. They might have been the builders, aged now, still tending what they made. They might be descendants of builders, raised to this single purpose. They might be pilgrims who arrived and stayed, transformed by the work into something else. They might have always been here, as permanent as the ceramic itself, and the tiles were built around them rather than by them. All possibilities seem equally plausible and equally strange.

Some are old—truly old, ancient in a way that suggests lifespans extended beyond normal human range. Their skin has a translucence that echoes the thinnest tiles, and their movements have a brittle quality, as if their bones have taken on ceramic properties. Others are young, though "young" is relative in a place without children. No one here is a child. The youngest are perhaps twenty, perhaps thirty—it's difficult to tell when everyone's hair is the same mineral-streaked pale color, when everyone moves with the same careful economy.

They acknowledge observers, if observers come, with neither welcome nor hostility. A glance, perhaps. A slight nod. Then back to work. Questions go unanswered not from rudeness but from apparent incomprehension, as if language has become so reduced here that only technical ceramic-vocabulary remains. They don't seem lonely. They don't seem to want company beyond each other, and even that appears to be more functional than social—bodies needed to lift a heavy tile, hands required to reach a difficult seam.

Occasionally, very occasionally, one will stop working and simply stand, looking out across the tiled expanse. These moments last for minutes or hours. The tender becomes a statue, water running down their loose clothing, pooling around their feet. Their eyes are open but seem to see nothing, or everything, or something in between. Then, without apparent trigger, they return to motion and continue their work.

They know the landscape intimately, individually. Each tile, perhaps. Certainly each major platform, each wall, each structure. They navigate in darkness or blinding light with equal facility, their feet finding purchase where visitors would slip and fall. They can predict where water will appear before it surfaces, arriving at seams moments before moisture wells up. They know which tiles sing when tapped, which ones shift underfoot, which ones weep more than their neighbors.

The work they do is maintenance, but maintenance of such depth that it becomes indistinguishable from creation. They don't repair so much as continuously remake, replacing tiles that have failed, re-grouting seams, adjusting angles to account for settling. The landscape is never finished because they never stop finishing it. It is both ancient and newborn, worn and fresh, permanent and in constant flux.

Their relationship to the water is not practical but profound. They don't manage it or control it—they attend to it. They watch where it goes, how it moves, what it carries. When water pools in a depression, they don't necessarily drain it. When it finds a new path, they don't redirect it. They observe, then adjust the tiles to accommodate what the water wants, as if the water has intentions and they are servants to those intentions.

Some bear scars—long white lines across forearms, crescents on palms, marks on knees and shins. These are not injuries from accidents but from the work itself: ceramic edges sharp enough to cut, falls onto unforgiving surfaces, the slow accumulation of small damages over years of contact. The scars are badges, perhaps, or simply inevitabilities. No one seems to treat injuries. Wounds heal on their own or they don't. The mineral water might have antiseptic properties, or their bodies have adapted to the constant small traumas.

Their names, if they have names, are never spoken aloud. They refer to each other with gestures when reference is needed—a tilt of the head toward a distant figure, a hand indicating direction. They might have named each other after tiles or platforms or water-patterns, their identities derived from the landscape rather than from personal history. Or they might be nameless, individual identity dissolved into collective purpose.

At certain times—intervals that might be lunar, might be seasonal in ways invisible to outsiders, might be tied to water cycles or ceramic stress-patterns—they gather. All of them, from across the entire expanse, converging on one of the central platforms. They sit in a circle, or a spiral, or concentric rings. They do not speak. They do not move. They simply sit, sometimes for days, water pooling around them, light moving across them, night falling and dawn returning. Then, as wordlessly as they gathered, they disperse, each returning to their section of the infinite tilework.

They are not unhappy. That distinction seems important. There is no suffering visible in them, no longing for elsewhere, no sense of imprisonment or obligation weighing on them. If anything, they seem content in a way that transcends happiness—they are doing what they are meant to do, in the place they are meant to be, and that alignment creates a kind of peace that radiates from them like the glow tiles emit at dusk.

## The Listeners

Fewer, rarer, harder to see. Perhaps only three or four across the whole expanse. They might be mistaken for Glaze-Tenders at first glance—same pale clothing, same weathered appearance—but they do not work. They do not touch the tiles except when moving across them. They simply listen.

They position themselves at acoustically significant points—the apex of a platform, the junction where three valleys meet, the center of the roofless structures. They sit, very still, for days or weeks. Their eyes close. Their heads tilt at slight angles. They are attending to something.

What they hear is unclear. The subtle creaks and clinks of the ceramic, certainly. The whisper of water in its eternal seeping. But perhaps other things—vibrations traveling through the tiles, information encoded in the patterns of sound, messages in the way different sections of the landscape sing against each other. They seem to be receiving data through their ears that others process through touch.

When they finally move, it is purposeful. They walk directly to a specific location—sometimes miles away—and wait there. Eventually, Glaze-Tenders arrive. The Listener indicates something—points to a seam, taps a particular tile, gestures at a sequence of surfaces. The Tenders investigate, find the problem the Listener somehow heard from a distance, and begin repairs. The Listener has already walked away, returning to their post or moving to a new one.

Their faces are different from the Tenders'—more remote, more internal. They have the look of people who live primarily inside their own skulls, for whom the external world is mostly data to be processed. They acknowledge no one. They eat the same clay-substance as the Tenders but seem to need less of it, or forget to eat for longer periods. They are thin in a way that suggests burning from within, consuming themselves to fuel whatever processing they do.

One of them hums, sometimes. Not a melody but a single tone, held for minutes, changing pitch slowly. Testing something. Calibrating. The sound is eerie across the quiet landscape—a human voice made inhuman by duration and lack of variation. The other Listeners don't hum. They are silent even beyond the general silence of this place.

## The Walker

Only one. Always only one, though perhaps when this one fails, another comes to replace them, maintaining the singular count across generations.

The Walker never stops moving. They cross the entire Porcelain Terrace in vast circuits that might take months to complete, moving at a steady pace day and night, stopping only for the briefest rests. They are older than old, their body reduced to essential elements—sinew and bone and will. Their face is a skull with skin, their hands are bird-claws, their feet are so calloused they seem hooved.

They carry nothing. They wear less than the others—just a wrapped cloth around their hips, their torso bare, their chest showing ribs like the corrugation in tiles. They should not be able to survive like this—the cold at night, the altitude, the sheer expenditure of energy. Yet they continue, animated by something other than normal metabolism.

They count. Their lips move constantly in silent enumeration. They are counting tiles, perhaps, though that seems impossible—there are billions. Or they're counting their steps, or their breaths, or the number of times water seeps from a particular seam. The counting never stops. Even when they pause, their lips continue moving, their fingers tapping against their thigh in rhythm with internal calculation.

The Walker's path is not random. They follow a route that touches certain points in sequence—specific platforms, particular wall-ends, designated tiles marked in ways invisible to others. The route might be a maintenance circuit, an inspection path, or something more esoteric—a prayer-walk, a binding ritual, a way of keeping the whole vast construction coherent through the act of continuous witnessing.

Others defer to the Walker. When they approach, Glaze-Tenders move aside. Listeners bow their heads slightly. There is respect, or recognition of burden, or acknowledgment of sacrifice. The Walker does not acknowledge this acknowledgment. They are too far into their task, too consumed by counting, too focused on the next step and the one after that and the ten million after that.

Their eyes have gone white—not blind, but bleached by whatever they've seen or see constantly. They navigate by feel and sound and perhaps memory so deep it's become instinct. They never stumble. They never slip on the wet surfaces. Their feet find purchase automatically while their mind is elsewhere, lost in numbers.

Once—perhaps once a year, perhaps once in longer cycles—the Walker stops. Truly stops. They stand at the highest peak, the eastern edge where the ceramic construction meets raw stone, and they spread their arms and they scream. The sound carries across the entire expanse, reflected and amplified by millions of tiles, a wail that might be anguish or ecstasy or the sound a person makes when touching infinity. It lasts until their breath runs out. Then they lower their arms, resume their walk, resume their counting, and do not stop again until the next cycle completes.

## The Sleepers

In the deepest structures, the ones with the narrowest openings, there are bodies. Not corpses—these are living people, but barely. They lie on the tiled floors in states of profound hibernation, their breathing nearly undetectable, their heartbeats slowed to once per minute or less. Water pools around them. Moisture condenses on their skin. They are slowly becoming ceramic themselves, or the ceramic is becoming them—it's difficult to distinguish where their pale skin ends and the tile begins.

There might be a dozen of them scattered across the Terrace in these hidden spaces. They are not sleeping naturally. This is a chosen state, or an inflicted one, or a necessary one. They are holding something—keeping something stable through the act of their suspended existence. The landscape might collapse without them, or the water might stop seeping, or some balance would fail. Or they are being preserved for a future need, kept in stasis until the time comes to wake them.

The Glaze-Tenders bring water to their lips occasionally, tilting heads, letting moisture trickle into mouths that swallow reflexively. The Listeners sit near them sometimes, attending to whatever sounds they make—breathing so shallow it's nearly subliminal, heartbeats like distant drums. The Walker's path touches each Sleeper's location, perhaps checking that they persist, that they haven't crossed from hibernation into death.

Who they were is unknown. What they will be, if they wake, is uncertain. They lie patient and pale and nearly gone, their presence a kind of foundation, their absence of action somehow crucial to the place's continuation.

## The Departed

Evidence of others, gone now, remains in the work itself. In certain tile patterns, in the specific way certain walls stack, in glazes that seem different from the rest—there are signatures, personal touches, individual styles embedded in the construction. These are the actual builders, perhaps, finished with their work and moved on, or dead, or transformed into the current inhabitants through some process of replacement.

They are present in absence—in the fingerprints pressed into clay, in the slight irregularities that reveal human hands shaping material, in the sheer patient accumulation of work that suggests lifetimes devoted to a single task. They are ghosts made of evidence, their names unknown, their faces forgotten, their existence proven only by what they left behind.

The current inhabitants tend what the Departed made, maintaining rather than creating, preserving rather than innovating. There is no new construction visible—no expansion of the tiled area, no additional structures rising. The work now is all maintenance, all preservation, keeping what was made from unmaking itself. Perhaps the original builders used up something in themselves that can't be recovered. Perhaps creation here was a one-time event, and now only stewardship remains possible.

Or perhaps the Glaze-Tenders are the Departed, aged beyond recognition, their personalities worn away by centuries of work until only function remains. Perhaps the Listeners heard so deeply they forgot how to speak. Perhaps the Walker began as someone who just wanted to see the whole thing once and got trapped in the seeing. Perhaps the Sleepers are the builders, resting now, waiting for the next phase, whatever that might be.

---

The Porcelain Terrace is inhabited by those who serve it, who tend it, who listen to it, who walk it, who sleep within it. They are not rulers of this place but servants, not masters but maintenance. The place made them, or they made the place and it remade them in turn, until the distinction dissolved. They persist because the tiles persist. They continue because continuation is what's required. They are content because they have become exactly what they need to be, nothing more, nothing less—human beings reduced to function, or elevated to purpose, or simply matched perfectly to place in a way that erases the boundary between inhabitant and habitat.

They are alone but not lonely, working but not laboring, alive but barely, human but becoming something else. They are what lives here: the patient tenders of infinity, the quiet stewards of an impossible task, the ones who chose—or were chosen by—ceramic and water and light, and found in that choice something like peace, or completion, or simply the end of needing to be anything other than what they are.
