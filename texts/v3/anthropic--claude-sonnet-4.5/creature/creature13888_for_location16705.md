# Who Lives in the Ascending Foundries

## The Casters

They stand at the crucibles with a stillness that seems learned rather than natural—backs straight, heads slightly tilted, watching the metal's surface for signs that take years to learn to read. Their arms are marked with old burns, constellations of small scars that map out decades of near-misses and the occasional lapse in attention. The burns don't hurt them the way they would hurt newcomers. Their skin has changed, become slightly thicker, slightly less reactive to heat. Some of the oldest casters can put their hands near the crucible's lip where the air shimmers and hold them there, testing temperature by proximity alone.

They know metal in its liquid state—how it moves, how it wants to flow, what it's telling them through its color and the way it catches light. They can see when the temperature is right, not by any instrument but by a quality of glow, a particular looseness in the pour. They work mostly in silence, but occasionally one will murmur something to the metal itself. Not words, exactly. Something between a hum and a whisper. When asked about this, they become embarrassed, change the subject. But they all do it.

Their hands are extraordinarily steady. They have to be. The molds require precision—the metal must fill completely, without air pockets, without cold shuts where the flow hesitates. A bad casting means wasted material, wasted time, and possibly a piece that's subtly wrong in ways that won't be discovered until someone tries to assemble it with others weeks later. The casters take this seriously. They check their molds three, four, five times before pouring. They've developed small rituals: tapping the mold's edge twice, running a finger along a seam, stepping back to view it from a different angle.

They sleep poorly. The heat stays in their bodies even hours after leaving the foundry. They lie in their bunks with skin still radiating warmth, minds still playing through the day's pours, reviewing each one for flaws they might have missed. Many of them develop a habit of running their thumbs across their fingertips repeatedly, a nervous checking of their own precision, their own steadiness.

The oldest casters—those who've been here longest—have a peculiar quality. Their eyes have taken on a slight reflectiveness, as if they've absorbed something from watching molten metal for so many years. In certain lights, their gaze seems to catch and hold brightness. They're respected but also slightly avoided. They speak even less than the younger casters, and when they do speak, it's often in technical terms so specific that only other casters fully understand. They seem to be thinking in the language of metal now, translating into words only when necessary.

## The Assemblers

Where the casters work with flow and heat, the assemblers work with angles and fit. They're different in temperament—more talkative, though still quiet by outside standards. They work in pairs or small groups, and they've developed a communication style that's half gesture, half technical shorthand. A tilt of the head means "that edge isn't flush." A specific hand motion means "rotate it seventeen degrees." They can convey complex spatial relationships without full sentences.

Their hands tell their stories differently than the casters' hands. Where casters have burns, assemblers have calluses in specific places—from gripping pieces, from using files and small hammers to make minute adjustments, from hours of holding components steady while a partner checks alignment. Their fingers are stained with metal residue that doesn't quite wash off, dark lines in the creases of their palms.

They have an almost obsessive attention to tolerance. A piece that's off by a hair's breadth troubles them deeply. They'll spend hours with files, with small grinding tools, shaving away microscopic amounts of material until the fit is perfect. They check and recheck, using tools but also using touch—running fingertips along edges, feeling for imperfections too small to see. Some of them insist they can feel differences measured in fractions of millimeters. Watching them work, you believe it.

They keep notebooks, most of them. Small books with graph paper where they sketch assemblies, note which pieces go together, record the order of construction. These notebooks are personal, idiosyncratic—each assembler develops their own notation system, their own way of representing three-dimensional fit on a two-dimensional page. They don't share these notebooks readily. There's something private about them, almost intimate.

The assemblers have theories. Unlike the casters, who seem content to work without broader understanding, the assemblers speculate. During meal breaks, you might hear them discussing what the assembled configurations might be for. "It's a resonator," one might say. "Look at the spacing of these perforations—they're harmonic intervals." Another might disagree: "No, it's structural. These are load-bearing geometries. We're building a framework for something." A third might suggest: "It's measurement. We're creating instruments that read something in the stone."

None of them know. But the theorizing continues, quiet and persistent. It's as if by understanding the purpose, they might understand their own presence here, their own necessity.

Some assemblers, after years of work, begin to see patterns. Not in the pieces—the patterns in the pieces are obvious, intentional—but in how the patterns relate to the architecture, to the peaks, to the fracture geometries in the stone. They start noticing correlations. "This assembly went into the south face of Peak Three," one might say, "and it has the same fundamental structure as that formation in Peak Seven's bedrock." When pressed, they can't quite articulate what they're seeing, but they're seeing something. These assemblers often request assignments that let them work in different locations, different peaks. They're mapping something, though they don't call it that.

## The Cable-Riggers

They're the most physically distinctive. Lean, always, because the work involves climbing and the altitude punishes excess weight. Their shoulders and forearms are heavily muscled from pulling themselves along the cable runs, from tensioning lines, from holding their own weight in awkward positions while making connections. They move with a climber's efficiency, economical and balanced.

They're comfortable with exposure in a way that seems inhuman until you understand it's simply professional. They'll stand on a finger-width ledge hundreds of feet above the next surface, working on a cable junction, utterly calm. They've learned to ignore the drop. Or not ignore it, exactly—they're always aware of it, always moving with the caution it requires—but they're not afraid of it. Fear would make the work impossible.

They have an acute sense of tension. They can look at a cable and tell you if it's properly tensioned or if it's off by a fraction. They read vibration—they'll put a hand on a cable and feel what it's telling them. "This one's carrying more load than it should," they might say. Or: "There's a weak point seventeen meters up." They're usually right.

Their hearing is acute. They work by sound as much as by sight—listening to the subtle creaks and hums that cables make, the particular note that indicates proper tension, the wrong sound that means a mounting is loosening. In the thin air where sound behaves oddly, they've learned to interpret it anyway.

They work alone more than other workers, out on the cables between peaks, suspended in space. This seems to do something to them over time. They become comfortable with solitude in a way that makes interaction with others slightly awkward. When they come back to the denser parts of the city, they seem vaguely surprised by the presence of other people, as if they'd half-forgotten. They eat quickly and return to the cables.

Many of them develop a habit of tying small knots in spare cord while thinking. Elaborate knots, precise and complex. Their quarters accumulate these knots—hanging from bunks, decorating cable ends, serving no purpose except as evidence of hands that need to be working. Some riggers give these knots away to other workers, a peculiar form of gift. Receiving one is considered good luck, though no one can say why.

The oldest riggers have something in their eyes similar to the oldest casters—a distance, as if they're always partly out there on the cables, suspended between peaks. They speak rarely and economically. They've learned that words are unnecessary for most things. Their hands, their movements, their positioning on a cable—these communicate everything that needs communicating.

## The Engineers

They're different from the other workers in that they seem to carry more of the weight of not-knowing. Where casters and assemblers can focus on their immediate tasks, engineers are required to think systematically, to see how pieces relate, to maintain the infrastructure. This means constantly confronting the question of what, exactly, they're maintaining it for.

They carry drawings always—rolled papers tucked into belts, folded sheets in pockets. The drawings show systems: cable networks, water management, power distribution. But the drawings are incomplete, or rather, they're sections of something larger that no single engineer has seen in its entirety. Each engineer maintains their domain—the cable systems for a particular peak, the water collection for a specific district—and they coordinate with other engineers, but no one holds the complete schematic.

This troubles them. They're trained to think holistically, but the whole remains elusive. They have meetings, irregular gatherings where they share information, try to piece together the larger picture. These meetings have a frustrated quality. "If we could just see the complete plan," one will say. But there is no complete plan available, or if there is, no one present has access to it.

They're meticulous record-keepers. Everything is documented—every repair, every adjustment, every measurement. They maintain logs in careful script, generating volumes of data. This data is stored in an archive building on Peak Two, shelves and shelves of bound records. Sometimes engineers go there to review old logs, looking for patterns, for clues to the larger design. They find consistencies, rhythms in the work, evidence of intentionality. But the ultimate intention remains obscure.

The engineers are the ones most likely to express doubt, though even they do it carefully, privately. "Do you ever wonder," one might say to another during a late shift, "if we're actually accomplishing anything?" The other will usually respond with something technical: "The systems are stable. The uplift is being accommodated. The structure is sound." Whether this answers the question is unclear.

They have a complicated relationship with the instruments they maintain. The instruments measure various parameters—tension in the cables, temperature gradients in the stone, rates of uplift, vibration frequencies. The data from these instruments is recorded faithfully. But what the data means in aggregate, what it's measuring toward, is not explained. The engineers sometimes feel less like they're monitoring systems and more like they're conducting a vast, incomprehensible experiment. They are both experimenters and subjects, and the distinction makes them uneasy.

Some engineers cope by becoming deeply specialized, narrowing their focus to a specific system, a specific mechanism. They learn everything about their domain, becoming experts of an extraordinarily particular kind. Others go the opposite direction, trying to learn as much as possible about everything, collecting knowledge promiscuously. Neither approach resolves the fundamental uncertainty, but both provide a sense of purpose.

## The Administrators

They occupy the less industrial buildings, the ones with more fully enclosed rooms, with desks and filing systems. They coordinate the work schedules, manage the warehouses where cast pieces are stored, track inventory, handle the logistics of meal provision, assign quarters. They're essential, but they're also slightly removed from the direct work, and this creates a subtle divide.

The workers view administrators with a mix of respect and faint suspicion. The administrators have access to more information—they see the requisitions, the work orders, the broader scheduling. They might know more about what's being built, what the plan is. But when workers ask them, the administrators provide the same vague answers everyone else does. Perhaps they genuinely don't know more. Or perhaps they've been instructed not to share. Either way, it creates distance.

The administrators are more social than other workers, perhaps because their work involves more coordination, more communication. They're the ones who might organize a communal meal, who remember birthdays, who notice when someone is struggling and quietly adjust their schedule. They maintain the human side of the operation, the social cohesion that keeps everything functioning.

They're also the ones who handle departures. Not that many people leave—the city is isolated, difficult to reach, and most who are here seem committed to staying. But occasionally someone decides they can't continue, that the altitude or the work or the uncertainty is too much. The administrators arrange transport back down. They don't judge. They process the departure efficiently and without comment. But each departure is recorded, noted, another entry in the logs.

Some administrators keep separate, unofficial records. Personal observations, notes on morale, on the social dynamics, on the small dramas that emerge in any community living in close quarters under demanding conditions. These records are not shared with anyone. They're a private attempt to understand the human dimension of this place, to see patterns in how people adapt or fail to adapt.

The head administrator—there's generally one person who coordinates the others—is usually someone who's been here longer than most. They have a quality of patient endurance, as if they've settled into the uncertainty and made peace with it. They provide stability not through answers but through consistent presence. Workers come to them with problems, and the head administrator listens, nods, finds solutions or accepts that some things have no solutions. They're a fixed point in a place where much remains unclear.

## The Maintenance Crews

They're everywhere and invisible. While casters and assemblers and engineers do their specialized work, maintenance crews move through the city keeping basic systems functional. They repair stairs, clear drainage channels, replace worn cable anchors, patch roofs (though many roofs are partial or absent), maintain the paths between buildings, check the structural integrity of bridges.

Their work is unglamorous but critical. Without them, the infrastructure would degrade, and the specialized work would become impossible. They know this and take quiet pride in it. They're the reason everything else functions.

They work in small teams, usually three or four people, and these teams develop strong bonds. They spend their days moving through the city together, talking more than most workers—not about anything profound, just the small talk that makes labor bearable. They notice things others miss: a crack in a wall that's widening, a drainage channel that's starting to redirect, a cable anchor that's working loose. They report these things to the engineers, who investigate and decide on repairs.

The maintenance crews know the city most intimately. They've been everywhere, into every corner and forgotten space. They can navigate the complex topography without thinking, knowing which passages connect, which routes are fastest, where the dangerous sections are. New workers sometimes ask maintenance crew members for directions, and the directions are always precise and reliable.

They have a practical creativity. When something breaks in an unusual way, they improvise repairs using available materials. They're not engineers—they don't design systems—but they understand how things work and how to keep them working. A cable frays; they splice it. A cistern cracks; they seal it with a cement mixture they've developed themselves. A stair collapses; they rebuild it slightly differently, accommodating the stone's slow shift.

They age into the work gracefully. Where other jobs might become harder with age, maintenance work accommodates a range of physical capabilities. The oldest maintenance workers often take on more supervisory roles, teaching younger crew members the particular tricks of keeping this city functional. They pass down knowledge: which patches of stone are stable, which are still settling; how to read the condensation patterns to predict where water will accumulate; which tools work best on which materials.

## The Record-Keepers

They're a small group, perhaps two dozen, working in the archive building and in smaller record stations scattered across the peaks. Their job is to receive the reports—from engineers, from administrators, from anyone generating data—and organize them, preserve them, make them retrievable.

They're quiet even by the standards of this place. They spend their days with papers and ledgers, rarely interacting directly with the physical work. They develop a kind of abstract understanding of the city, knowing it through documentation rather than through lived experience. They can tell you which foundry produced the most castings in a given month, which cable system required the most repairs, what the uplift rate was three years ago versus today. But many of them have never watched a casting being poured, never climbed out on the cables.

They're creating a monument of data. The archives grow steadily, volume after volume. The record-keepers sometimes wonder if anyone will ever read it all, if it serves any purpose beyond the act of recording itself. But they continue, meticulous and faithful. Perhaps the purpose will become clear later. Perhaps the accumulated data will reveal patterns that aren't visible year by year. Perhaps it's simply necessary to document what's happening here, whatever is happening here.

Some record-keepers develop specializations, becoming experts in particular types of data. One might focus on geological measurements, becoming intimately familiar with the uplift rates and fracture patterns. Another might focus on production records, tracking the types and quantities of pieces being cast and assembled. These specialists sometimes publish internal reports, attempts to analyze what the data shows. The reports are read by other record-keepers and occasionally by engineers or administrators, but they rarely lead to anything actionable. They're more like scholarly exercises, attempts to find meaning in the accumulation.

The oldest record-keeper, a woman who's been here since the earliest days that anyone can remember, maintains what she calls "the continuous log." It's a single, ongoing document that she updates daily with a summary of the day's events. Nothing detailed—just a paragraph or two noting the major work completed, any incidents, any anomalies. She's been doing this for years, and the continuous log has become a narrative thread, a story of the city told day by day. No one has read it in its entirety except her. She says she doesn't read it sequentially anymore, just adds to it. But sometimes you'll see her paging back through earlier volumes, reading passages from years ago, her expression unreadable.

## The Newcomers (Rare and Temporary)

Occasionally someone new arrives. Not often—perhaps once or twice a year. They come from below, from the valleys, though the route they take is arduous and not well documented. They arrive exhausted, struggling with the altitude, wide-eyed at the strangeness of the place.

They're always skilled workers. You don't come here by accident, and you don't come here without something to offer. They're casters who've mastered particular techniques, engineers with specialized knowledge, riggers who've worked in extreme conditions. They've heard about the Ascending Foundries somehow—through what channels is unclear—and they've made the journey because they want to be part of whatever this is.

The existing workers watch newcomers with a mix of curiosity and patience. The newcomers ask the questions everyone asked when they first arrived: What are we building? What's the purpose? When will it be finished? And they receive the same non-answers, the same redirection to the work itself. Some newcomers accept this quickly, settling into their roles. Others struggle for weeks or months, frustrated by the lack of clarity.

The altitude affects them severely at first. They tire easily, breathe hard even from simple tasks, suffer headaches and nausea. The existing workers remember this—they went through it themselves—and they're accommodating. Newcomers are given lighter duties initially, allowed time to acclimate. Some never fully adapt and eventually leave. Others adjust over months, their bodies changing, and they become indistinguishable from everyone else.

Newcomers provide news from below, though the news is often strange and distant-seeming. They speak of cities and events that feel abstract to those who've been in the Ascending Foundries for years. The existing workers listen politely, but there's a sense that below has become irrelevant, or at least separate. This is here. Below is somewhere else. The two don't quite connect anymore.

## The Long-Timers

There are those who've been here from the beginning, or near enough. Ten years, fifteen, perhaps longer—time blurs at this altitude, with the repetitive work, with the slow changes. These long-timers are recognizable not by any single feature but by an accumulation of small things: the way they move through the city with unconscious ease, the depth of their tan from years under intense sun, the particular patina of their work-worn hands, the economy of their speech.

They remember when there was less, when the city was smaller. They speak occasionally of earlier times, when there were fewer peaks connected, when certain cable systems didn't exist yet, when a particular foundry was new. Their reminiscences are factual, not nostalgic. They're not sentimental about the past; they're simply noting change.

They've seen people come and go. They remember workers who stayed for years and then left, whose names are now mostly forgotten. They remember accidents—a cable-rigger who fell, a caster who was burned too severely, a maintenance worker who suffered heat stroke and never fully recovered. These memories are carried quietly. There are no monuments, no formal remembrances. The work continues.

The long-timers have a particular relationship with the uncertainty. They've lived with it long enough that it's become simply a condition of existence, like the altitude or the heat. They don't express frustration about not knowing the ultimate purpose anymore. They've either made peace with it or exhausted their capacity for questioning. They do the work. The work is what there is.

Some of them have changed in subtle ways that might be the altitude, might be the years of exposure to the peculiar environment, might be something else. Their reflexes are slightly off from what you'd expect—they react to things a fraction of a second slower or faster in ways that seem random but perhaps aren't. They sometimes pause mid-sentence, as if listening to something no one else hears, then continue as if nothing happened. They claim they sleep normally, but other workers report seeing them standing on platforms late at night, staring out at the valleys below or up at the stars, motionless for long periods.

They're respected but also slightly separate. Newer workers defer to them, ask their advice, but don't quite connect with them socially. The long-timers seem content with this arrangement. They eat together, usually, in a corner of the communal dining area, their conversations quiet and abbreviated, full of references that only they understand.

## The Ones Who Are Changing

And then there are those—a small subset, scattered across the various worker groups—who are changing in more obvious ways. It's hard to say exactly when it starts or what causes it. Perhaps it's duration of exposure, perhaps it's some individual susceptibility, perhaps it's the nature of their specific work bringing them into closer contact with whatever is essential about this place.

Their eyes change first, taking on that reflective quality mentioned with the old casters, but more pronounced. In low light, their eyes almost seem to glow faintly, catching and holding any available illumination. They don't seem to notice this themselves, or if they do, they don't comment on it.

Their sense of time becomes unreliable. They might think ten minutes have passed when it's been an hour, or vice versa. They miss meals not through forgetfulness but because they genuinely didn't realize it was mealtime. They show up for shifts at odd hours, confused, insisting they have the timing right even when they clearly don't.

They start to perceive things others don't. They'll mention seeing patterns in the stone, patterns that aren't visible to anyone else. Or they'll talk about hearing the mountain, a deep vibration below audible frequency that tells them something, though they can't articulate what. They'll insist that certain cast pieces are warm even when they're demonstrably cool to everyone else's touch.

The other workers notice, of course. There's concern, but it's measured. These individuals are still functional, still capable of doing their work. In fact, sometimes their work improves—a caster who's changing might produce castings of exceptional quality, as if they've developed an even deeper understanding of the metal. An assembler who's changing might solve fit problems that stumped everyone else, seeing solutions that seem obvious once implemented but that no one else could visualize.

The administrators keep track of these individuals discreetly. There's no official policy, no intervention. But they're monitored. If someone who's changing becomes unable to work safely, they're reassigned to less critical tasks. But mostly they're left alone. There's a sense that this might be part of the process, whatever the process is. That these individuals are somehow further along in whatever integration with this place means.

The ones who are changing don't seem distressed. If anything, they seem calmer, more certain. When asked about their experiences, they struggle to explain. "It's like understanding something you always knew," one might say. Or: "The work makes more sense now." Or: "I can feel what needs to happen." None of these explanations clarify anything, but the individuals themselves seem satisfied with them.

They begin to spend more time in the deep sections, in the tunnels that go farthest into the mountain. The places where the pale things might exist in peripheral vision. They come back from these excursions unable or unwilling to describe what they experienced, but they seem... renewed, somehow. Reinforced in their purpose.

## The Collective Quality

Despite the diversity of roles and the individual differences, there's something shared among everyone here. A particular quality of focus, yes, but more than that. A sense of being part of something—that phrase again—without knowing what the something is. They're cooperating in a vast work, each contributing their piece, and the cooperation itself has become its own justification.

They're not a community in the traditional sense. There's no shared culture beyond the work culture, no festivals or traditions, no collective identity except as workers in the Ascending Foundries. They don't necessarily like each other or form deep friendships. But they're bound together by their presence in this place, by their participation in this ongoing project.

There's a competence to them, a general capability that comes from doing difficult work in a demanding environment. They've learned to be reliable because unreliability has immediate consequences. They've learned to be precise because precision is required. They've learned to endure because there's no alternative.

And perhaps most significantly, they've learned to live with mystery. With not knowing. With doing work that might be essential or might be futile, with no way to determine which. This acceptance—not resignation, exactly, but acceptance—marks them all. They've become people who can function without certainty, who can commit to work without guaranteed meaning.

In this, they're suited to the place. The Ascending Foundries is itself uncertain, still becoming, still rising toward something undefined. And these people, these workers in all their varieties, are rising with it, changing with it, becoming part of whatever strange completion it moves toward. They are the city's inhabitants and also its instruments, its builders and also its evidence. They live here, work here, and in doing so, they make it real—this impossible place, this city of heat and stone and careful, patient, mysterious work.
