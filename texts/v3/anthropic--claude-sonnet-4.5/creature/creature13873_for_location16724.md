# The Inhabitants of the Drowned Plateau

## The Technicians

They live in rotation because no one lives here permanently—not really, not in any way that suggests putting down roots. Three months on, one month off, though the ratios vary by company, by contract, by which iteration of the operation you're working under. They arrive by transport, though you never see the transports land—the flight schedules are managed elsewhere, coordinated through systems that don't consult the surface workers. You just find that someone new has appeared at a station, or that someone familiar is suddenly absent, their bunk reassigned, their section of the logbook complete.

They are functionaries of maintenance. Their job is to ensure continuation, not to innovate or expand. Check the seals on the pumping equipment. Monitor the power draw on the cables. Verify that the sensors are logging correctly. Replace components before they fail, according to schedules determined by algorithms they don't question. Most are competent in that specific way that long-term infrastructure workers develop—they can diagnose a pump problem by sound alone, can read a pressure gauge's flutter and know what it means three failures down the chain. But their competence is narrow. Ask them about the larger operation and they shrug. That's someone else's jurisdiction.

They dress for the cold in layers of industrial synthetic: base layers that wick moisture they never produce because the air is too dry, insulation that's rated for temperatures lower than what the plateau offers but somehow never feels adequate, outer shells in high-visibility orange or yellow that fade to a dull pink-orange after months of thin UV exposure. Gloves are constant. Boots are heavy, insulated, with aggressive tread that's unnecessary on the basalt's rough surface but required by safety specifications written for more varied terrain. Hoods and face masks are common, not for anonymity but because exposed skin dries and cracks in the wind, and maintaining skin integrity is easier than treating the splits and fissures that develop otherwise.

Their faces, when you see them, show a particular kind of weathering. Not suntanned—the light here isn't strong enough—but wind-scoured, dehydrated, with fine lines around the eyes from constant squinting against the flat glare. Their eyes themselves often have a distant quality, a focus that's calibrated for long sightlines and empty horizons, so that in the rare interior spaces, they seem to look *through* things at conversational distance. Their hands are rough but not especially dirty—the basalt dust doesn't cling, the machinery is sealed, there's no soil to dig in. Calluses accumulate in specific places: where tools are gripped, where gloves bunch, where equipment is carried.

They smell like the plateau, which means they smell like salt and cold and the faint ammonia of the fauna, overlaid with machine oil and the plastic-synthetic smell of their gear. In the heated stations, when they strip down to base layers, there's also the smell of bodies under stress—not unwashed, exactly, because hygiene protocols are followed, but metabolizing differently, running on slight oxygen deficit, processing unfamiliar trace minerals in the water they drink.

Their social structures are minimal and functional. Conversations trend toward the technical: pressure readings, maintenance schedules, supply levels. Personal history is rarely discussed, not from hostility but from a kind of mutual understanding that previous lives are not relevant to current function. Names are used but feel provisional, as if everyone here is a placeholder for the role they fill. "The technician at Station 47." "The pump specialist." Identity becomes job description.

Some have been doing this for years, cycling through rotations with the methodical rhythm of people who have found a niche they can tolerate. They move between stations, following assignments, accumulating time in service. They know the plateau's geography in practical terms—which routes are walkable, which stations have better heating, where the cable runs are most vulnerable to thermal stress. They navigate by infrastructure, not by terrain features. "Seventeen kilometers north-northeast of the main pumping cluster, follow the number seven power line to the junction, then take the freshwater pipe west."

Others are newer, still adjusting to the scale and the emptiness and the sound. You can identify them by their questions, their discomfort with the long silences in conversation, the way they stay too close to the stations, reluctant to trust the equipment will be there when they return. They learn, or they don't complete their first rotation. The plateau selects for a particular temperament: people who can tolerate minimal social input, who don't require environmental variety, who can perform repetitive tasks without the stimulation of novelty. Not sociopaths, not hermits—most are personable enough in the limited contexts that require it—but something adjacent. Comfortable with absence.

They sleep poorly, most of them. The sound penetrates the station walls, the insulation, the hearing protection some wear to bed. The industrial hum works its way into dreams, so that sleep becomes a state where you're still aware of the machinery, still monitoring for changes in pitch or rhythm that might indicate malfunction. REM cycles are shallow. People wake frequently, check chronometers, realize that subjectively minutes have been objectively hours, or vice versa.

Some develop rituals to cope with the monotony. Morning coffee prepared with elaborate care, even though the water tastes of desalination and the coffee is bulk-purchased generic. Evening logs written in detailed longhand, describing conditions that barely vary day to day. Exercise routines performed in the cramped station interiors—pushups, sit-ups, squats—repeated until muscle fatigue provides the satisfaction of change that the external environment denies. Reading, for those who can maintain concentration against the sound; the books are usually technical manuals or fiction so formulaic it requires no real attention.

A few crack. Not explosively—there's nowhere for explosive energy to go—but gradually, a drift into dysfunction. They stop maintaining their gear. They miss shifts. They stand at the plateau's edge for hours, looking out at the water, not responding when called. The protocols for this are established: document the behavior, contact coordination (somewhere over the horizon, accessible only by radio), wait for extraction. The person is removed, replaced, and the station continues. It happens rarely enough that each instance is notable but frequently enough that everyone knows the pattern.

There are subgroups, sometimes, based on specialty. The pump technicians form a loose network, trading information about specific models, sharing workarounds for common failures. The sensor array specialists have their own jargon, their own priorities. The supply runners—who transport materials between stations on tracked vehicles you occasionally hear but rarely see—develop a different relationship with the plateau, knowing it as a space to traverse rather than locations to inhabit. These groups overlap at the larger stations, where multiple specialties converge, and in those overlaps you get brief flowerings of something like community: shared meals, collective problem-solving, stories about particularly difficult repairs.

But the specialization also fragments. Each technician's knowledge is deep but narrow. The person who maintains the atmospheric sensors couldn't service a desalination pump. The electrician who works on the power distribution couldn't interpret the seismic data. This seems intentional, though no one states it explicitly. If knowledge is distributed and compartmentalized, no single person can comprehend the full operation. Whether this is a security measure, a management philosophy, or an emergent property of the system's scale is unclear.

## The Fauna

They are not a single species but a spectrum, variations on a theme of articulated exoskeleton and multiple appendages, ranging across sizes and specializations but unified by basic architecture. Segments. Joints. Plates of shell that overlap like roof tiles. Limbs that number anywhere from six to forty, depending on body size and function, each limb terminating in structures adapted for their use: claws, paddles, rasps, manipulators.

The most numerous are the *small scrapers*, dog-sized, low to the water, designed around the business of working the basalt's surface. They cluster in such density that the waterline appears to be a living crust, a pale mobile layer that shifts and writhes constantly. Each individual has a body plan that's essentially cylindrical, about a meter long and thirty centimeters in diameter, with a dozen legs arranged in two parallel rows. The front of the body tapers to a head that's more mouth than anything else—a complex arrangement of mandibles and rasping plates that can grip and grind the basalt. They work methodically, clamping onto the stone, abrading it with movements that are small but ceaseless, then moving a few centimeters and repeating. The sound they produce collectively is that textured grinding, like industrial sanders running in overlapping rhythms.

Their shells are cream-colored, slightly translucent when young, becoming more opaque and sometimes faintly yellow with age. The shell surface has a fine texture, almost like brushed bone, and it's harder than it looks—strike one with a tool and the tool bounces back, the shell unmarked. They smell strongly of ammonia, which seems to be a metabolic byproduct. When they die, which they do constantly and casually, their bodies are immediately set upon by others—cannibalism or scavenging, the distinction meaningless—and within hours the soft tissue is consumed, the shell either broken down for some nutrient content or left to accumulate.

They have eyes, or structures that might be eyes: clusters of dark spots arranged around the head, each cluster perhaps functioning like a compound eye, though their behavior doesn't suggest sophisticated vision. They react to shadows, to movement, but not with any obvious intelligence. Touch one and it pauses briefly, antennae (or something like antennae—slender, segmented appendages emerging from near the mouth) waving in exploration, then resumes its scraping. They don't flee. They don't attack. They incorporate the intrusion into their reality and continue.

The *swimmers* are larger, operating primarily in the water but occasionally hauling themselves onto the lower rocks. These are seal-sized or larger, with bodies that are more streamlined, tapered at both ends. Their legs are modified into paddle-like appendages, eight or ten of them, working in coordinated waves to propel them through the dense brine. Their shells are paler, almost white, and smoother than the scrapers, adapted for hydrodynamics. They move with surprising speed in the water, their bodies undulating, creating currents that you can feel if you're standing at the waterline. They feed on something in the water column—plankton analogs, perhaps, or suspended organic particles—their mouths gaping open as they swim, filtering as they go.

When they come ashore, it's usually in groups of five to twenty, and they drag themselves up the rocks with obvious effort, their paddle-limbs poorly suited for terrestrial locomotion. They progress in lurches, heaving their bulk forward, resting, heaving again. Why they come ashore is unclear. Reproductive behavior, possibly—some are observed clustering in depressions in the stone, their bodies pulsing in rhythms that might be egg-laying or sperm-release or something else entirely. They vocalize more than the scrapers: those hydraulic hisses, pressure-release sounds that seem to involve forcing air (or gas) through spiracles along their body segments. The sound carries, sharp and percussive, cutting through even the grinding of the smaller fauna.

In the water, you occasionally see the *deep forms*, the large ones. They don't surface completely, so observations are fragmentary: a segment of shell passing beneath the waterline, larger than a vehicle. A limb breaking the surface, thick as a person's torso, withdrawing before you can count its joints. Shapes in the deeper water visible only when one of the industrial lights happens to illuminate them—massive cylindrical bodies moving with slow beats of many limbs, heading toward or away from unknowable business.

The technicians avoid them, not from explicit fear but from practiced caution. There are no recorded attacks, but there's also no data on what these creatures eat, how they behave when approached, whether they have territorial instincts. The submersibles that work the depths presumably encounter them regularly, but those crews don't communicate their experiences to the surface workers. Protocol is to observe, document if possible, and do not engage.

All the fauna produce the clicking and whistling, though the mechanisms vary. Some species generate it through their mouths—you can see the mandibles working in patterns that don't match their feeding motions. Others seem to produce sound through their shells, vibrating plates or scraping segments against each other. The whistles are high-pitched, almost beyond the range of easy hearing, creating a feeling of pressure in the skull as much as a heard sound. The clicks are sharper, percussive, and they occur in rapid strings: click-click-click-click-pause-click-click. Whether this is communication, echolocation, or something else is unknown. The patterns don't repeat in ways that suggest language. They don't correlate obviously with behavior changes. They're just constant, layered, another component of the soundscape.

The fauna have no apparent predator-prey relationships visible at the surface level. The small scrapers don't flee from the swimmers. The swimmers don't hunt the scrapers. Everything seems to feed on the basalt, or on what's in the water, or on each other only opportunistically when death provides occasion. It's an ecology without obvious hierarchy, which is itself strange—usually, competitive pressure produces stratification, specialization, arms races of adaptation. Here, everything coexists in what appears to be equilibrium, densities stable, behaviors unchanging.

They breed constantly but populations don't grow. The waterline is always this density—exactly this density—of massed bodies. Which means mortality matches reproduction so precisely that the system never shifts. No seasonal variations. No boom-and-bust cycles. Just stasis, maintained by mechanisms that aren't apparent from observation.

## The Deep Crew

You don't meet them, but you know they exist. The lights in the water at night are evidence. The fact that the pumping stations draw from infrastructure maintained at depths no surface worker ever visits. The occasional communications that crackle over the radio, in technical language about pressure tolerances and seal integrity, originating from somewhere below.

They are the operators of the submersibles, the maintenance workers for the deep machinery, the people who go down into the three-kilometer brine and do whatever needs doing at pressure. Their vessels are built for function—reinforced hulls, minimal viewports, manipulator arms for repair work, ballast systems that can adjust to the brine's strange density. The vessels themselves are rarely seen at the surface; they dock at underwater facilities, submerged hangers or access points that connect to the vertical infrastructure descending from the plateau's edge.

The deep crew's rotations are shorter than the surface technicians'—a month down, two months off, because the physiological stress is greater. Pressure exposure, even in a vessel, does something to the human body over time. Nitrogen loading, maybe, or effects from the brine's exotic chemistry leaching through seals, or simply the psychological weight of working in an environment where equipment failure means not injury but instant death, compressed to pulp by pressures that turn water into something almost solid.

They speak differently on the radio, their voices flat and deliberate, communications stripped to essential information. "Descending to sector nine. ETA forty minutes." "Seal replacement complete. Ascending." No acknowledgment of discomfort, no small talk. Whether this is training or selection or what happens to people who spend enough time in the deep is unknown.

Some surface technicians are former deep crew, rotated up either by choice or by medical necessity. They don't talk about it much, but they carry the experience visibly: a particular caution around the water, a tendency to check equipment multiple times before use, dreams that wake them gasping. One, encountered at the monitoring station, mentioned the *pressure sense*, the way you start to feel depth even inside a hull, a phantom sensation of weight accumulating on your chest, your skull, your joints. "You get so you can estimate depth without instruments," he said, "just by how heavy the dark feels."

The deep crew maintains the machines that produce the industrial hum. The nature of these machines is not specified in any documentation available on the surface. Pumps, certainly—vast pumps drawing brine up through the plateau's stone, through pipeline networks that must penetrate the basalt like roots. Processing facilities, perhaps, separating water from the brine's mineral content at pressures where the chemistry works differently. Power systems, drawing on geothermal differentials or chemical reactions enabled by the brine's composition.

And possibly other things. Research equipment. Experimental apparatus. There are hints in the logbooks, oblique references to "the array at depth" or "core sampling in sector twelve," suggesting that the industrial operation isn't purely extractive but also investigative. What is being investigated? The plateau's geology. The fauna's origins. The brine's properties. The planet itself, maybe—this place as a site for understanding whatever strange equilibrium produced it.

## The Coordinators

They are voices on the radio, mostly. Occasionally a physical presence when something goes wrong enough to require direct intervention, but usually just electromagnetic patterns resolving into instructions, queries, schedule updates. They exist somewhere—in the interior stations, presumably, the ones too far from the edge to visit casually, where the power generation happens and the pipeline networks converge and the data from all the sensors is compiled.

Their voices are professional, neutral, with that particular cadence of people reading from scripts or protocols. They ask standardized questions: "Confirm your current supply status." "Report any deviations from normal operating parameters." They respond to problems with either immediate solutions drawn from troubleshooting databases or promises to escalate, though escalation to whom is never specified.

Sometimes you can hear them talking to each other in the background when they forget to close the channel, and those conversations are different—more casual, sometimes joking, occasionally frustrated. Human, in other words, in ways their formal communications aren't. But those glimpses are rare and brief, and they don't reveal much beyond the fact that the coordinators are people, working in offices or control rooms, managing a system whose scale makes individual human presence almost irrelevant.

They have authority—they can approve supply requests, authorize maintenance, pull technicians off rotation for reassignment—but it's bureaucratic authority, the power to say yes or no within established parameters. They don't make policy. They don't explain the operation's larger purpose. They maintain.

## The Transients

A few people move through who don't fit the categories. Researchers, sometimes, with specialized equipment and specific questions about the fauna or the basalt or the water's chemistry. They arrive, conduct their studies, leave. They write papers that no one on the plateau reads. Their presence is tolerated but not integrated—they occupy stations temporarily, require support from the regular technicians, and contribute nothing to the maintenance work that is the plateau's real economy.

There are also the lost, occasionally. People who were passing through, misjudged distances or weather, ended up stranded and were retrieved or found their way to a station. The plateau is too empty for true wilderness survival—there's nothing to forage, no shelter beyond the stations—but the distances are vast enough that getting lost is easy. The protocol is to render assistance, provide supplies, coordinate extraction if the person can't self-recover. These encounters are brief and awkward. The lost person is usually disoriented, sometimes hypothermic, grateful but also embarrassed.

Very rarely, you encounter someone who doesn't fit any classification. They're at a station when you arrive, but they're not on the schedules. They have access, but they don't explain their presence. They ask questions that are too broad or too specific. They leave before you can report their presence to coordination. These encounters are unsettling, not because of overt threat but because they violate the plateau's fundamental characteristic: predictability. Everything here is supposed to be documented, authorized, logged. An unexplained person is an error in the system, and errors here are rare enough to feel wrong.

The plateau doesn't stop them, though. You can't lock the stations effectively—anyone with basic tools can bypass the seals, and there's no security presence to enforce exclusion. So sometimes people exist here who aren't supposed to, and they do whatever they're doing, and they leave or don't, and the only record is a mention in a logbook: "Unknown individual at Station 73, refused identification, departed east. Equipment inventory complete, no losses."

## The Bacterial Mats

They are alive, technically, but calling them inhabitants feels generous. They occupy the water-filled depressions, the shallow basins in the stone where liquid accumulates and stagnates. They are primary producers, building organic molecules from the minerals in the water and the basalt, using chemical energy or the thin light that reaches them. They grow slowly—a mat might expand a few centimeters in a year—and they grow nowhere else, only in these isolated pools.

The colors are vivid against the gray stone: rust-red mats that might be iron-oxidizing bacteria, sulfur-yellow ones that smell faintly of rotten eggs, occasionally green ones that might be photosynthetic. The colors are intense, pure, almost artificial-looking in their saturation. The mats have a texture like wet felt, dense and slightly gelatinous. Touch one and it's cold, slick, unresponsive.

They serve as the base of local micro-ecosystems in the pools. Small fauna, millimeter-scale arthropod things, graze on the mats or on each other. The pools develop stratification: layers of different bacterial types at different depths, each adapted to specific chemistry, specific light levels. But these ecosystems are closed and tiny. They don't expand. They don't connect. Each pool is its own world, isolated by meters or kilometers of dry stone from the next.

They might be the planet's original inhabitants, life that predates the flood, adapted to existence in volcanic pools and now persisting in the only niches that resemble their original habitat. Or they might be recent colonizers, bacteria brought by the industrial operation, thriving in the artificial water sources created by spray and runoff. There's no way to tell from observation, and no one on the plateau has the expertise or equipment to analyze them properly.

## The Unseen Architects

Someone designed the operation. Someone decided to build pumping stations at these intervals, to run power lines across these distances, to extract water at this scale. Someone maintains the system at levels above the coordinators, making decisions about resource allocation, operational scope, long-term strategy.

These people are never present, never even mentioned directly in communications. They are inferred. They exist in the same way that gravity exists—as a force that shapes everything but is never directly observed.

The technicians don't think about them much. The work is the work; why it's being done is outside the scope of concern. But occasionally, in the longer conversations at the staffed stations, someone will speculate. "This is terraforming." "No, it's resource extraction for off-world export." "It's a research project that got out of hand and now sustains itself." "It's punishment. Prison labor. We're all here as alternative sentencing." The theories don't cohere. The evidence—the scale, the precision, the evident expense—suggests purpose, but the purpose remains opaque.

Perhaps the architects are also confused. Perhaps the operation has run long enough that its original intent has been forgotten, and now it continues because systems of this size develop inertia, become self-justifying. The infrastructure exists. The technicians are trained. The fauna provide a stable interface with the environment. The water is extracted, processed, distributed. Does it need a reason beyond its own existence?

The plateau doesn't answer. It just continues: stone and water and sound and the slow mechanical pulse of an operation too large to see whole, maintained by people who know only their part, serving purposes that may or may not exist beyond the bare fact of continuation. The inhabitants are less people living in a place than components in a system, organic parts of a machine, persisting not through choice but through the momentum of structure, the weight of procedure, the absence of compelling alternatives.

And underneath, always underneath: the sound. The industrial hum. The grinding and clicking. The hiss of pressure release. The sound that makes the plateau not a place you live but a place that lives in you, that fills you, that becomes the baseline state against which silence—if you ever encounter it again—will feel like absence, like loss, like something has been taken away.
