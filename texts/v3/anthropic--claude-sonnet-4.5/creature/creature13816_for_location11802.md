# What Lives There

In the oldest depressions—the ones where water has gathered and regathered through so many cycles that the stone itself remembers the pattern—live the *stillmouths*. They are the size of dinner plates, sometimes larger, flat bodies pressed against the basin floors like pale leather pancakes. They have no eyes because sight is a useless extravagance here, but they have that mouth, always open, a circular rim lined with fine filaments that wave gently in water that has no current. 

The filaments are not for filtering—or not only for filtering. They are chemoreceptors, taste-organs, proximity sensors. A stillmouth knows everything that enters its depression: the specific mineral content of today's condensation, the metabolic signature of every creature that drinks from its pool, the approach of a floating gasbody whose trailing filaments might be caught and consumed. The mouth doesn't close. It can't close. It is purely receptive, purely patient, a hole into a body that is mostly digestive tract wrapped in thin muscle.

They barely move. Over hours, a stillmouth might shift position by inches, repositioning itself under a particularly productive condensation point or away from a stone edge where the chemistry is wrong. They reproduce by fragmentation—a stillmouth growing too large simply tears itself, not violently, just gradually splits along a seam that forms over weeks, and both halves are complete, are wholes, each with their mouth and their patience and their flat leather body. There is no way to tell how old a stillmouth is. The largest might be decades old, or centuries, time measured in the slow accumulation of minerals in their tissues, in the depth of bacterial colonies in their skin.

When prey items come—and they do come, because the pools are the only drinking water, because the pools are gathering points, because even here there must be exchanges—the stillmouth does not strike. It is not built for striking. Instead, when something small enough swims or wades close to the filaments, the filaments adhere, gently, using a mucus that is part glue and part anesthetic and part something else, something that makes the prey not want to leave, that makes leaving feel like the wrong thing to do. And the prey, stuck lightly to the filaments, unable quite to remember why it wanted to move, drifts closer or is pulled closer—the distinction is philosophical—until it contacts the mouth proper, and the mouth accepts it with the gratitude of stone accepting water, and digestion begins, slow enough that the prey might live for hours inside the acidic warmth, its struggle gradually becoming not-struggle, its form gradually becoming nutrients.

Some stillmouths have scars on their backs—bite marks, scrapes, the signature of something larger that tried to eat them and found them unpalatable, or too tough, or wrong in some chemical way. The scars heal pale against pale, almost invisible. The stillmouths don't seem to mind them. They don't seem to mind anything. They are pure receptivity, pure slow hunger, embodied waiting.

In the air, among the floating gasbodies that drift patient and aimless, there are the *threadknots*. 

They are almost nothing: a central node no larger than a marble, from which extend dozens of fine filaments, each filament several feet long, each covered in microscopic hooks and chemical sensors. A threadknot drifts on the same thermals as the gasbodies, but unlike those creatures—simple things, plant-like in their passivity—the threadknots are hunters.

The central node contains a ganglion that might generously be called a brain, a cluster of nerve tissue capable of processing the chemical information from all those filaments, of coordinating them into something like action. When a threadknot's filaments brush against prey—a smaller floating organism, a crawling creature on a rare venture into the air, another threadknot—the filaments respond. Not quickly. Quick is not possible here, not sustainable. But deliberately.

The filaments wrap, slowly, tangling around the prey over minutes. The hooks engage, not deeply, but sufficiently. And then the threadknot begins to reel itself in, or reels the prey in, the filaments contracting with glacial patience, drawing the prey toward the central node, which opens—it can open, splits along hidden seams—to reveal a cavity lined with digestive enzymes and something like teeth, sharp calcium deposits that help tear flesh that has already begun to break down from the enzymes the filaments injected during the wrapping.

Feeding takes hours. The threadknot drifts while it feeds, its filaments contracted into a tangled ball around its meal, looking like a small floating burr, a seed-pod of some impossible plant. When it finishes, the filaments extend again, probing the darkness, and the indigestible remains—shell fragments, bones if the prey had bones, the hardened outer layers of other threadknots—are released, falling slowly through the humid air to join the detritus on the ground below.

Threadknots mate by entanglement. Two threadknots, brushing filaments, sometimes decide—through what decision-making process, who can say—to wrap each other, to pull close, to allow their central nodes to touch. They exchange genetic material through contact, through pores that open only for this purpose, and then they separate, drifting apart over hours, and weeks later each will fragment, the node splitting into multiple smaller nodes, each growing its own filaments, juvenile threadknots that begin their long, slow, drifting hunts.

They live for years, possibly decades. There is no way to know. They accumulate trace minerals in their tissues, grow slightly larger, grow slightly more filaments. An old threadknot might have a hundred filaments, each one a sensor and a snare, creating a sphere of influence ten feet across as it drifts, a slow-moving net that catches everything small and unlucky enough to touch it.

They make no sound. They have no eyes. They are pure touch and chemistry, pure patient predation, embodied hunger drifting through darkness on thermals you cannot feel.

On the surfaces—the rock faces, the plant stems, the wooden posts and concrete blocks—live the *scrapelings*.

They are the most numerous of the Condensery's fauna, the base of many food chains, the grazers who harvest bacterial films and the thin layers of algae-analog that grow wherever moisture condenses. They are small, most no larger than your thumbnail, with bodies like flattened slugs armored in chitin that weeps condensation from its surface in steady droplets. They have a foot, muscular and broad, that holds them to surfaces even upside-down, even on glass-smooth stone. And they have a radula, a tongue covered in tiny teeth, which they use to scrape their feeding grounds with methodical patience.

A scrapeling moves at speeds measured in inches per hour. It follows chemical trails—its own previous trails, the trails of others of its kind, the scent-signatures of optimal bacterial growth. It grazes in slow motion, its radula working across the stone, leaving a clean trail behind it where the bacterial film has been harvested down to the substrate. Behind the clean trail, the bacteria begin to regrow immediately, preparing for the scrapeling's return in days or weeks, the same path traversed over and over in cycles too long for you to observe.

They come in several varieties, though whether these are species or morphs or life stages is unclear:

The *mottled scrapelings* have shells patterned in gray and darker gray, camouflage that serves no obvious purpose in lightless environments but which they possess nonetheless, perhaps a holdover from some ancestral form, some earlier place. They are generalists, eating any bacterial film, any algae-analog, any organic residue.

The *clear scrapelings* are almost transparent, their shells like glass, their internal organs faintly visible as dark shapes. They specialize in feeding on the condensation itself, filtering dissolved nutrients directly from the water droplets that form on their shells, which they then absorb through specialized membranes. They move even slower than their mottled cousins, or sometimes don't move at all, simply existing as stationary filter-feeders, living condensation collectors.

The *ridged scrapelings* have shells with sharp parallel ridges running from head to tail, and these ridges serve a purpose: they scrape more aggressively, harvesting not just bacterial films but the stone itself, consuming mineral content along with organic matter. Their trails are deeper, actual grooves worn into the rock over time, and they are responsible for much of the weathering visible on older surfaces.

Scrapelings reproduce by broadcast spawning—once per year, or what passes for a year in their slow metabolisms, they all release gametes into the humid air simultaneously, responding to some cue imperceptible to outsiders, some shift in atmospheric chemistry or temperature or the electromagnetic hum of the posts. The gametes are microscopic, drift on air currents, meet randomly, settle on surfaces as larvae smaller than grains of salt. Most die. Some grow, slowly, reaching adult size over months or years.

They are eaten by everything. Every predator in the Condensery considers scrapelings a viable food source. They are too numerous to fully consume, too prolific to exterminate, too fundamental to the ecosystem to remove. They are the living interface between inorganic substrate and organic life, the creatures who turn stone and water and bacteria into flesh that other flesh can eat. Without them, the entire system would collapse, or would be different, or was never possible in the first place.

They are blind, deaf, almost without senses except for touch and chemistry. They do not know they are the foundation. They do not know anything except the immediate presence of food or its absence, the immediate safety of their shell or its violation. They scrape, they move, they spawn, they die and are eaten, and their shells accumulate in drifts at the bases of rocks, crunching underfoot with a sound like walking on gravel, like walking on the compressed history of millions of patient lives.

In the soil—the thin, young soil that barely deserves the name—live the *rootworms*.

Except they are not worms, not really, any more than they are roots. They are something else wearing a form that suggests both. A rootworm is segmented like an earthworm, muscular and flexible, able to move through soil by peristaltic contractions. But it has a hard head, a pointed tip made of hardened chitin or keratin or something similar, which it uses to bore through packed earth and soft stone. And it has tendrils—thin filaments that emerge from each segment, extending into the surrounding soil, drawing nutrients directly from the substrate like roots, like the mycorrhizal networks of fungi, except these networks are part of a single animal that might be thirty feet long, coiled and folded through the upper layers of soil in three-dimensional tangles.

They are ambush predators and filter feeders simultaneously, which should be contradictory but isn't. The tendrils absorb dissolved nutrients from the moisture-saturated soil, feeding the rootworm the way roots feed a plant. But the tendrils also sense vibration, sense the movements of other burrowing creatures—other rootworms, the larvae of things that will become something else, the deep-dwelling morphs of scrapelings that have adapted to subterranean life. When a tendril detects nearby prey, it releases a chemical signal, and the rootworm responds by growing that tendril longer, faster, guided by the chemical trail, until the tendril encounters the prey and pierces it, injecting digestive enzymes, liquefying the prey's insides, drawing the resulting slurry back through the tendril into the main body.

It happens slowly. A rootworm might detect prey and complete a kill over the course of hours. The prey, if it realizes what's happening, tries to move away, but the tendril pursues, flexible and relentless, growing at a rate that barely exceeds the prey's escape velocity. It is pursuit at geological pace, chase scenes that take half a day to resolve.

Rootworms grow continuously throughout their lives, adding segments, extending their tangles through ever-larger volumes of soil. They might live for decades, reaching lengths of fifty feet or more, their body-mass spread through cubic yards of earth, a distributed presence more than a discrete organism. They reproduce by fragmentation—a rootworm simply breaks apart into segments when it grows too large, when maintaining coordination across its length becomes untenable, and each segment with enough tendrils becomes a new individual, begins growing again, begins extending its network.

They have no brain, or rather, they have many small brains, a ganglion in each segment, all communicating through chemical signals that move slowly along the body. A rootworm's decisions are collective, emergent, the sum of its segments responding to local conditions. The front end doesn't always know what the back end is doing. Sometimes two ends of the same rootworm are pursuing different prey, or one end is fleeing from a predator while the other end is hunting. The contradictions resolve slowly, through chemical consensus, through the gradual alignment of segmented interests.

They create structure in the soil. Their movements aerate it, mix the layers, distribute nutrients. Their tendrils bind loose particles together, prevent erosion in areas where there is no erosion because there is no wind or water flow, but would be erosion if there were, and the rootworms are ready for that hypothetical, their tendrils holding everything together just in case.

When they die—from predation, from parasites, from the rare fungal infections that somehow find purchase in their tissues—they do not decay quickly. Their segments remain intact for weeks, months, slowly breaking down, becoming soil themselves, becoming the substrate through which their offspring will burrow. The tendrils persist longer, becoming tough fibers that give the soil a stringy texture in older areas, a resistance when you dig, as if you're excavating through ancient muscle tissue, which in a sense you are.

Among the plant-clusters, in the spaces between, moving with patience that exceeds even the Condensery's usual standards, live the *walkers*.

They are large compared to most fauna here—the size of cats, sometimes small dogs—and they are hexapedal, six legs arranged in radial symmetry around a central body, like starfish that learned to stand. But they are not echinoderms; they are something else, something that developed six-fold symmetry independently, for its own reasons. Each leg ends in three flexible digits, giving them eighteen contact points with the ground, spreading their weight, allowing them to move across the fragile thin soil without disturbing it, across the still water in the depressions without breaking the surface tension, across vertical rock faces without apparent effort.

Their bodies are soft, almost blob-like, pale flesh covered in mucus that glistens with condensation they cannot prevent and do not try to. They have six mouths, one at each vertex between pairs of legs, each mouth surrounded by short tentacles that probe the environment, that bring food to the opening: small fauna, scraped-off scrapelings, the bodies of dead things, plant matter when animal matter is scarce. They are omnivores of necessity, opportunivores, eating whatever their tentacles encounter during their slow patrols.

They have no eyes, but they have photoreceptors scattered across their skin, vestigial organs that can detect the faint foxfire glow of bacteria but provide no useful information in the absolute darkness elsewhere. Perhaps their ancestors lived in lighter conditions. Perhaps they are adapted for a future that hasn't arrived. Perhaps the photoreceptors serve some other function—detecting heat, detecting the electromagnetic fields of the humming posts, detecting something else entirely.

Each walker moves through a territory of roughly half an acre, a circuit that takes days to complete. They know their territories with intimate precision, know every rock placement and plant cluster and depression's depth, navigate by memory or by senses too subtle to identify. When two walkers' territories overlap—and they do overlap, at the edges, in neutral zones—the walkers sometimes meet. These meetings are prolonged, ritualized. The two walkers approach each other slowly, extend tentacles from all six mouths, touch tentacle-to-tentacle in combinations that might be communication or might be simply mutual examination. Sometimes they mate, tentacles intertwining for hours, exchanging genetic material through mechanisms that leave no external evidence. Sometimes they do not mate, simply touch, assess, and part, resuming their circuits.

Walkers carry ecosystems on their bodies. Their mucus hosts bacterial colonies distinct from the environmental bacteria, species that can survive only on walker-skin, that provide some benefit—perhaps they produce antibiotics that prevent fungal infection, perhaps they synthesize nutrients the walker cannot produce internally, perhaps they are simply tolerated passengers. Small scrapelings graze on the walkers' backs as they walk, eating the bacterial films, and the walkers allow this, do not dislodge them, might benefit from the cleaning service or might simply not notice.

They reproduce rarely, perhaps once in their lives, perhaps less. A walker that has mated will eventually—weeks or months later—extrude a mass of eggs from a pore on its dorsal surface, hundreds of them in a gelatinous matrix. The walker finds a suitable depression, deposits the egg-mass in shallow water, and abandons it. The eggs develop over months, through larval stages that look nothing like adults, that float briefly in the water before settling to the bottom and metamorphosing into tiny six-legged crawlers that will spend years growing, moving outward from their birthplace, establishing their own territories in the spaces between adult territories, patient as everything else here.

The walkers are among the few fauna that seem capable of something like curiosity. When you stand still long enough, a walker will sometimes approach, extend tentacles to touch your boots, your legs, your hands if you offer them. The tentacles are cool and wet and surprisingly strong, gripping gently, exploring texture and chemistry. The walker learns you, decides you are not food, not threat, not mate, not anything it has categories for, and eventually withdraws, resumes its circuit, untroubled by your presence but not ignoring it either. You have become a feature of its territory, temporary, noted, incorporated into the mental or chemical map it maintains.

When they die—and they must die, eventually, though you never find fresh corpses—they must be consumed quickly and completely by something, because there are no walker skeletons, no preserved bodies. Perhaps other walkers eat them, recycling their nutrients. Perhaps the rootworms below drag them down into the soil. Perhaps they dissolve, their soft bodies breaking down rapidly once the living processes cease. Or perhaps old walkers simply walk away from their territories, seeking some place at the Condensery's edge, some boundary you haven't reached, where they go to die beyond observation, beyond discovery, maintaining their privacy even in death.

In the concrete building with the rusted-away door, in the darkness thicker than outside darkness, in the two inches of utterly still water, lives something the fauna avoid.

You cannot see it—you cannot see anything in there—but you hear it breathing. Slow breaths, each one lasting thirty seconds or more: a long inhalation that draws air across water with barely audible ripples, a pause, a long exhalation that fogs the interior (you imagine) with warmth and moisture and breath that smells of ozone and metal and something organic, something old. The breathing is regular as a clock but slower than any clock you'd want to watch.

What breathes in there is large, or occupies significant space, or exists in a way that makes breathing a major project requiring its full attention. The breathing does not change when you enter, does not pause or accelerate. Either it doesn't notice you or it noticed you from much farther away and has already decided you're not interesting, not threatening, not relevant to whatever purposes it maintains.

Other fauna know it's there. You can tell by their absence—within a hundred feet of the building, the density drops dramatically. Scrapelings are scarce on nearby surfaces. Threadknots drift around the area rather than through it. Even the rootworms below seem to route their tendril-networks away from the building's foundation, leaving a cylinder of undisturbed soil beneath and around it.

The building contains machinery remnants: empty conduit, junction boxes with their covers missing, concrete mounting blocks for equipment long removed. But it also contains water, that shallow layer covering the floor, and the water is warmer than the water in the depressions outside. Something heats it, or something in the water generates heat through metabolism or through chemical processes or through mechanisms less easily categorized.

You consider the possibilities:

It might be a creature that has claimed the building as its den, something large enough and patient enough to spend its time breathing in darkness, waiting for prey to enter or for some other condition to be met. Something that was here before the machinery was removed, or that moved in after, finding the space suitable for reasons of its own.

It might be something that was part of the Condensery's operations, something bred or created or invited to serve a function—biological processing of water, thermal regulation, something that required a large living component. And when the Condensery stopped, it remained, still performing its function out of habit or instinct or because stopping was never part of its design, patient as the humming posts.

It might be a person, or what remains of a person, someone who worked here and stayed when the others left, or stayed because they couldn't leave, or stayed because leaving seemed less desirable than remaining. Changed by time or by the place or by isolation or by choice, breathing slowly in the darkness, waiting for the Condensery to resume operations, waiting for instructions that will never come, waiting simply because waiting is what they know how to do now.

It might be something less discrete than a single creature, a colony or collective, many organisms working in sufficient coordination to produce a single breathing pattern, a emergent property of bacterial mats or rootworm tangles or something else, something that has filled the building and learned to breathe together, to function as a unit, to occupy the abandoned space with patient inevitability.

It might be the building itself, or some property of the building, some interaction between the water and the air and the residual electromagnetic fields from the defunct machinery, creating a phenomenon that mimics breathing, that produces warmth and ozone and the sense of presence without requiring an actual organism at all. The Condensery bends other rules; why not this one?

You don't investigate further. Some knowledge feels less useful than uncertainty. The breathing continues behind you as you leave, and ahead of you as you walk away, and possibly around you in all directions, because sound behaves strangely here, because the building might be one of many, because the breathing thing might be everywhere, might be the Condensery itself achieving some low level of collective respiration, the whole place breathing through its scattered structures, its abandoned stations, its thickness and depth and patient strangeness.

Other fauna live their lives around it, avoiding it, accepting it, incorporating its presence into their territories and cycles. It has been there for years, decades perhaps. It will be there for years more. It breathes, and that is what you know, and that might be all there is to know, or all you're capable of knowing, or all that's safe to know.

Deeper in the soil than the rootworms, at the boundary between thin earth and parent stone, live the *stone-eaters*.

They are barely animals at all, more like motile rocks, rough spheres the size of golf balls or baseballs, their surfaces encrusted with minerals they've consumed and incorporated into shells so hard they're effectively inorganic. They move through stone—not around it, through it—by secreting acids that dissolve the rock ahead of them, creating narrow passages they seal behind them with excreted minerals, with their own waste products compressed and hardened.

They are excavating the Condensery from below, not intentionally, not with any purpose you could identify, but as a consequence of their metabolism. They eat stone—the young basalt, the fresh rock—and extract from it trace minerals, rare elements, chemical energy locked in crystalline structures. The bulk of the rock, the silicon and oxygen and common elements, they excrete, compressing it into denser forms, fusing it through body-heat and chemical processes into new configurations.

The passages they leave behind are smooth-walled tunnels inches in diameter, running in three dimensions through the upper bedrock, intersecting and diverging in networks that might be random or might follow chemical gradients or electromagnetic lines imperceptible at the surface. In cross-section, these tunnels would form patterns, mazes, labyrinths, but there is no cross-section, no way to see the full extent except by inference, except by drilling cores that reveal passage after passage after passage, the stone riddled with them, honeycombed, structurally compromised or structurally reinforced depending on how you interpret the dense material filling some passages, the reinforcing lattices the stone-eaters inadvertently create.

They reproduce by fission—when a stone-eater accumulates too much mass, grows too large for efficient movement, it simply splits along an internal seam, becomes two smaller individuals, each taking half the parent's shell and beginning to grow their own. They might live for centuries; there's no way to know. Their metabolisms are so slow, their movements so gradual, that watching one make progress would require observations spanning years. They might tunnel a foot per decade, or faster, or slower, depending on the stone's chemistry, on their internal state, on factors that remain mysterious.

They are eaten by nothing, or by very few things. Their shells are too hard, their bodies too mineral, their internal chemistry too toxic with concentrated metals and exotic compounds. When they die—and they do die, eventually, perhaps from accumulated heavy metals, perhaps from mechanical failure of their biological systems—they simply stop moving, become permanent parts of the stone, indistinguishable from the passages they created except for a slight variation in density, a shadow in the rock that might be a fossil or might be a stone that was briefly alive.

The Condensery's surface, its depressions and gentle rises, might be partly consequence of the stone-eaters' work below, the tunnels creating weaknesses that allow subtle subsidence, the reinforced areas resisting erosion that isn't happening yet but might happen someday. The place is being slowly transformed from below, reshaped by creatures that experience the world only as chemical gradients in stone, that know nothing of air or water or the abundant fauna above, that are themselves halfway between life and geology, barely decisively on one side of that boundary.

In the rare deeper pools, the ones that have persisted long enough to develop actual depth, actual ecosystems, live the *silt-swimmers*.

They are elongated, eel-like, with frilled fins running the length of their bodies that undulate in patterns too complex to follow, each fin-ray moving independently, creating propulsion through water so still it might as well be gelatin. They are blind—eyes are useless in pool water black as the air above it—but they have lateral lines, pressure-sensitive organs along their sides that detect the tiniest movements, the presence of other creatures, the slowly settling silt that gives them their name.

They feed on the silt, or rather on what lives in the silt: bacteria, microscopic fauna, the dissolved organics that accumulate in pool sediments over months and years. A silt-swimmer descends to the bottom—and these pools can be surprisingly deep, ten feet or more in the oldest depressions—and positions itself mouth-down in the sediment. It creates a current through internal pumping, drawing silty water through its mouth, filtering it through gill-rakers that capture particles, expelling the cleaned water through gill slits. It feeds like this for hours, barely moving, a living pump, a biological filter converting sediment into flesh.

They are hermaphroditic, each individual possessing both male and female reproductive organs, and they mate by spiraling together in the water column, rising slowly from bottom to surface in intertwined helices, releasing eggs and sperm simultaneously in clouds that mix and fertilize in the still water. The fertilized eggs sink, settle into sediment, develop there over weeks into larvae that look nothing like adults, that crawl on rudimentary legs through the muck before metamorphosing into the elongated swimming form.

They are preyed upon by the walkers, who will occasionally wade into deeper pools and use their tentacle-mouths to probe the water, capturing silt-swimmers by touch alone, by the vibrations the swimmers produce despite their slow movements. They are preyed upon by larger silt-swimmers—they are cannibalistic when other food is scarce, the largest individuals consuming smaller ones with indifference, perhaps not recognizing them as kin, perhaps recognizing them but being unburdened by sentiment.

Some pools contain dozens of silt-swimmers, dense populations feeding in layers, the larger individuals near the bottom where the sediment is richest, smaller ones higher in the water column filtering whatever escapes the lower layers. Other pools contain only one or two, solitary individuals claiming entire pools as territory, attacking intruders with surprising violence, biting with small sharp teeth, driving rivals away or consuming them if they're small enough.

The pools are worlds unto themselves, isolated ecosystems connected to the larger Condensery only by the water that seeps in through stone, by the occasional fauna that falls in and drowns and becomes food, by the rare overflow events when a pool fills beyond capacity and spills across the surface for hours before the excess seeps away. The silt-swimmers are adapted to these isolated worlds, spending their entire lives in single pools, or in the network of pools within a small area connected by subsurface channels the stone-eaters inadvertently created, channels that allow silt-swimmers to move between pools, to find mates, to colonize new depressions.

They make sounds, rare among Condensery fauna: clicks produced by snapping their jaws, used perhaps for echolocation, for mapping their pools in acoustic detail, for communication with other silt-swimmers. The clicks are sharp, brief, carrying through still water more effectively than through air, creating a sporadic acoustic landscape in the deeper pools, a sense that the water itself is speaking, that the darkness below is different from the darkness above, more populated, more aware.

And there are the *bloom-mites*, microscopic almost, living on the surfaces of the pale plants, so small and numerous they appear as a texture, a roughness on the otherwise smooth leaves and stems.

They are arachnid-analogs, eight-legged, with bodies like translucent spheres barely visible even when you look directly at them, which you can't in the darkness, so they remain mostly hypothetical, known by inference, by the damage they do to plants, by the tiny webs they spin between leaves, by the shed exoskeletons that accumulate in plant crevices like dust.

They feed on the plants' surface cells, piercing them with needle-like mouthparts, drawing out cell contents, leaving behind desiccated husks. But they feed slowly, taking only what the plant can regenerate, maintaining a balance. A plant covered in bloom-mites is not damaged plant but a plant in partnership, because the mites also graze on fungal spores and bacterial invaders that would otherwise harm the plant, and they produce waste that the plant absorbs as fertilizer, and they regulate the condensation on the plant's surface, their tiny movements breaking up water droplets, preventing fungal growth in the humid stillness.

They reproduce explosively when conditions permit: when a plant flowers (and some do flower, rarely, extrusions of colorless petals that produce scent in lieu of visual display), the bloom-mites synchronize their breeding, producing generations that mature in days rather than weeks, populations that bloom like their namesake, covering the flowers in moving carpets of microscopic bodies that assist in pollination, that carry pollen between plants on their legs and backs, that enable sexual reproduction in flora that might otherwise be limited to clonal growth.

They are eaten by specialized scrapelings that have evolved to harvest them, that scrape across plant surfaces consuming mites by the hundreds. They are eaten by the smallest threadknots, juveniles whose filaments are fine enough to catch individual mites, to wrap them and consume them one at a time in feeding sessions that must seem endless, that must require capturing thousands of mites to satisfy minimal nutritional needs.

They are short-lived, most dying after weeks, but their populations persist across generations, across years, colonies that might be millennia old even as individual mites live and die in quick succession. They create continuity through reproduction, through the constancy of their presence, through the ecological role they fill so completely that the plants depend on them, that removing the mites would doom the plants, would cascade through the ecosystem, would change the Condensery fundamentally.

They are invisible to casual observation but constitute significant biomass when summed across all surfaces, all plants, all the square miles of the Condensery. They are among the most numerous animals here, perhaps outnumbering even the scrapelings when counted individually, though their total mass is less, their individual insignificance profound. They live lives too small and fast for the Condensery's usual timescale, exist in their own temporal pocket, quick things in a slow place, necessary things that go unnoticed, that do their work in darkness on surfaces in darkness, under skies that aren't skies, in air that's turning to water, patient in their own frantic way, waiting in their own immediate urgency.

---

There are others, of course. There must be others.

In the spaces you haven't explored, in the variations you glimpsed but didn't investigate, in the areas where the weirdness concentrates, where reality bends more severely, there are fauna adapted to conditions you haven't witnessed, forms that don't survive in the regions you've walked through.

There are flying things that never land, that spend their entire lives airborne, feeding on the floating organisms, mating in flight, dying in flight and decomposing as they fall, their bodies consumed by specialists before they reach the ground.

There are parasites inside other fauna, living inside the walkers and the silt-swimmers and even inside the rootworms' distributed bodies, eating their hosts slowly or living in careful symbiosis, providing benefits or simply being tolerated, evolving alongside their hosts in intimate coevolution.

There are scavengers that appear only when something dies, that detect death from distances impossible in the stillness, that arrive in numbers to consume the corpse, to recycle it, to disappear again until the next death, living in the spaces between, in the gaps, emerging only when opportunity presents.

There are things in the deeper stone, below the stone-eaters, that live at pressures and temperatures that would sterilize surface life, that constitute a separate ecosystem, a buried Condensery under the Condensery, connected by rare passages, by seepage, by mechanisms unknown.

There are the machine-dwellers, fauna that have claimed the abandoned stations, that nest in the junction boxes and conduit, that have incorporated metal and ceramic into their bodies or their nests, that might be part of the machinery's attempt to restart, or might be preventing its restart, or might be irrelevant to its status, simply coexisting.

There are the products of the weirdness itself, things that shouldn't exist but do because reality here permits them, things with biologies that violate thermodynamics or conservation laws or causation itself, things that are alive in ways that don't map to standard definitions, that breed true despite impossibility, that have been here as long as anything else and might be more native than the apparently-natural fauna.

And there are the observers, perhaps, things that watch, that track movements through their territories, that maintain awareness of the whole system or large parts of it, that might be coordinating the ecology or might simply be witnessing it, intelligence of some sort, distributed or centralized, biological or otherwise, present in the breathing of the building, in the humming of the posts, in the patient movements of the largest walkers, in patterns you can't perceive because you're moving too fast, living too quickly, thinking in the wrong timeframe.

---

The fauna of the Condensery are adapted not just to darkness and moisture and the sparse resources, but to the strangeness itself, to the flexible reality. They incorporate it, perhaps unconsciously, perhaps as unconsciously as they incorporate oxygen or temperature. They move through the spaces where distances are uncertain. They feed in the pockets where time runs differently. They are diverse beyond what the apparent resources should support, filling niches so specific and overlapping that the ecosystem seems more complex than it should be, more layered, more intricate, as if the abundance of strangeness allows for abundance of life, as if weirdness itself is a resource they exploit, a factor in their equations of survival.

They are beautiful, perhaps, in their adaptations, in their patient solutions to difficult problems, in the way they've made lives in this place, in the intricate dependencies and predations and partnerships they've evolved. Beautiful in the way deep-sea creatures are beautiful, in the way extremophiles are beautiful, in the way anything that persists against odds is beautiful, even if you can't see them clearly, even if you see them only in fragments and inferences, even if beauty isn't quite the right word but no better word exists for what you feel observing them in their slow dances, their patient cycles, their dense abundance in the warm wet dark.

They are the Condensery made flesh, made life, made movement. They are what happens when biology meets a place like this and refuses to retreat, adapts instead, claims the space, fills it, makes it home across generations, across species, across kingdoms of life, until the place and the life become inseparable, become aspects of the same system, breathing together at geological pace, metabolizing darkness and strangeness and condensation into continuation, into persistence, into the quiet ongoing fact of being alive here, now, in the depths of the patient dark, where the water forms constantly and the fauna wait and feed and breed and die and become food for others, and the cycle proceeds, and the Condensery continues, inhabited, populated, alive with life that has learned to live here, that knows no other way, that persists.
