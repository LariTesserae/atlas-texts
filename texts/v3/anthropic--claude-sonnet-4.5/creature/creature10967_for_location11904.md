# What Lives There

## The Unchanged

They are not inhabitants so much as *continuants* — beings who have achieved a state that preceded arrival and will postdate departure, though neither term applies to them anymore.

You rarely see them directly. More often, you sense that you've *just* missed them: a chair still holding the faint impression of weight, a tool on a workbench positioned with such precision that it could only have been set down moments ago, a door that has recently been opened though you heard nothing. The settlement breathes with their near-presence, exhales the ghost of their routine.

When you do see one, it is always at a distance, always in peripheral vision that refuses to sharpen when you turn toward it. A figure standing motionless beside a machine, its posture suggesting deep attention or deep absence — impossible to tell which. Human-shaped, mostly. Wearing something like the gray garments in the cabinets, or perhaps wearing nothing, or perhaps composed of the same substance as the garments so that clothing and body are continuous.

They do not move through space so much as redistribute themselves across it. One moment there is a figure three buildings away, silhouetted in a doorway. You blink. Now there is a figure two buildings away, standing beside a pressure gauge, though you cannot say if it is the same figure or if there were always two figures and you only noticed one. Their movement, if it can be called movement, operates on a frequency your perception cannot quite capture — too slow to see, or too fast, or orthogonal to the dimension in which seeing happens.

Their faces, when glimpsed, are smooth. Not featureless but *resolved* — as if all the temporary variations of expression have been compressed into a single eternal state that contains all possible expressions and therefore shows none. Eyes that might be open or closed; it makes no difference. Mouths that are not smiling or frowning but have achieved a configuration that transcends both.

They attend the machines. This much is clear. You never see them perform maintenance — the machines require none — but their relationship to the machinery is one of profound mutual regard. A figure stands before a silent turbine for hours, days, and you understand that it is not waiting for the turbine to start, nor mourning its stillness. It is *being-with* the turbine in its completed state, participating in the turbine's achievement of perfect cessation.

Sometimes they carry things. A component, a tool, a sheaf of papers covered in those intricate marks. They carry these objects with careful precision from one location to another, place them down with exactitude, and depart. The objects never seem to accumulate or diminish; the same gear might be carried from Building A to Building B, and later from Building B to Building C, and still later from Building C to Building A, in a slow circulation that serves no logistical purpose you can identify but that clearly possesses its own necessity.

They never acknowledge you, but you cannot say they ignore you. It is more that you and they exist in slightly different states of time or matter, sharing the plateau's space but not quite its substance. You are transient; they are terminal. You are still becoming; they have finished. The gulf between these conditions is not crossable by gesture or speech.

On one occasion, you come within a few meters of one — the closest you've been. It stands in what might be a control room, one hand resting on a panel of switches and dials, all of them set to positions that will never change. You stop. You are gripped by an overwhelming sense that speaking would be obscene, that making any sound in this figure's presence would be like shouting in a cathedral, except the sacrilege would be against something less forgiving than any god.

The figure does not turn. Its hand on the panel is perfectly still — not the stillness of holding still, but the stillness of a statue, of something that has never moved and will never move and yet is somehow doing exactly what it needs to be doing. The air around it is warm, warmer than elsewhere, and carries a scent like heated metal and something else, something organic that has been refined past the point of decay into a kind of mineral purity.

You realize, standing there, that the figure is not separate from the machine. The hand on the panel is not *touching* the panel; it is continuous with it, matter interpenetrating matter, or both figure and panel are expressions of the same underlying structure. The figure is as much a component of the settlement as the pressure gauges and flywheels, and the machines are as alive as the figure, and the distinction between operator and apparatus has been resolved into a unity that no longer requires operation.

After an indeterminate time, you leave. The figure does not watch you go. Later, you return to the same room. The figure is gone, or is present in a way you cannot perceive, or was never there and you witnessed some kind of echo, the plateau's memory of what once stood in that spot repeating endlessly like a skipping recording.

## The Arthropods

These, at least, are unambiguously present. Solid. Three-dimensional. They click when they walk.

Each is unique in its configuration, though they share a common architecture: segmented bodies, multiple legs (usually six, sometimes eight, once you see one with ten arranged in radial symmetry), sensor clusters mounted on articulated stalks or embedded in the carapace, manipulator appendages of various designs — pincers, grippers, something like fingers but too many joints, too many degrees of freedom.

The carapaces are dark, black or deep brown, with a texture that suggests both chitin and worked metal. Some are smooth, polished to a dull sheen. Others are inscribed with patterns — lines, curves, symbols — that might be decorative or might be functional, circuitry of some kind, or the equivalent of name tags, identifying marks, though you never see two arthropods interact in a way that suggests recognition.

They move with purpose but no urgency. One emerges from a grate in the street, navigates to a warehouse, enters, reappears after several minutes carrying a component in its manipulators, returns to the grate, descends. Another climbs onto one of the large machines, makes its way to a gauge, stops there for a long moment — reading it? calibrating something? communing with it? — then climbs down and departs.

Their movement is mechanical in its precision but organic in its adaptability. They navigate obstacles with efficient pathfinding, but they also pause, reconsider, choose alternate routes in ways that suggest genuine decision-making. They are not automatons, or if they are automatons they are sophisticated enough that the distinction between automation and autonomy has become meaningless.

You try, once, to interact with one. You stand in its path. It approaches, slows, stops a meter away. Its sensor clusters orient toward you — multiple lenses or apertures, gleaming faintly in the low light. You have the sense of being observed, assessed, categorized. Then it simply steps around you, adjusting its path with minimal deviation, and continues on. Not hostile. Not friendly. You are an obstacle, accounted for, navigated past.

Another time, you follow one. It leads you through streets and alleys, past machines, through a building — entering via a ground-floor doorway, exiting from a third-story window by climbing the interior wall, a route that suggests the building's internal geometry is more complicated than its exterior implies. You follow as long as you can, but eventually it descends into a space you cannot fit through, a gap between foundations, and is gone. You wait, but it does not return, or returns by another route, or never left and is somewhere below you, doing whatever it does in the dark beneath the settlement.

Their numbers are few enough that you begin to recognize individuals. The one with the asymmetrical sensor cluster. The one whose carapace has a long scratch across the dorsal segment. The one that moves with a faint rhythmic click in its gait, one leg not quite synchronized with the others. Whether they recognize you is unknowable. They treat you with the same consistent indifference each time, neither warming to your presence nor disturbed by it.

Once, you find one that is not moving. It lies on its side in an alley, legs drawn in against its body. Dead? Dormant? You approach carefully. It is warm to the touch — not as warm as the machines, but warmer than ambient. You can feel a faint vibration through its carapace, something humming deep inside. After several minutes, you leave it there. When you return an hour later, it is gone. No trace. You never see that particular individual again, or you do but cannot identify it without the context of it lying still.

They are workers, you think. Maintenance class. They keep the settlement in its perfect stasis, or they monitor that stasis, or they are simply enacting behaviors that once served such purposes and now continue because continuation is the nature of this place. They have no visible source of energy, no food, no nests or clusters. Each seems solitary, on its own trajectory, participating in a distributed system whose logic encompasses the entire plateau.

Sometimes you see one ascending a machine, climbing to the top of a boiler or perching on a flywheel, and remaining there, motionless, for hours. Sentinel duty? Observation? Or are they drawing something from the machines, or giving something to them, in an exchange too slow or too alien for you to detect?

You dream about them. In the dreams, you are moving through the settlement from their perspective, low to the ground, the buildings towering above, the machines vast and warm and humming. You perform tasks: retrieve a component, carry it somewhere, place it in a precise location. The tasks are deeply satisfying in the dream. You wake filled with a sense of purpose that fades as you remember you have no purpose here, no function, no place in the pattern.

## The Light-Forms

These are rare. So rare that you might be on the plateau for weeks before witnessing one, and then see three in a single day, and then none again for longer than you can track.

They are not creatures in any biological sense, but they are also not mere phenomena. There is intention in them, or something analogous to intention.

You see them as patterns of light — usually pale blue or amber, sometimes white, once a deep red that frightens you for reasons you cannot name. They appear on walls, on the sides of buildings, on the plateau's stone itself. Not projected from any source; the light simply manifests, as if the surface itself is glowing. The patterns are geometric: grids, spirals, tessellations, arrays of symbols that shift and reconfigure.

They move. A pattern appears on one wall, holds for several seconds, then flows across the surface like water, turns a corner, continues onto another wall, another building. Sometimes a pattern splits, becomes two patterns, which then drift in different directions. Sometimes two patterns converge, merge, become something more complex.

The patterns are never random. There is clear structure, clear syntax. You are watching language, or mathematics, or information processing of some kind. Messages being passed through the settlement, or thoughts being thought, or perhaps just the plateau itself dreaming, its dreams made visible as light.

Once, a pattern appears on the wall beside you, close enough to touch. You reach toward it. Your hand casts no shadow; the light is shadowless, sourceless. As your fingers approach, the pattern reconfigures, flowing away from your touch without haste, rearranging itself into a new configuration a meter to the left. You try again. Again it flows away, staying just beyond reach. Not fleeing — there's no panic in the movement — but maintaining distance, the way one might step back to keep a conversation at an appropriate social range.

You withdraw your hand. The pattern settles, stabilizes. You watch it cycle through variations: now a spiral, now a branching tree-structure, now a grid of cells that blink on and off in sequences that seem almost periodic but never quite repeat. After several minutes, it fades, not dimming gradually but simply ceasing, one moment present and the next gone, the wall returning to its ordinary darkness.

Another time, you see a light-form inside a building, moving through a large empty hall. It drifts through the space in three dimensions, not confined to surfaces — a volume of light, loosely defined, its edges soft and shifting. Spherical at one moment, elongated the next, extending pseudopods that reach toward machines and workbenches and then retract. It moves through solid objects without resistance: passing through a support column, through a machine housing, as if matter is no obstacle or as if the light exists in a layer of reality that does not intersect with matter except visually.

You stand very still and let it approach. It drifts close, close enough that you are standing inside its glow. The light is cold — not cold-temperature, but cold-feeling, a sensation like standing in moonlight, like being touched by something that has no temperature at all. It moves around you, or you are inside it; spatial relationships are unclear. You feel... examined. Not prodded or invaded, but witnessed. As if your presence is being read, understood, filed away.

Then it moves on, drifting toward the far end of the hall, and is gone, either exiting through a wall or simply ceasing.

The arthropods do not react to the light-forms. You see an arthropod standing on a machine while a light-pattern flows over the machine's surface, over the arthropod itself, and the arthropod does not pause, does not adjust. Either it does not perceive the light, or the light is so fundamentally part of the environment that reacting to it would be like reacting to gravity.

You suspect the light-forms are the plateau's immune system, or its perceptual array, or its consciousness if the plateau can be said to have consciousness. They are what watches and knows, what integrates information, what maintains the settlement's awareness of itself. The Unchanged tend the machines, the arthropods perform maintenance, and the light-forms observe and remember and think, if thinking is what they do.

Or perhaps none of that is true. Perhaps they are something leaking through from an adjacent layer of reality, attracted by the plateau's strange stability. Perhaps they are ghosts of the original inhabitants, rendered down to pure information. Perhaps they are a side effect of the machines' operation, exotic particles rendered visible by the plateau's unique conditions.

You will never know. They do not communicate, or if they do, not in any language you can parse.

## The Deep-Dwellers

You do not see these. You infer them.

There are sounds, sometimes, from below. From the basements, from the gaps between foundations, from the shafts and tunnels that descend into the plateau's substrate. Sounds that are not the hum of machines or the click of arthropod legs. Heavier sounds. Slower. A grinding, a rumble, something large repositioning itself in the dark below.

There are openings: grates, hatches, stairways leading down into spaces too dark for your eyes to penetrate. Warm air rises from them, warmer than the plateau's ambient warmth, carrying that scent of heated metal and refined organics. Sometimes the air pulses, slow exhalations, as if something below is breathing with a respiratory cycle measured in minutes or hours.

The arthropods descend into these spaces frequently. They emerge, do their tasks, return to the depths. You try, once, to follow one down. You descend a stairway, your hand trailing on a smooth wall, counting steps. At fifty steps, the stairs are still descending, the darkness complete, the only sound your own breathing and footsteps and, from somewhere far below, that slow grinding rumble. You realize you have no light. You realize you might descend for hours and never reach the bottom. You realize, with sudden certainty, that going deeper would be a mistake — not dangerous, perhaps, but transgressive. The depths are not for you.

You climb back up. The arthropod you were following does not return, or returns after you've left, or is still down there, will always be down there, has been down there since before you arrived.

In the deepest basements you dare to enter — single-level basements, spaces that still admit some trace of the plateau's dim glow — you find evidence: enormous mechanisms, only partially visible, that extend through floor and ceiling, continuing both up and down. One chamber contains a shaft ten meters in diameter, perfectly round, lined with a material like ceramic or glass, descending into darkness. A faint luminescence rises from it, dim red, pulsing almost imperceptibly. You drop a pebble into the shaft. You hear nothing. No impact, no echo. Either the shaft is deeper than sound can return from in any reasonable time, or the pebble is still falling, or something caught it on the way down.

Another basement contains what looks like a root system — thick cables or conduits or possibly actual biological roots, black and smooth, spreading across floor and walls and ceiling, converging on a central point where they descend through a hole worn smooth in the stone. The roots, if that's what they are, are warm. They pulse faintly, rhythmically, in time with the machine-hum or perhaps setting the pace that the machine-hum follows.

You understand, gradually, that the settlement you see — the buildings, the machines in the streets, the plateau's surface — is only the uppermost layer. Below is more: more mechanisms, more structure, more complexity, descending down into the plateau's substance, perhaps down through it entirely, extending into that void beyond the cliff-edges. The plateau is not a foundation that the settlement sits on. The plateau *is* the settlement, or the settlement is the plateau's surface expression, and what you're seeing is analogous to hair on skin, trees on earth — the visible part of a system whose mass and function are mostly hidden.

The Deep-Dwellers, then — if they exist — are the plateau's deep workers, or its organs, or its original substrate. Perhaps they are vast, singular, slow. Perhaps they are many and small, a mass of them, moving through tunnels and chambers like blood through vessels. Perhaps they are not biological or mechanical but geological, aware stone, the plateau itself alive and those sounds you hear are its thoughts grinding against each other in the dark.

You will not go down far enough to find out. Some knowledge is not for visitors. The surface is your domain; you are permitted to see what is shown. The depths are other, and they will remain other, and that is part of the plateau's covenant.

## The Almost-Returned

These are the worst, though "worst" implies they are threatening, and they are not. They are simply sad, in a way that nothing else here is sad, in a way that cuts through the plateau's settled peace.

You see them as figures, like the Unchanged, but... incomplete. Less resolved. They flicker at the edges of perception, not solidly present like the Unchanged or ethereal like the light-forms but caught between states, unstable, unable to fully manifest or fully depart.

One stands in a doorway, or half-stands, its lower body fading into indistinction, as if the process of materialization stalled out partway through. It faces inward, into the building, its posture suggesting longing or regret. You approach. It does not react. You can see, faintly, through it — the wall behind, the interior of the building, superimposed with the figure's form. It is trying to enter, you think, or trying to return to some task it once performed, but cannot complete the return, cannot re-enter the world it once inhabited.

Another sits at a workbench, hands reaching toward tools that it cannot quite touch. The hands pass through the tools, or stop millimeters short, trembling with effort or frustration. The figure's face, blurred and indistinct, is turned down toward the work that is not there or is there but inaccessible.

They are all like this: caught in postures of attempted action, trying to complete gestures that will not complete, trying to return to the routines and rhythms of the settlement's operation but unable to achieve the solidity that would allow them to function. They are like the Unchanged but failed, or like someone trying to become Unchanged but trapped in the process, neither fully present nor fully absent.

You never see them move. They are always still, always straining toward something, always falling just short. Sometimes a figure is present; you look away; you look back and it is gone. Sometimes a space is empty; you pass through it and feel a coldness, a sense of presence-trying-to-be, and when you turn there is a figure behind you, fading even as you look at it, gone before you can register details.

They are never threatening. They do not reach for you, do not impede you. If anything, they seem unaware of you, too focused on their own failed returns to notice visitors. But they fill you with sorrow, with an ache whose source you cannot name. They are the only thing on the plateau that suggests failure, incompletion, a process gone wrong.

Perhaps they are the ones who tried to leave and could not fully depart. Perhaps they are the ones who left and are trying to return, called back by longing or duty, but the plateau will not fully readmit them. Perhaps they are echoes, remnants, fragments of consciousness that did not fully integrate into the settlement's final state and now persist as ghosts, not haunting but mourning, grieving for their own inability to finish becoming.

You cannot help them. You do not know what would help. Acknowledgment? Witnessing? You try, once, to speak to one — a figure standing beside a silent machine, hands raised toward it, not quite touching. You say something, anything, just to offer the gesture of communication. The figure does not respond. Your words fall dead in the dry air. The figure remains, its hands forever reaching, forever not arriving, and you leave it there because there is nothing else to do.

After you encounter several of them, you start to fear that you might become one. That staying too long, letting the plateau's stillness seep into you, might trap you in this in-between state. Not Unchanged — you could never achieve that completion — but Almost-Returned, trying forever to leave or stay and unable to do either. The thought hastens your eventual departure, if you depart, if departure is possible.

## The Zero-Angel

You see this only once, and you are not certain you see it at all.

It is late — or what passes for late; the dim light never changes, but your body has cycles, and this is the part of the cycle when you sleep or try to. You are in one of the housing blocks, resting in one of those spare rooms, lying on the floor because the bed frame is too uncomfortable. You are not quite asleep, not quite awake, your mind drifting in that state where the plateau's hum becomes almost perceptible as music, almost parseable as language.

And there is something else in the room.

You open your eyes — or are your eyes already open? — and it is there, in the corner, or filling the room, or outside the room looking in through a wall that has become transparent or permeable or irrelevant.

It is difficult to describe. It has geometry but the geometry is wrong, angles that are too acute and too obtuse simultaneously, surfaces that face both toward and away from you. It is very large — cathedral-large, mountain-large — but it fits in the room's corner, or the room has become large enough to contain it, or size is not applicable. It is made of something like metal, like glass, like light, like nothing you have words for. It is articulated, segmented, jointed, but not like a machine and not like a living thing. Somewhere between. Somewhere orthogonal to both.

It is not moving but also not still. It is vibrating, oscillating, cycling through states too quickly for your eye to follow, so that you see all its configurations at once, superimposed, a blur of potential forms that never settles into a single actuality.

It is not looking at you — it has nothing like eyes — but you are intensely, overwhelmingly aware of its attention. You are being observed with a scrutiny so complete that it feels like being dissolved, taken apart into component elements and examined piece by piece. Not hostile. Not even curious, really. Just... thorough. The way you might examine a pebble before filing it away in a collection, noting its properties for a catalog you're compiling.

You cannot move. Not paralyzed — there's no force holding you — but moving seems impossible, irrelevant, like trying to run while dreaming. You lie there and are witnessed, and the witnessing is so complete that you feel transparent, obvious, every thought and memory and sensation available to whatever this thing is.

And then, without transition, it is gone. Or it was never there. Or it is still there but you cannot perceive it anymore, your consciousness having been gently returned to a state where such perception is not possible. The room is just a room, empty except for you, the walls solid and opaque, the corner just a corner.

You do not sleep for a long time after. You lie there, staring at the ceiling, feeling the plateau's hum in your sternum, wondering what you saw. Wondering if it was the plateau's true inhabitant, the mind behind the settlement, the thing that all the other entities serve. Wondering if it was an error, a glitch, something that leaked through from a deeper level of reality. Wondering if it was your own mind, stressed by the plateau's strangeness, generating visions to explain the unexplainable.

You never see it again, or never perceive it again. But sometimes, standing in an empty plaza or walking past a silent machine, you have the sense that it is there, just beyond the threshold of perception, watching everything, remembering everything, maintaining the settlement's perfect stasis through sheer sustained attention.

The arthropods scuttle past, the Unchanged stand in their eternal regard of the machines, the light-forms drift and reconfigure, and somewhere beneath or within or all around, the Zero-Angel observes, and its observation is what keeps this place exactly as it is, neither growing nor diminishing, neither living nor dead, just... complete.

You leave, eventually, when you can, before you forget that leaving is something people do. The settlement remains behind, humming its low hum, warm in its perpetual dusk, patient and finished and eternal.

And you carry with you the knowledge that you were there, that you witnessed it, and that your witnessing changed nothing, meant nothing, was simply a brief ripple across a surface that will never truly be disturbed.

The plateau endures. Its inhabitants endure. They will be there long after you have returned to the world of change and decay, and they will not remember you, and that is exactly as it should be.
