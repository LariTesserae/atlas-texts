# What Lives There

## The Choir

The pool-singers are not born and do not die in any way that leaves evidence. Their population holds at two hundred and seventy-three—this number discovered through patient observation, through learning to distinguish individuals by the subtle variations in their trumpet-mouths, the particular arrangement of the concentric rings, the idiosyncratic patterns of scarring on their rubberized hides.

They do not eat. Dissection of a specimen found dead at the pool's edge (a rare occurrence, perhaps once in what might be years) revealed a body that is mostly resonating chamber—the internal architecture is hollow spaces, baffles, chambers within chambers, structures that look almost architectural, almost *designed*. A digestive system, if one exists, is vestigial, a closed tube leading nowhere. The trumpet-mouth connects to these internal spaces through a maze of passages lined with membranes that vibrate, that modulate the air passing through them. They are living instruments. They are bodies built around the single purpose of producing sound.

They have something like intelligence, but it is not individual. Watch a single pool-singer and you see only stimulus-response: it surfaces, draws breath, vocalizes, submerges, hangs suspended in the grey-green water with minimal movement. It shows no curiosity, no initiative. It does not play. It does not explore. Isolated, it would be almost mechanical in its simplicity.

But the group—the *choir*—demonstrates something else entirely. The patterns in their collective vocalization are too complex to be accidental, too structured to be mere instinct. They respond to each other in ways that suggest listening, that suggest memory. A particular sequence of tones from one singer will, minutes later, be echoed by another across the pool, transformed, elaborated. Themes emerge, develop, recur with variations. Long cycles build over what might be hours or days—without external time markers, it's impossible to be certain—huge compositional structures that seem to have movements, progressions, something like narrative.

They coordinate positions in the water with acoustic precision, arranging themselves into geometries that must affect the sound-field in calculated ways. Sometimes they form dense clusters, their bodies touching, and the sound becomes focused, coherent, like a beam. Sometimes they spread to the pool's perimeter, each equidistant from its neighbors, and the sound becomes diffuse, omnidirectional, filling the basin like weather.

They are aware of the station around them—this much is clear. When maintenance-creatures enter the water, the singers part around them, creating channels of empty water, and the vocalization changes character, becomes higher-pitched, more regular, almost mechanical, as if they are *matching* the presence in their midst. When certain pieces of equipment activate—the ones that send vibrations through the pool's water—the singers respond immediately, shifting their positions, changing their tones, sometimes falling silent in groups while others intensify, creating complex interference patterns that seem designed to interact with the artificial frequencies.

They have something like a sleep-state, though not all at once. The choir never silences completely. But individuals will descend to the pool's bottom, settle against the concrete steps, and rest there, their trumpet-mouths closed, their bodies still. The resting phase lasts perhaps an hour, perhaps three—duration is ambiguous here—and then they rise again, resuming their position in the chorus as if they had never left.

Their skin is not skin. Close examination—touching a specimen, which they permit with indifferent patience—reveals that the surface has no pores, no individual cells that can be distinguished. It is more like a synthetic material, like rubber or latex, but warm, blood-temperature, and with a faint electrical charge that makes your fingers tingle. The material is self-repairing: small tears close themselves over the course of hours, the edges drawing together as if magnetized. It does not scar except where something has damaged the internal structures beneath, created a change in the resonating chambers that alters the voice permanently.

Each singer has a unique voice-print, a particular character to its sound that remains consistent. That one produces a grinding undertone beneath its main frequency. This one has a flutter in its upper register, a rapid oscillation that sounds almost like vibrato. Another holds perfect tones, pure sine waves, with mechanical precision. Over time—and it takes considerable time—you learn to recognize individuals by ear, to track which singer is where in the pool by sound alone.

They respond to their own recordings. Play back their vocalizations through speakers and they fall silent, listening, their trumpet-mouths oriented toward the source. When the recording ends, they resume, but altered—they incorporate elements from what they heard, quote passages, respond to themselves across time. This suggests memory, suggests something like self-awareness, at least at the collective level. The choir *knows itself*, recognizes its own voice, and modulates its performance in response to that recognition.

New arrivals—when they occur—come through underwater passages that connect the pool to deeper reservoirs. A new singer emerges from the depths, rising slowly, and the choir reacts: a sudden intensification of sound, almost aggressive, a wall of noise directed at the newcomer. The new singer responds with its own vocalization, and for a period that might be minutes or hours there is a kind of dialogue, a testing, an establishment of relationship. Eventually the choir subsides, the new voice integrates into the pattern, and the population holds steady at two hundred and seventy-three until some other singer departs—descending into those same dark passages, leaving for wherever they go, keeping the number constant.

They are not friendly. They are not hostile. They permit close approach, allow you to swim among them (if you can tolerate the cold water, the press of their bodies, the overwhelming loudness at that proximity), but they do not acknowledge you as a presence that matters. You are not part of the acoustic environment they care about. You make no sound they find interesting. Your movements in the water create splashes and currents they route around with automatic precision, the way they route around the maintenance-creatures, the way water flows around stones.

But sometimes—rarely, without warning—the entire choir will orient toward something: all two hundred and seventy-three trumpet-mouths turning in unison to face the same direction, tracking some stimulus that is not visible, not audible to human senses. They will hold this orientation for long seconds, utterly silent, and the absence of their sound is shocking, makes the mechanical hum of the station suddenly loud, suddenly lonely. Then the moment breaks, they resume their positions and their song, and nothing in the environment has apparently changed. What they sensed, what they attended to, remains unknown.

## The Attendants

The maintenance-creatures—no one calls them that, there is no one to call them anything, but that is their function, their apparent purpose—are more obviously *made* than the pool-singers. Their segmented construction, the plates of dark keratin or chitin or something similar, the clicking precision of their joints: these speak to design, to engineering, even if the engineering is biological.

Each stands roughly seventy centimeters at the shoulder, built low and wide for stability. The four legs are double-jointed, capable of gripping, of climbing, of bracing in complex positions that allow them to operate equipment clearly not designed for their anatomy. The hooked claws at each foot can grip metal grating, can turn valve wheels, can press buttons, can manipulate tools in limited ways.

The flexible neck allows the head to rotate through nearly three hundred and sixty degrees, to extend upward to twice the body's height, or to retract down until the head is level with the shoulders. The head itself is almost entirely sensory apparatus. The compound eyes—four of them, positioned to give complete spherical vision—are dark, multifaceted, reflecting light in strange ways, sometimes showing rainbows, sometimes absorbing light completely and looking like holes in reality.

The mouthparts are complex: multiple mandibles that work independently, lined with fine manipulators, capable of gripping objects as small as screws, as large as pipe sections. These mandibles are also how they communicate—the high-frequency chirps come from structures within the mouth-cavity, organs that vibrate, that produce sounds above human hearing range. The chirps convey information, clearly: one attendant will chirp, another will respond, and actions follow, coordinated efforts to move something heavy or to reconfigure a system that requires simultaneous adjustments at multiple points.

The tendrils around the head—somewhere between twelve and sixteen per individual, the count varies—are in constant motion, tasting the air, sensing electrical fields, detecting vibrations. They respond to sound, orienting toward novel noises, but they also seem to sense things that make no noise: electromagnetic fields, temperature gradients, perhaps vibrations conducted through the stone, through the metal infrastructure. An attendant will sometimes stop, tendrils fully extended, motionless, reading the environment in ways human senses cannot access, then proceed with certainty toward some hidden problem, some variance in the system that needed addressing.

They work in groups, but the groups are fluid, temporary assemblies formed for specific tasks. Five attendants might converge on a malfunctioning pump, working together to disassemble it, diagnose the problem, replace components. Once the task is complete, they disperse, each moving off on separate trajectories toward other duties. There is no obvious hierarchy. Decisions seem to be collective, emergent from the chirped exchanges, the touch of tendrils, the observation of what others are doing.

They carry things in their mouths, gripped carefully in those complex mandibles. Corroded bolts. Replacement gaskets. Strange organic components—pods of something that might be biological lubricant, membranes that seem to serve as electrical insulation, bundles of fiber that they weave into repairs for fraying cable. Where these materials come from is part of the hidden infrastructure: they descend into access tunnels, emerge later with what is needed. There must be stores, workshops, fabrication facilities deeper in the rock, spaces where materials are kept or grown or synthesized.

They maintain the recording equipment with particular care, showing what might be reverence or might be deeper programming. The tape machines are cleaned of dust with specialized tools they carry—brushes made from their own shed fibers, perhaps, or something similar. Degraded components are replaced preemptively, before failure occurs. The attendants seem to understand not just operation but *preservation*, keeping the systems in working order but also in good condition, preventing the slow drift toward entropy.

They do not sleep in any way that is obvious. There may be rest periods, but they are not synchronized, not visible. At any given time, attendants are moving through the station, checking, adjusting, repairing. The work is constant because the systems are extensive, because things wear and need tending, because this is what they are for.

They are not hostile, but they are not *safe* in the way a clearly mechanical system might be safe. They have mass, momentum, strength. An attendant focused on reaching a valve will push through obstacles, including living obstacles, with patient force. They do not attack, but they do not stop. Step into the path of an attendant carrying a heavy component and it will simply continue, shoving you aside if you do not move, not with malice but with the complete indifference of water flowing downhill.

Their relationship to the pool-singers is attentive but not interactive. Attendants regularly patrol the pool's perimeter, checking the water level against markers only they seem to recognize, testing the pipe outputs with their tendrils, tasting the water chemistry with their mouth-parts. When they enter the water, the singers part around them as if around debris, without interest. The attendants descend to inspect underwater equipment—submerged sensors, intake pipes, the mysterious apparatus at the pool's deepest point—and they do this work with the same mechanical efficiency they bring to everything else.

Occasionally an attendant will carry a dead or dying pool-singer from the water, gripping it carefully, carrying it away through the tunnels. Where the bodies go, what happens to them—recycling, disposal, transformation into something else—is not visible. The attendants return empty, resume their rounds.

They show something like problem-solving. A door jammed shut will be examined, prodded, tested. If force does not work, they will fetch tools, or they will find alternate routes. If equipment fails in novel ways, they do not simply repeat futile actions: they try different approaches, consult with other attendants, modify their strategies. This suggests flexibility, suggests learning, or at least sophisticated programming that can handle contingency.

But they are not *curious*. They do not investigate things outside their maintenance domain. A novel object placed in the station will be examined only if it interferes with their work, and then only to the extent necessary to route around it. They show no interest in art, in pattern for its own sake, in anything that does not relate directly to the function of the station. Their intelligence, such as it is, is *narrow*: deep competence in their domain, complete indifference to everything else.

The population seems stable—somewhere around forty individuals, though exact counts are difficult since they appear and disappear through the tunnels unpredictably. Unlike the pool-singers, they seem more truly individual: you can learn to recognize specific attendants by their gait, by particular scarring on their plates, by small variations in size and coloration. That one has a chip missing from its upper left eye. This one moves with a slight hitch in its rear right leg, some old injury that healed imperfectly. Another is noticeably larger than average, its plates darker, almost black.

They are old—this much is clear from the wear on them, the accumulated scars and repairs, the way their movements have the confidence of long practice. How old is impossible to gauge. They might be decades into their work. They might be longer-lived than that, existing in this stable environment without the usual pressures that limit lifespan. They do not seem to age, do not slow down, do not accumulate frailty. They maintain themselves as they maintain the station, replacing worn components, keeping their bodies functional.

Sometimes, very rarely, an attendant will cease functioning. It will stop in place, tendrils drooping, and remain there, motionless. Other attendants will eventually come, examine it with their tendrils, confer in their ultrasonic chirps, then carry it away through the tunnels. A replacement—new or relocated from some other area—will appear soon after, maintaining the working population at its necessary level.

They are the station's continuity, its persistence made physical. They are why the machinery still runs, why the buildings have not collapsed, why the listening continues. Without them, the station would fail—not quickly, not catastrophically, but steadily, systems drifting out of tolerance, repairs undone, the slow victory of entropy. With them, it endures, holds its state, remains functional for purposes that the attendants themselves may not comprehend but that they serve with absolute fidelity.

## The Listeners

There are others.

They do not live in the station—or they do not live *obviously*, not in the way the attendants patrol and the pool-singers swim. But they are present. Evidence accumulates. A chair positioned to face a viewing window, set at a height wrong for human proportions but right for something larger. Doors that open to darkness, to passages that go deeper than the station's visible infrastructure, passages that show signs of use, of passage, but never when you are watching.

Instruments that monitor the monitors, recording the recordings. In the room full of tape machines, there is a secondary system—harder to notice at first, integrated into the walls—that captures what the primary systems capture, creating redundancy upon redundancy. These systems are maintained by the attendants but seem designed for a different user, different purposes.

Sometimes, in the building with the suspended bell, you will find evidence of recent occupation: condensation on metal surfaces in patterns that suggest a large body pressed against them, seeking coolness or warmth. Scuff marks on the concrete floor, not from attendant claws but from something else, something that drags or slides. A smell—faint, hard to characterize—that is not the usual mixture of machine oil and mineral dampness but something organic, something *other*.

The attendants behave differently in certain areas, moving with what might be deference or might be caution, taking longer routes to avoid particular spaces. There are rooms they enter only briefly, performing minimal maintenance before withdrawing. There are sections of catwalk where they do not go at all, where the grating accumulates dust and condensation undisturbed, where you can walk and hear your footsteps ring in the empty space and feel watched by something you cannot locate.

In the bunker-like listening station at the basin's north edge, the equipment is sometimes reconfigured in your absence. You leave with the microphones angled one way, return to find them adjusted, focused on different areas of the pool, tracking different members of the choir. The recording devices show signs of review: tape reels advanced or rewound to different positions, wax cylinders removed and replaced, the photosensitive papers examined (you find handprints—but no, not handprints, the proportions are wrong, the number of digits incorrect—in the dust beside them).

There are moments of presence: a sound from the deep tunnels that is not attendant-clicks, not pool-singer bellowing, not mechanical hum, but something else, something vast and slow, like breathing, like the creak of weight shifting. Vibrations that come up through the stone, through the metal framework of the catwalks, that have rhythm and intention. The sense—impossible to verify but impossible to dismiss—that something large is moving in spaces you cannot access, attending to the station's deeper purposes.

The bell in its empty room is not struck, but it is *used*. This becomes clear if you observe it long enough, if you note the precise quality of its vibration, the way it sometimes rings with tones that do not match the ambient sound, that seem to come from within the metal itself. Something is listening through it, using it as an antenna or a transducer, drawing information from the acoustic field in ways the conventional recording equipment cannot.

The attendants bring offerings—the only word that fits. To certain locations, usually doorways leading to deeper passages, they carry objects and leave them: still-functional components removed from the station's systems, pieces of interesting mineral formation broken from the basin walls, sometimes dead pool-singers arranged in careful positions. The offerings are always gone within what might be hours or days, taken by something, accepted.

There is intelligence here, vast and patient and operating on scales that make human comprehension difficult. The station is not abandoned—it is *tended*, but tended by something that does not need to be visible, that works through intermediaries, that listens from depth.

The recordings themselves suggest this. If you play back the tapes, the cylinders, if you examine the colored patterns in the glass tubes and the dark whorls on the photosensitive papers, patterns emerge that are not in the original sound. The equipment is not simply recording but *processing*, finding structure, extracting signal from noise, and the results point to complexity that the choir alone could not produce. The pool-singers are the instruments. Something else is the composer, or perhaps the audience, or perhaps something that makes no distinction between those roles.

Sometimes the grey light changes quality—not in brightness, but in *character*, becoming warmer or cooler for brief periods, as if something vast is paying attention, leaning in to observe more closely, then withdrawing. The air pressure shifts slightly, though the barometers show no change, as if the space itself is breathing. The sound of the station—already overwhelming—sometimes develops an additional layer, a subsonic component you feel more than hear, a pressure in your chest and skull that suggests vast activity occurring just below the threshold of perception.

The listeners—and there must be more than one, the evidence is too varied, too distributed—do not interfere. They do not prevent exploration, do not block access, do not harm. But they are *present*, shaping the space through absence, through suggestion, through the infrastructure they maintain and the purposes they serve. The station exists for them, or they exist for the station, or the distinction is meaningless, their purposes so intertwined that separation is impossible.

They are why this continues. The attendants maintain the machinery, the pool-singers provide the raw material, but the listeners give *purpose*, give reason for the maintenance and the singing and the endless recording. They are the audience for whom the performance continues, the consciousness—if consciousness is even the right word—that integrates the data, that understands what the noise means, that has built this entire complex ecology of sound and stone and strange flesh as a way of knowing something that cannot be known more directly.

You will not see them. Perhaps no one sees them. Perhaps seeing is not relevant to what they are, to how they perceive. But you will feel their attention, you will know you are in a place that *matters* to something, that serves a function beyond itself, that exists as part of a larger system whose true scale remains hidden in the grey light and the constant sound and the passages that go down into darkness where the attendants come and go and bring their offerings to whatever waits in the depth, listening, always listening, patient and vast and utterly alien in its purposes.

The station persists because they need it to persist. The equilibrium holds because they require its holding. And the bellowing choir sings on, the machines record, the attendants maintain, and somewhere in the architecture of sound and stone and strange biology, meaning is being made, information is being gathered, a vast and patient purpose is being served that will continue long after any human observer has departed, long after human understanding has given up trying to parse what this place is and why it matters and what listens from the grey light and the grey water and the deep stone beneath.

The place is not empty. It is not abandoned. It is *inhabited*—just not in any way that offers comfort, not in any way that includes you. You are permitted to observe, but you are not part of the purpose. You are weather passing through, a temporary disturbance in the acoustic field, a small anomaly that the system routes around and then forgets.

And always, always, the choir sings its vast industrial song into the grey air, and the machines faithfully record, and the attendants make their clicking rounds, and something vast and patient *listens*, gathering whatever it is gathering, knowing whatever it is knowing, in the cool and constant and utterly strange place that is the Listening Station, where the work continues, where the work has always continued, where the work will continue until the grey light finally fails or the water stops seeping or the stone itself gives way—and perhaps not even then, perhaps not even then.
