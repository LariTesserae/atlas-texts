# Those Who Dwell in the Thundering Gardens

They are here, but barely. Not in the way of absence, but in the way of a breath held so long it becomes part of the breathing itself.

## The Moss-Readers

Most often, you'll encounter them by accident — a shape that resolves wrong against the green, movement where you'd dismissed everything as vegetation. They wear the moss. Not clothing *made* from moss, but moss itself, cultivated directly on fabric underlayers of felted fiber so old and compressed it's nearly stone-cloth. The moss lives, grows, changes with the moisture cycles, and they tend it as you'd tend hair, encouraging certain varieties, discouraging others, creating patterns that shift with the seasons and the altitude.

They are small-statured, as if the ancient builders were selected by the place for compactness, for efficiency of motion through narrow spaces. Their hands are broad-palmed with long fingers, constantly moving, constantly reading surfaces — touching stone to feel for moisture patterns, brushing moss to check its health, trailing fingers through pool water to sense temperature and current. Their eyes are grey-green, adapted to the filtered light, wide-set in weathered faces that seem simultaneously young and impossibly old. They blink rarely. They watch.

Speech, when it comes, is sparse and pitched oddly — voices trained to cut through the water-song without shouting, using tones that complement rather than compete with the mountain's harmonics. They can speak normally if they must, but prefer not to; the effort seems to tire them. Instead they use their constantly-moving hands, a gesture language so fluid it looks like dancing, like conducting the wind-music itself. Among themselves, they often say nothing at all, communicating through postures, through the angle of a shoulder, through decisions to move or be still.

They walk the slopes in what seems like wandering but isn't. Each has routes, territories not of ownership but of attention — a region of pools they monitor, watching the water levels and the creatures within; a stretch of stone face where they read the patterns of erosion like you'd read news; a grove of the sideways-growing trees whose health they track across years. They move between these stations in cycles that synchronize with the mist patterns, with the water flows, with rhythms not immediately apparent to outsiders.

Their needs are minimal, almost theoretical. They eat — small fish from the pools, taken by hand, eaten raw; the inner parts of certain ferns, stripped and chewed; insects gathered from under stones, selected with great care, some eaten, others ignored according to knowledge passed down through generations. They seem to eat rarely, as if they've learned to subsist partially on the moisture in the air, on the green light filtered through moss, on the sound itself. Watching them, you sometimes forget they need food at all, and when they do eat, it's with the same economical motion they apply to everything else — swift, efficient, immediately returned to stillness.

Water is everywhere, so thirst is not a concern, though they're particular about which pools they drink from. Some they avoid entirely, and whether this is due to contamination or taste or some other property they can sense, they won't explain. They cup water in their broad palms, drink deeply, move on. They bathe infrequently — the constant mist does most of that work — but when they do, it's ritual: selecting a specific pool, submerging completely, staying under for an uncomfortably long time, emerging without apparent need to gasp for air.

They sleep in the old structures, one person to a building usually, though sometimes in the larger nodes you'll find two or three sharing space without seeming to interact — each in their own corner, wrapped in additional layers of moss-cloth that they shed like cocoons come morning. They sleep lightly, sitting upright or lying curled on the stone benches, and they wake frequently, listening, checking, then settling back. Full sleep seems to come in accumulated fragments rather than solid blocks. They dream — you can see their eyes moving beneath closed lids, their fingers twitching in gesture-language conversations with absent partners — but they don't speak of dreams.

## The Sound-Watchers

Rarer, more scattered, are those who have specialized into something else. You might see one crouched motionless beside a particular crevasse for hours, head tilted, listening to the tones the wind produces as it enters and exits the stone. They're mapping the mountain's voice, tracking its changes, learning to predict weather and stone-shifts and water-course alterations from variations in the harmonic structure.

These ones are even more silent than the moss-readers, as if they've allocated all available attention to auditory processing and have none left for other forms of communication. Their moss-coverings are thinner, less carefully tended, sometimes patchy. They look half-starved, though whether from actual hunger or simple intensity of focus is unclear. When you approach them, they hold up one hand — wait, not now — and the gesture is so absolute you find yourself frozen until they release you with a nod, sometimes hours later.

They carry instruments, if you can call them that: hollow reeds of different lengths that they blow across, matching tones to the mountain-song; stones selected for resonance that they strike with other stones, creating counter-rhythms; frames strung with fiber that they pluck, adding harmonic lines to the wind-music. They're not making music, exactly — they're *conversing*, testing propositions, asking questions of the mountains and waiting for answers in the responding tones.

Some of them no longer live in the structures at all. They've found caves, hollow places deeper in the stone where the acoustic properties are different, where they can hear the underground rivers more clearly, where the wind plays different notes. They emerge occasionally, blinking in the grey light, disoriented by openness, and they gather food in distracted handfuls before retreating back into their resonant darkness. The moss-readers treat them with a mixture of respect and concern, leaving food near cave mouths, checking that they're still alive, but not interfering with whatever work they're doing in there.

## The Water-Walkers

And then there are the children, though calling them children is almost wrong — they're too self-sufficient, too integrated into the landscape's patterns. They range in age from perhaps five years to early adolescence, at which point they seem to transition into moss-reader practices, but while they're young, they exist in a different category entirely.

They move in loose groups of three or four, never more, ranging widely across the mountains with a fearlessness that looks like recklessness but isn't. They know the stone better than anyone, know which surfaces are stable and which will crumble, know where temporary streams will appear before they appear, know the deep pools from the shallow ones by sight. They leap from boulder to boulder, balance on wet stone that would send an adult sliding, wade through rushing water that should knock them over but doesn't. They're learning the mountain not through observation but through their bodies, building an instinctive map that will serve them the rest of their lives.

They make noise — the only ones who do. They call to each other in high, clear voices that carry across vast distances, using the mountain's acoustics deliberately, bouncing calls off cliff faces to reach someone out of sight around a slope. They laugh, which is startling in this place of patient silence. They sing, making up songs that weave through the wind-music, harmonizing with it unselfconsciously. The adults never shush them. The noise is part of their learning, part of how they claim their relationship to the place.

They're the ones who find things — old tools, interesting stones, the strange geometric flowers in their brief blooming. They bring their discoveries to the adults, who examine them gravely and sometimes keep them, sometimes let the children carry them off to secret caches hidden in crevices known only to the young. They're accumulating, learning value through handling, through the adults' reactions, through their own developing aesthetic sense.

They eat more frequently than the adults, still growing, still burning energy in their constant motion. They're less particular about what they eat, trying things — you see one bite into a fern frond, make a face, spit it out, move on. Trial and error, with the resilience of youth protecting them from serious mistakes. The adults watch from a distance, intervening only rarely, when the danger is genuine rather than merely uncomfortable.

At night, the children often sleep in clusters, piled together for warmth in the larger structures or in moss-nests they build themselves in sheltered hollows. They settle like birds, still chattering, gradually quieting, finally silent. In the morning, they explode outward again, scattering across the slopes, beginning the day's exploration.

## The Very Old

There are a few — three, perhaps four in the entire range you can see — who have become something else entirely. They've been here so long they're nearly geological. They sit in specific places, sometimes for weeks without moving, moss growing on them not as clothing but as actual colonization, their stillness sufficient for the plants to take root in the fabric on their backs, their hair, even — if you look closely — in the creases of their skin.

They breathe. You can see that if you watch long enough. The moss rises and falls slightly. But otherwise, they could be structures themselves, more ancient human-made things slowly returning to the mountain. Their eyes are open, grey-green faded to almost white, and they watch nothing in particular, or everything, or something happening on a timescale too slow for normal perception.

The moss-readers visit them, touching their shoulders gently, leaving food within reach that often goes uneaten. Sometimes the very old will stir, will pick up the offering and consume it with slow, mechanical movements, then resettle into stillness. Sometimes they don't. The food disappears eventually anyway — taken by animals, decomposed back into the moss layer, the cycle continuing.

Do they speak? Occasionally. A moss-reader will sit with them for an hour, two hours, waiting, and then the old one will make a sound — not quite a word, more like a tone that harmonizes with the mountain-song — and the moss-reader will nod, satisfied, having received whatever information they came for. Or they'll gesture, one of those long-fingered hands rising with arthritic slowness to point at something — a particular pool, a shift in the mist pattern, a bird passing overhead — and the gesture conveys volumes to those trained to read it.

They're becoming the place, these old ones. In another few years, they'll stop moving entirely, and the moss will cover them completely, and they'll be lumps in the landscape, slowly eroding, returning their minerals to the stone. The younger people don't seem disturbed by this. It's the proper end, the right way to finish — becoming indistinguishable from the mountains they've spent their lives reading.

## The Mark-Makers

Occasionally, you'll encounter someone doing something that looks almost like art. They're at one of the old structures, scraping new grooves into the stone walls with a piece of harder stone, adding to the patterns left by previous generations. Or they're stacking stones in careful arrangements near one of the pools, building something between a cairn and a sculpture, balanced so precisely that it shouldn't stand but does.

These aren't specialized roles — any of the moss-readers might suddenly spend an afternoon doing this, then return to their usual rounds. It seems to be more like a periodic need, a way of marking time or processing experience or communicating something too complex for their gesture language. The marks they make don't represent anything obvious — not pictures, not writing — but they're not random either. There's intention in every line, every placed stone.

Sometimes you'll see two of them working on the same wall from opposite sides, adding patterns that somehow complement each other despite the makers being unable to see what the other is doing. They work in silence, occasionally pausing to touch the stone, to feel what's been added, then continuing. When they finish, they step back, examine the work briefly, then walk away without ceremony. The pattern remains, added to the accumulated marks of centuries, slowly eroding like everything else.

## Their Knowledge

What they know is extensive but specialized. They can predict the mist patterns with uncanny accuracy, reading signs in the quality of the light, the way the moss smells, the specific harmonics of the wind-song. They know where the temporary streams will flow before they flow, can see the paths water will take across stone by understanding erosion patterns invisible to outsiders. They know which pools contain fish and which contain only insects, which trees will bear the spiral flowers and when, which ferns can be eaten and which will make you sick.

They don't know, or don't care to know, much about anything else. The world beyond the mountains is theoretical to them, mentioned rarely, regarded with polite disinterest when outsiders speak of it. They have no visible trade goods, no interest in acquisition. Metal is almost absent here — the few tools are stone, have always been stone, will presumably always be stone. They don't weave cloth in the traditional sense, using instead compressed moss and felted fibers that they seem to make through some process involving water and time and pressure, the details unclear.

Their medicine is simple: water for washing wounds, moss poultices that do seem to have antiseptic properties, and mostly just time and rest in the humid air that apparently promotes healing. They suffer few injuries — they're too careful, too integrated with the landscape's dangers. Illness is rare, whether due to the clean water, the thin air, or simple genetic selection over generations.

They have names, but use them rarely, preferring to identify each other through context, through location, through pattern of movement. When names are necessary, they're simple — sounds more than words, often incorporating elements of the wind-music, so that calling someone's name is like singing a fragment of the mountain's song.

## Their Children, Their Continuity

The children don't come from nowhere. There are pairings, though they're subtle, non-possessive. Two moss-readers will begin spending more time together, sharing structures at night, moving in synchronized patterns during the day. Eventually, a child appears — well, a pregnancy first, the woman's movements becoming more careful, her belly swelling beneath the moss-clothing. The man stays close, bringing her choice food, but otherwise life continues largely unchanged.

Birth happens in the structures, attended by two or three experienced women who bring water and moss and patient hands. It's quiet — even in labor, these people don't scream, don't thrash. The sound would be wrong here, would violate something essential. The mother breathes in rhythm with the mountain-song, works with her body's contractions, and eventually the infant emerges into the grey-green light, into the constant sound of water and wind, already being trained to the place's rhythms.

Infants are carried constantly, strapped to their mother's back with moss-fiber wrappings, riding along on her daily rounds. They learn the terrain through her movements, feel the textures of different mosses, hear the variations in the wind-song. By the time they can walk, they already know the basics. By the time they're five or six, they're ready to join the loose child-groups, to begin their own exploration.

Not all children survive to adulthood. The mountains are gentle but unforgiving, and mistakes — a pool deeper than expected, a stone more slick than it looked, a sudden water surge through a channel — can be fatal. The people mourn these losses quietly, sitting in stillness for a day, making marks on stone, then continuing. Life here doesn't permit extended grief. The practical demands of survival resume immediately.

## The Outsiders They Tolerate

When travelers arrive — which is rare, the mountains being remote and the paths unclear — the residents don't exactly welcome them but don't drive them away. They simply continue their routines, occasionally glancing at the newcomers with those grey-green eyes, assessing without judgment. If the travelers are respectful, quiet, able to move safely through the terrain, they're allowed to stay. If they're loud, clumsy, disruptive, the residents don't confront them directly but simply... withdraw. The travelers find themselves alone, without guides, without the subtle assistance the residents provide each other, and usually they leave on their own, unsettled by the isolation.

A few outsiders have stayed longer, learning the gesture language, beginning to understand the patterns. Some have even tried to join the moss-readers, wearing moss-coverings, attempting to sync with the rhythms. Most fail — they can't tolerate the constant moisture, the relentless sound, the diet, the isolation. Their nervous systems aren't adapted. They break after weeks or months and flee back to drier lands, quieter valleys, more conventional human society.

Very rarely, one succeeds. You can identify them by their awkwardness — they've learned the movements intellectually rather than instinctively, and there's a microsecond delay in their responses, a slight stiffness in their gestures. But they're trying, genuinely, and the born residents tolerate them, even assist them, perhaps appreciating the validation of someone choosing this life deliberately rather than simply being born to it.

## Their Relationship to the Past

The old structures, the ancient tools, the marks on walls — these aren't relics to them but continuity. They see themselves as the same people who built those first buildings, not descendants but extensions, the same culture in the same place doing the same work. Time is different here, not linear but cyclical, layered. The grooves they carve today will erode and be re-carved by someone in fifty years who will feel exactly as they feel now, standing in the same place, holding the same type of stone, adding to the same never-finished pattern.

They don't tell stories about the past in the traditional sense — no epic narratives, no hero tales. Instead they have observations, accumulated wisdom about the mountains' behavior. "The pool near the split stone fills before rain" isn't a story, but it's passed down just as carefully, generation to generation, the knowledge base building microscopically, each generation adding a few more observations to the collective understanding.

The very old, in their semi-geological stillness, are living archives. When a moss-reader sits with them, they're not just visiting — they're accessing memory, receiving knowledge about patterns that play out over decades, over lifetimes, patterns only visible from the perspective of extreme age. The old ones have seen the trees grow from sprouts to their gnarled maturity. They've watched pools appear and disappear, stone faces shift imperceptibly, wind patterns change. They carry this information and release it in those sparse gestures, those occasional harmonic tones, transferring wisdom to those who will outlive them.

## What They Want

Nothing, apparently. Or everything they want is already here: the moss growing in its layers, the water following its paths, the wind playing its tones, the pools reflecting the grey-green light. They have no visible ambitions, no projects beyond maintenance, no desire for change or expansion or improvement.

Except — there's something in how they pause sometimes, how they stand at the edge of a high slope looking out across the ranges, how their fingers move in gesture-conversations with themselves. They're engaged with something, puzzling at something, working toward understanding of something. Not the practical knowledge of when mist will come or which pools contain fish, but something deeper: the pattern underneath the patterns, the reason the mountains sing these particular harmonics, the meaning — if meaning exists — in the geometric flowers' brief blooming.

The sound-watchers especially seem driven by this, listening so intently they forget to eat, mapping harmonics with desperate precision as if the answer is encoded in the resonances, as if understanding the song completely would reveal something essential. Whether they ever find what they're seeking is unclear. They grow old still listening, become the very old sitting in stillness still processing those tones in their increasingly quiet minds, finally erode away with the question presumably unanswered.

Or perhaps answered. Perhaps the stillness at the end is satisfaction, completion, the puzzle solved but the solution incommunicable, experiential rather than verbal. The younger moss-readers, watching the old ones settle into their final positions, sometimes nod as if understanding something, as if seeing in that perfect stillness a rightness that validates the entire project of living here, of giving yourself to this place's rhythms until you become indistinguishable from its stones.

## Their Relationship to Each Other

Affection exists but is expressed through proximity and practical care rather than demonstrative gestures. Two moss-readers who've paired will move in parallel across the slopes, not touching but synchronized, their paths crossing and re-crossing in patterns that speak of deep familiarity. A sound-watcher emerging half-starved from their cave will find food waiting, placed there by someone who remembered them, who checked on them, who cared enough to carry supplies across difficult terrain.

The children receive collective attention — any adult will correct a child's mistake, guide them away from genuine danger, share food with them. There's no possessiveness, no "this is my child." All children belong to the mountains, are being raised by the place itself, with the adults merely assisting in that education.

Conflicts are rare and when they occur, they're resolved through withdrawal rather than confrontation. If two moss-readers have a dispute over territory or interpretation of the wind-signs, they simply avoid each other for a while, ranging in different areas, until the disagreement becomes irrelevant through time and distance. The forced proximity of the structures at night never seems to cause problems — they're all too tired, too deep in their own thoughts, too synchronized with the mountain's rhythms to generate the kind of sustained interpersonal friction that would require resolution.

Jealousy, greed, ambition — these seem absent, not suppressed but simply irrelevant. What is there to covet? The pools belong to no one. The moss grows everywhere. The wind-music plays for anyone who listens. The structures are available to anyone who needs shelter. Food is abundant enough for their minimal needs. Competition would be absurd here, pointless, a waste of energy better spent on attention, on learning, on the slow work of understanding.

## Their Joy

It exists, though it's subtle. A moss-reader finding a new variety of moss, kneeling to examine it closely, fingers tracing its structure with a expression of quiet delight. A sound-watcher matching a harmonic perfectly, their face relaxing into satisfaction. The children discovering a pool full of the translucent water-creatures, laughing as they watch them dart and swirl.

And sometimes, on the rare clear days when the sun breaks through completely and the whole landscape explodes into brightness, all of them — young and old, moss-readers and sound-watchers — will pause in their activities and simply look. Stand or sit or crouch in place, faces turned toward the light, eyes half-closed, absorbing it. Not smiling exactly, but peaceful, content, present in the moment with complete attention.

Then the mist returns, the greyness settles back, and they resume their rounds, their listening, their careful movement through the eternal dampness. But something has been renewed, some essential connection to the place reaffirmed, and they continue their work with fresh purpose, or if not purpose then fresh acceptance, which amounts to the same thing here.

## What Happens When They Die

The young who die through accident are mourned as mentioned — briefly, quietly — and then left where they fell, or moved to a pool if they drowned, or covered lightly with moss if they died on open stone. The mountains reclaim them quickly. Water and insects and the ever-present moisture do their work. Within weeks, little remains. Within months, nothing. The moss grows over the site, the bones dissolve into soil, the person becomes part of the landscape they spent their life learning.

The adults who die — from age or injury or the occasional illness that even their adapted bodies can't fight — are treated similarly. They're moved to one of the old structures, laid out on a stone bench, covered with the best moss-cloths. The community sits with them for a night, not vigil exactly but presence, company for the transition. Then they're left, the structure becoming a temporary tomb, though the walls are too gap-filled to keep out scavengers, the roof too porous to prevent rain.

Within a season, the body is gone, processed back into the mountains' cycles. The structure is cleaned — the moss removed, the stones washed with pool water — and it becomes available again, ready for the next person who needs shelter, who will sleep on the same bench where someone's body decomposed, unbothered by this, seeing it as proper, as the way things should be.

The very old, who die in their sitting positions already half-covered with moss, are left exactly where they are. They're marked, perhaps — a small cairn built nearby, a pattern carved into adjacent stone — but otherwise undisturbed. They remain visible for years as they erode, as the moss fully covers them, as they slowly slump and settle and finally collapse into a mound that could be boulder or ancient human, indistinguishable, identical, the difference no longer mattering.

## How They Continue

New children are born at a rate that roughly matches deaths — not through planning but through some emergent property of their lifestyle, their diet, their patterns. The population stays stable at perhaps thirty people across the entire visible range, scattered so widely you might walk for days and see only two or three.

They teach the children everything they know, not through formal instruction but through accompaniment, through doing the work while young eyes watch and young hands imitate. The gesture language is absorbed before speech. The knowledge of plants and pools and wind-signs is accumulated through experience, corrected gently when wrong, reinforced through repetition.

Some knowledge is surely lost when the very old die, observations too specific or too complex to have been fully transferred. But new observations are being made constantly, new patterns noticed, new harmonics mapped. The total knowledge base fluctuates but remains roughly constant, sufficient for survival, sufficient for the deep engagement with place that defines their culture.

Will they continue indefinitely? Unknown. They've been here for countless generations already — the oldest structures suggest at least centuries of continuous occupation, possibly millennia. They show no signs of decline, no loss of adaptation, no desire to leave. The mountains provide everything they need. The life, while austere, is sustainable. The children grow up strong and capable, ready to assume their roles as moss-readers and sound-watchers, ready to raise the next generation in turn.

Threats exist, certainly. A catastrophic rockfall could bury several structures at once, kill multiple people. A change in climate could alter the mist patterns they depend on. A disease introduced by travelers could sweep through their small population. But these are hypotheticals. For now, they persist, as patient and constant as the moss they wear, as the pools they monitor, as the ancient mountains themselves.

## Their Secret

And there is something else, something they don't share with outsiders, something visible only in the way they pause sometimes, all of them simultaneously, wherever they are across the ranges. They turn their heads in the same direction, listening to something beneath the audible, responding to a signal that travels through stone or air or the moss-network itself.

When this happens, their faces show something beyond their usual calm — not fear but alertness, anticipation. They listen for perhaps thirty seconds, perhaps a minute, absolutely motionless. Then they relax, resume their activities, but with a subtle change in their posture, as if they've received information, as if they've been reminded of something important.

What they're hearing — or sensing, or remembering — remains unclear. The mountains themselves, perhaps, communicating directly with those who've lived long enough on their slopes to develop the sensitivity required. Or the accumulated presence of all the previous generations, their knowledge somehow encoded in the landscape, accessible to those trained to perceive it. Or something else entirely, some property of this place that hasn't been named because it exists only here, only in the Thundering Gardens, only for those who've given themselves completely to learning its patterns.

They won't explain, or can't, or see no need to. When asked, they make a gesture that means something like "listen yourself" or "be here longer" or "the answer is obvious to those who know." Then they return to their work, their patient rounds, their slow accumulation of observations across their lifetimes, adding their individual awareness to the collective understanding, maintaining the continuous thread of attention that connects the first builders to the very old to the children currently leaping across wet stone, learning the place through their bodies, preparing to take their turn as guardians of these ancient singing mountains.

The moss grows. The water flows. The wind plays its tones. They listen, they watch, they tend, they endure. That is enough. That has always been enough. That will, presumably, always be enough, until the mountains themselves finally erode away and there is nothing left to read, nothing left to listen to, nothing left to understand.

But that is so far in the future it might as well be never. For now, there is today, there is this pool, this patch of moss, this particular harmonic produced by this morning's wind. There is work to do. There is attention to pay. There is life to be lived in synchronization with stone and water and green, here in the Thundering Gardens, where the mountains have been mountains so long they have forgotten they were ever anything else.
