# Who Lives There

## The Descent Cats

They are the plateau's inheritors, though they did not displace anyone. They simply learned the stone's secrets more thoroughly than anything else.

A descent cat at rest becomes nearly invisible—not through camouflage but through stillness so complete it reads as geology. They lie on the warm basalt in the copper hours of afternoon, their bodies arranged in precise relation to family members: adults touching at shoulder or flank, juveniles pressed against their sides, yearlings at the periphery maintaining contact through outstretched tails. The physical connection seems to matter. They share warmth though none is needed. They share presence.

Their eyes are their tell: too large, too reflective, catching light at angles that reveal their position even when the body has become just another shadow on stone. In daylight the pupils contract to vertical slits that can close almost completely, leaving only thread-thin openings. At night they expand until almost no iris shows—black pools that gather the bioluminescence from the fissures, the starlight, the occasional ember-glow from oxidizing metal.

Their bodies are built for the third dimension. The front legs are longer than the rear, giving them a slight forward slope when standing, allowing them to lean into descents. The shoulders are massive and heavily muscled—all the strength for climbing resides there. The hips are narrow, flexible, allowing the rear legs to extend forward almost alongside the body when descending a narrow fissure. The spine articulates with unusual freedom. They can twist their bodies along the vertical axis while maintaining their grip with all four feet, allowing them to navigate spiraling descents through cracks that shift angle as they drop.

The claws are their architecture. Each foot has five toes, each toe bearing a claw as long as a finger, curved slightly, sharp at the tip, retractable into sheaths that keep them pristine. When they grip stone, the claws emerge slowly, feeling for purchase points invisible to eye, finding the microscopic roughness, the tiny crystals of basalt, the variations in surface that provide friction. They can hang from their front claws alone, rear legs dangling, while they test the wall below for the next hold. They can wedge themselves between opposing walls and rest, suspended, supported by pressure and friction and the precise distribution of their weight.

Their tails are as important as their legs. Prehensile, muscular, covered in the same fine scales as their bodies but with a softer ventral surface that grips like leather. They use their tails as a fifth point of contact, wrapping them around protrusions, letting them trail against walls to sense space in darkness, sometimes hanging briefly by tail alone while repositioning their feet. Young cats play by hanging inverted from machinery, gripping only with their tails, building the strength they'll need in the fissures.

They make almost no sound in daily life. No growls, no hisses, no purrs. They communicate through posture and touch and occasionally through scent—they have glands beneath their jaws that produce a marker they rub onto stone, onto each other, onto the industrial installations they use as territory boundaries. The scent is subtle, almost neutral, with a faint mineral quality like wet slate.

But during mating season—which comes to different families at different times, staggered across the year—they produce the hum. It starts in the chest, resonates through the body cavity, transfers through their feet into the basalt itself. A frequency in the subsonic range that travels better through stone than air. Other cats feel it before they hear it: a vibration in their bones, in their teeth, in the inner ear. It says *here* and *available* and *territory claimed*. It carries for kilometers through the interconnected basalt. The whole plateau becomes an instrument, vibrating with the announcements of descent cats seeking mates.

They hunt primarily at night, though "hunt" suggests more activity than the reality. Mostly they wait. They position themselves near fissures where the thread-lizards and hook-mice emerge to feed on the microbiotic crust. They lie motionless, sometimes for hours, their night-vision watching the approaches. When prey moves within range, they strike—one motion, explosive, the front claws extending and closing in the same moment. It's over in a second. Then back to stillness, eating slowly, methodically, wasting nothing.

They descend into the fissures to hunt the blind fish and translucent salamanders, but also for other reasons harder to name. They spend time below that serves no obvious purpose. Sometimes a cat will descend and remain underground for a day and a night, emerging eventually with no visible result—no prey, no fresh scent marks, nothing accomplished. They seem to simply need the depth, the darkness, the enclosure of stone. They are more themselves in the fissures than on the surface.

Family groups hold territories measured in tens of kilometers. The territories overlap slightly—there are zones of shared access, fissures used by multiple families, watering chambers reached by different descent routes. Conflicts are rare. When two families meet they usually acknowledge each other and separate. Occasionally there are displays: standing tall, extending claws, opening mouths to show the teeth they rarely use. Actual fighting almost never happens. A family that loses members cannot hold as large a territory, so they contract, yield space, consolidate. A family that grows expands gradually into adjacent areas. The whole system shifts slowly, a liquid distribution across the plateau.

The females are slightly larger than the males and make the territorial decisions. They choose the den sites—usually chambers thirty or forty meters down in the fissures, accessible only through climbs difficult enough to deter most threats. They birth there in darkness, producing litters of two or three young that are born blind and helpless and remain underground for their first weeks. The males bring food to the fissure entrance; the females descend to retrieve it. Eventually the young emerge, pale from their time without sun, and begin learning the surface.

Teaching the young to descend takes months. Adults demonstrate on shallow fissures first—only a few meters deep, wide enough for easy climbing. The young watch, then attempt, clumsy and fearful. They fall often in the beginning, but the adults position themselves below to catch them, or the fissures are chosen for having ledges that prevent long falls. Gradually the young learn: how to test stone, how to distribute weight, how to trust their claws, how to become creatures of vertical space.

They live perhaps fifteen years, sometimes twenty. They gray as they age, their scales losing luster, their movements becoming stiffer. Old descent cats climb less frequently into the fissures, spending more time on the surface, hunting the easier prey. Eventually they slow enough that hunting fails, and then they descend one final time to the deep chambers and do not return. The others do not retrieve the bodies. The fissures keep them.

They seem to understand the industrial installations as permanent features, like the basalt plates or the thornbush. They den under machinery, use drill-holes as convenient access to the depths, rub their scent markers on rusted metal with the same casual ownership they show toward stone. The depot is divided among three families whose territories meet there—it serves as a kind of neutral ground where they tolerate each other's presence. Young cats play on the depot's machinery, learning to climb on frameworks that are more regular and predictable than natural stone, building confidence.

They avoid the incomprehensible installations. The tiled pit, the spiral pipework, the vast concrete pad—these they detour around, giving them space measured in hundreds of meters. If forced by circumstance to pass near one—driven by territorial pressure, pursuing prey, fleeing the rare grass fire that sometimes ignites from lightning—they move quickly, low to the ground, showing signs of distress. They do not mark these areas. They do not hunt near them. It's as if the installations emit something the cats can sense but others cannot, some quality of wrongness that their instincts recognize and reject.

When the fissures emit the strange cold exhalations, the cats react with alert curiosity rather than fear. They approach the breathing cracks, stand at the edges, sometimes descend partway and pause, sensing. They seem to be gathering information, though what information and for what purpose is unclear. Afterward they often stay near that fissure for hours, watching it, as if expecting something further.

They watch the sky. Not constantly, but regularly, particularly at night. They track the thermal-kites by day as potential threat—the kites are large enough to consider a juvenile cat as prey. But at night they watch empty sky, their large eyes fixed on apparently vacant darkness, following something that moves or exists there, maintaining visual contact for minutes at a time. Then they look away, return to normal behavior, offer no explanation through their postures or actions.

Sometimes, rarely, a descent cat will behave aberrantly. It will separate from its family, wander alone, spend days moving across the plateau without apparent purpose or destination. It will stop eating. It will descend into fissures and emerge immediately, descend again, over and over, as if searching for something it cannot find. The other cats avoid it. After days or weeks of this behavior, it will either rejoin normal life—reintegrating with its family or finding a new one—or it will vanish. The others do not follow when one vanishes. They seem to understand it as a known risk, a thing that happens, part of the terms of existence here.

But mostly they persist in their patterns: the hunting, the resting, the slow climbing through darkness to reach water and prey and the deep chambers where they are most themselves. They are the plateau's primary consciousness, its main capacity for awareness and intention and memory. They know the stone intimately. They know its breathing, its hidden hydrology, its rules. They have become native here across generations, shaped by the stone and shaping their lives to fit its offers and demands.

## The Thermal-Kites

They are the plateau's surveillance, though they watch without interpreting. They ride the thermals that form over the sun-heated basalt, spiraling upward on columns of rising air, barely moving their wings, gaining altitude through pure patience. From above, the plateau reveals its patterns—the fissure network, the distribution of vegetation, the locations of the industrial installations, the territories of the descent cats rendered visible through paths of regular movement.

They are large birds, improbable in their scale. Three-meter wingspans that seem too vast for the body they support—the body is surprisingly small, compact, mostly hollow bone and flight muscle with minimum mass devoted to anything else. The wings are broad and slotted, the primary feathers at the tips spreading like fingers to spill air and create the lift that keeps them aloft for hours without effort. They are colored in earth tones: brown and rust and weathered grey, countershaded, dark above and pale below, difficult to see against either sky or stone depending on angle.

Their heads are small and their eyes are large and forward-facing, giving them binocular vision, depth perception, the ability to judge distances necessary for the killing strike. The beaks are hooked and sharp. The feet are equipped with talons, though smaller than one might expect for a bird this size—they hunt prey that doesn't require massive talons to subdue.

They make almost no sound except during breeding season. Their flight is silent—no whistle of wings, no cry to announce themselves. They are presence without sound, shadows that cross the land, shapes that circle in the high air. During most of the year a thermal-kite seen is a thermal-kite silent.

They hunt by watching. They circle in the thermals, sometimes at altitudes where they are barely visible from the ground, scanning the plateau below with vision that can resolve a hook-mouse from hundreds of meters up. They watch for movement: the scatter of thread-lizards through wire-grass, the emergence of creatures from the fissures, the shift of anything living against the static background of stone. When they see prey, they adjust their circle, tighten it, losing altitude gradually, positioning themselves. Then they fold and drop.

The strike comes from above and behind the prey's line of sight. They fall in silence, wings half-folded to control descent, adjusting with tiny motions of feathers, trajectory calculated through instinct and practice. At the last moment the talons swing forward, the wings flare to brake the fall, and they take the prey in a motion that looks gentle despite its lethality. Then back into the air, wings working now, carrying the weight upward to find another thermal, or gliding toward a perch where they can eat.

They nest on the broken edges of the plateau, on the isolated towers and mesas where the basalt fragments into verticality. The nests are large constructions of wire-grass and thornbush branches, built over years, added to each season, becoming substantial platforms wedged into cliff faces or balanced on tower tops. The same nests are used generation after generation, some of them decades old, their foundations compacted into solid masses that have become part of the rock formations.

Breeding is not annual but follows some longer cycle—perhaps tied to the descent cat population, which forms their primary prey, or to rainfall patterns that affect the overall abundance of the plateau's life. Some years every thermal-kite on the plateau seems to breed simultaneously. Other years almost none do. When breeding happens, the silence breaks.

The calls are harsh and raucous, somewhere between a hawk's scream and a crow's caw, carrying across kilometers of open air. The birds become territorial, aggressive, vocal. They chase each other in aerial combat that looks playful but serves deadly serious purposes—establishing hierarchy, defending nesting sites, proving fitness. Two thermal-kites in a territorial dispute will gain altitude together, circling each other, calling constantly, until one makes a move—diving at the other, forcing it to evade, pursuing. These chases can last for minutes, covering vast distances, the birds diving and climbing and rolling with a maneuverability their soaring flight doesn't suggest they possess.

Pairs form, though whether they maintain pair bonds between breeding seasons is unclear—the birds are too widely distributed and too similar in appearance to track individuals reliably. The pairs build or repair their nests together, both birds bringing material, arranging it with their beaks and feet. Courtship involves aerial displays: synchronized soaring, where both birds ride the same thermal in perfect parallel; mutual diving, where they drop together in spirals that intertwine; talon-grappling, where they lock feet and tumble through the air in controlled falls that seem catastrophic but end with smooth separation and resumed flight.

They lay two eggs, sometimes three. The eggs are pale, speckled, surprisingly large. Both parents incubate, trading places in shifts that last hours. The one not incubating hunts and brings food. They feed each other at the nest, a ritual of beak-to-beak transfer that looks tender against the harshness of their calls and territorial violence.

The young hatch blind and helpless, covered in grey down that will be replaced by juvenile feathers over weeks. Both parents feed them, regurgitating prey directly into their gaping mouths. The young grow rapidly, their appetites enormous, demanding constant hunting. The parents range wider during this period, hunting at the edges of their territories, sometimes crossing into others' ranges and triggering conflicts. The sky becomes a network of tensions as breeding thermal-kites balance the need to feed young against the need to defend space.

The young fledge after months in the nest, their first flights awkward and brief, flapping flight that lacks the efficiency of adult soaring. They learn gradually: how to find thermals, how to position themselves in rising air, how to translate the flapping of youth into the stillness of adult flight. The parents continue to feed them for weeks after fledging, though with decreasing frequency. Eventually the young wander off, searching for unoccupied ranges, or remain at the edges of their parents' territory until the next breeding cycle pushes them out completely.

They are long-lived. A thermal-kite might see thirty years, forty, possibly more. They mature slowly—not breeding until their fourth or fifth year. The oldest birds are recognizable by size alone, having grown incrementally throughout their lives until they are noticeably larger than young adults. These old birds hold the best territories, the nesting sites most protected from weather, the ranges with the highest prey density. They defend these positions with experience rather than energy, knowing how to threaten effectively, when to fight and when to concede minor intrusions, how to maintain status without exhausting themselves.

They roost at night on high points: the depot tower, the tops of drilling installations, the broken mesas. They tuck their heads beneath their wings and become motionless, though not unaware. They wake at intervals to look around, check the sky, monitor the plateau below. Sometimes they shift position, stretch, preen briefly, then return to rest. They are never deeply asleep in the way of mammals—always partially alert, ready to launch if necessary.

They interact little with the descent cats beyond predation. An adult cat is too large to hunt, but juveniles are vulnerable. The cats are aware of this and become intensely alert when thermal-kites circle above their families. The adults position themselves between the birds and the young, ready to strike if a kite dives. The kites know the risks—an adult cat can leap surprisingly high, can strike fast enough to damage or kill a bird at the bottom of its stoop. So they hunt juvenile cats only when desperate or when an opportunity presents with unusual clarity: a young cat separated from its family, hunting alone, unaware of the shadow that grows above it.

Sometimes thermal-kites mob the descent cats for reasons unclear. A group of birds will circle above a cat family, calling harshly, diving close but not striking, forcing the cats to remain alert and defensive. This can continue for an hour or more before the birds lose interest and return to normal hunting. It resembles territorial defense but doesn't correlate with nest locations or breeding season. Perhaps it's a cultural behavior, learned and passed on, serving purposes that made sense generations ago but now persist as tradition.

They ignore the small creatures—thread-lizards, hook-mice, most insects. These are too small for the energy expenditure of hunting them from altitude. The thermal-kites need prey large enough to justify the hunt: juvenile cats, wire-hoppers, occasionally the pale fish from the fissures if the birds happen to see them in pools exposed by evaporation. They are specialists of the middle scale, too large to bother with insects, too small to threaten the adults of most species.

They sometimes land on the industrial installations and rest there, perched on machinery, folding their enormous wings with care. They defecate casually, whitewash accumulating on the rusted metal, adding to the weathering. They preen, drawing their beaks through their feathers, maintaining the integrity of the structures that keep them airborne. They watch the plateau from these perches with the same scanning attention they give from the air, always monitoring, always tracking movement, always hungry or preparing to be hungry soon.

During the strange cold exhalations from the fissures, the thermal-kites react by gaining altitude. They spiral upward, climbing higher than their normal hunting height, and circle there until the exhalation ceases. They seem to want distance from whatever produces that wrongness, that reversal of expected temperature. When they descend again they avoid the fissure that exhaled for hours afterward, hunting other areas, giving it space.

They are drawn to the bioluminescence. When a fissure glows with the cold blue light of disturbed polyps, thermal-kites will sometimes circle above it at night, visible only as shapes that block stars, their presence revealed by their movement against the fixed points of the sky. What they're doing is unclear—they don't hunt at night, don't need light to navigate, don't interact with the phenomenon in any obvious way. They simply watch it from above, circling, observing, until the glow fades. Then they return to their roosts.

They have been observed—rarely—at the incomprehensible installations. Perching on the rim of the tiled pit, walking along the spiral pipework. They show no particular avoidance but also no attraction. They treat these structures as they treat all structures: potential perches, vantage points, markers in the landscape. If they sense whatever wrongness the descent cats perceive, they don't express it through their behavior.

They die in the air sometimes. Old birds losing the strength to stay aloft, or injured birds failing, dropping out of their soar and falling to the basalt below. The bodies are scavenged by the hook-mice and the small things, picked apart quickly, leaving only bones and the large feathers. The feathers scatter across the plateau, caught in thornbush, wedged in fissures, bleaching in the sun. They mark the passage of thermal-kites through time and space, the deaths that feed back into the landscape.

But mostly they persist in flight, in the eternal circling, in the patient watching from the sky. They are the plateau's eyes, the perspective that sees pattern and motion from above. They know the place as a whole in a way ground-dwellers cannot. They know where the prey concentrations move, where the water appears at the edges, where the fissures network most densely. They hold the map in their instincts and pass it to their young through genetic memory and demonstrated flight paths. They are the plateau aware of itself from the outside, looking down, always watching, always hungry, always riding the thermals that the stone produces from its interaction with the sun.

## The Small Ones

They are numbered in billions, distributed across the plateau in densities that would seem sparse if measured per square meter but accumulate to overwhelming presence across the continental scale. They are the foundation of biomass here, the food web's base, the cycles that turn the microbiotic crust into movement and the movement back into crust when they die.

### Thread-Lizards

Each one is the length of a human finger, thin enough to thread through the wire-grass that names them, colored in the same silver-grey as the grass so that they vanish when motionless. Their scales are minute, giving them an almost smooth appearance, and they move with liquid precision through their habitat.

They hunt insects smaller than themselves: mites that graze the microbiotic crust, flying things that hover over the fissures, the larvae of various beetles that burrow in the thin soil that accumulates between basalt plates. They hunt by ambush and by pursuit. They wait in the grass until movement reveals prey, then strike—fast for their size, though in absolute terms not particularly quick. Or they thread through the grass stalks in active search, their heads swiveling, their tongues flicking to taste air, following chemical trails to their prey.

They are cold-blooded but manage their temperature through behavior. Morning finds them on the basalt plates, lying flat, absorbing warmth from the stone, becoming active as their body temperature rises. Midday drives them into the shade—under thornbush, against the edges of fissures, anywhere that offers relief from the direct heat. Evening brings another period of activity. Night sends them into hiding, into crevices, into the grass itself, where they become torpid and vulnerable.

They are solitary except during breeding, which happens after rain when the flush of insect activity provides abundant food. Males display by rising on their front legs and bobbing their heads, showing the bright orange dewlap under their throats—the only color they possess. Females respond or ignore. Mating is brief and ungraceful. The females lay eggs in the thin soil, tiny leathery orbs buried just deep enough to hide them. The eggs are abandoned. Most are eaten by hook-mice or other thread-lizards. A few survive, hatching into miniature versions of adults that must immediately begin hunting or be hunted.

They live perhaps three years. Predation keeps most from reaching that age. They are prey to everything larger: descent cats, wire-hoppers, other thread-lizards, hook-mice, the hook-worms that emerge from fissures. Their strategy is abundance and rapid reproduction. Their population fluctuates wildly with the rains, booming after precipitation, crashing in the dry periods, maintaining some baseline that slowly grows and shrinks with the larger cycles of prey availability.

They are silent except for the faint rustle of their movement through grass. They have no calls, no territorial displays beyond the breeding bob, no sounds at all. They are the plateau's quiet motion, its background animation, the movement that the thermal-kites watch for from above.

### Hook-Mice

They are not mice—they are convergent on the mouse concept, filling that ecological role, but their ancestry is other. They are the size of a fist, covered in coarse fur that ranges from grey to rust-brown to a pale tan that matches the basalt. Their most distinctive feature is the arrangement of their front teeth: paired hooks that curve outward, used for gripping insects and for grooming fur, for fighting rivals and for manipulating the seeds they cache in tiny hoards hidden in basalt crevices.

They are nocturnal by preference, emerging from their daytime retreats as the warmth lessens, searching for food in the hours between dusk and dawn. They eat insects, seeds from the wire-grass and thornbush, the eggs of thread-lizards, carrion when available, and occasionally the microbiotic crust itself when nothing else can be found. They are generalists, adapted to take advantage of any food source, surviving through flexibility rather than specialization.

Their strategy when threatened is freezing. They stop moving, stop breathing visibly, become absolutely still. Their coloring helps—they resemble small stones when motionless. They can maintain this freeze for minutes, waiting for the threat to pass. If the threat persists or gets too close, they break and run, fleeing in erratic patterns, using the cover of wire-grass and thornbush and the edges of fissures. They are faster than they look, capable of surprising bursts of speed.

They den in shallow depressions in the basalt, often under thornbush or against the base of industrial installations. They line these dens with wire-grass and their own shed fur, creating slight insulation against the minor temperature swings between night and day. They are loosely social—dens often house pairs or small family groups, though the groups have minimal interaction beyond sharing space. They tolerate each other rather than seeking each other out.

Breeding is opportunistic, triggered by food availability. A well-fed female will produce litters of four or five young every few months. The young are born blind and helpless, developing rapidly, weaned within weeks, capable of independence within months. The parents provide minimal care—warmth, protection of the den, food brought to the young while they're immobile. Once mobile, the young hunt for themselves. Most die in their first season. Those that survive disperse, seeking their own dens, sometimes traveling kilometers across the plateau before settling.

They are prey to descent cats primarily, but also to the wire-hoppers that share their nocturnal activity period, and occasionally to thread-lizards large enough to take juvenile hook-mice. They are aware of their vulnerability and spend much of their active time in tension—feeding quickly, pausing frequently to scan for threats, never far from cover. Their lives are measured in months of hypervigilance punctuated by brief moments of relaxation in their dens.

They make faint squeaking sounds when in their dens—communication between family members, contact calls that maintain social cohesion. They fight with each other over den sites and food sources, grappling briefly with their hooked teeth, usually breaking off before serious injury occurs. They have territories of a sort—areas they know well and defend casually—but the territories overlap significantly and boundaries are fluid.

They cache food obsessively, hiding seeds and dead insects in crevices, returning to these caches when hunting is poor. They remember cache locations with surprising accuracy, though not perfectly—some caches are forgotten, their seeds eventually sprouting if conditions allow, distributing wire-grass and thornbush across the plateau through this accidental gardening.

### Globe Beetles

They are the size of a thumbnail, their carapaces perfectly spherical, giving them their name. The carapaces are textured with microscopic grooves that collect atmospheric moisture—dew when it forms, the humidity that comes from the fissures' exhalations. They drink this condensed water without ever needing to find free water, surviving on what they gather from the air itself.

They are detritivores, feeding on dead plant matter, the droppings of other animals, the microbiotic crust during its dormant phases. They wander the basalt at night, their movements slow and deliberate, mandibles working constantly as they graze the thin layer of organic matter that accumulates on the stone. They are numerous enough that their combined grazing has ecological impact—they process the plateau's dead matter, breaking it down, returning it to forms that the crust can reabsorb.

They can retract completely into their carapaces, sealing the opening with a flexible membrane that makes them nearly impervious to small predators. Thread-lizards largely ignore them—too much effort for too little food. Hook-mice can crack them if hungry enough, but usually bypass them for easier meals. The beetles' main predation comes from the bristle-worms that can inject digestive enzymes through the seal, liquefying the beetle inside its own shell.

They live for years—unusually long-lived for their size. They grow slowly, adding layers to their carapaces incrementally, the layers visible as faint rings when examined closely. Old beetles are noticeably larger than young, their carapaces thicker, more weathered, sometimes scarred from predation attempts that failed to breach the shell.

They reproduce through some chemical trigger—likely humidity-related. When conditions are right, beetles congregate in areas where fissures exhale dampness. They pair up through processes of chemical signaling too subtle to observe directly. They mate at the edges of fissures, the act lasting hours, both beetles motionless afterward as if exhausted. Females lay eggs in the cracks of the basalt, cementing them in place with secretions that harden into waterproof coverings. The eggs remain dormant until rain comes, then hatch rapidly, the larvae emerging to feed on the rain-activated crust.

The larvae look nothing like adults—soft-bodied, pale, grub-like. They are vulnerable and remain hidden in crevices, extending only their feeding parts to graze nearby surfaces. They molt through several instars before pupating. The pupae are encased in cells built from cemented particles of basalt, becoming part of the stone. Eventually adults emerge, their carapaces soft at first, hardening over days, achieving the perfect spherical form that defines them.

They are the plateau's recyclers, its decomposers, the cycle that prevents accumulation of the dead and returns all matter to circulation. Without them the sparse organic matter would accumulate in locked forms, unavailable to the crust, breaking the food web's base. They are small and slow and easy to overlook, but they are essential infrastructure, as important as the hidden water to the plateau's ability to support life.

### Bristle-Worms

They live in the fissures primarily, emerging at night to graze the microbiotic crust on the surface. They are segmented, covered in fine bristles that give them their name, capable of extending to the length of a forearm though usually seen compressed to half that. They are pale—nearly white—adapted to the darkness of the fissures, their few light-sensing organs reduced to simple photoreceptive patches that detect light intensity without forming images.

They move through muscular contractions that pass along their segments in waves, pulling themselves forward with surprising speed when necessary. The bristles provide traction, gripping microscopic irregularities in the stone. They can climb vertical surfaces, cross ceilings, move through spaces barely wide enough to admit them by compressing their segments and extending through in sequence.

They graze the crust methodically, their feeding parts rasping the surface, consuming the bacteria and fungi and primitive plants, leaving clean stone in their wake. They are selective—they seem to prefer certain species in the crust over others, grazing in patterns that create mosaics of different crust compositions. This selective grazing maintains the crust's diversity, preventing any single species from dominating.

They are predators as well as grazers. They can sense vibrations through the stone and will approach sources of vibration to investigate. If the source is suitable prey—a beetle, a small injured animal, anything immobile—they attack by injecting digestive enzymes through a retractable proboscis. The enzymes liquefy tissue. The worm then drinks the liquefied matter, leaving only indigestible parts.

They reproduce through fragmentation and through sexual reproduction. If injured or stressed, a bristle-worm can break itself into segments, each segment potentially regenerating into a full individual. This allows rapid population recovery after predation or environmental stress. Sexual reproduction happens in the water chambers of the fissures, where worms congregate seasonally, releasing gametes into the water in synchronized spawning events. The fertilized eggs develop into larvae that drift through the underground hydrology before settling and growing into juvenile worms.

They are prey to the descent cats, which hunt them in the fissures, and to the blind fish, which ambush them in the water chambers. On the surface they are vulnerable to wire-hoppers and occasionally to thread-lizards large enough to overpower them. They defend themselves by curling into tight coils, protecting their vulnerable head and tail regions, presenting only their bristled segments to attackers. The bristles can irritate mouth parts and eyes, making them unpleasant prey.

They are sensitive to light and will retreat rapidly if illuminated, fleeing into the nearest fissure. They seem particularly disturbed by the bioluminescence in the fissures—when the polyps glow, the bristle-worms disappear, hiding in the darkest recesses, not emerging until the light fades. Whether they're avoiding predation made easier by the light or reacting to some quality of the bioluminescence itself is unclear.

They leave trails—not visible trails, but chemical ones, pheromones that other bristle-worms can detect. These trails guide worms to good feeding areas, to water sources, to mating aggregations. The trails create an invisible map of the plateau's surface and fissures, a network of information accessible only to creatures with the right sensory apparatus.

### Wire-Hoppers

They are small birds, sparrow-sized, named for their habit of perching on and hopping along the wire-grass stalks. They are brown and grey with sharp beaks adapted for gleaning seeds and catching insects. They are the plateau's most numerous birds, filling niches that in other landscapes might be occupied by a dozen different species—here there are just wire-hoppers, generalized, adaptable, successful through flexibility.

They are active during the day, though they adjust their activity to avoid peak heat. Morning and evening see the most movement—foraging, singing, territorial disputes. Midday they shelter in the shade of thornbush or the frameworks of industrial installations, sitting quietly, conserving energy, panting slightly to dissipate heat.

They eat primarily seeds from wire-grass and thornbush, supplemented with insects when available. After rain, when insect abundance spikes, they shift to almost entirely insectivorous diet, gorging on the sudden bounty. They feed their young exclusively on insects—the protein necessary for growth. Outside breeding season they return to granivory, their digestive systems handling the shift with practiced efficiency.

They nest in thornbush primarily, building small cup nests from grass and plant fibers, lined with their own feathers and sometimes with shed fur from hook-mice. They nest opportunistically in the industrial installations—in the machinery's crevices, under roof overhangs, inside empty tanks. These nests are safer from predators but more exposed to temperature extremes and less sheltered from rain.

They breed after rain when insect populations surge. Males sing from prominent perches—the tops of thornbush, machinery, exposed basalt plates. The songs are simple, repetitive, carrying across the quiet plateau, creating a soundscape that lasts for weeks until breeding concludes. Pairs form quickly, build nests quickly, lay eggs—usually four—and raise young in a rush to take advantage of the temporary abundance.

Both parents feed the young, making constant trips between nest and foraging areas, bringing beakfuls of insects and spiders and beetle larvae. The young grow rapidly, fledging within weeks, still awkward but capable enough to escape nests that have become crowded and obvious to predators. Juvenile mortality is high—thermal-kites take many, as do descent cats and occasionally the bristle-worms if young birds land near fissures.

They are social in their way, nesting semi-colonially, with multiple pairs building nests in the same thornbush thicket or the same installation. They defend immediate nest areas but tolerate close neighbors. They mob predators collectively—descent cats that venture too close during nesting season are surrounded by dive-bombing wire-hoppers, their calls harsh and scolding, the attacks not dangerous but annoying enough to drive cats away.

They bathe in the rare surface water after rain, splashing vigorously, shaking their feathers, preening afterward with oil from glands at the base of their tails. They dust-bathe in dry periods, finding patches of fine dust and working it through their feathers to control parasites. They are meticulous about feather maintenance, spending significant time each day preening, keeping flight feathers aligned, keeping body plumage clean.

They have a relationship with the descent cats that goes beyond predation. They follow resting cat families, landing nearby, hopping around them, picking through their fur for parasites and loose scales. The cats tolerate this, sometimes even seem to invite it, lying still, adjusting position to allow the birds access. It's unclear if the cats derive actual benefit or if they simply tolerate the birds because they're too small to bother hunting and their presence causes no harm.

They live three or four years typically. They are replaced constantly by new generations. Their population fluctuates with the rain cycles—booming after good rains, crashing during long dry periods, maintaining overall stability through rapid reproduction during favorable times.

They are the plateau's voice during daylight hours. Their songs, their contact calls, their alarm calls when predators appear—these provide an audible layer of information about the plateau's state. Silent wire-hoppers indicate danger nearby. Singing wire-hoppers indicate safety, food, the proper functioning of their world.

### The Others

There are more—species less numerous or less visible or less easily described:

The translucent salamanders that live in the water chambers, their internal organs visible through their skin, their movements slow and considered, their lives spent in darkness that has made their eyes vestigial but enhanced other senses to compensate.

The blind fish—pale, elongated, their lateral lines exquisitely sensitive to water movement, hunting by sensing pressure waves, congregating in the deepest pools where the water never evaporates, a population divided among isolated chambers, evolving separately in each, diversifying slowly into forms that might eventually be called separate species.

The filter-feeding polyps that attach to the walls of flooded chambers, their tentacles extended into the water, capturing microscopic organisms, bioluminescing when disturbed as a defense mechanism that may startle predators or attract their predators in turn—the purpose unclear but the effect undeniable, those cold blue glows that seep through fissures and create light in the plateau's depths.

The various insects—too many to catalog fully, but including: the flies that appear after rain, their larvae developing in temporary pools; the moths that pollinate the thornbush flowers, emerging only during the rare flowering events; the ants that build small colonies in basalt crevices, farming fungi on collected organic matter; the spiders that weave webs between wire-grass stalks, catching the small flying things; the mantises that wait motionless on thornbush, colored perfectly to match the grey branches, striking when thread-lizards or young wire-hoppers pass within range.

Each species is sparse in any given location but numerous across the plateau's scale. Each has found its strategy for extracting life from the minimal resources available. Each contributes to cycles of predation, decomposition, nutrient transfer. Each is woven into the web that holds the plateau's ecosystem in its sparse but stable configuration.

Together they are the plateau's biomass, its movement, its transformation of stone and sun and water into life. They are small individually, forgettable, often invisible. But collectively they are the foundation that makes descent cats and thermal-kites possible. They are the plateau alive, breathing, cycling, persisting across the spans of time that see industrial installations rise and fall and disappear while the small ones continue, adapt, endure, generation after generation, finding their ways through the heat and the sparseness and the vast flat expanse that extends beyond any horizon they could conceive of or care about.

## The Incomprehensible

There are humans. Not many. Not often. But they come sometimes, and they have never entirely left.

### The Maintenance Workers

They arrive without schedule—months or years between appearances. They come in vehicles that move low over the basalt, riding on cushions of air or on wheels that somehow manage the fissures. The vehicles are weathered, functional, old in a way that suggests they've been making these trips for decades. They produce sound—engine hum, the hiss of air cushions, the mechanical grind of suspension systems absorbing the rough passage over the plateau.

There are usually two or three workers per vehicle. They wear clothes in faded colors—orange or yellow that has aged to rust-tones, greys that match the basalt. They wear hats with broad brims against the sun. They move with the economy of people conserving energy in heat, and with the casual confidence of people who have been here many times before.

They come to service the installations. They bring tools and replacement parts—valves, seals, sensors, components whose purposes are unclear. They work on the drill stations, opening access panels, checking readings on instruments that may or may not still function, replacing parts according to some maintenance schedule or checklist. They perform these tasks with thoroughness but without urgency, working through the heat of the day, breaking occasionally to drink water from containers they carry, to sit in the shade of their vehicles and rest.

They speak to each other rarely and briefly—short exchanges about the work, confirmations of measurements, requests for tools. Their voices are quiet, almost defeated in tone, as if they're performing duties they know are ultimately futile but continue from habit or obligation. They never speak loudly enough to disturb the plateau's quiet significantly.

They interact minimally with the fauna. They ignore the wire-hoppers that perch on machinery near them. They watch descent cats from a distance but make no attempt to approach or avoid them particularly. When they encounter a thermal-kite nest on an installation they're servicing, they work around it, leaving the nest undisturbed. They seem to view the animals as the rightful inhabitants and themselves as temporary intruders.

They eat meals sitting on the basalt or on the steps of their vehicles—simple food from containers, eaten without ceremony. They produce small amounts of waste, which they collect carefully and take with them when they leave. They urinate in the fissures, adding their own small contribution to the underground hydrology.

They sleep in their vehicles or sometimes in the depot's intact buildings, unrolling sleeping pads, lying down in the heat, sleeping fitfully if their shifting positions and occasional waking are any indication. They do not seem comfortable here, but neither do they seem particularly distressed. They endure the conditions as part of the job.

Sometimes they take readings from the incomprehensible installations—the tiled pit, the spiral pipework, the concrete pad. They approach these structures with the same casual thoroughness they show elsewhere, setting up equipment, recording data on handheld devices, dismantling the equipment and moving on. They show no more caution or concern around these structures than around the simple drill stations. Either they don't sense what the descent cats sense or they've learned to ignore it.

They stay for days typically—a week at most. They work through multiple installations, following a route that seems planned in advance. Then they pack their vehicles, perform final checks, and depart. The vehicles trace back across the plateau in the direction they came, disappearing eventually beyond the horizon, their engine sounds fading until the plateau's silence reasserts itself completely.

They leave the installations slightly altered—some parts newer, some panels closed that were open, some instruments reset or recalibrated. But the overall impression remains one of slow decay. Whatever they're maintaining, they're not maintaining it successfully. They're slowing the collapse, not preventing it. They seem to know this.

### The Surveyors

They come less frequently than the maintenance workers—years or decades between visits. They arrive with more equipment, more purpose, more systematic investigation. They are mapping something, measuring something, investigating something. What exactly is never clear.

They are usually groups of four to six people. They establish a base camp at the depot or at one of the larger drill stations. They set up temporary structures—shelters made of fabric and poles, equipment stations powered by portable generators that hum through the night and disturb the plateau's fauna until the animals adjust and learn to ignore the sound.

They deploy sensors across the plateau—small devices placed at regular intervals, left in place for days or weeks while the team occupies the base camp. They visit the sensors periodically, collecting data, performing maintenance, repositioning them according to criteria that guide their placement. They map the fissure networks, sometimes descending into them with lights and ropes and recording equipment, emerging hours later dusty and tired.

They take core samples from the basalt—drilling small holes, extracting cylinders of stone, labeling and storing them carefully. They sample the water from the underground chambers, lowering containers on lines, hauling up water that they test with portable equipment, recording results, pouring most of it back. They collect specimens of the fauna—thread-lizards, hook-mice, insects—preserving them in bottles, cataloging them, treating them with a care that suggests scientific rather than utilitarian purpose.

They are more vocal than the maintenance workers. They discuss their findings, debate interpretations, express confusion or satisfaction with results. They are clearly educated, specialized, brought here for their expertise. But their expertise seems only partially applicable—the plateau presents them with puzzles they can describe but not solve, patterns they can measure but not explain.

They treat the incomprehensible installations with more respect or caution than the maintenance workers show. They approach the tiled pit carefully, taking readings from various distances, deploying sensors around its perimeter, descending into it on ropes to examine the ceramic tiles more closely. They spend time at the spiral pipework, following its geometry, testing the pipes for... something. Contents, residue, radiation, magnetic fields—some property they can detect with their equipment but that means nothing to outside observation.

They avoid working at the incomprehensible installations at night. They schedule their investigations for daylight hours and return to base camp before darkness. Whether this is protocol or superstition or practical safety measure is unclear. But the pattern is consistent across different survey teams and different visits.

They interact more with the fauna than the maintenance workers do. They observe the descent cats extensively, filming them, recording their behavior, taking non-invasive samples when possible—shed scales, droppings. They seem particularly interested in the cats' relationship with the fissures, documenting which cats use which fissures, how deep they descend, how long they remain below. They watch the thermal-kites through scopes, documenting their hunting patterns, their territories, their nesting behaviors.

They react with visible interest when the fissures produce the strange cold exhalations. They rush to deploy sensors, take readings, collect air samples. They film the events, document the temperatures, map which fissures exhale and when. Afterward they review the data intensely, comparing it with previous records they carry in their databases, searching for patterns.

They stay longer than the maintenance workers—weeks sometimes, occasionally months. They work systematically, thorough, patient. Eventually they pack their equipment, dismantle their base camp, load everything into their vehicles, and depart. They leave the plateau as they found it, taking only their samples and data, leaving the sensors behind—whether by accident or intention is unclear, but the sensors remain, slowly weathering, their power sources eventually exhausted, becoming just more artifacts of the industrial presence.

### The Others

Rarely—perhaps once a decade, perhaps less—others come who fit neither category.

Single individuals who arrive alone in vehicles even more weathered than those of the maintenance workers. Who establish no camp but simply wander the plateau for days, moving from installation to installation, sitting for hours at the incomprehensible structures, apparently doing nothing. Who carry no visible equipment except water and basic supplies. Who interact with no one because there is no one to interact with. Who seem to be pilgrims of a sort, visiting sacred or significant sites, though for purposes that remain private.

Groups that arrive with armed escorts and restrict their movements to specific installations, particularly the incomprehensible ones. Who deploy security perimeters, deny access even to the maintenance workers if they overlap. Who work quickly and secretively, their activities shielded by temporary barriers, their vehicles equipped with sensors that might detect approach. Who leave after days or hours, taking whatever they came for or confirming whatever they came to confirm.

Scientists who arrive with specialized equipment that suggests academic rather than corporate or governmental purpose. Who seem to be investigating specific hypotheses—taking readings that require precise timing, deploying instruments configured for particular measurements, collecting samples of extreme specificity. Who show frustration or satisfaction with their results, who confer with distant colleagues through communication equipment, who treat the plateau as a laboratory that might reveal something significant if only the right question is asked in the right way.

And once—documented by a survey team's cameras but never explained—a group that arrived at night, moved to the tiled pit, performed some kind of ritual or ceremony that involved lights and sounds and movements that might have been dance, and then departed before dawn. The footage shows figures in dark clothing, lights arranged around the pit's perimeter, some kind of patterned movement or procession, sounds that might be chanting or singing. Then dispersal, vehicles departing in multiple directions, vanishing into the darkness. The survey team found nothing at the site afterward—no traces, no offerings, no signs of what had been done or why.

## The Presence

The humans are ghosts here, intermittent, insubstantial, having minimal impact on the plateau's ongoing life. They built the installations long ago and they maintain them now in desultory fashion, but they do not inhabit the place. They visit, investigate, service, and leave. The plateau continues between their visits exactly as it continues during them—the cycles unbroken, the animals untroubled except for brief disturbances, the stone indifferent.

The descent cats have learned that humans are not threats and not prey. They watch humans working at installations, observing from the distance with apparent curiosity, sometimes approaching closer when the humans are still and quiet. They seem to categorize humans as a kind of large, slow, occasionally present feature of the landscape—notable but not important, interesting but not concerning.

The thermal-kites learned long ago that human vehicles are not dangerous. They perch on stationary vehicles without hesitation, treating them as they treat any structure. They have never been given reason to fear humans—no human has shot at them, trapped them, or shown aggression. They are unbothered by human presence.

The smaller creatures are either oblivious or quickly habituate. Wire-hoppers nest in active base camps, building in equipment stacks, foraging around the humans' feet. Hook-mice emerge at night regardless of whether humans are sleeping nearby. The thread-lizards hunt insects attracted to human lights. The life of the plateau proceeds with humans as minor, temporary nodes of activity—warm spots that attract insects, shade sources, structures to perch on—but not as predators or competitors or anything requiring behavioral adjustment.

What the humans seek on the plateau is never made explicit. The maintenance suggests the installations still serve some purpose, though what purpose machinery decades old and mostly defunct could serve is unclear. The surveys suggest ongoing questions about the plateau's properties or history or meaning. The incomprehensible installations draw particular attention, implying they hold significance beyond simple industrial function.

But the plateau keeps its secrets. The humans take their measurements and samples and leave without obvious satisfaction. They return years later to repeat the process. The pattern suggests not success but continuation—an ongoing project without conclusion, a question asked repeatedly because no answer has been found or because the answer found requires verification or because the asking itself is the point.

The humans are one more species here, rare and periodic, occupying a niche that involves moving across the plateau, interacting with installations, and departing. They are less numerous than the thermal-kites, less permanent than the descent cats, less integrated than the wire-hoppers. They are visitors who built permanent structures long ago and now return occasionally to remember or maintain or question what those structures were for and whether they still matter and what it means that they're still here while whoever built them is gone.

The plateau accepts their presence as it accepts everything: without judgment, without resistance, incorporating their activities into its vast emptiness and vast patience and the slow cycles that will outlast all species that come here, human and descent cat and thermal-kite and the small ones alike, until the basalt itself erodes away and even the memory of the plateau dissolves into geological time.
