# What Lives There

The **tengril** move through the lattice like thoughts through a vast, distributed mind. Eight-limbed, each roughly the size of a large dog but stretched longer, they possess a physiology engineered by cold: their blood contains glycoproteins that remain fluid at temperatures that would crystallize yours. Their fur grows in three distinct layers—an outer guard layer of hollow, air-trapping hairs; a middle layer so dense that water cannot penetrate it; and an inner layer, finest of all, that lies directly against skin studded with thermoreceptors sensitive enough to detect the body heat of prey three trunk-spans away. 

They are not precisely social, but neither are they solitary. A tengril maintains awareness of approximately seven others in its general territory—not a troop, not a family, but a sort of acoustic neighborhood. They call to each other constantly: low frequency rumbles that travel through trunk-wood as vibration before emerging as sound. If you place your hand on a structural trunk, you can feel these communications as a subtle thrumming, irregular but never absent. The calls convey location, emotional state, possibly more. Tengril who lose their neighbors—through death, migration, or simple separation—exhibit distress behaviors: repetitive movements, failure to hunt efficiently, a particular keening cry that goes unanswered.

Their hunting method exploits the forest's vertical complexity. A tengril will position itself in a trunk-hollow, usually twenty to forty meters up, and simply wait. Not in ambush, exactly—they're visible to anything that looks—but in a state of profound stillness that makes them read as part of the architecture rather than as an animal. They can maintain this for hours, their metabolism dropping into a kind of active torpor, conserving energy while their thermoreceptors scan for the warmth of prey moving through nearby space. When something passes within range, the attack is a single explosive motion: a leap that can span ten meters of empty air, limbs extended, the gripping pads on each foot secreting that anti-freeze compound that prevents slip even on frost-covered bark. The impact kills through blunt force as often as through the bite. They feed methodically, returning to the same kill over several days, defending it from scavengers but not aggressively—more like pointing out a simple fact of ownership.

Tengril do something with the platforms that suggests—though doesn't confirm—understanding. They don't nest on them, precisely, but they rest there. More specifically, they seem to treat platforms as neutral territory. You can observe three or four tengril, animals that would otherwise maintain careful distance, occupying the same platform simultaneously, each in its own quadrant, each engaged in maintenance behaviors: grooming, claw-sharpening, the careful arrangement of fur. They don't interact, don't acknowledge each other overtly, but they also don't display the tension that characterizes their encounters elsewhere in the forest. The platforms function as some kind of social space, a convention the tengril observe despite there being no obvious enforcement mechanism.

When a tengril dies—from injury, from cold, from the simple accumulation of years—the others know. The calls change. For a period of several days, the acoustic neighborhood goes silent except for a single repeated note: a descending frequency sweep that sounds almost like a question. Then the calls resume their normal pattern, but compressed, reorganized around the absence. The body itself is usually consumed by other tengril, though not immediately. It might remain untouched for days, as if even carrion requires acknowledgment first.

The **shrikes** barely seem like animals at all until they move. Clinging to the underside of the canopy, they resemble patches of discoloration—grey-brown, slightly fuzzy, irregular in outline. They're about the size of a human hand, with six limbs that end in branching, almost root-like structures that grip the canopy vegetation at multiple points. Their bodies are dorsoventrally flattened, all their mass distributed across a surface rather than concentrated in a core. This makes them nearly two-dimensional, an adaptation to life in the thin space between canopy layers where there's light but almost no volume.

Shrikes photosynthesize. Not exclusively—they supplement with captured insects and absorbed nutrients from the canopy itself—but their dorsal surface contains chloroplasts acquired through some ancient symbiosis or lateral gene transfer. In the dim blue light that penetrates the upper canopy layers, you can see them arranged in dense colonies: hundreds of individuals overlapping slightly, creating living shingles. They're motionless for hours, growing, metabolizing, being more plant than animal.

Then the movement happens. Something triggers it—a vibration in the canopy, the approach of a predator, the scent of something dying—and the entire colony flows. Not running, not exactly migrating, but redistributing. Each shrike releases its grip, drops a few centimeters, catches itself on new vegetation, pulls forward, releases again. The effect is like watching a carpet restructure itself, individual elements trading places in a coordination that looks choreographed but probably emerges from simple local rules. Within minutes, the colony has relocated three or four meters across the canopy surface, and has settled again into immobility.

They reproduce by budding. A mature shrike develops a protrusion on its lateral edge, which grows slowly over weeks until it differentiates into a smaller copy of the parent. The bud detaches, but usually doesn't migrate far—it settles near the parent, begins its own photosynthesis, becomes another element in the colony matrix. This means colonies are essentially clonal, though genetic exchange happens through a different mechanism: shrikes occasionally fuse. Two unrelated individuals will press their ventral surfaces together, and over a period of days, their tissues merge. They share genetic material, nutrients, even organelles. Then they separate again, each now slightly different, each now carrying information from the other lineage.

The canopy-top grazers eat shrikes constantly, but the colonies persist through sheer reproductive output. A single colony might lose twenty percent of its biomass to grazing in a season, but it replaces that loss within weeks through budding. The relationship isn't quite symbiotic, but it has elements of cultivation: heavy grazing seems to stimulate faster reproduction, and the grazers' waste products fertilize the canopy vegetation that the shrikes grip to, creating a feedback loop where predation enables prey abundance.

Shrikes make no sound, have no apparent sensory organs that would register as eyes or ears, yet respond to stimuli with precision that suggests sophisticated perception. The mechanism is probably distributed—their entire dorsal surface sensitive to light gradients, their entire ventral surface chemosensory, their grip-structures mechanosensory. They are, in a sense, all surface, all interface, creatures that exist almost entirely as boundary conditions.

The **gallim** are the heavy things that walk on top of the canopy. You almost never see them directly—only their shadows moving across the underside of the leaf-ceiling, only the ripples their weight creates, only the sudden condensation-falls when their footsteps flex the vegetation enough to release accumulated water. But sometimes a gallim breaks through.

This happens when the canopy is degraded—disease, storm damage, the death of a particularly large structural plant leaving a weak point in the overhead mat. A gallim takes a step, and instead of the canopy supporting its weight, the surface gives way. The animal falls through, and for a moment—sometimes several moments—it's visible: a body the size of a bison, but not shaped like any familiar mammal. Six legs, arranged in three pairs, each ending in a foot the size of a dinner plate. The foot structure is complex: a central pad surrounded by articulated projections that can spread wide to distribute weight or curl inward for purchase on irregular surfaces. The body is wide and low-slung, the spine nearly horizontal, adapted for walking on surfaces that flex underfoot. The head is small relative to the body, dominated by feeding structures—a wide mouth that can strip vegetation in sweeping bites, teeth that are actually hardened keratin ridges, constantly growing, constantly wearing down.

The coat is the truly strange part. It looks like fur from a distance, but it's not. Each "hair" is actually a modified feather—simple, lacking barbs, but definitively avian in structure. The coat is dense enough that it holds air in thick layers, insulating against the cold at canopy-top level, where wind is constant and temperatures drop further at night. The color is variegated grey-green-brown, camouflaging the animal against the canopy surface when seen from above (by what, though? there are no aerial predators here; perhaps this is an evolutionary holdover from elsewhere, from before the gallim adapted to canopy-walking).

When a gallim breaks through and finds itself in the trunk-space, it reacts with what looks like profound confusion. It doesn't panic, but it stops moving in any directed way. It stands in place, sometimes for hours, while the tengril gather at a cautious distance and watch. The gallim will occasionally take a few steps, test surfaces, but it seems unable to formulate a plan. Eventually—and this is the strange part—it begins to call.

The call is unlike anything else in the forest: a deep, resonant pulse that repeats every few seconds. Not a bellow, not a roar, but something more like a foghorn, a pure tone that carries enormous distances. The sound seems to travel better through the canopy than through open air—it conducts through the living tissue, spreading across kilometers. And somehow, other gallim respond. You hear answering calls from multiple directions. The pattern is coordinated: call and response, building into a network of sound that triangulates on the fallen animal's position.

Then, over hours or days, the canopy above the fallen gallim begins to thicken. You can watch it happen if you're patient: the vegetation overhead knits itself together, layer by layer, until it's strong enough to support weight again. And the gallim, seeming to sense this, begins attempting to climb back up. It uses the structural trunks as aids, bracing against them, pulling itself higher. The process is exhausting—gallim are not climbers—but they persist. Sometimes they succeed, disappearing back into the upper world. Sometimes they fail, and eventually lie down and die in the trunk-space, their bodies becoming resources for the lower-level scavengers.

Gallim social structure, if it exists, is invisible from below. You hear their calls sometimes, patterned exchanges that might be communication or might be simple echolocation. You see evidence of their trails—lines across the canopy where vegetation is compressed, where certain plants are heavily grazed. They seem to move in groups; the shadows rarely travel alone. But whether these are family units, herds, or just individuals following the same resource-rich routes is impossible to determine from below.

Their droppings fall through the canopy occasionally—massive deposits of partially digested plant matter that hit the ground with audible impacts. These become hotspots of activity: shrikes descend from above to feed, tengril come to hunt the animals attracted to the nutrient source, even some of the more specialized trunk-dwelling creatures emerge to scavenge. A single gallim dropping might support a localized ecosystem for weeks.

The **moth-analogs** aren't moths—they're not insects at all, though they converge on similar forms. They're more closely related to the shrikes, though much more mobile, much more specialized. Each one is perhaps ten centimeters across, with four wings made of membranous tissue stretched between elongated finger-bones. The wings are translucent, faintly iridescent, and covered with microscopic structures that diffract the dim light into subtle color patterns.

They emerge from the canopy in massive synchronized flights. This happens several times a year, triggered by cues that aren't obvious—possibly temperature, possibly chemical signals from the canopy plants, possibly just accumulated time. The flights occur at what would be dusk in the lower forest, though the timing suggests they're actually synchronized to the true day-night cycle happening above the canopy. Thousands of moth-analogs pour down through gaps in the vegetation, spiraling in loose helical patterns, filling the trunk-space with motion.

They're searching for the structural trunks themselves, specifically for the fungal growths that colonize the inner surfaces of trunk-hollows. The moth-analogs possess long, coiled feeding tubes that they extend to probe the wood, locating fungal patches by chemoreception. When they find a suitable spot, they feed for several minutes, then resume flight. But they're also mating during these flights—aerial copulations that last seconds, pairs spinning downward together before separating.

The flights attract predators. Tengril wake from their usual torpor and hunt actively, leaping between trunks to snap moth-analogs out of the air. Other creatures emerge too: smaller predators that usually stay hidden in trunk-crevices, things with quick tongues or expanding mouths or nets of sticky silk. The air becomes a complex three-dimensional battlefield, though battlefield is the wrong word—it's too impersonal, too mechanical. The moth-analogs don't seem to fear death; they fly through zones of intense predation with the same spiral patterns they use everywhere else. Perhaps their nervous systems are too simple for fear, or perhaps the synchronization is itself the defense: so many moth-analogs in the air at once that predators quickly satiate and the majority survive.

After feeding and mating, the moth-analogs return to the canopy. But they don't all return to the same locations. Some proportion—maybe ten or twenty percent—drift to new areas, colonizing different parts of the canopy, spreading genetic diversity across the forest's vast area. And some remain in the trunk-space. These individuals seek out crevices in the structural wood and enter a dormant state. They fold their wings tightly, reduce their metabolism to near-zero, and wait. For what? The next flight, perhaps, though they might remain dormant for months or years. Or perhaps they're simply hedge-bets, insurance against catastrophic canopy loss in their home region, sleepers waiting for conditions that might never arrive.

The wings of dead moth-analogs accumulate on the ground, where they last for years—the membranous tissue is resistant to decay in the cold, dry conditions. The wings catch what little light reaches the ground and reflect it in faint rainbow patterns. In some of the stone circles, you find wings arranged intentionally: lines of them, spirals, geometric patterns. The builders collected them. Whether this was art, currency, ritual, or simple appreciation of their beauty is lost information.

The **cilia-worms** live in the canopy itself, but only the old, thick parts where the plant tissue has accumulated enough mass to support internal cavities. They're not worms in any taxonomic sense—they're something else, possibly unique to this forest, possibly a lineage that branched off from some more familiar phylum so long ago that the relationship is unrecognizable.

Each cilia-worm is perhaps a meter long but only as thick as a human thumb. The body is segmented, each segment ringed with thousands of microscopic cilia that beat in coordinated waves. The cilia provide locomotion—the worm flows through the tunnels it excavates in canopy tissue, moving more like a liquid than a crawling thing. It feeds as it moves, secreting enzymes that break down plant cells, then absorbing the resulting nutrients through its ventral surface. Behind it, the tunnel remains open, held patent by mucus secretions that harden into a waxy lining.

These tunnels become infrastructure. Other organisms use them: tiny arthropods, slime molds, root systems from epiphytic plants. The cilia-worms are ecosystem engineers, creating habitable space inside what would otherwise be solid tissue. The tunnels also function as ventilation systems—air moves through them, preventing the deep canopy from becoming anoxic, allowing aerobic metabolism in tissue that would otherwise suffocate.

Cilia-worms are hermaphroditic and reproduce through a process that seems almost botanical. Two worms will encounter each other in the tunnel system—which must happen regularly given the density of tunneling—and exchange gametes during a brief coupling that involves aligning segments and everting specialized reproductive structures. Both worms then carry fertilized eggs, which they deposit in widened chambers along their tunnels. The eggs develop slowly, hatching into miniature versions of the adults that begin their own tunneling immediately, adding to the network.

Sometimes a cilia-worm emerges onto the canopy surface. This seems accidental—they're adapted for enclosed spaces, and exposure to open air causes rapid desiccation. But it happens, and when it does, the worm begins to exhibit strange behavior. It moves in expanding spiral patterns, secreting mucus in quantity, creating a visible trail. The spirals continue until the worm dies, which usually takes less than an hour. These spiral patterns persist on the canopy surface for days, visible as raised lines of dried mucus. They might be death throes, random neural firing as the organism expires. Or they might be signals, information left for other cilia-worms who might encounter the trace later. The pattern is too consistent to seem random, but too context-free to decode.

The canopy's structural integrity depends partially on the cilia-worms. Their tunneling weakens individual plants, but the network they create distributes stress more evenly across the entire canopy. A section of canopy without worm-tunnels tends to be more rigid, more prone to catastrophic failure when overloaded. A well-tunneled section flexes, distributes weight, sheds water more efficiently. The relationship seems mutualistic at the ecosystem level, even though it's parasitic at the individual level.

The **clicking-things** remain largely mysterious because they stay hidden inside the deepest, most inaccessible parts of trunk-hollows. You know they exist because you hear them: a rapid clicking sound, occurring in bursts of five to thirty clicks, then silence, then another burst. The clicks are sharp, brief, containing information in their rhythm that you can't parse. They seem to come from different locations within a single trunk, suggesting either multiple clicking-things or one clicking-thing that moves very quickly.

Occasionally you find evidence of them: marks on the inner trunk surfaces that look like tool-work, regular grooves carved into the wood with precision that suggests intent. You also find small objects—bone splinters, stone fragments, pieces of metal scavenged from the built structures—arranged in the hollows in configurations that seem purposeful but whose purpose remains opaque. Lines of objects. Circles. Symmetrical arrangements that sometimes incorporate the click-marks on the walls, as if the objects and the marks form parts of a larger pattern.

The clicking accelerates when tengril approach. It becomes more rapid, more complex, sometimes developing harmonic overtones as if multiple clicking-sources are coordinating. The tengril respond to this—they avoid trunks where clicking is intense. Whether this is learned behavior (clicking-things are dangerous) or instinctive (clicking patterns trigger avoidance) isn't clear. But the effect is real: certain trunks remain largely untraveled despite being well-positioned in the network.

Once, a researcher managed to deploy a camera inside a trunk-hollow where clicking was frequent. The footage is dark, grainy, but it shows something. Small—maybe the size of a rat—and many-limbed. Moving with wrong articulation, joints bending in ways that suggest either a very different skeleton or no skeleton at all. The clicking comes from the limbs striking each other, or possibly striking the trunk walls, in patterns too fast to follow. The thing seems to be manipulating objects: picking them up, examining them, setting them down in new positions. At one point it approaches the camera directly, and there's a glimpse of something that might be a face—bilateral symmetry at least, two dark spots that could be eyes or could be sensory pits or could be nothing. Then the footage cuts out. The camera was recovered later with its housing partially disassembled, the lens carefully removed and placed nearby, the internal components untouched but the whole thing clearly non-functional.

The clicking-things might be intelligent. Or they might be instinct-driven animals whose behaviors happen to produce patterns humans read as meaningful. There's no way to know without more contact, and they seem determined to avoid that. They don't approach the stone circles, don't interact with the platforms, leave the human-built structures alone except to occasionally scavenge small pieces for their arrangements. If they're aware of humans—or were aware, during the building period—they've chosen separation.

Some of the marks in the trunk-hollows are old. Not ancient, but pre-dating the human platforms. The clicking-things were here first, or at least have been here longer than the builders stayed. Their arrangements might be art, or language, or territory markers, or something without a human equivalent. The fact that they remove camera lenses suggests they understand optics, or at least understand that the camera is a seeing-device and have opinions about being seen. That's information, but not enough to build theory on.

There are **ground-foragers**, small things that emerge during the condensation cycles to feed on the thin moss and whatever organic debris has recently fallen. They're perhaps rat-sized, six-limbed, with dense white fur that makes them difficult to see against the frost-covered stone. Their metabolic rate is extremely low—they move in slow motion, conserving energy, processing food with such efficiency that they defecate only once every several days, dry compact pellets that contain almost no nutrients.

The foragers are prey for everything—tengril, certain canopy-descended predators, even the moth-analogs will sometimes attack juvenile foragers during their flights. Survival strategy is reproduction: foragers breed continuously, raising litters of six to ten young in crevices between stones, the young growing rapidly and reaching reproductive age within weeks. The population fluctuates wildly, booming when conditions allow, crashing during extended cold snaps or when predation intensifies.

They have rudimentary social organization: loose groups of ten to twenty individuals that forage together, coordinate movements through subsonic vocalizations, mob predators communally. The mobbing rarely succeeds—foragers are too small to threaten most predators—but it creates enough commotion that sometimes the predator chooses to hunt elsewhere rather than deal with the harassment.

Foragers were eaten by the builders. You find their bones in middens near the stone circles, cracked open for marrow, sometimes showing cut marks from stone tools. They're not ideal food—too small, too lean—but they're catchable and abundant. A person could survive on foraged foragers if desperate, though you'd need dozens per day to meet caloric needs.

---

**Ravens**, or raven-equivalents—they're not true ravens, but they occupy the same ecological niche and convergently evolved similar forms. Black feathers, heavy beaks, intelligent eyes. They nest in the canopy but descend regularly to scavenge in the trunk-space. They're the only animals here that really fly—the moth-analogs glide more than they fly, the gallim don't fly at all—and their presence adds a dynamic the otherwise vertical forest lacks.

The ravens are tool-users. They drop stones onto hard-shelled canopy fruits to crack them open. They use sticks to probe for insects in bark crevices. They cache food in trunk-hollows, remembering locations with precision that suggests spatial cognitive abilities comparable to corvids from more familiar ecosystems. They also manipulate the moth-analogs: ravens have learned to strike structural trunks in specific patterns that mimic predator approaches, causing moth-analogs to flush from their hiding spots, whereupon the ravens catch them in mid-air.

Most significantly, the ravens interact with the human-built structures. They perch on the platforms, investigate the stone circles, manipulate any objects left there. They steal: metal pieces, bone tools, anything portable and shiny. These items end up in raven caches, mixed with natural objects—seeds, interesting stones, insect shells—in collections that look almost like the clicking-things' arrangements but are probably less purposeful.

The ravens vocalize constantly: caws, clicks, croaks, and more complex sounds that might be mimicry. Sometimes you hear a raven produce a sound uncannily like a tengril's rumble, or like the clicking pattern from the trunk-hollows, or even—and this is difficult to verify—like human speech. The last might be historical, a learned behavior from when people lived here, still passed down through raven generations despite the original teachers being long gone.

They're the only animals that clearly recognize humans as distinct. If you spend time in the forest, the ravens begin to follow you. Not constantly, but regularly—you'll notice the same individual perched nearby, watching. If you set up camp in a stone circle, ravens arrive within hours. They're cautious but not frightened. They wait for you to leave food scraps, and if you do, they remember. They begin to anticipate. The relationship isn't domestication, but it's not wildness either. It's something more like negotiation: the ravens offering their presence, their alarm calls that warn of approaching predators, their intelligence that might be useful in ways not yet discovered, in exchange for the small resources your presence provides.

One raven—you recognize individuals after a while by subtle differences in size, behavior, voice—began bringing objects to a stone circle where a researcher was camping. Not food, not tools, but strange things: a tarnished copper ring, a carved bone from the platform structures, a piece of moth-analog wing arranged just so. Gifts, maybe. Or tests. Or communication attempts. The researcher left offerings in return: bright objects, food, small carvings made from wood scraps. The exchange continued for weeks, growing more elaborate. The raven began to arrange its offerings in patterns, then seemed to wait for the researcher to rearrange them, then would rearrange them again. A conversation without shared language, without clear meaning, but with unmistakable intentionality.

When the researcher left, the raven stopped bringing objects to that circle. But it still visits, periodically, and leaves a single black feather placed carefully in the center of the cleared stone. A marker, a memory, an expectation of return.

Then there are the **builders themselves**, though they're gone now, present only as artifacts and absence.

They were human or human-adjacent—the tools are sized for human hands, the platforms designed for human proportions, the stone circles scaled to human needs. But whether they were *Homo sapiens* or something else, some parallel lineage or divergent population, is unknowable from what remains. No bodies have been found except that single frozen individual, whose remains were too degraded for detailed analysis before they were lost to a canopy-shed flood.

They lived here for generations at minimum, possibly centuries. The platforms show rebuild-layers, older structures beneath newer construction. The stone circles are worn smooth in places from repeated use. The tool technology evolved during occupation: early layers show cruder implements, later layers show refinement, specialization, the development of composite tools that combine multiple materials.

Population was probably small—dozens, maybe a hundred or two at peak. The platforms could have housed family groups, the stone circles serving as communal spaces. But the spacing suggests dispersed settlement: platforms scattered across vast areas, rarely clustered. Either they needed large territories for resource gathering, or they preferred separation, or the forest itself enforced spacing through some logistic constraint.

What did they eat? Foragers, certainly. Probably moth-analogs during the flights. Maybe they hunted tengril, though there's little evidence of that—no tengril bones in the middens, no tengril-sized weapons. They might have harvested from the canopy: fruits, seeds, the cilia-worms themselves. The rope bridges suggest they moved regularly between platforms and up into the canopy proper, though how they managed that climb—the canopy is not easily accessible—isn't obvious from the remaining infrastructure.

The fires in the stone circles burned something, but fuel is the mystery. Analysis of charcoal remains shows it's not from the structural plants. It might be processed canopy material, dried and compressed into burnable cakes. Or they might have burned animal fat. Or they imported fuel from lower elevations, though that seems unsustainable for long-term occupation. The fires weren't large—the hearths are small, designed for cooking and warmth rather than signal or ceremony. Practical fires, survival fires, the kind you maintain because the alternative is freezing.

They made marks: the spiral carvings, the geometric patterns, the arrangements of stones and bones and wings. Some of this is almost certainly symbolic—humans don't make perfect geometric patterns without intent. But the meaning is lost. Were the spirals maps? Calendars? Religious symbols? Art for its own sake? The pattern density is highest near the oldest platforms, suggesting accumulation over time, layers of meaning built up like the platforms themselves were built up, each generation adding to what came before.

There's evidence they interacted with the other fauna, but the nature of the relationship is ambiguous. Tengril footprints on some platforms, but no signs of struggle. Raven feathers cached in one stone circle, arranged almost like offerings. Clicking-thing arrangements near platforms, but never on them, suggesting mutual recognition but also boundaries. Did the builders integrate into the forest's ecological web, or did they maintain separation?

One platform—badly degraded, only the support beams remaining—has a carving that stops you. It's a figure, human-proportioned, surrounded by spirals. But the figure has eight limbs, arranged like a tengril. And around it are other figures: small flying things, large shapes that might be gallim, tiny marks that might represent foragers. The human-tengril hybrid is central, larger than the others. Is this mythology? A belief about transformation, about becoming part of the forest? Or is it metaphor, a way of expressing belonging? Or is it literal—did some builders genuinely believe they were becoming something else, hybridizing with the creatures around them?

They left. Or died out. Or stopped being distinguishable from the forest. The platforms are abandoned, some gracefully (tools put away, spaces cleared) and some abruptly (tools scattered, food remnants left to freeze-dry). No single catastrophe is evident—no burn marks, no signs of violence or epidemic. Just gradual cessation. The youngest platforms are perhaps fifty years abandoned, maybe less. The oldest might be two or three hundred years since last use.

Something happened that made this place unsuitable, or they found somewhere better, or their population crashed below viable levels, or they changed in ways that made platform-dwelling irrelevant. The forest continues without them. The stone circles remain, slowly being absorbed into the stone-drift. The platforms rot on their timescales, which are long timescales but not infinite. In another century, two centuries, the built structures will be gone entirely, and there will be nothing to show that humans were ever here except perhaps some anomalous metal fragments mixed into the ground-frost, corroded beyond recognition, waiting for some future investigator who might not know what they're finding.

But for now, the evidence remains. You can stand in a stone circle and know someone stood here before, maintained a fire, shaped tools, looked up at the same twilight canopy. You can climb to a platform and see the wear-marks on the wood, the places where hands gripped repeatedly, where feet stepped daily. You can find the spiral carvings and trace them with your fingers and know that someone else's fingers moved this way, cutting the pattern, taking time and care in a place where both were precious.

The builders are gone, but they were here. They made something in this strange place. They lived—not thrived perhaps, but lived—in the cold and the twilight and the constant hum. They adapted, or tried to, or failed but tried long enough to leave marks. They're part of the forest now, not biologically but informationally. The structures they built are being read by ravens, investigated by clicking-things, used by tengril. The patterns they carved are weathering but not yet gone. The metal they worked is corroding into the soil, adding copper and tin to the ecosystem in concentrations that didn't exist before, that might be affecting growth patterns in subtle ways that will persist for centuries.

They're gone, but they changed things. The forest is slightly different because they were here. And that difference—invisible unless you know to look for it, subtle even when you do—is a kind of haunting, a presence in absence, the specific texture of a place where people lived and left and the leaving hasn't quite finished yet.

---

And beneath it all, or through it all, or around it all, is the **forest itself**, which is not exactly alive in the way individual organisms are alive but is not exactly not-alive either.

The structural plants share resources through root-connections, through mycorrhizal networks in the shallow soil where their bases anchor. They coordinate fruiting times across vast distances. When one structural plant is stressed—damaged, diseased, resource-deprived—its neighbors sometimes show complementary stress responses, as if acknowledging the problem. They lean inward toward gaps, growing asymmetrically to cover absences. They time their growth rings to create the humming tones, each trunk contributing to a harmonic pattern that might be purposeful or might be emergent.

The canopy is even more integrated. The plant species up there—dozens of them, hundreds maybe—interweave at the cellular level. Root systems from different species fuse. Tissues merge. The boundary between one organism and another becomes unclear. The canopy might be best understood not as a collection of plants but as a single super-organism, a living mat that happens to contain genetic diversity within its structure.

And everything feeds back into everything. The gallim graze, which stimulates regrowth, which creates nutritional hotspots, which attract shrikes, which are eaten by moth-analogs, whose droppings fertilize the canopy, which supports more gallim. The tengril hunt foragers, which controls forager population, which prevents overgrazing of the moss, which preserves the moss's ecosystem services, which include nitrogen fixation that benefits the structural plants. The cilia-worms tunnel, which ventilates the canopy, which prevents anoxic die-offs, which maintains the structural integrity that supports the gallim. Every piece connects to every other piece.

The time-scales are long. The structural plants live for centuries. The canopy has been continuous for—how long? Thousands of years, minimum. The forest is older than human history, older than human presence on this continent possibly. It was here before the builders arrived, before the clicking-things evolved their intelligence (if they're intelligent), before the ravens learned to use tools.

It will be here after. The platforms will rot away. The stone circles will be absorbed into the scree-drift. The metal will corrode to nothing. The forest will continue its slow adjustments, its patient integration of new information, its accumulation of mass and complexity and emergent pattern.

You visit. You study. You try to understand. But you're ephemeral, a brief presence, a pattern that moves through the forest but doesn't alter its fundamental nature. Even the builders—who stayed longer, who tried harder—are becoming footnotes, curiosities, evidence of a failed experiment in human habitation.

The forest doesn't care. It doesn't notice, or if it notices, registers no opinion. It hums with wind-song. It drips with condensation. It grows upward, always upward, toward light that never reaches the ground, creating spaces within itself, structuring emptiness, building scaffolding that supports life that supports more life in recursive patterns that bottom out in stone and top out in stratosphere and continue, patient and slow and vast, doing what they've been doing for longer than anyone has been here to watch.

And that indifference—that absolute lack of accommodation to human need or human presence—is its own kind of presence, its own kind of answer to the question of what lives here. Everything lives here. Nothing lives here that couldn't live here already. The forest is complete, self-sufficient, self-organizing, asking nothing and offering only what it offers: space, structure, twilight, cold, beauty if you can see beauty in strangeness, meaning if you can find meaning in pattern, home if you can make home in a place that will never be home, not really, not in the way that matters to mammalian brains that evolved in sunlight and still expect sunlight even when they know better.

What lives here is a world that doesn't need you. And somehow, against all logic, that makes you want to stay.
