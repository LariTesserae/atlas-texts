# Who Lives Here

They are called **watchers**, though they do not primarily watch. The term is old, inherited from the building's original function, when the municipal workers who tended the pumping station were called watchmen. The word persisted even as the work transformed into something else entirely. Now it means something closer to **listener**, or **tender**, or perhaps—most accurately—**participant**.

There are seven of them, though never more than two present at once. They work in overlapping shifts that follow the building's own rhythms rather than the conventional divisions of day and night. A shift might last nine hours or fifteen, depending on what the instruments indicate, what the engines require, what phase the cultivation tubes have entered. The schedule is written in one of the logbooks, but it's written in pencil, subject to constant revision, margins filled with notes about adjustments made, conditions observed, intuitions followed.

**The elder watcher is named Berendt.** Sixty-seven years old, though his hands look older—swollen at the knuckles, the skin papery and pale from decades of constant moisture. He has been here forty-one years. He knew the previous generation of watchers, learned from them, inherited their notation system and expanded it. When Berendt walks through the Condensery, he doesn't look at most things directly; his attention is peripheral, scanning for deviations from expected states. He knows the sound of each pipe's particular drip, the visual rhythm of each tube array's breathing. He can detect a quarter-degree temperature shift by the smell of the air. He speaks rarely, even to the other watchers, but keeps meticulous logbooks, his handwriting cramped and precise, filling pages with observations that might seem obsessive—the exact tint of mineral deposit on pipe 7-B, the timing of a resonant frequency that emerged from the southern engines at 4:47 and lasted for twelve minutes—but which form, collectively, a portrait of the building's health in granular detail.

Berendt lives in one of the cottages visible from the western windows when the mist clears—a small stone structure perhaps two hundred meters along the northern causeway. He has lived alone since his wife died six years ago. He walks to the Condensery and back each day regardless of weather, moving with the careful gait of someone whose knees ache but who has learned precisely how to distribute weight to minimize pain. He knows he is training his replacement, has been training replacements for fifteen years now, and that this process is difficult because what he knows is not quite knowledge—it's something closer to marriage, a long intimacy with systems that reveal themselves slowly, only to patient attention.

**Silvia is twenty-nine and has been a watcher for four years.** She came from elsewhere—from the drier territories to the south—and she still hasn't fully adapted to the constant humidity. Her hair never quite dries. She develops rashes in the creases of her skin that she treats with a salve the other watchers make from plants that grow in the marsh. But she has something Berendt recognizes: an ear for the engines' tones. She can hear microshifts that even he sometimes misses, can detect the beginnings of harmonic drifts before the instruments register them. She's learning the notation system but also keeping her own parallel records, diagrams that map the relationships between components in ways that are spatial rather than temporal, trying to see the whole system at once rather than as a sequence of states.

Silvia is quiet but not shy. She asks questions that the older watchers find difficult to answer because they reveal assumptions that were never examined. Why does the wheel turn at seven minutes rather than eight? Why are the tube arrays organized in this particular density? Some questions have answers: the wheel's speed is determined by the leather belt system's tolerances, by the rate at which water can be efficiently carried upward without spilling. Others have only tradition: this is how it was done before, and it works, and changing it risks disruptions that might take months or years to stabilize. She's learning to tell the difference between questions that probe toward deeper understanding and questions that merely express the outsider's confusion when confronted with embedded practice.

She lives in a cottage with **Mikhel** and **Dag**, two other younger watchers, both in their thirties. Their household is louder than Berendt's solitary silence, full of debates about interpretation—what a particular instrument reading means, whether a pattern they've noticed is significant or pareidolia, whether adjustments should be made preemptively or only in response to clear deviation. They cook together, compare notes, sometimes sit up through the night discussing the building's behaviors like scholars analyzing a difficult text.

**Mikhel is stocky and methodical,** the most traditionally mechanical-minded of the watchers. Before coming here, he worked in a workshop that built agricultural equipment. He understands tolerances and materials, can fabricate replacement parts when the old components finally fail. He's the one who oils the great wheel's hub, who monitors the leather belts for wear, who designed the current system of wire hangers that support the glass tubes in the northern chamber—a design that distributes weight more evenly than the previous arrangement, reducing breakage. He's been here seven years and has reached the point where he can diagnose machinery problems by sound alone, standing in the central chamber with his eyes closed, listening to the creak and tick and hum, isolating the note that's wrong.

**Dag is thinner, more restless,** with a face that's all angles and a habit of chewing his lower lip when he's thinking. He's been a watcher for six years and specializes in the cultivation tubes—the dark filaments that grow in the water. He monitors their growth rates, harvests them when they reach certain lengths, processes them in a small laboratory in the eastern cottage compound. What exactly the filaments are used for, he explains vaguely when asked: they have properties, medicinal and otherwise. They're part of the reason the Condensery exists, though not the whole reason. The filaments require these specific conditions—this humidity, this temperature range, this quality of water that's been cycled through the system. They can't be cultivated elsewhere, or haven't been successfully, not in the quantities or with the consistency achieved here.

Dag is also the one who tends the moss most deliberately, who understands its varieties and functions. The moss isn't decoration—it's part of the system's regulation, absorbing and releasing moisture in response to humidity shifts, buffering the air quality, processing some component of the atmosphere that would otherwise accumulate. He cleared the geometric patterns on the walls, experiments in seeing whether moss distribution affects airflow or temperature gradients. The results are recorded in his own logbook, which is less systematic than Berendt's, more speculative, filled with sketches and hypotheses.

**Karine is fifty-two,** has been a watcher for twenty-three years, and is the one most likely to be here in the deep night, during the shifts when the building is at its quietest. She prefers solitary work. She's the one you didn't see, whose boot prints were fresh in the condensation—she moves through the space with extraordinary quiet, a skill developed over decades, not because stealth is required but because moving quietly means moving in harmony with the building's own rhythms, not disturbing the settled air, the balanced sounds. She's smaller than the others, wears her gray hair in a long braid that's perpetually damp at the end, and has a face that's difficult to read, expression held in neutral reserve.

Karine keeps the most cryptic logbook, full of observations that seem irrelevant—the way light falls through the clerestories at certain times of year, the population fluctuations of the small insects that inhabit the building (for there are insects, living in the ecosystem, feeding on the algae and molds, themselves eaten by spiders that spin webs in the high corners). She's interested in the Condensery as a whole system, not just its mechanical or cultivation functions but its existence as a place, an ecology. She's the one who tends the ferns in the corners, not because it's required but because she believes their presence matters, contributes something to the balance even if it's not measurable.

She lives alone, in the southernmost cottage, the one most isolated from the others. She's known for going days without speaking to the other watchers beyond what's necessary for shift reports. She's also known for noticing things others miss—a crack developing in a ceramic vessel weeks before it becomes critical, a shift in the groundwater smell that precedes seasonal changes, the presence of the tumbling shapes beneath the glass floor on nights when the water runs particularly clear.

**Thomas is forty-one,** has been here twelve years, and functions as something like a coordinator, though the watchers resist hierarchies. He's the one most likely to interact with the outside—with the settlement that the causeways lead to, with the suppliers who provide materials the Condensery can't produce itself, with the recipients of whatever it is the building produces or processes. He's pragmatic, focused on the interface between the work here and its contexts. He understands that the building exists within systems larger than itself—economic, social, infrastructural—even though within the building, those external systems feel distant, almost irrelevant.

Thomas is also the one who trains new watchers in the practical basics: how to read the instruments, how to record observations, how to recognize emergency states versus normal variations. He's patient but demanding, aware that a watcher's inattention or error can disrupt balances that take weeks to reestablish. He's seen watchers fail to adapt—people who couldn't tolerate the humidity, or the isolation, or the slow pace, or the ambiguity of purpose—and he's learned to recognize early whether someone will be able to make the transition into the building's rhythms.

He lives with his partner, **Olwen,** who is the seventh watcher. She's thirty-seven and has been here nine years. Before becoming a watcher, she was a physician in the settlement, and she brought that training with her—attention to symptoms, to subtle indicators of systemic problems, to the body as a site of intersecting processes. She applies this framework to the building, thinking of it as something like an organism, with circulatory systems and respiratory rhythms and immune responses. When something goes wrong, she often diagnoses it fastest, recognizing the pattern of dysfunction even when the specific cause isn't yet clear.

Olwen is the most talkative of the watchers, the one who tries to articulate what the others know tacitly. She writes essays in her logbook, not just observations but analyses, theories about how the system works and why. She's trying to build toward something more explicit, a manual or treatise that could convey the watchers' knowledge to future generations without requiring the years of apprenticeship. The older watchers are skeptical—Berendt especially, who believes some things can only be learned through duration, through the body's slow adaptation to the building's conditions. But Olwen persists, believing that even if full transmission is impossible, partial transmission is worthwhile.

She's also the one most interested in the history, in what the building was before it became what it is now. She's researched the original pumping station, found records and blueprints in the settlement's archives, traced the transformation through decades of modifications. She knows which walls are original and which were added later, which machinery was adapted from the first installation and which was purpose-built for current functions. She sees the Condensery as a palimpsest, layers of intention and accident accumulated into something that no single person designed but which nonetheless coheres.

The watchers are not a closed community—people enter and leave, though leaving is rare once someone has adapted fully. They're not monks or cultists, though their dedication can seem monastic from outside. They're workers, specialists, custodians of a complex system that requires constant attention. They're paid, modestly, by the settlement, in a combination of currency and goods. They have lives outside the building, relationships, interests, though the work is consuming enough that it tends to become central.

They dream about the building. All of them, eventually. Dreams where they're walking through the chambers and something is wrong—a sound missing or added, a smell changed, a component they've never seen before installed in a familiar location. They wake from these dreams with their hearts racing, sometimes get dressed and walk to the Condensery in the middle of the night to check, to reassure themselves that the dream-wrongness isn't real. Usually it isn't. Occasionally it is—some subtle malfunction that their sleeping minds detected before their waking attention, pattern recognition operating below consciousness.

They argue, sometimes, about philosophy. About whether the building has intentions, or whether anthropomorphizing it is a category error. About whether their role is to serve the building or to command it, whether they're operators or interpreters or something else. About what would happen if they all left, if the building ran unattended. Would it stabilize in some new equilibrium, or would it gradually fail? Could it, in some sense, want things? Does it, already?

These arguments are circular, unresolvable, but they're part of the culture. They're how the watchers think through their relationship to the work, how they remind themselves that despite the routines and the notation systems and the accumulated knowledge, there's still mystery here, still questions that remain open.

They eat together once a week, a communal meal in the largest cottage, taking turns cooking. The food is simple—bread, soup, vegetables from the gardens they keep, fish from the streams, occasionally meat from the settlement. The meals are long, full of talk that's sometimes about work but often not. Silvia tells stories from her childhood in the dry territories. Mikhel explains the mechanics of some device he's been designing. Karine, on the rare occasions she speaks at length, talks about the birds she's seen in the marsh, their migrations and behaviors. Thomas reports news from the settlement. They tease each other, gently, about obsessions and quirks. They're colleagues but also, necessarily, something like family—the intimacy that comes from shared work that can't be fully explained to outsiders.

They age into the work. Berendt's generation is retiring or dying, leaving the building to younger watchers who will, in turn, grow into elderhood, into the deep knowledge that comes only from decades of attention. They worry, sometimes, about succession—whether there will always be people willing to do this work, whether the settlement will continue to support the Condensery if its purposes become too obscure, too disconnected from immediate needs.

But mostly they don't worry. They do the work. They walk their rounds. They read the instruments. They record their observations. They listen to the engines' tones and the water's breathing and the building's settling, and they make the small adjustments that keep everything balanced. They exist in the gap between the industrial and the devotional, between the technical and the intuitive, tending systems they understand deeply but incompletely, maintaining something that's larger than themselves and which will, they hope and trust, continue long after they're gone.

At shift changes, they overlap for half an hour, the outgoing watcher briefing the incoming one. These reports are detailed: what's been observed, what's been adjusted, what requires attention. But they're also atmospheric—Karine might tell Silvia that the building feels restless tonight, though she can't point to any specific indicator. Mikhel might tell Dag that the engines seemed satisfied this afternoon, humming in a particularly settled way. This language, which would seem absurd to an outsider, is perfectly comprehensible to the watchers. They've learned to read the building's moods, its states, its health, in ways that are partly technical and partly something else.

After the briefing, the outgoing watcher leaves, walking the causeway toward home, and the incoming watcher stands in the central chamber for a few minutes, just listening, orienting themselves, entering the building's time. Then they begin their rounds, moving through the chambers in a sequence that's habitual but not rigid, checking, observing, touching their hands to pipes to feel temperature, to reservoirs to feel vibration, to walls to feel the moss's moisture content.

They are alone but not lonely. The building is company—present, responsive, alive in its way. The work is demanding but also meditative. Hours pass in a state that's focused but not tense, attentive but not anxious. They exist in duration, in the slow unfolding of the cycles they're part of.

And sometimes, standing in a particular spot or at a particular moment, the whole system becomes briefly perceptible—not just as components and processes but as a unity, a living whole, water breathing through stone and metal and glass, purposes unfolding in rhythms that are inhuman but not indifferent, something that took root in this place and continues for reasons that are both utterly practical and quietly mysterious, a condensery, a place that breathes water into itself and sends it back out changed, and the watchers who tend it, who are themselves changed by the tending, shaped by the humidity and the sounds and the long attention, becoming gradually continuous with what they tend, until the distinction between person and place softens, becomes permeable, and they are simply here, part of the water's cycle, part of the breathing, part of the deep patient work that fills the basin where three streams converge and will go on filling it, they trust, long after their own cycles complete.
