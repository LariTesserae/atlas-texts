# What Lives There

The Attendants came first, or have been here longest—the distinction matters less than their presence, which feels as permanent as the engines themselves.

They are human-shaped, mostly. Bipedal, upright, with two arms and a head, but the details have... settled, like sediment finding its angle of repose. Their skin—if it is skin—has taken on the quality of worn metal: not metallic exactly, but possessing that same matte surface, those same color variations. One individual might tend toward oxidized copper, green-brown and verdigris. Another is the pale gray of leached stone. A third carries the deep rust-orange of iron left to weather. These aren't decorations or coverings; this is what their surfaces have become, or always were, or have been becoming for so long that origin is irrelevant.

They move slowly, not from age or infirmity, but with the deliberation of things that have long ago abandoned hurry. Each step is placed with full weight, full commitment, as if they're demonstrating the concept of walking rather than simply traveling. Their joints don't quite bend the way human joints should—the angles are slightly wrong, the pivots too smooth, creating movements that are graceful in an uncomfortable way, too efficient, too perfect. When they raise an arm, it describes an arc of such geometric precision that you understand: these bodies have been performing the same motions for so long that variation has been worn away, leaving only the optimal path, repeated endlessly.

Their faces are the most difficult part.

Features exist—suggestions of eyes, nose, mouth—but flattened, simplified, like a sculptor's early maquette that was never refined. The eyes are shallow depressions, darker than the surrounding surface, that might contain something liquid or might simply be hollows that catch shadow. They don't blink. They don't track movement with the small corrections human eyes make. Instead, the entire head turns, slowly, to orient those depressions toward whatever they're observing. The mouth is a seam, lipless, that doesn't move when they communicate—and they do communicate, though not often, through sounds that emerge from somewhere within their chests: low tones that harmonize with the engine-hum, that blend into the soundscape so thoroughly you sometimes don't realize you're being addressed until the tone shifts, modulates, creates meaning through subtle variations in pitch and duration.

They wear clothing of a sort: heavy canvas-like material in colors that match the mineral palette, constructed in simple geometric cuts—rectangles and cylinders wrapped and fastened with metal clasps. These garments are old, patched with squares of slightly different shades where repairs have been made, but they're maintained. The Attendants mend their clothes with the same patient precision they bring to everything else, sitting for hours beside puddles with needle and thread—actual needle and thread, though the thread looks like fine wire and might be—drawing stitches of such regularity they could have been made by machine.

What the Attendants do is difficult to parse as labor, exactly.

They walk circuits between engines, following paths worn into the mineral surface by countless repetitions—not visible grooves, but subtle polish-lines where feet have passed. They carry tools: wrenches, measuring devices, containers for collecting condensation. They approach an engine, and one will climb—there are handholds built into the structures, clearly meant for this—while others wait below. The climber reaches some inspection point, some valve or gauge, and remains there, motionless, for ten minutes, twenty, an hour. They're not reading the gauge in the way a human would glance and note and move on. They're... attending. Witnessing. Confirming the engine's state through prolonged observation, as if the confirmation requires duration to be valid.

Sometimes they make adjustments. A valve turned one-eighth rotation. A bolt tightened. Condensation wiped from a surface with a cloth that's then carefully wrung into a container. These actions are performed with ceremony—not religious ceremony exactly, but the weight of ritual, of significance beyond the mechanical function. When a valve is turned, the Attendant's entire body participates: feet planted, both hands on the wrench, a slow rotation that takes thirty seconds to complete though the resistance is minimal. It's as if they're demonstrating the action to the engine itself, confirming through deliberate performance that this adjustment is intentional, considered, correct.

They don't speak to each other while working, but they coordinate perfectly. One will finish at a high valve and descend; another will already be approaching with a different tool. They hand off equipment in silence, a choreography so smooth it suggests either long practice or something deeper—shared awareness, perhaps, or individual consciousness that matters less than collective purpose. When one Attendant begins to move, others adjust their positions in response, maintaining some spatial relationship that's never violated, some optimal distribution across the landscape.

Their dwellings—if that's what they are—are located between engines, placed in the same seemingly random but fundamentally intentional pattern as everything else here.

These structures are low and rectangular, built from the same compressed mineral as the ground itself, cut and stacked into walls barely higher than the Attendants are tall. The roofs are flat metal sheets, probably salvaged from old engine components, that collect rainwater and channel it to storage tanks at each corner. There are no windows—just doorways covered with heavy canvas flaps. The interiors, glimpsed briefly when a flap moves, are nearly empty: a raised sleeping platform, a few storage containers, tools arranged on wall pegs with absolute precision. No decoration. No personal items that suggest individuality or history beyond function.

But outside each dwelling is a small cleared area where tube-forms grow more densely than elsewhere. These seem to be cultivated somehow—not planted, but encouraged, perhaps by careful placement of mineral-rich water, by some slight modification of drainage patterns. The Attendants sit in these spaces during what might be rest periods, positioned among the tubes as steam releases sound through them, creating small concerts of breathy tones. The Attendants don't react visibly to this music, but they orient toward it, those face-depressions angled toward the tubes, and they remain still for hours, perhaps listening, perhaps simply present to the sound in the way they're present to everything else: as witness, as attendant, as confirmation that the thing exists and is observed existing.

They eat, though rarely and strangely.

Every few days, an Attendant will kneel beside a puddle where bacterial film has grown particularly thick, and they'll... harvest isn't quite right. They'll touch the surface with their palms, and the film will adhere to them, or be absorbed by them—it's difficult to see exactly what happens. Then they'll sit back and remain motionless for an hour or more, palms pressed together, and the film will be gone, somehow internalized. Whether this is nutrition or symbiosis or something else is unclear. They never eat more than this. They never prepare food, never hunt or gather beyond this simple collection of bacterial mat. Perhaps they need very little. Perhaps the engines provide something in addition to heat—radiation, vibration, some energy the Attendants can metabolize. Perhaps they're winding down so slowly that minimal input sustains them for periods that would starve a human in days.

Water they drink more recognizably, though still strangely. They collect condensation from engines in metal containers, let it cool, then drink in a continuous pour that lasts minutes—no swallowing, no breathing breaks, just continuous intake until the container is empty. Their throats don't move. The water seems to simply flow into them, disappearing into whatever mechanisms sustain their function.

There are not many Attendants—perhaps twenty across the visible range of the flats, perhaps a few more beyond sight. Enough to maintain this section of the engine-field, but no more than necessary. They don't congregate except when labor requires it. They don't socialize in any recognizable way. But there's a moment, once per cycle—perhaps when some particular arrangement of engine tones aligns—when all of them stop whatever they're doing and stand, turning to face the same direction, toward some specific engine in the mid-distance. They hold this orientation for perhaps five minutes, a forest of still figures distributed across the landscape, all facing the same point. Then, without signal, they return to their tasks. Whether this is reverence, communication, calibration, or simply observance is unknown. They offer no explanation, and their chest-tones during this time are unchanged from the engine-hum itself.

The Humms are not intelligent in the way Attendants are intelligent, but they're not simple either.

They're the low-bodied creatures seen rarely, but once you learn to notice them, they're everywhere. Perhaps fifty centimeters long at maturity, with segmented carapaces that might be chitin but look like articulated metal plates, rust-orange or blackened iron, sometimes with streaks of copper-green. The segments number thirteen invariably—this seems to be a fixed characteristic of their body plan—each slightly overlapping the next, allowing flexibility without exposing the softer body beneath.

They have six legs, three to a side, emerging from the anterior segments. These legs are multi-jointed, ending in broad flat pads that distribute weight across the mineral surface, allowing them to move without scraping, without sound beyond the faintest tick of claw on stone. They walk with a rippling gait, segments flexing in sequence, creating a wave-motion down the body that propels them forward at a pace that seems slow but is actually relentless—they cover distance through absolute consistency, never stopping, never hurrying, maintaining the same speed whether crossing open flats or navigating around obstacles.

The head is a specialized first segment, slightly larger than the others, with a mouth on the ventral surface—a complex arrangement of radial plates that scrape and filter. They have no visible eyes, but they orient unerringly toward puddles, toward bacterial mats, toward the specific locations where their food grows. Perhaps they sense chemical gradients. Perhaps they detect moisture content in the air. Perhaps they navigate by sound, by the varying tones of distant engines creating an acoustic map. They never collide with each other, never with tube-forms, never with Attendants, though they show no sign of seeing any of these things.

What they do is eat, slowly and thoroughly.

A Humm approaches a puddle where bacterial film has formed, enters the water without hesitation—they're fully comfortable submerged—and positions itself over the thickest growth. Then the mouth plates begin to work: scraping, grinding, creating a faint rasping sound that's almost below hearing. They feed for hours, sometimes an entire day, methodically clearing one small area before shifting position slightly and beginning again. They don't eat the entire mat—they leave a border, a minimum viable population to regrow. Whether this is instinct, intelligence, or simply limitation of their mouth-reach is unclear, but the effect is the same: they crop the bacterial mats like grazing animals, maintaining but not destroying their food source.

After feeding, they withdraw from the puddle and remain still for a period, segments pressed flat against the ground, as if digesting or simply resting. They seem to require long periods of inactivity between activities—not sleep exactly, but quiescence, a shutting-down of non-essential function. During these times, they're nearly impossible to distinguish from the mineral surface itself. The camouflage appears to be accidental, a side effect of color and texture, but it's so perfect that you can walk within arm's reach of a resting Humm and not notice it until it moves.

They molt occasionally—perhaps once per year, though time-sense here is unreliable.

The old carapace splits along the dorsal line and the Humm extracts itself, leaving behind a perfect shell, hollow and articulated, that will persist for weeks before wind and occasional rain begin to break it down. The new carapace underneath is softer initially, paler, but it hardens within hours and darkens over days to match the old coloration. These molted shells accumulate in certain areas—not randomly, but in specific locations between engines where Humms apparently prefer to undergo the process. Why these locations? Proximity to particular engine-harmonics? Mineral content of the substrate? Some quality of air circulation? The shells pile up, a dozen, two dozen, eventually breaking down into fragments that look like industrial debris, like metal shavings, slowly being reincorporated into the mineral matrix of the flats.

Humms interact with each other only minimally. When two meet, they touch antennae—yes, there are antennae, small and easily missed, emerging from the head segment—for a moment, then continue on their separate paths. Whether this is communication, identification, or simply acknowledgment is unknown. They don't compete for feeding sites; if one arrives at a puddle already occupied, it simply moves to a different area or waits at a distance until space is available. There's no aggression, no territoriality, just patient coexistence.

Occasionally, very occasionally, a Humm will deviate from the puddle-to-puddle routine and approach an engine directly. It will climb—they're good climbers, those leg-pads adhering to vertical metal—up to where condensation collects in specific grooves and channels. There they feed on something else: not bacterial mat, but some film or growth that forms on the engine surfaces themselves, perhaps a different organism, perhaps something metabolized from the steam, perhaps mineral deposit with nutritional value. The Attendants never interfere with this. They work around the Humms, adjusting their timing so they're not on the same section of engine simultaneously. Whether this is respect, indifference, or recognition of the Humms' role in maintaining the engines—cleaning certain surfaces, perhaps—is uncertain.

The Humms are not immortal, but they're long-lived. A carapace found broken, the body inside desiccated, is a rare discovery. They seem to simply persist, year after year, performing their rounds, feeding and resting and molting, wearing grooves into their own neural pathways until they are less organisms than processes, mobile phenomena maintaining equilibrium between bacterial growth and mineral substrate.

The Gliders are seen infrequently but remembered vividly—movement in this landscape is so rare that flight becomes momentous.

They're not birds. The body plan is all wrong: horizontal, elongated, perhaps a meter from nose to tail, with the wings emerging not from the sides but from the dorsal surface, a single membrane structure that's been divided by evolutionary or design history into three segments per side. The wings don't flap so much as adjust—subtle angle changes, small ripples running through the membrane, constant microadjustments that keep them aloft on thermals rising from the engines.

The body itself is streamlined, covered in something that might be scales or might be overlapping plates, pale gray with darker striations that create disrupted patterns—camouflage perhaps, though against what is unclear. The sky here has no predators visible, no larger flyers, nothing to hide from. Perhaps the patterning is vestigial, or perhaps it breaks up their outline in ways that matter for reasons not obvious to ground-based observation.

The head is small, wedge-shaped, with a mouth that's just a horizontal slit. No visible eyes, or eyes so small and flush with the head that they're undetectable from below. The tail is long and forked, acting as rudder, allowing them to spiral upward on thermals with extraordinary precision, adjusting their ascent through tail-angle alone while the wings remain nearly static, just trembling slightly in the rising air.

What's most striking is their silence. No calls, no wing-beats creating whoosh or whistle, nothing but the faintest susurrus as air moves over membrane, and that's barely audible even when they pass directly overhead. They're ghost-movements, shadows crossing shadows, present but nearly imperceptible.

They hunt—if hunt is the right word—in slow motion.

A Glider will ride a thermal up from one engine, gaining height in a spiral that takes ten minutes to complete, then glide in a long, shallow arc toward the next engine, losing altitude gradually across hundreds of meters. During this glide, they descend close enough to pass over puddles, and their mouths open—just a few centimeters, but revealing an interior of fine combs or bristles—and they skim the surface. They're filter-feeding, collecting something from the air immediately above the water: perhaps organisms too small to see, perhaps spores from the bacterial mats, perhaps some chemical precipitate from evaporation. The mouth closes, they continue their glide, reach the next thermal, begin climbing again.

They never land, or at least are never seen landing. Perhaps they can't—perhaps their bodies are too specialized for flight, legs reduced to non-functional vestiges or absent entirely. Perhaps they land rarely, only to reproduce or molt or in extremity. Perhaps they're born aloft and die aloft and spend every moment between in continuous motion, sleeping somehow while gliding, sustained by whatever minimal nutrition they filter from the air above puddles.

There might be a dozen Gliders in the visible range of the flats, maybe fewer. They don't fly together—each is solitary, following its own route from thermal to thermal, sometimes not seeing another of its kind for what might be days. But occasionally two will spiral up the same thermal, one above the other, maintaining precise distance, and they'll ride together to altitude before diverging to different engines. Whether this is coincidence, coordination, or something like companionship is unknowable.

The Attendants watch them. When a Glider passes overhead, an Attendant will stop, will track its passage with that slow turn of the head, will maintain orientation until the Glider is out of sight or has caught the next thermal. There's something in this attention that suggests... not reverence exactly, but recognition. Perhaps the Gliders are what the Attendants would be if they could: unbound from the ground, still serving the engines—feeding on what the engines enable—but free to move in three dimensions, to experience the plateau from above, to see the full pattern of engine placement that's only guessed at from the ground.

Once—just once in a long observation—a Glider descended lower than usual, skimming close over an Attendant who stood beside a puddle. The Attendant raised one arm, slowly, in what might have been greeting or offering or simply acknowledgment. The Glider adjusted its path slightly, passed within meters, then caught air and rose. The Attendant held the raised arm for a full minute after, then lowered it and returned to work. Nothing was said—the Attendant's chest-tones didn't change. But something had been exchanged, some confirmation of shared existence in this place.

The Crevice-Dwellers are barely entities—more like rumors of entities, glimpses and suggestions.

They exist in the spaces between mineral layers, in the hairline cracks where compression isn't quite perfect, where microscopic gaps persist through geological strata. They're pale, completely colorless, no more than a few centimeters long, with segmented bodies that might be worm-like or centipede-like—it's impossible to see clearly before they retreat from any light source, pulling back into darkness faster than eyes can follow.

What can be determined: they have many legs, or cilia, or something that creates rippling motion along their length. They leave traces sometimes—faint mucus trails at crack entrances, or scrape marks in mineral dust, or tiny shed skins translucent and empty. They feed on something within the cracks—mineral content, perhaps, or bacterial penetration into the stone, or each other. They're never seen above ground except by accident—when a stone is lifted, when a crack is temporarily widened during rare thermal expansion events, when some disturbance exposes their habitat briefly before they vanish again.

The Attendants don't acknowledge them. Whether this is because the Crevice-Dwellers are beneath notice, or irrelevant to engine-maintenance, or so fundamental to the substrate that mentioning them would be like mentioning the mineral itself, is unclear. The Humms occasionally pause near cracks, antennae quivering, perhaps sensing the Dwellers' presence, but then continue on. The Gliders, from their aerial perspective, have no interaction with them at all.

Yet they're part of the ecosystem, part of the life-web here. They process something within the stone, perhaps contribute to the slow breakdown of mineral matrices, perhaps prevent certain types of bacterial penetration that would destabilize the compressed layers. They're maintenance organisms, cleaners of microscopic spaces, existing in perpetual darkness at the interface between stone and void.

If you press an ear to the ground in certain places, you can hear them—or hear something. A faint scratching, rhythmic and patient, the sound of countless tiny bodies moving through countless tiny passages, creating a subsonic whisper that's transmitted through the mineral itself. It's not unlike the engine-hum, actually—another layer of constant sound, another tone in the chord that this place sustains endlessly. Perhaps that's their true role: to be the biological equivalent of the engines, maintaining through patient industry a state that must be maintained but cannot be achieved through mechanical means alone.

And there are the Visitors—humans, or things still recognizably human, who come sometimes to the flats and stay for periods before leaving or not leaving.

They arrive changed by the journey here—however long it took, whatever it required—and they change further during their stay. The Attendants don't welcome them or turn them away; they simply observe, adjust their patterns slightly to accommodate new presences, continue their work. There's no hostility, no curiosity, just acceptance that sometimes other things exist on the flats alongside what always exists here.

The Visitors—the ones who stay longer than a few days—begin to slow down. Their movements become more deliberate. They stop speaking, not from prohibition but because speech begins to feel wrong, too variable, too disruptive of the soundscape. They sit for hours beside Attendants, learning to hear the engine-tones, learning to distinguish one engine's frequency from another's, beginning to understand that the hum carries information if you attend long enough: this engine is warmer, that one cooler, this one's steam-release is due soon, that one's was recent.

Their skin doesn't change—not at first, not in any timeframe they'll witness—but they begin to feel it changing, or wanting to change, as if their cells are aware of the Attendants' surfaces and are being given permission to aspire to that state. Their movements become more efficient. They find themselves walking in time with the Attendants' rhythms, placing feet with the same deliberation, discovering that hurry is just wasted motion, that everything accomplished here is accomplished through patience.

Some Visitors leave before the change goes too deep. They wake one morning and realize they can't remember the last time they spoke, the last time they wanted something beyond water and a handful of bacterial paste, the last time they thought about the world beyond the flats. This frightens them—suddenly, sharply, cutting through the settling calm—and they walk outward, toward whatever edge exists, back to variability and chaos and the terrible freedom of places where things change.

Others stay. They build small shelters from mineral blocks, mimicking the Attendants' dwellings. They begin to follow engine-routes, observing what the Attendants observe, their presence becoming part of the maintenance routine. They don't become Attendants—the transformation doesn't go that far, or takes longer than a human lifetime—but they become attendant, which is perhaps the same thing in a different timeframe.

Eventually they're found sitting beside their shelters, or near a favorite engine, or at a puddle where they fed daily, not dead but not-moving, having reached some terminal stillness, having slowed down completely and stopped. The Attendants don't bury them or memorialize them. The bodies are left where they fell, and over time—months, years—they desiccate in the warm air, mummify, begin to take on the color of the mineral around them, slowly becoming part of the landscape like everything else here: present, patient, permanent in the only way permanence exists in a place where even the eternal is subject to slow wearing-away.

---

What lives here has been shaped by the engines, or has shaped itself around them, or has been here so long that the question of causation is meaningless. Everything is adapted to constancy, to low-energy persistence, to minimal change across maximal time. There are no predators because predation is too expensive, requires too much variability. There are no plants because plants require cycles—day/night, season/season—and this place has transcended cycles in favor of steady states.

What lives here has learned to subsist on almost nothing: bacterial film, condensation, minerals, thermal energy, sound itself perhaps—the vibrations transmitted through stone might carry some value, some sustenance for those patient enough to absorb it. Metabolism has been turned down to the minimum setting that still permits life, and that minimum is so low that the distinction between living and non-living becomes philosophical rather than biological.

What lives here doesn't compete because there's nothing to compete for—everything is sufficient, abundant in its minimalism, endlessly renewed at the same rate it's consumed. Territory doesn't exist because space is functionally infinite and homogeneous. Reproduction is rarely witnessed because it happens so slowly, or so infrequently, or in ways that don't resemble reproduction elsewhere—perhaps the Attendants bud new members once per decade, or the Humms lay eggs in deep crevices that take years to hatch, or the Gliders are sterile and maintained by some mechanism beyond biology entirely.

What lives here has one shared characteristic: patience. The capacity to do nothing for long periods. The ability to wait without anticipation, to exist without urgency, to continue without hoping for completion because completion is impossible and irrelevant. They've all achieved a state that humans occasionally touch in deep meditation or extreme exhaustion: pure presence, existence without narrative, being without becoming.

And in their various ways—the Attendants with their maintenance rituals, the Humms with their endless feeding rounds, the Gliders with their thermal-riding circuits, even the Crevice-Dwellers with their invisible patient scratching—they're all doing the same thing. They're attending. They're witnessing. They're confirming through their presence and their patient attention that the thermal flats exist, that the engines run, that the system persists.

Perhaps that's what life is here: a form of observation. The universe requires witnesses, and these are the witnesses that emerged or were made or volunteered for this specific place. They watch the engines and in watching, they maintain them—not mechanically, but existentially, through the sheer fact of sustained attention. The engines hum and are heard humming and therefore continue to hum. The cycle completes not through causation but through witness.

And maybe that's enough. Maybe in a place that has achieved its final form, that has reached equilibrium and settled into endless present, life doesn't need to grow or evolve or accomplish. It just needs to be here, to observe here, to confirm through its quiet persistence that this place—this warm, damp, nearly-empty, engine-humming place—is real, is witnessed, is attended, and therefore permitted to continue being exactly what it is, forever, or at least until forever isn't necessary anymore.

The thermal flats extend toward the horizon. The engines hum their chord. The Attendants walk their circuits. The Humms graze their puddles. The Gliders ride their thermals. The Crevice-Dwellers scratch in their darkness. And all of it continues, slow and patient and absolutely certain, a landscape and its inhabitants having achieved a state beyond change, dwelling together in the eternal present of settled thunder.
