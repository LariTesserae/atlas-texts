# What Lives There

The birds are the blood of this place, and like blood, they move in circuits that never cease.

The **cushion-nesters** are the most numerous—seven species, though they blur into each other, and perhaps the word "species" is already becoming imprecise here, where isolation and time have worked their slow changes but not yet completed them. They are small-bodied, compact, their bones hollow past the point of fragility into something else, a different calculus of weight and strength. Their wings beat faster than lowland birds', compensating for air that gives less purchase. Their hearts are enormous for their size, their blood carries oxygen with an efficiency that borders on the chemical miraculous.

They are brown, mostly. Gray-brown, yellow-brown, the colors of stone and dead plant matter, but in the right light—and the light here is always somehow the right light—there are iridescences, structural colors in the feather barbs that flash green, copper, violet. Not for beauty. For recognition. Each species has its pattern, and within each species, regional variations, and within regions, family lines that have held the same nest-dome for centuries and have developed their own markers, their own tiny differences that mean nothing to you but everything to them.

They eat insects with a voracity that seems impossible given their size. A single bird might consume hundreds daily—beetles, flies, the tiny springtails that live in the plant matrix, moth larvae extracted from the dome-tunnels with surgical precision. They have learned the plateau's daily rhythms of insect emergence: which species hatches when, where the flying insects congregate in the warm columns of rising air, which beetles come to the surface after rain. Their knowledge is encoded genetically but refined by learning, passed from parent to offspring not through teaching but through observation, through the simple fact of hunting together until the young know what the old know.

Their nests are deep in the domes, tunneled through years of excavation—not by any single bird, but by lineages. A good nest chamber might be thirty centimeters into the plant mass, spherical, lined with grass and their own shed feathers and the soft inner fibers they pull from the dome itself. The entrance tunnel is narrow, just wide enough for the bird, a defense against the weasel-hunters and the smaller raptors. Some domes are riddled like termite mounds, twenty or thirty entrance holes, families living in proximity but not quite communally, their territories the size of the tunnels they occupy.

They are loud. Their calls are sharp, percussive, designed to cut through the noise of ten thousand other birds calling. Contact calls between mates: a quick double-note, repeated constantly. Territorial declarations: a longer trill, descending. Alarm calls: a harsh chatter that spreads like contagion through the population, one bird starting it and hundreds taking it up, a wave of warning that races across the domes. The begging calls of nestlings are somehow even louder, a scratchy desperation that compels the parents to hunt past exhaustion.

They breed in pulses tied to the rains. The plateau's weather is not seasonal in the conventional sense, but there are patterns—weeks of daily mist followed by weeks of dryness, then the brief violent storms that flood the channels. After the storms come the insects, emerging in response to moisture, and after the insects come the eggs. Three to five per nest, pale blue-green, speckled with brown. The incubation period is short—twelve days. The fledging period even shorter—sixteen days from hatching to flight. Speed is survival here. The faster you grow, the more breeding cycles you can complete before conditions shift, before the predators find you, before the random cruelty of weather intervenes.

But within this uniformity—seven species, all small, all brown, all numerous—there are specialists:

The **grass-dwellers** nest not in the domes but in the wire-grass tussocks, weaving complex basket structures suspended between stems. They are fractionally lighter than the cushion-nesters, their bills fractionally longer and more curved, specialized for extracting seeds and the tiny beetles that live among grass roots. Their plumage has more yellow in it—they match the grass when it's dry, which is most of the time. They are fewer in number but more widely distributed, and their calls have a musical quality absent in the others, a three-note whistle that sounds almost plaintive. They are preyed upon heavily by the falcons because their nests are visible, exposed, and they compensate by breeding faster, more often, producing more young per season and accepting the mathematics of loss.

The **crevice-sleepers** do not nest in the domes at all but in the cracks and fissures in the stone pillars, in the rare outcrops where the ancient rock shows through. They are darker, more gray than brown, and their behavior is different—less social, more territorial, maintaining larger spaces around their nest sites. They feed on the insects that live in the lichen zones, particularly the beetles that graze on the orange and yellow crusts. Their bills are short, thick, strong enough to crack beetle carapaces. They are quieter than the others, their calls infrequent, a low chirrup that barely carries. They seem to exist at the edge of the cushion-nesters' world, overlapping but separate, using the same plateau but dividing it along invisible lines of resource preference.

The **mist-followers** are strange. They are one of the seven species but behave differently enough that you notice them immediately. They are slightly larger, their wings longer and more pointed, built for sustained flight rather than the short hops between domes. They do not maintain territories in the usual sense. Instead, they follow the mist walls as they move across the plateau, feeding on the flying insects that congregate at the moisture boundary. They nest in the domes like the others, but only briefly—they lay their eggs, incubate quickly, and as soon as the young can fly they abandon the nest site entirely and return to their wandering. They appear suddenly wherever the mist is, dozens of them materializing from the sound-mass of other birds, and they feed frantically during the hour or two when conditions are perfect, then disperse again. Their calls are longer, more complex, and they seem to communicate information—perhaps about mist location, perhaps about insect density, perhaps something else. They are the plateau's nomads, unbound by fixed territories, constantly moving.

Then there are the **pillar-doves**, though they are not doves at all. They are the largest of the small birds, approaching the size of your fist, heavier-bodied, with more robust legs and feet. They spend more time on the ground than the others, walking in the channels between domes, feeding on seeds and the occasional small snail that emerges after rain. They are pale gray, almost white in certain lights, their feathers dense and soft. They nest in cavities at the bases of stone pillars, and they are the only species that appears to pair-bond across multiple seasons, possibly for life—the same two birds returning to the same nest site year after year. Their calls are soft cooing sounds, ventriloquial, difficult to locate. They seem almost placid compared to the frenetic energy of the cushion-nesters, moving through the world at their own pace, unbothered by the chaos around them.

The **dawn-singers** are the smallest, no larger than your thumb, and they possess an energy that seems impossible for something so tiny. They are never still. They hover like hummingbirds, though they are not hummingbirds—their wings beat in a blur, holding them stationary as they pluck insects from the air or probe the tiny flowers that grow on the dome surfaces. They are iridescent green on their backs, their throats flash gold, and despite their size they are aggressive, defending feeding territories with a ferocity that drives away birds twice their size. At dawn—hence their name—they sing, and their song is the one beautiful sound in the plateau's cacophony, a liquid warbling that lasts for five or ten minutes as the sun breaks the horizon, then stops abruptly as they return to the serious business of feeding.

And there are two more species, harder to characterize, existing in smaller numbers: the **stone-creepers** that climb the vertical surfaces of pillars and exposed rock faces, their feet specially adapted with curved claws, feeding on insects hiding in crevices and lichen patches, brown and bark-like and nearly invisible until they move; and the **night-callers**, which are active primarily after dark, their eyes larger, their plumage softer for silent flight, feeding on nocturnal moths and beetles, their presence announced by a repeated two-note call—"kee-yu, kee-yu"—that echoes across the darkness.

Seven species, but tens of thousands of individuals of each, perhaps hundreds of thousands, the numbers beyond easy counting. They fill the air with their movement, the domes with their nesting, the channels with their feeding. They are the plateau's dominant presence, the reason you cannot think of this place without thinking of wings and sound.

Above them, the **plateau falcons**.

They are not large—slightly bigger than the pillar-doves, compact and powerful, built for speed and maneuverability in the thin air. They are dark slate-gray above, barred white and gray below, their eyes enormous and forward-facing, their talons disproportionately large and hooked. They are the apex, the end point of the energy flow that starts with sunlight and moves through plants and insects and small birds and terminates here, in these predators.

They hunt in different ways depending on the individual, the time of day, the conditions. Some perch on the stone pillars and watch, waiting for a small bird to make an error—flying too far from cover, emerging from a dome entrance in the wrong moment. The strike is sudden, a short powered flight that covers twenty or thirty meters in a second, the impact, the grapple, the small body carried back to the perch to be plucked and torn. Others hunt on the wing, soaring on the updrafts from the plateau edges, watching from above, selecting targets from among the thousands of birds below. The stoop is almost too fast to follow—wings tucked, a living bullet, the collision with the target in mid-air, a brief explosion of feathers, then the falcon pulling up with the prey clutched in its talons.

They are not numerous—perhaps one falcon per several square kilometers, territorial, maintaining hunting ranges they defend from other falcons. But within those ranges they are constantly present, a threat that never fully recedes, and the small birds know this. The alarm calls are often triggered by falcon sightings, and when they sound, the small birds react but do not flee—they cannot flee, there is nowhere to flee to—instead they become more cautious, they stay closer to dome entrances, they reduce their exposure time in the open.

The falcons nest on the stone pillars, on small ledges or in shallow cavities, building minimal nests—just a scrape lined with a few feathers. They lay two or three eggs, and the young grow slowly compared to the small birds, taking weeks to fledge. The parents hunt constantly to feed them, and around active nests the small bird population appears reduced, though whether this is actual reduction or just behavioral avoidance is unclear.

They are vocal primarily during breeding season—harsh, repeated "kek-kek-kek" calls, territorial announcements. Otherwise they are silent, hunting without sound, pure efficiency.

But there are other falcons, rarer, seen only occasionally: larger, paler, with longer wings, soaring at even higher altitudes, taking not the small birds but the plateau falcons themselves, and the few other medium-sized birds that exist here. These are visitors, not residents, coming from mountain ranges beyond the plateau, hunting its edges, occasionally crossing its interior. They are glimpsed as distant silhouettes, their calls never heard at ground level.

On the ground, in the channels and spaces between domes, the **pikas**.

They are lagomorphs, related to rabbits but compact, round-eared, short-legged, covered in dense gray-brown fur. They are the size of a fist, perhaps slightly larger, and they exist in family groups—five to ten individuals occupying territories centered on good shelter sites: spaces under boulders, gaps between dome roots and stone, complex tunnel systems in the rare earth deposits.

They are herbivores, feeding on the toughest plants—the wire-grass, certain leaves from the cushion plants, the tiny flowering plants that nothing else seems to eat. They clip vegetation and carry it to their territories, creating hay piles that they dry in the sun and then store under cover, insurance against periods when fresh food is scarce. These hay piles are substantial—knee-high mounds of dried plant matter, carefully curated, defended.

Their calls are the sharp whistles that cut through the bird noise—alarm calls, primarily, warning of predators. They are preyed upon by the falcons, by the weasel-hunters, possibly by the larger raptors. They are vigilant, constantly scanning, but they cannot afford to stay in cover indefinitely—they must feed, must gather hay, must move between shelter and resource areas. Their strategy is speed: they dash between cover points, rarely staying exposed for more than a few seconds.

They are less numerous than the birds, obviously—perhaps dozens per square kilometer rather than thousands—but they are a visible presence, their whistles marking their territories, their hay piles dotting the landscape. They breed once or twice per year, litters of two to four young, and the young grow quickly, reaching independence in weeks.

They seem unperturbed by humans, if humans are rare enough here. They watch from the edges of boulders, their round faces curious, then whistle and vanish. But they are intensely territorial with each other—encounters between neighboring pika families involve ritualized displays, chasing, occasionally brief fights, then retreat to respective territories.

The **weasel-hunters** are ghosts.

You might spend days on the plateau and never see one, then glimpse a sinuous brown form flowing through a channel, disappearing into a dome entrance, and it's gone before you can fully register it. They are long-bodied, short-legged, built for entering the tight spaces where pikas and nesting birds shelter. They are perhaps thirty centimeters long, half of that tail, covered in brown fur that lightens to cream on the underside.

They are solitary, maintaining large territories that they patrol constantly, hunting by scent and sound. They are fast enough to catch the small birds if they encounter them in the tunnels, strong enough to kill the pikas, bold enough to enter occupied nests and take eggs or nestlings. They are the plateau's other apex predator, operating in a different dimension than the falcons—not in the air but in the tight spaces, the dark tunnels, the hidden corners.

They are rarely heard—occasionally a chittering call, possibly related to mating, possibly territorial. Their presence is known mostly by absence: a nest found empty, scattered feathers, a pika hay pile disturbed, the occupants gone.

They den in deep rock crevices or under large boulders, sites that offer protection from the falcons, which will take them if given opportunity. They breed once per year, litters of three to five young, and the young stay with the mother for a season, learning to hunt, before dispersing to find their own territories.

The **insects** are the foundation, and they are everywhere and nowhere, visible only when you look closely or when they emerge en masse.

In the dome matrix live the **cushion-beetles**—dozens of species, most unnamed, ranging from tiny black dots smaller than pinheads to thumb-sized carabids with metallic green carapaces. They feed on the plant tissue, on fungal hyphae that thread through the dead interior layers of the domes, on each other. Some are active on the surface, slowly grazing on the living plant layer. Others spend their entire lives inside, tunneling through the compressed peat-like interior, processing the ancient dead matter and converting it into nutrients that eventually return to the plant.

The **springtails** exist in unimaginable numbers—millions per square meter, perhaps more. They are primitive, tiny, barely visible even when you're looking directly at them. They feed on decomposing material, on fungal spores, on bacteria. They are food for everything—beetles eat them, birds eat them, even some insects eat them. They are the plateau's primary decomposers, breaking down the bird droppings, the dead insects, the shed feathers, the plant debris, keeping the nutrient cycle moving.

In the water-channels, when water is present, there are **aquatic larvae**—midges, mosquitoes, other flies—developing in the temporary pools. The pools might exist for only a few days after rain, but that's enough. The larvae hatch, feed frantically on algae and bacteria, pupate, emerge as adults, breed, lay eggs, all within the window of available water. The adult flies join the aerial insect mass, become bird food within hours or days, but not before reproducing, ensuring the next generation is ready for the next rain.

The **moths** are diverse and unexpected. Many are day-flying, their wings transparent or pale brown, flitting between flowers or attracted to the cushion plants. At night, other species emerge—larger, furred, with feathered antennae, drawn to the starlight or moonlight or perhaps navigating by other senses entirely. They feed on nectar from the tiny flowers, pollinating in the process, or they don't feed at all, living only to breed. The night-caller birds specialize in catching them, snatching them from the air during their mating flights.

The **grasshoppers** are survivors from before, from when the plateau was different, perhaps less dominated by the cushion plants. They are large, beautifully patterned—green and brown and yellow, some with warning colors, reds and blacks. They feed on the wire-grass primarily, occasionally on the flowering plants. They are too large for most birds to handle easily, though the pillar-doves occasionally take them, and the pikas ignore them entirely. They exist in moderate numbers, not dominating but persisting, their loud rattling flights startling, their presence a reminder that this ecosystem has layers, niches, specialists.

There are **spiders** in the channels and between stones—small web-builders stringing their traps across likely insect flight paths, ambush hunters hiding in crevices, waiting for beetles to pass. They are eaten by the stone-creeper birds, occasionally by the beetles themselves if the predator becomes prey through miscalculation.

And there are **mites** everywhere, parasitic and free-living, feeding on birds and insects and plants and each other, so tiny they're invisible without magnification, but present in enormous numbers, part of the hidden complexity that underlies everything visible.

The **snails** emerge only after rain, appearing from unknown shelter sites—tiny, their shells no larger than seeds, brown and banded. They feed on the lichens, on algae that grows on wet stone, on detritus. They are slow-moving, vulnerable, taken by pillar-doves and probably by beetles. They retreat as the water dries, presumably into cracks in the stone, entering some dormant state until the next rain.

There are no trees, so there are no tree-dependent species. There are no large mammals—the plateau cannot support them, the primary productivity is too diffuse, the plant matter too sparse despite its visual dominance. There are no amphibians—no permanent water. There are no reptiles, though possibly small lizards exist at the plateau's edges, where elevation is slightly lower and conditions marginally warmer.

What lives here has been filtered by altitude, by climate, by the specific conditions of this place, and what remains has been refined by time into perfect fit. The birds fit the insects fit the plants fit the stone fit the weather, all pieces of a single interlocking system.

But there are oddities, exceptions, visitors:

Occasionally, **ravens** appear, flying over from mountain ranges elsewhere, soaring on the updrafts, calling their deep croaking calls. They land on the stone pillars, rest briefly, then depart. They are too large to nest here, perhaps, or the food is wrong for them, or they are merely passing through, traveling to somewhere else.

Once in a season, perhaps, a **vulture** might drift over, enormous and silent, riding thermals at impossible heights, circling, searching for death. But death on the plateau is processed quickly by the beetles and flies, and the vulture finds nothing and moves on.

There are rumors—or perhaps just speculation—of **snow leopards** at the plateau's lower edges, where the cliffs descend to valleys, but they would never come up here, where there is no prey large enough for them, where the hunting would be unprofitable. Still, people claim to have seen tracks, though tracks on stone are impossible, and what they saw was probably something else, or nothing.

The **humans** are ghosts too, more thoroughly gone than the weasel-hunters who are at least present. They were here once—the cairns prove it, the stone walls, the carved posts, the scattered tools. They lived here or visited regularly or used this place for purposes lost to time. Perhaps it was sacred. Perhaps it was merely high, and height mattered for reasons that made sense then.

They left fragments: worked stone, built structures now collapsed beyond recognition, and possibly their own bones, though bones would not last long in this place—the beetles would consume them, the weather would dissolve them, the plants would incorporate them.

If there are people now, they are so rare that the plateau does not acknowledge them. A hermit living in a stone shelter somewhere in the vastness, subsisting on pika meat and birds' eggs, is possible. Occasional shepherds bringing their flocks to graze the plateau edges during certain seasons, though what grazes wire-grass and cushion plants without dying is unclear. Pilgrims crossing the plateau to reach some shrine beyond its far edge, enduring the journey as a trial.

But more likely, humans simply don't live here anymore. They found it unsuitable—too high, too cold at night, too exposed, the resources too diffuse, the constant noise of birds making rest impossible. They came, they tried, they built their walls and placed their markers, and then they left, and the plateau absorbed what they made and continued without them.

What lives here lives in tremendous abundance within narrow niches, lives in perfect adaptation to harsh constraints, lives in a density that seems impossible until you see it, lives in sound and motion and constant activity, lives in cycles so tight and efficient that almost nothing is wasted, lives in an interconnection so complete that removing any single element would cascade through the entire system.

The plateau is full. That is what lives here: fullness itself, made manifest in wings and calls and insect legs and plant growth, a completeness that has achieved equilibrium and maintains it through the sheer momentum of its own complexity, a system that hums with the satisfaction of having solved its own equation and now exists in the continuous present of that solution, alive beyond measure, loud beyond bearing, ancient beyond comprehension, ongoing.
