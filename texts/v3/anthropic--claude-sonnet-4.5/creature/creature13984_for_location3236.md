# The People of Teln Avaar

They have the stillness of stone about them, these plateau dwellers, but it's the stillness of limestone—porous, absorptive, shaped by patient accumulation rather than dramatic force. When they move, they move like water finding channels, following the paths of least resistance not from laziness but from an intimate understanding of how energy should flow through space.

Their bodies show the plateau's influence. Broad-chested from breathing thin air since childhood, though the altitude isn't extreme enough to create the barrel-ribbed look of true highlanders. Their hands are what you notice—disproportionately strong in the forearms and wrists, fingers blunt-tipped and precise, from working stone that demands both power and exactitude. They can hold a chisel steady for hours. They can feel through bronze tools into the limestone's internal structure, sensing where it wants to split, where it will hold.

Their skin weathers to a particular tone—not the leathered darkness of people who work under harsh sun, but something more subtle, a kind of mineral patina. The vesh-moisture keeps their skin from cracking, but the constant cool air and the limestone dust that hangs perpetually at the edges of breath creates a weathering nonetheless. By middle age, they have a grayed quality, as if they're slowly becoming the same color as the stone they live among. Their hair goes silver early, without the yellow undertones of ordinary aging—pure silver like the plateau grasses.

They're neither tall nor short as a people, clustering around a medium height that makes sense for working in buildings with ceilings just high enough, for reaching into cisterns of standard depth, for sitting comfortably on benches cut to particular dimensions. You sense they've calibrated themselves to their architecture over generations, or the architecture has calibrated itself to them—the same process that produces the perfect fit of unmortared stone blocks.

Their clothing moves with them like a second skin. The wool they wear is worked soft through techniques that must take weeks—carding, washing, working with fats that make it supple without being greasy. The resulting fabric drapes rather than bunches, flows rather than constrains. They layer it: a long under-tunic, a shorter over-tunic, a sleeveless vest, a cloak that can be thrown back or wrapped close depending on whether the vesh is coming. Everything in those undyed tones—cream, gray, brown, the occasional darker charcoal.

They tie their clothes with cords, not buttons. The cords are braided wool, surprisingly strong, ending in small carved wooden toggles that slip through loops. You watch someone dress and it's a matter of seconds—the efficiency of a system refined to pure function. The cords never come untied accidentally; they loosen only when intentionally pulled at a particular angle.

Their hair they bind back from their faces—both men and women—using those same braided cords. Long hair is common, worn to shoulder-length or longer, parted in the center, tucked behind ears. It's kept clean (you smell the herb-scent of whatever they wash with, something like rosemary but sharper) and left otherwise unornamented. Occasionally you see someone with a single small braid at the temple, no thicker than a cord, worked with what might be deliberate intention or might just be habit.

The children are quieter than children elsewhere, but not suppressed—naturally quiet, as if they've absorbed the sound-dampening quality of vesh-time into their fundamental nature. They run, but their feet make little noise on the packed earth. They call to each other, but not loudly. They play games that involve arranging stones in patterns, stalking the bold mice, climbing on the lower portions of buildings where hand-holds have been intentionally left. They're learning the city's rhythms, internalizing the patterns that will later guide them through tasks without conscious thought.

You see a child of perhaps six winters standing absolutely still beside a cistern, watching a salamander cross the stone. The stillness is complete—not the fidgeting restraint of a child forced to wait, but genuine absorbed attention. The salamander completes its journey. The child blinks, nods slightly as if confirming something, walks away. This happens repeatedly with different children and different phenomena: a child watching moss grow (surely not actually watching it grow, yet standing there for an hour observing moss). A child following a raven from building to building, maintaining a precise distance, stopping when it stops.

The elders are simply older versions—the same quietness intensified, the same precision more deeply ingrained. Their movements become more economical with age rather than more labored. An old woman grinding grain uses exactly the motion required, no excess, the stone describing perfect circles, her breathing synchronized to the rhythm. An old man carving a wooden toggle works with hands that don't shake despite their evident age, the blade removing curls of juniper wood in long continuous spirals.

These elders sit in doorways and you realize they're positioned as information nodes. People pass by, mention something in a few quiet words—"the western cistern needs attention," "the pear tree in the north quarter is ready," "the copper gutter at the edge-point has split"—and the elder nods, and somehow, within hours, someone arrives to address whatever was mentioned. The elders aren't directing anyone; they're simply holding information until the right person passes by to receive it, the right person being whoever feels the pull toward that particular task at that particular moment.

There's no visible governance. No councils convening, no leaders emerging, no one giving orders or making pronouncements. Yet decisions happen—which quarry void to develop next, when to harvest the hive skeps, how much grain to grind. The decisions emerge through a process you can't quite observe, like watching a flock of birds turn simultaneously without any bird leading. Someone begins working on a project and others drift over to help, or they don't, and the project either completes or gets absorbed back into future possibility.

They speak in a language that sounds like stone and water—hard consonants that click against teeth, vowels that flow between them. The language is economical; they don't use ten words where three will serve. Conversations happen close, nearly whisper-range, even in open spaces. You hear fragments: "The vesh comes early today." "Yes." "The blue water is collecting in the second basin." "I saw." That's an entire exchange, carrying information, confirmation, shared observation, mutual acknowledgment.

They have names, but you rarely hear them used. Instead, they identify each other contextually—"the one who tends the eastern herbs," "the one carving at the quarry-garden," "the one whose bread rises highest." These identifiers shift; the same person might be known differently depending on what they've been drawn to recently. It suggests either a fluid sense of identity or a deep understanding that identity is less fixed than the culture you come from assumes.

Their faces have a particular cast—difficult to read by outsider standards because the micro-expressions you'd normally track are subdued. They're not blank, not unexpressive, but their emotional range displays differently. What might be joy elsewhere is here a slight softening around the eyes. Concern shows as a barely perceptible tightening at the mouth's corners. Anger—if they feel anger—doesn't manifest in anything you can detect. You watch for hours and see no arguments, no raised voices, no conflict. Either they've transcended it or they've compressed it into registers you can't perceive.

But they're not emotionless. You see tenderness in how someone guides a child's hands on a grinding stone, teaching the circular motion. You see something like wonder when someone pauses before an especially perfect stone joint, running fingers along the invisible seam. You see contentment in how someone sits in afternoon light, eyes closed, face tilted slightly upward. The emotions are present but quieted, like sound during vesh-time—still there, still real, but dampened, intimate.

They touch rarely. A hand on a shoulder, brief. Fingers brushing when passing an object. Parents and children have more contact—a child leaning against an adult's leg while they work, an adult's hand resting on a child's head—but even this is understated. They're not a people who embrace. They're not cold, exactly, but they maintain a physical distance that matches the spacing between their buildings, the same principle of architecture breathing applied to bodies moving through space.

Their work ethic is peculiar. They work constantly but never hurriedly, moving from task to task with the seamlessness of water flowing between levels. Someone finishes grinding grain and the next task simply presents itself—perhaps because they notice the copper gutter needs attention, perhaps because they feel drawn toward the herbs that need harvesting, perhaps because standing up and turning puts them face-to-face with someone who needs help moving stone. There's no clock-watching, no sense of task-completion as achievement. Tasks flow into each other like one continuous action distributed across hours and people.

They eat communally but not ceremonially. Food appears on the stone tables—bread, fruit, herbs prepared various ways, occasionally cheese or dried meat from valley trade—and people take what they need when they pass by. You never see anyone eating alone in a private space, but you also never see a formal meal with everyone gathered. Eating is distributed through the day: someone breaks off a piece of bread while crossing a plaza, eats it in five bites while walking, continues to wherever they're going.

The food itself is simple but not plain. The bread has the complexity of long fermentation—you taste the grain and something else, something slightly sour, slightly sweet, some culture they maintain in ceramic crocks that gets mixed into each batch. The fruits are small but intensely flavored, concentrated by the espaliering technique that limits production. The herbs are used with precision—you taste thyme in the bread, sage in the charred vegetables, something like oregano but more mineral in the cheese.

Water they drink from copper cups kept at the cisterns, dipping them into the filtered water, drinking in place, replacing the cup on its stone shelf. The water is cold and clean and tastes faintly of minerals—calcium from the limestone, something metallic (perhaps from the copper, though the patina shouldn't leach), a finish that's almost sweet. They drink frequently but in small amounts, staying hydrated in the dry air without the bloated feeling of drinking too much at once.

They maintain hygiene through systems that reveal themselves slowly. Each cluster has a building with a copper-lined basin fed by cistern water, with a drain that carries water away through ceramic pipes toward the cliff edges. Here they wash—bodies, clothes, tools—in water cold enough to make you gasp but which they enter without hesitation. The washing is quick, efficient, daily. They emerge with that herb smell (whatever they add to the water for cleaning) and that wet-stone smell of the vesh, clean in a way that seems appropriate to a city of limestone and careful water.

Their sleep patterns match the light. They rise when dawn begins to gray the eastern sky, before the vesh arrives. They work through morning and midday. As afternoon extends toward evening, the work gradually decreases, people gravitating toward the plaza circles, sitting, speaking quietly or not speaking. As the lamplighters begin their rounds, people drift toward the buildings, entering doorways, disappearing into dim interiors. By full dark, the city is still. You hear no night sounds from the buildings—no conversation, no movement, as if they've all entered some collective suspended state until dawn.

But someone must wake in the night, because the lamplighters extinguish and relight in patterns you can't predict, because objects in the doorless shrines get rearranged, because sometimes you wake in darkness (you've stayed long enough to sleep in one of the buildings they've indicated is available) and hear footsteps outside—soft, measured, purposeful—someone moving through the limestone-glowing city on business they don't explain.

They have knowledge systems that aren't written but aren't purely oral either. You watch two people examining a stone block, one tracing a crack with a finger while speaking quietly. The other nods, pulls out a small wooden rod carved with notches, holds it against the block, makes a sound of understanding. The rod goes back into a belt pouch. The block gets moved to a different location. Later, you see someone else examining that block, pulling out a similar rod (or the same rod—they might share tools), making measurements, then beginning to work the stone with chisel and mallet. They're communicating through physical objects that encode information, through spatial relationships, through the stones themselves.

The specialists among them—the coppersmiths, the water engineers, the ones who understand the blue-tinted pools—aren't marked as different. They dress the same, move the same, participate in the same distributed tasks. Their specialization is something they flow into and out of as needed. You watch someone repair a complex copper joint in a cistern filtering system, work that requires deep technical knowledge, and then later that same person is harvesting herbs with the same attention, no hierarchy between the tasks, both given the same quality of focus.

They interact with the city's fauna as if negotiating with colleagues. The bold mice sit upright and the people work around them, neither shooing them away nor encouraging them, simply acknowledging their presence and adjusting accordingly. The ravens that walk between buildings get spoken to—one-sided conversations, brief: "The eastern plaza has crumbs," or "Mind your feet." The ravens behave as if they understand, veering toward the mentioned plaza or stepping aside. Coincidence, surely, yet it happens consistently enough to seem like genuine communication.

The salamanders they treat with particular respect. When the orange-and-black forms emerge during vesh-time, people stop walking, waiting for them to cross paths before continuing. If someone must cross a salamander's trajectory, they step wide, giving it space. You see someone kneel beside a salamander—not touching, just observing—the salamander pausing as if allowing inspection, then both continuing their respective journeys. The salamanders are indicators of water quality, yes, but they're treated as more than instruments. They're treated as... citizens might be too strong, but something in that direction.

They mark time not by days or seasons but by cycles you gradually recognize. The vesh cycle—its arrival and departure. The bread cycle—the rhythm of baking that happens at intervals determined by consumption and grain availability. The stone cycle—quarrying, moving, placing, which proceeds continuously but with natural pauses that create a rhythm. The fruit cycle—different trees ripening in sequence, creating a wave of harvest that rotates through the city. These cycles overlap and intersect, creating a temporal texture that's complex but never confusing to them, though you struggle to track it.

Their relationship with the valleys below is distant but not disconnected. Traders arrive occasionally—you can tell them apart immediately by their hurry, their noise, their bright-dyed fabrics—bringing wool, bringing foods that don't grow on the plateau (you see olives, grains different from what's grown here, dried fish). The plateau people receive them politely, lead them to specific plazas where trade happens, offer water and bread. The traders spread their goods on woven mats. The plateau people examine items, take some, leave behind worked copper, carved wood, limestone blocks cut to specification. No bargaining, no discussion—the value equivalents are understood by both parties, or are irrelevant.

The traders stay never more than a night, sleeping in buildings designated for that purpose at the plateau's edge. They seem uncomfortable here—the stillness disturbs them, the vesh unsettles them, the perfectly fit stones and the regulated patterns feel wrong to people accustomed to urban chaos. They leave quickly, their departure creating a brief wound of noise and rushed movement that heals immediately after they descend the trail.

The plateau people must have come from somewhere originally—valley people who ascended, refugees from some lowland pressure, or something stranger. But they don't tell origin stories, or if they do, they tell them in ways you don't recognize. The carvings on the edge-markers might be historical records, genealogies, or purely abstract patterns. The arrangements in the doorless shrines might commemorate ancestors or might just be aesthetic compositions. Without text, without explicit narrative, their past exists only in physical forms that resist interpretation.

They do have art, though it takes forms that require attention to recognize. The way a stone block is placed isn't just functional—there's a consideration of color variation, of how light will strike the surface, of how the stone's grain aligns with the building's overall pattern. The espaliered trees aren't just efficient fruit production—they're living sculptures, geometric forms that change subtly with seasons that barely exist. The copper gutters are arranged to create particular sounds when water flows through them during vesh-time, different pitches from different buildings creating an unplanned (or is it planned?) composition.

The wooden carvings—toggles, tool handles, the small figures in the shrines—show a distinct aesthetic. Simplified forms, emphasis on smooth curves, minimal detail. A carved bird is barely more than an egg-shape with a protruding beak, yet unmistakably a bird. A human figure is a column with suggestions of shoulders and head, yet unmistakably human. The simplification isn't primitive; it's sophisticated, retaining only what's essential, removing everything excessive.

They sing, but not the way singing happens elsewhere. No performances, no audiences. Instead, while working, someone will begin a tone—a single note, sustained, somewhere in the middle of their range. Others nearby might join, adding harmonics, creating chords that hang in the air then dissolve. It lasts perhaps thirty seconds. Then silence. Then, minutes later, someone else begins a different tone. This happens throughout the day, distributed through the city, brief moments of collective music that aren't coordinated but somehow cohere into a larger pattern.

Their relationship with death is difficult to observe because you never see it happen. The city contains elders but you don't see them aging dramatically, don't see them declining. They're present one day and then, perhaps, absent. When you notice someone's absence—"the one who carved at the south quarry"—and inquire through the indirect communication methods available, you receive a response that's more spatial than verbal: a gesture toward the cliff-edge, toward the valleys below. Bodies are returned below, perhaps. Given to the earth they came from. The city itself doesn't hold death; it remains perpetually populated by the living, the count stable at around three hundred, the distribution of ages consistent.

Or perhaps they don't die here. Perhaps the city somehow sustains them in that perfect stasis everything else exhibits. Perhaps they age to a certain point and then simply... maintain, like the buildings that never deteriorate past a specific threshold. You can't tell. They won't say, or can't say, or don't think about it in ways that translate to your questions.

What's certain is that children are born here—you see infants, you see pregnant women moving through tasks with that particular careful grace. So there's generation, there's continuation, even if there's no visible endpoint. The children grow to adulthood at normal rates, as far as you can observe. So time passes through bodies even if it doesn't pass through stone. But where's the boundary? When does aging stop? You watch for the telltales—gray hair, yes, but everyone has that by their thirties. Weathered skin, yes, but is that person forty or eighty? They move the same, work the same, show the same capacity.

They have healing knowledge—you see someone treating a cut, washing it with water that's been infused with particular herbs (you recognize calendula, yarrow, something else), then binding it with clean linen. The cut heals normally, as far as you can tell, scabbing and fading over days. So they're not invulnerable. But serious illness—you don't see it. Chronic conditions—absent. The injuries that occur are minor: cuts, bruises from dropped stones, the occasional twisted ankle. These are treated competently and heal. Nothing worse happens, or happens elsewhere, beyond your observation.

Their memory seems different. Not absent—they clearly remember how to perform complex tasks, remember each other, remember the patterns that govern city life. But they don't reminisce. Conversations never begin with "Remember when..." They don't tell stories about past events. They exist in continuous present, the past accessible through embodied knowledge (how to fit stones, how to work copper, how to read water signs) but not through narrative. The past is in their hands, not their mouths.

This creates an odd effect: you can't learn their history by asking, but you can learn it by watching them work. The stone-fitting technique is ancient, clearly—it requires generations to develop. The water management systems are sophisticated, the product of long refinement. The herb knowledge is deep, multi-layered. They're the inheritors of vast accumulated expertise, but they hold it as practice rather than story, as gesture rather than text.

When they look at you—and they do look, direct eye contact, neither shy nor aggressive—you see something in their gaze that's hard to define. Curiosity, slightly, though muted. Assessment, perhaps. But also something else: a kind of recognition, as if they're seeing not you specifically but the category you represent, the type you embody. They're seeing "outsider" or "valley person" or "one who hasn't yet stilled" more than they're seeing your individual features. It's not dehumanizing, exactly, but it's not quite the personal recognition you're accustomed to.

They answer questions, when asked directly, but their answers curve around direct statement. You ask, "How do you make the stones fit so precisely?" They respond, "The stone knows where it wants to sit." You ask, "Why does the moss grow in stripes?" They respond, "Watch the water move." Not evasion, but a different epistemology—one that locates knowledge in observation and relationship rather than explanation and principle.

You ask, "Are you happy?" They look confused, or perhaps you're misreading their expression. After a long pause: "The city provides what's needed." You ask, "Do you ever want to leave?" They gesture around at the buildings, the gardens, the paths: "Where would we go that isn't here?" Not rhetorical—genuine puzzlement at the question's premise.

They're not trapped. The trail down to the valleys is open, passable, used by traders. But they don't descend. They stand at the edge-rails, look out at the forested hills, the distant mountains, the smoke rising from valley settlements, and then they step back, return to their tasks. The world below exists—they acknowledge it, they trade with it—but it's not their world. Their world is this three-mile plateau of limestone and vesh, herb gardens and cisterns, perfectly fitted stones and copper gutters singing water-songs.

What they are, finally, is *integrated*—with each other, with the city, with the patterns that govern this place. They've achieved what might be the endpoint of civilization or might be a peculiar trap: perfect adaptation to a specific environment, perfect balance within a closed system, perfect execution of sustainable patterns. They've become human limestone—porous, absorptive, slow to change, strong in compression, beautiful in aggregate.

They're happy, probably, if happiness is the same as contentment, if contentment is the same as lack of desire for change, if lack of desire for change is the same as fulfillment. They're successful, certainly, if success is measured in sustainability, in equilibrium, in the absence of crisis. They're alive, absolutely—moving, breathing, working, resting, reproducing, continuing.

But are they *free*? That's the question you can't answer, watching them move through their pattern-governed days, their task-flowing hours, their vesh-regulated mornings. They're free from want, from conflict, from uncertainty. But they're also free from spontaneity, from chaos, from the unexpected. They've traded something—you're not sure what to call it, perhaps *trajectory*, perhaps *becoming*—for something else: *stability*, *presence*, *eternal now*.

They are the people of Teln Avaar, the plateau dwellers, the stone-fitters and vesh-collectors, the ones who've found the frequency at which human life can resonate indefinitely without amplification or decay. They are the steady state made flesh, the equilibrium embodied, the pattern given human form. They are, in their way, perfect—and perfection, you realize, is both the most beautiful and most unsettling thing a people can achieve.
