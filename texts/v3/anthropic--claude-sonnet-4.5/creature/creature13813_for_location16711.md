# What Lives on the Plateau of Suspended Chronologies

The plateau has its own answer to the question of what constitutes an individual.

## The Sieve-Trees' Interior Ecology

Within the hollow cores of *Arbor cribrosa*, there exists an atmosphere distinct from the plateau's ambient air. The trees breathe in and filter, but they also retain—creating microenvironments in their central cavities where humidity might reach seven, even nine percent, where temperature fluctuates less severely, where the molecular composition of air has been pre-processed, pre-sorted, enriched with specific ratios of gases.

Things live in these spaces.

The core-climbers are the most visible: arthropod-analogues with bodies segmented like millipedes but compressed laterally, allowing them to navigate the narrow vertical shafts within tree-trunks. They're thirty to forty centimeters long, with countless legs that grip the fibrous interior walls through a combination of mechanical hooking and molecular adhesion—van der Waals forces amplified by specialized foot-structures. Their bodies are translucent, showing internal organs as shadows: a digestive tract that spirals the length of their form, a circulatory system that glows faintly blue from copper-based respiratory pigments.

They feed on the tree itself, but selectively. Not the living vascular tissue—that would be parasitism, and the trees have defenses against such relations. Instead, they consume the shed interior cells, the metabolic byproducts that accumulate in the hollow cores, the crystallized minerals that precipitate from the tree's internal chemistry. They're janitors as much as residents, maintaining the trees' hollow spaces in a state of functional cleanliness.

When multiple core-climbers meet in the trunk-cavity, they engage in behaviors that look almost like negotiation. They touch antennae in specific patterns—not quite communication, but perhaps information exchange through chemical gradients, pheromonal vocabularies. They seem to coordinate their feeding, ensure distribution throughout the trunk's vertical space, avoid over-harvesting any particular section.

The core-climbers emerge occasionally, climbing the exterior bark during the dim-phase to mate. They arrange themselves in helical patterns around the trunk, dozens of individuals spiraling upward in synchrony, their translucent bodies catching bioluminescent glow from the environment and refracting it strangely. The matings are chemical exchanges—individuals pressing specialized segments together, swapping genetic material through direct cellular contact. Then they return to the interior, to the hollow darkness, where they'll deposit eggs in carefully chosen cavities: niches where temperature and humidity remain most stable.

Higher in the trunk-cores, near where the main trunk begins to branch into the fractal canopy, there are the suspension-weavers: organisms that might be distant relatives of spiders if spiders had decided to become mostly lung. They're nearly all respiratory surface—bodies that are primarily thin membranes stretched across minimal structural frames, maximizing surface-area for gas exchange in the slightly richer atmosphere of the trunk interior.

They spin webs, but not for prey. The webs are hammocks, suspension systems that hold them in optimal positions within the air-column, where the tree's internal ventilation—the passive flow of air through the hollow core—brings the richest mixture of oxygen and the complex hydrocarbons the trees exhale. They feed on these hydrocarbons directly, absorbing them through their membrane-bodies, metabolizing them through chemical pathways that shouldn't provide sufficient energy but somehow do, perhaps through catalytic enhancement by trace metals they've incorporated into their tissues.

The suspension-weavers are colony organisms. What looks like a single individual is actually thirty to fifty genetic individuals who've woven themselves together, shared their web-structures until the colony becomes a unified metabolic entity. They can disaggregate—separate into component individuals—during reproductive phases, then re-aggregate with different partners, creating genetic mixing through physical mixing, through literal embodied exchange.

They produce sounds: faint clicking and humming that resonates with the tree's own acoustic signatures. Whether this is communication, echolocation, or simply mechanical byproduct of their respiratory movements remains unclear. But they seem to respond to the tree's songs, adjusting their web-tensions to vibrate at sympathetic frequencies, perhaps extracting vibrational energy from the sound-field itself, adding it to their metabolic budgets through mechanisms that involve piezoelectric proteins in their membrane-tissues.

## The Substrate Dwellers

Below the surface—in the compressed plant-matter that forms the plateau's substance—there exists a three-dimensional maze of galleries, chambers, and channels. This is the domain of the excavation ecology.

The bore-worms are primary architects. They're segmented, as mentioned, but the details matter: each segment can operate semi-independently, coordinating through nerve-bundles that run the creature's length but also capable of local decision-making. A bore-worm encountering an obstacle doesn't need to consult its entire length—the leading segments assess and respond, choosing whether to go around, go through, or retreat.

They excavate through both mechanical and chemical means. The head-segments have mandibles that can grind through compressed cellulose, but they also secrete enzyme-cocktails that partially dissolve the plant-matter, liquifying it enough to be consumed and passed through the digestive tract. What emerges from the posterior is compressed further, combined with mineral secretions, extruded as smooth pellets that line the tunnel walls, preventing collapse.

These pellets become habitat. They're rich in concentrated nutrients—the bore-worms extract only part of what they consume, leaving the pellets as high-quality substrate for other life.

The pellet-gardens grow specialized fungi—not true fungi, but convergent forms, organisms that grow thread-networks through the nutrient-rich pellet material, producing fruiting bodies that the bore-worms consume on return journeys through their own tunnels. The relationship is farming, but farming that emerged through evolutionary negotiation rather than intentional cultivation. The bore-worms who happened to produce pellets that supported these particular fungi had more food available in their territories. The fungi that happened to fruit in response to bore-worm chemical signals got dispersed more effectively. Over deep time, they became inseparable: bore-worms carry fungal spores in specialized gut-pouches, inoculating new tunnels automatically.

But the tunnels themselves become habitat for others. The gap-runners are creatures built like flatworms but behaving like pursuit predators. They're ten to fifteen centimeters long, flexible enough to navigate the narrowest bore-worm galleries, fast enough to chase down other substrate-dwellers. They hunt by vibration-sense, feeling through the tunnel walls for the movements of prey, then intercepting them with bursts of peristaltic speed that seem too rapid for their body-plan.

Gap-runners are partially photosynthetic. Their dorsal surfaces contain chloroplast-like organelles stolen from consumed prey and maintained in their own tissues—kleptoplasty taken to extreme. They can survive on light alone if they position themselves near air-shafts where dim illumination penetrates, but they prefer to hunt, using photosynthesis as supplementary energy during lean periods.

There are also the aggregation-forms: colonial organisms that exist as mobile masses of individual cells only loosely committed to multicellularity. They flow through substrate galleries like slow protoplasm, engulfing organic debris, fungal masses, occasionally immobilized prey. They're everywhere in the substrate, but diffuse—you might excavate a cubic meter of compressed plant-matter and find it shot through with thin veils of aggregation-form tissue, the organism distributed throughout the space like mycorrhizal networks but mobile, capable of concentrating mass toward food sources, then dispersing again.

These organisms are the plateau's distributed digestive system. They break down everything organic that enters the substrate, converting it back into simpler molecules that plants can access. They're why nothing accumulates, why the dead become the ground so efficiently. And they're why excavating carelessly can be dangerous—disturbing a large aggregation-form concentration can result in the organism flowing toward the disturbance, engulfing whatever caused it, beginning the dissolution process before realizing the target is still alive.

Deeper still—at the boundary between compressed plant-matter and bedrock—there are the lithophytes: organisms that blur the line between plant and stone. They grow into the rock itself, sending crystalline structures into microscopic cracks, expanding them gradually through a combination of chemical dissolution and physical pressure. Over centuries, they fragment the bedrock, increasing the total surface area available for their root-analogues.

They're drawing something from the stone—trace minerals, elements that the plateau's ecology needs but that aren't available from atmosphere alone. Iron, magnesium, phosphorus: nutrients that must be mined from geology because atmospheric chemistry can't provide them. The lithophytes concentrate these minerals, and when they die—when their slow metabolisms finally cease after lifespans that might span millennia—their mineral-rich tissues are consumed by bore-worms, incorporated into pellets, made available to fungi, eventually cycling up to surface plants through mechanisms of transport that involve dozens of intermediary species.

## The Shepherds' Society

The hexapedal shepherd-forms are not simple. Each individual massing perhaps eighty kilograms, standing two meters at their highest extent, they move through the landscape with consideration, with something that looks increasingly like intention the longer you observe them.

Their bodies are bundles of modified plant-stems, yes, but the modification is profound. The stems have been convinced to grow in aligned fascicles, creating cable-like muscles that contract through osmotic pressure changes—water moving between cells in response to electrical signals, creating movement through hydraulic principles. The system is slow compared to animal muscle, but powerful and efficient.

Their sensory clusters—calling them "heads" implies too much centralization—contain multiple types of receptors. The light-pits are compound eyes of a sort: dozens of individual photoreceptive units that each capture a slightly different angle, the whole processed into something resembling vision by neural ganglia distributed throughout the body. They see the plateau in ways humans can't: they perceive polarization patterns in the diffuse light, can track the subtle movements of sieve-tree branches against the grey sky, detect the body-heat signatures of anemophytes as faint infrared against the cool background.

The chemical receptors are sophisticated—able to discriminate between hundreds of airborne compounds, to track scent-trails across kilometers, to detect the pheromonal communications of other shepherd-forms from vast distances. They're constantly tasting the air, reading the plateau's chemical weather.

And there are the electromagnetic sensors: structures that contain crystalline arrangements of magnetite particles suspended in gel, connected to sensory neurons. They feel the plateau's weak magnetic field, can navigate by it, but also detect the electrical fields generated by living things. Every organism here produces bioelectricity—nerve signals, muscle contractions, cellular processes—and the shepherds perceive this, seeing the landscape as a field of electrical signatures overlaid on visual and chemical perception.

Their behavior suggests cognition of a distributed kind. Individual shepherds are intelligent, but shepherd-groups are *more* intelligent—not through communication exactly, but through harmonic entrainment. When multiple shepherds gather, their humming synchronizes, and their neural activity seems to synchronize as well, creating temporary collective processing that's greater than the sum of individual capacities.

They have relationships with specific anemophyte herds—not ownership, but something like partnership. A shepherd-form will follow the same herd for years, learning its patterns, defending it against threats, guiding it toward optimal feeding areas. The shepherd knows each anemophyte individually, can track them by their unique electrical signatures, seems to mourn when one dies.

The shepherds also interact with humans, though the nature of the interaction is debated. They approach settlement areas deliberately, position themselves at boundaries, hum in patterns that seem responsive to human activity. Some humans insist the shepherds are attempting communication, are waiting for humans to learn their language. Others argue the shepherds are simply curious, are studying humans the way humans study the plateau.

There are accounts—difficult to verify, spoken of carefully—of shepherds intervening in human crises. A person lost in dim-phase, disoriented, circling uselessly, finding a shepherd-form suddenly present, humming in a specific pattern, then moving in a clear direction—and following, the lost person finds their way back to settlement paths. A child who wandered too close to a storm-track during a developing vortex, scooped up gently by a shepherd's manipulator-limbs, carried to shelter, deposited carefully and then the shepherd departing without waiting for acknowledgment.

Are these deliberate rescues? Or are they the plateau's distributed cognition manifesting through the shepherd-forms, the environment protecting its human components the way an organism might protect necessary cells?

The shepherds reproduce rarely—perhaps once every several years for a given individual. The process is vegetative: a shepherd-form will position itself in a mat-plant area and remain motionless for weeks while its body sends down root-structures, tapping into the substrate. Slowly, a new growth emerges beside the parent: a miniature shepherd that develops while still physically connected, receiving nutrients and possibly information through the shared tissue-bridge. When the juvenile reaches sufficient size, the connection severs, and the parent moves on, leaving the young one to learn the plateau alone—or more likely, to be found and integrated into a shepherd-group, raised collectively.

## The Aeroplankton

The air itself is populated. Between the mat-plants and the sieve-tree canopies, through the vertical space that humans move in, there exists an aerial ecology of microscopic and near-microscopic life.

Floating spores—not reproductive structures, but free-living organisms that have reduced themselves to near-cellular minimalism. They're photosynthetic, covered in surface structures that maximize light capture, buoyant through gas-vesicles that keep them suspended. They drift in the air currents, extracting what they need from atmosphere, reproducing through simple division, dying and falling to become food for surface-dwelling life.

These spores are the plateau's primary productivity at the microscopic scale. They're everywhere, in concentrations of thousands per cubic meter of air, creating an invisible photosynthetic fog that contributes substantially to the plateau's oxygen production.

Feeding on them are the filter-fliers: organisms the size of gnats, with wings that beat at frequencies in the ultrasonic range. But the wings aren't primarily for propulsion—they're for creating feeding currents. The filter-fliers fly through spore-clouds with their mouths open, their wing-beats generating vortices that concentrate spores into their oral chambers, where sticky mucus traps them and enzymatic action begins digestion immediately.

The filter-fliers themselves are prey for larger aerial forms—the pseudo-birds, which hunt by echolocation during dim-phase, snapping up filter-fliers by the dozens, processing them through digestive tracts that are brutally efficient, extracting nearly every molecule of energy before excreting desiccated pellets that fall to the mat-plants below.

But there are also aerial predators that aren't birds: the drifters are balloon-like organisms, bodies that are mostly gas-filled sacs providing buoyancy, trailing tentacles thirty meters long. The tentacles are covered in sticky cells that paralyze small prey on contact. The drifters float passively, rising and falling with thermal currents, their tentacles sweeping through the air like living nets.

They look like jellyfish that have somehow adapted to air, and perhaps that's not far wrong—they might be descended from aquatic forms that colonized the plateau when it was wetter, that adapted to increasingly arid conditions by becoming lighter-than-air, by treating the atmosphere as a new ocean.

The drifters are hermaphroditic, reproducing through clouds of gametes released during the bright-phase, when air currents are most active. The gametes are themselves buoyant, drifting until they encounter each other, fusing in mid-air, developing through larval stages that gradually inflate, becoming new drifters over the course of months.

And threading through all of this—the spores, filter-fliers, pseudo-birds, drifters—are the wind-threads: living filaments, sometimes a hundred meters long but only millimeters thick, that drift through the air like gossamer. They're motile, able to undulate and steer themselves through air currents, following scent-gradients toward food sources.

They feed through their entire length—every cell of their bodies capable of absorbing dissolved nutrients from the moisture-poor air, extracting the trace organic molecules that the sieve-trees exhale. They're living filters, cleaning the air of complex hydrocarbons, incorporating the carbon into their own structures.

The wind-threads tangle sometimes, dozens of individuals weaving together into aerial masses that look like floating hair-balls, but the tangles are temporary communities—the threads exchange genetic material through direct cellular contact, then disentangle and disperse. They might be a single distributed species, each thread a temporarily independent fragment of a genetic commonwealth that's spread across the entire plateau's airspace.

## The Night Ecology

During dim-phase, when bioluminescence becomes the primary light source, different organisms emerge into prominence.

The glow-bodies—those fruiting structures on mat-plants that phosphoresce—are actually animal-plant symbioses. The plant portion provides structure and nutrients from photosynthesis during bright-phase. The animal portion—a tiny organism living embedded in the fruiting-body tissue—generates light through chemical reactions, attracting pollinators and prey.

Because yes, the glow-bodies are carnivorous. Small substrate-dwellers are drawn to the light, approaching close enough for sticky secretions to trap them. The captured prey is digested by the animal component, nutrients shared with the plant partner. It's reversed from typical carnivorous plants—here the plant is the passive provider, the animal the active hunter.

The flying photophore-bearers—those small forms that trace blue-white arcs through dim-phase air—are hunting as they fly. They're pursuing even smaller aerial life, organisms too small for human eyes to track, but which the photophore-bearers can see clearly through specialized vision adapted to their own light-frequencies. The bioluminescence is spotlight, not advertisement, though they accept the risk of making themselves visible to larger predators in exchange for hunting advantage.

And during the darkest periods of dim-phase, the echo-forms become active. These are organisms—calling them organisms is convenient but perhaps inaccurate—that exist as standing wave patterns in the sieve-trees' acoustic fields. They're not physical entities in the conventional sense, but rather concentrations of acoustic energy that persist through feedback loops between trees.

They move through the sound-field like thoughts through neural networks: patterns of interference that propagate, interact, sometimes seem to behave with something resembling intention. They're not alive by standard definitions—they're more like stable vortices in the plateau's sonic ocean—but they persist, they replicate (one wave-pattern spawning similar patterns in adjacent acoustic spaces), they consume (absorbing energy from the trees' sonic output), they even die (when the pattern destabilizes and dissipates).

Humans with the right sensory adaptations can perceive the echo-forms as presences: zones of acoustic density that move through the landscape, that seem to approach or avoid, that correlate with subjective feelings of being observed, accompanied, surrounded. Are they actually aware? Or are they just sufficiently complex patterns that humans can't help but read intention into their movements?

The question matters less than the fact that they're there, that the plateau has evolved something like life from pure sound, that information and energy have organized into persistent patterns that blur every definition of what constitutes an organism.

## The Visitors from Below

Occasionally, fauna from lower elevations attempt to colonize the plateau. They almost never succeed, but the attempts create temporary additions to the ecology.

Climbing insects arrive during warm periods, ascending the gradual slopes from the more oxygen-rich zones below. They find the plateau's air too thin, the food sources incompatible with their metabolisms. Most die within days, their bodies becoming food for substrate-dwellers.

But some undergo emergency adaptation. Their metabolic rates crash, entering torpor-states that reduce oxygen demands. They hide in substrate crevices, in the sheltered spaces beneath mat-plant edges, waiting—for what? Return of conditions they can survive in? Evolutionary rescue through random mutation? They rarely achieve either, but occasionally one adapts, develops the necessary modifications through phenotypic plasticity, becomes a new permanent resident.

These refugees from below are usually malformed, barely functional versions of their baseline species. But over generations, if they survive, they're refined by selection into something that belongs here, something that couldn't exist anywhere else.

The pattern suggests the plateau accretes its ecology gradually, accepting immigrants from more conventional environments and transforming them, incorporating them into its specific conditions, its specific solutions to the problems of existing at impossible altitude.

There are also avian visitors—high-altitude fliers from below that occasionally rise to plateau level. True birds, with feathers and conventional metabolisms, they can survive brief visits, feeding on the plateau's fauna before descending again to richer air.

But some get trapped. Weather changes, storms make descent dangerous, they wait—and waiting consumes energy, requires food, forces them to feed more, to stay longer. Eventually they're caught, unable to leave, forced to adapt or die.

The pseudo-birds—the plateau's resident aerial fauna—might all be descended from such trapped visitors, lineages that lost their feathers over generations as filament-structures proved more efficient in the plateau's specific conditions, that rewired their metabolisms, their reproductive strategies, their entire biology to match this place.

## The Human-Adjacent Forms

Around the settlement areas, unique ecologies have emerged: species that exist specifically in relation to human presence.

The structure-dwellers are the most obvious: organisms that grow in the walls of human habitations, feeding on the molecular-assembler byproducts, the trace organic molecules that accumulate wherever humans live. They're fungal-analogues, mostly, creating networks through the grown-structure walls, occasionally producing visible fruiting bodies that must be periodically cleared to prevent structural compromise.

But they're also beneficial. They metabolize human waste, help process the settlement's organic refuse, contribute to the wall-structures' temperature regulation through their own metabolic heat. They're tolerated, even cultivated—certain strains are encouraged because they produce particularly effective insulation or because their fruiting bodies are bioluminescent, providing interior lighting.

Then there are the followers: small substrate-dwellers that have learned to track human movement, to follow the paths people use regularly, to feed on the disturbed soil, the crushed mat-plant fragments, the skin cells and hair and organic detritus that humans shed continuously.

They're not domesticated—they remain wild, skittish, avoiding direct contact. But they're dependent on human presence, have adapted their entire life-cycles to the patterns of human activity. They breed in areas near settlements, time their reproductive seasons to when human traffic is heaviest and food most abundant.

Some humans feed them deliberately, leaving scraps of synthesized organic material at path-edges, creating feeding stations. The practice is debated—is it creating dependency? Disturbing natural selection? But it continues, driven by the human need to nurture, to create relationship even with forms of life that can't reciprocate meaningfully.

More strange are the learning-forms: organisms that seem to modify their behavior based on observation of human activity. They're anemophyte relatives, but smaller, more mobile, capable of faster responses than their grazing cousins. They watch humans work in gardens, then later those same areas show evidence of the learning-forms having manipulated plants in ways that mimic human cultivation techniques.

They're not intelligent in any conventional sense—they're probably operating through instinct modified by imprinting, behavior patterns that lock onto novel stimuli during developmental windows. But the effect is eerie: seeing a non-human organism performing recognizably human actions, transplanting seedlings, clearing competing growth, arranging plants in almost-geometric patterns.

Are they developing proto-agriculture? Or are they simply mimicking without understanding, performing cargo-cult versions of behaviors they've observed but can't comprehend?

## The Deep-Time Residents

In the oldest areas of the plateau—where the compressed plant-substrate is deepest, most ancient—there are organisms that might predate human presence, might predate even the current configuration of the plateau's ecology.

They're slow beyond measure. Individual organisms that might be thousands of years old, growing through the substrate at rates measured in millimeters per century, metabolisms so reduced that they border on non-life, but maintaining some irreducible minimum of biological process that keeps them categorized as living.

They're often mistaken for geological features—lumps and nodules in the substrate that seem mineral but prove, on examination, to have cellular structure, to show signs of metabolism when instruments sensitive enough to detect it are employed.

What they feed on is unclear. Perhaps they're extracting energy from radioactive decay of trace elements in the bedrock. Perhaps they're tapping into chemical gradients at the substrate-stone boundary, using electron-flows too subtle for conventional organisms to access. Perhaps they're in some form of metabolic stasis, living off reserves accumulated during epochs when the plateau was different, when conditions were more favorable.

They reproduce through fragmentation—pieces breaking off when substrate shifts, each piece potentially becoming a new individual if it lands in viable conditions. The process is essentially geological: reproduction measured in seismic time.

And they might be interconnected. There's evidence—tenuous, requiring interpretation—that these deep-time residents form networks through filaments too thin to see without microscopy, that they exchange signals or substances through these connections, that they might constitute a distributed organism spanning the entire plateau, as old as the substrate itself, as large as the landform, thinking thoughts that require millennia to complete.

## The Threshold Organisms

Finally, there are forms that challenge categorization entirely: entities that exist at the boundaries between living and non-living, between organism and ecosystem, between individual and collective.

The crystal-forests appear after storms, growing from frost-deposits in the hours before sublimation reclaims the water. They look like mineral structures—branching ice-crystals—but they grow too purposefully, in patterns that respond to local conditions in ways that suggest sensing and response. They might be extremely rapid life-cycles, organisms that germinate, grow, reproduce, and die all within the few hours that frost persists. Or they might be crystallization processes that have become so finely tuned to biological templates that they mimic life without possessing it.

The boundary-films appear at interfaces: the line between substrate and bedrock, between air and mat-plant surface, between tree-bark and air. They're molecular-scale, only a few cells thick, but they're metabolically active, extracting energy from the chemical and electrical gradients that exist at such boundaries. They're everywhere, coating every surface, though invisible without magnification.

Are they organisms? Or are they self-organizing chemical systems that happen to meet some definitions of life while violating others? They reproduce, but through simple division that might just be surface-area increase. They metabolize, but through reactions that blur chemistry and biology. They evolve, but through mechanisms that might be Darwinian selection or might just be chemical thermodynamics finding stable configurations.

And there are the sound-eaters: forms that draw energy from the acoustic field itself. They're mostly theoretical—observed effects that suggest their existence without clear evidence of their physical form. Areas where the sieve-trees' songs dampen unexpectedly, where acoustic energy seems to be absorbed by something, where standing wave patterns collapse in ways that suggest intervention.

If they exist, they're probably biofilm-like, coating surfaces in the acoustic shadow of obstacles, metabolizing the sound-pressure that washes over them, converting acoustic energy through piezoelectric processes into chemical bonds. They'd be the plateau's most alien life—organisms that eat vibration, that feed on waves.

---

What lives here is everything that's found ways to exist at the knife-edge of impossible. The plateau is an exercise in testing the boundaries of viability, in finding solutions to problems that shouldn't have solutions. Its ecology is a testament to life's stubborn refusal to accept limits, to the deep creativity of evolutionary processes given enough time and pressure.

And increasingly, what lives here is human-influenced. The nano-technological modifications in the air, in the substrate, in the human bodies themselves—these are leaking into the wider ecology, being taken up by other organisms, creating hybrid forms that are part natural selection, part directed evolution, part technology, part biology, all blurred together until the categories lose meaning.

The plateau's life is becoming weirder, more complex, more thoroughly integrated with the human presence. In another few generations, everything here might be symbiotic with human technology at the molecular level, the distinction between natural and artificial erased completely.

Or perhaps that's already happened. Perhaps the sieve-trees' molecular chemistry, the mat-plants' distributed cognition, the shepherd-forms' electromagnetic sense—perhaps all of these already incorporate nano-technological elements, are already hybrid, have been for longer than anyone realizes.

The plateau doesn't distinguish. It incorporates everything, tests everything, keeps what works and discards what fails. It's utterly indifferent to questions of natural versus artificial, biological versus technological, alive versus unliving.

What matters is persistence. What matters is function. What matters is finding a stable configuration and maintaining it against entropy, against time, against the thin cold air that should allow nothing to exist here at all.

And so it continues: the slow accretion of complexity, the patient exploration of possibility space, the stubborn insistence on life in all its forms, no matter how strange those forms must become.
