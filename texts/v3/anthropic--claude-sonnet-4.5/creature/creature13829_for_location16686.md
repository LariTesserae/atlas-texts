# Who Lives in the Foundry Marsh

They are **the Arrived**.

Not refugees, not colonists, not workers imported for a purpose—these categories are too clean for what's happened here. They are people who heard about a place with ground and space, who followed rumors through maritime networks and overland routes, who came because elsewhere had become untenable or unbearable or simply less possible than here. They arrive on cargo vessels that dock at improvised piers, on the decks of fishing boats, sometimes overland from wherever the aggregate finally connects to older earth. They carry what can be carried. They stay because leaving requires resources they've already spent.

**The Fabricators** work the metal shops and factories, and they are perhaps the plurality, though counting is impossible in this flux. Men and women with burns scarring their forearms in identical patterns—the mark of a particular kind of press, a shared industrial education from a dozen different origins. They speak to each other in the technical pidgin of manufacturing: borrowed words for tolerances and alloys, hand gestures describing the behavior of hot metal. They came because there is work, endless work, and the work asks no questions about credentials or immigration status or the gaps in employment history.

A woman named Vix (or Vex, or Vekks—the transliteration varies) runs a fabrication shop in the shadow of piling forty-seven, sector nine, if the sectors mean anything. She is somewhere between thirty and fifty, age obscured by the particular weathering that comes from standing near furnaces in cold air. Her hands are oversized, the fingers thickened by decades of precise gripping, and she can pick up hot metal with bare fingers for the three seconds necessary to position it. She employs seventeen people, or forty, depending on whether you count the permanent crew or everyone who cycles through for piecework.

Vix came from a coast city to the south—she mentions it matter-of-factly but won't name it—after the sea walls failed. Not dramatically, not all at once, but with the grinding inevitability of infrastructure meeting its design horizon. She describes leaving not as flight but as "following the work north." She has a child here, a daughter of maybe twelve, who navigates the catwalks with absolute confidence and is teaching herself electrical engineering from manuals salvaged from one of the abandoned construction offices. The daughter's name sounds like "Sil" but might be "Zil" or something else entirely. The girl speaks four languages with the fluid code-switching of the marsh-born generation.

Vix is not unusual. Multiply her by thousands: the lathe operator who was a political prisoner, the welder who was a farmer before the drought, the machinist who was a machinist but whose city no longer exists. They are practical people made more practical by circumstance. They fix what breaks. They modify equipment to perform functions it was never designed for. They work in shifts that align with power availability and material delivery and the mysterious tides of factory operation.

**The Harvesters** work the strait and the thickets and the strange productive chaos of this place. Fishermen and fisherwomen who came from maritime cultures around this sea—though "this sea" is a polite fiction, as they come from different seas entirely, now connected to this strait by economic and environmental collapse elsewhere. They know cold water fishing, net mending, the signs of weather in wave patterns. They've had to learn the strait's particular strangeness: the unusual stillness, the dark water, what's safe to eat and what isn't.

An old man—actually probably only fifty but aged by sun and cold—called Peiter tends a fishing station built into the pilings at the water's edge. He speaks a language no one else here speaks as their first tongue, communicates primarily through gesture and the limited pidgin that's emerging. He was a deep-water fisherman before, he indicates, drawing his hand in a gesture that encompasses enormous distance. Now he fishes from platforms and teaches younger people to do the same. He knows which of the constructs will help pull up nets (the small spider-forms sometimes assist, for unknowable reasons) and which to avoid (anything with red markings will cut lines).

Peiter has discovered that the fish from the strait—pale things with too many fins, dark things with bioluminescent patches, normal-looking cod that taste of minerals—can be processed in ways that preserve them for weeks. He's developed a smoking technique using resin from the thicket plants, and his fish are traded throughout the settlement. He lives in a structure built into a gap between pilings, partially over water, accessible only by ladder. He has no family here that anyone knows of, but he's adopted a rotating cast of apprentices: young people who need to learn a trade, who are drawn to the water, who are comfortable with the precarity of tide-dependent work.

The thicket foragers are a different type: opportunists who've learned that the aggressive vegetation is useful. They harvest the willow scrub for fiber that's woven into textiles. They collect the horsetail rushes, which contain something that works in metal polish. They know which of the moss varieties are edible (the darker chartreuse, bitter but nutritious) and which are mildly toxic (the lighter yellow-green, causes skin irritation). They move through the thickets with the confidence of people who understand a landscape, even a landscape this young and strange.

A person called Kreeg—gender ambiguous, undefined, or simply not relevant in the way they present themselves—leads foraging expeditions into the deeper thickets toward the inland edge. Kreeg is tall and thin in a way that suggests long-term under-nutrition overcome, moving with careful efficiency. They came from somewhere inland, somewhere hot, and speak of it rarely but with unmistakable relief at being in a cold place. They know plants but had to learn these plants specifically, experimenting systematically to determine what's useful.

Kreeg has discovered that the thickets support animal life no one planned for: the rabbit-bird things that leave mixed tracks, small predators like ermines, and something larger that stays unseen but leaves territorial markings. They trap some, observe others, and have developed an understanding of the thicket ecology that they share through demonstration rather than lecture. They're training a cohort of younger foragers, street children mostly, who were starving when they arrived and are now lean and competent, moving through vegetation like fish through water.

**The Constructors** are building constantly, and they are perhaps the strangest category because they include both the desperate and the visionary. People who need shelter immediately, who are assembling four walls and a roof from whatever's available, working with the frantic efficiency of those who've been homeless. But also architects and engineers—some trained, some self-taught, some simply possessed of three-dimensional thinking—who see in this unfinished place a kind of freedom.

A collective called the Bridgers, seven or nine or twelve people depending on who's answering, specialize in the catwalks and elevated connections. They're led—if "led" is the right word for what seems like radically horizontal organization—by a woman named Tohr, who was a structural engineer in a previous life. She's maybe forty, compact and muscular, with the direct gaze of someone who's watched things collapse and learned from it.

Tohr speaks precisely, in any of three languages, about load distribution and material stress and failure modes. She came here deliberately, she says, because "it's all still negotiable." She means the city's form, its shape, its possibility space. The Bridgers don't just connect existing structures; they impose a logic on the chaos, creating routes that make sense for human movement, that account for the vertical space. They're often hired but sometimes just build because they see a need. They mark their constructions with a particular knot pattern in the support cables—a signature, a guarantee.

The Bridgers include a young man named Edder who was a circus performer and understands tension and balance in his bones. An older woman called Aunt (probably not anyone's actual aunt) who was a rigger on sailing vessels and knows rope work. A teenager, name unclear, who is absolutely fearless at heights and serves as the crew's tester, walking new construction to feel for weaknesses. They work in weather that would stop other trades, because stopping means someone can't get home, can't reach work, is isolated in their dwelling.

Then there are the **Builder-Dwellers**, people who construct their own habitations with obsessive specificity. A man named Fenj has spent two years building a structure that spirals upward around a piling, seven levels of increasingly complex joinery, each level slightly rotated from the one below. He works in salvaged timber, mostly, and the construction is both home and artwork and mathematical proof of some concept he can't or won't articulate. He lives on the third level, uses the others for storage, meditation, experiments in spatial organization.

Fenj came from a city of towers—he mentions this—where he was an academic of some kind. He's thin, abstracted, often covered in sawdust, occasionally cuts himself badly because he's thinking about angles rather than fingers. People bring him food sometimes, because it's unclear if he remembers to eat. His structure has become a landmark, a navigation point: "three levels up from Fenj's spiral."

**The Providers** run the markets, the food stalls, the services that keep the workers working. They are often women, though not exclusively, and they are often older, though not always. They understand networks and obligation and the complex economics of a place where currency is unreliable and barter has nuance.

A woman everyone calls Moth—small, maybe sixty, with white hair cut close and hands that move constantly, expressively—operates a food stall that migrates around the settlement. She serves soup, mostly, made from strait fish and thicket vegetables and things she sources from other providers. The soup is always hot, always cheap enough that even the poorest workers can afford it, and it's probably keeping a hundred people alive who would otherwise be malnourished.

Moth came here with a husband who died in the first year, in an accident involving a crane and bad timing. She speaks of him matter-of-factly, as someone who existed and then didn't, and whose absence created the need to survive. She has no children but has adopted strays: an elderly man with dementia who helps at the stall, performing simple tasks with dignity; a girl of maybe eight who's an orphan or might as well be, who runs errands and is learning the trade.

The stall is a social hub. People eat standing up, exchange news, find work through recommendations. Moth knows everyone, forgets nothing, and occasionally extends credit to people she's judged worthy. Her economic model is mysterious—she should go bankrupt daily but never does. She's respected in a way that transcends commerce; she's part of the settlement's immune system, keeping the body fed.

There are other providers with other specialties: a man called Thread who repairs clothes and teaches sewing, working from a space so small he has to sit cross-legged but which is warm and lit and accepts no payment from those who have nothing. A woman named, improbably, Doctor (she is not a doctor, or wasn't, or was something close enough), who provides basic medical care—wound cleaning, splinting, herbal remedies learned here and elsewhere—from a clinic that's open when she's awake, which is most of the time.

Doctor is maybe fifty, heavily built, with a face that suggests both exhaustion and absolute refusal to acknowledge it. She came from a hospital that closed, or that she left, or that was destroyed—the story changes slightly each time she tells it, as if the specifics matter less than the trajectory. She treats the injuries this place produces: burns, crush wounds, infections from the water, respiratory problems from the industrial air. She accepts payment in kind, or labor, or nothing. She's training several apprentices, teaching them what she knows, which is a mix of formal medicine and improvisation.

**The Children** are their own category, and they're perhaps the most adapted to this place.

The older children, teenagers, work adult jobs or nearly so. They're in the factories, on the fishing platforms, doing courier runs through the vertical city with a speed adults can't match. They form crews, loose affiliations based on geography or trade or affinity. They have their own hierarchies, their own disputes, their own justice systems that occasionally intersect with adult concerns but often operate independently.

A girl of maybe fifteen called Spark—definitely a nickname, probably earned through personality rather than incident—leads a crew of couriers who navigate the settlement carrying messages, small packages, information. She's wire-thin, burns energy at an implausible rate, speaks the emerging pidgin as a first language and several other languages partially. She was born elsewhere but arrived young enough that she claims no other home.

Spark's crew includes a boy called Tick (all the children seem to have these compressed names, sounds that carry), who is mechanically brilliant and often fixes equipment as he passes it, just casually, as if he can't help himself. A pair of twins whose names sound identical to most speakers but whom everyone can somehow tell apart. A younger girl, maybe twelve, called Stone, who is the crew's muscle despite her size—she's been in the marsh since age five and has a quality of absolute fearlessness that borders on concerning.

These children don't attend school because there is no school, but they're learning constantly: mathematics from managing trade, engineering from observation and experimentation, politics from negotiating with adults who try to cheat them. They're teaching each other to read when they have time, passing around salvaged books and tablets with partial charges. They're inventing games that use the three-dimensional space: tag that involves climbing, hide-and-seek in the thickets, something involving the constructs that seems dangerous but which the constructs permit.

The younger children are wilder, less integrated into the economy. They move in packs, foraging and playing and sometimes stealing, though the theft is usually minor and often ignored because what's the alternative? They're malnourished in a way the settlement is gradually correcting—there's more food now than there was, the systems are stabilizing. They're being absorbed into households that aren't necessarily their biological families: people take them in, feed them, put them to work in ways that might be exploitation or might be education or might be both.

A boy of maybe six, name uncertain (he answers to several), lives between households, sleeping in a different place each night, fed by whoever's nearby. He's bright-eyed and undersized and moves through the settlement like he owns it. He knows where the constructs are at any given time, can predict the eleven-minute rains with uncanny accuracy, finds things people have lost. He might be an orphan or might have parents who can't care for him or might simply prefer this life of radical independence. Adults worry about him and also rely on him; he's become a kind of mascot, a lucky symbol.

**The Maintainers** form an unofficial caste, people who've taken responsibility for the settlement's infrastructure. They're electricians working on the unreliable grid, plumbers managing the water systems, technicians trying to understand the constructs.

A man named Osk, heavy and slow-moving with the careful patience of someone who works with dangerous systems, maintains the power grid in sector seven. He's taught himself electrical engineering through trial and error that's left him with burn scars and a deep respect for voltage. He's maybe forty, maybe older, came from an industrial background but won't specify what kind. He works odd hours, responding to outages, upgrading connections when he finds better materials.

Osk has concluded that the grid is semi-intelligent, that it responds to demand in ways that aren't purely mechanical. He's probably wrong, or he's noticed something real, and either way he talks to the system, warning it before he makes changes, thanking it when power flows correctly. He's training two apprentices: a woman named Sil (different from Vix's daughter) who was a data technician in a previous life and is learning hardware, and a young man called Bronze (probably for his hair color) who has no prior training but intuitive understanding.

There are maintainers for the water systems, people who've mapped the underground springs and the drainage channels and who respond when new water sources emerge. A crew that works on the pilings themselves, checking for structural integrity, reinforcing weak points, because if the pilings fail entire sections of the city collapse. Technicians who study the constructs, trying to predict their behavior, trying to communicate, trying to redirect their efforts toward useful ends.

One of these construct-specialists is a person called Vey, thin and intense with the quality of someone on a research obsession. Vey was an AI researcher, or a roboticist, or something in that field—the specifics are vague because the institutions they worked for may no longer exist. They've been documenting the constructs systematically: photographing them, tracking their movements, recording their actions. They have a theory that the constructs are running on ancient programming that's degrading in interesting ways, creating emergent behaviors.

Vey lives in what was probably designed as a communications hub, a small building full of equipment bays, most empty, some still functioning. They've set up a lab of sorts, screens showing construct movements, notes covering walls. They barely sleep. They accept food from neighbors who worry about them. They occasionally announce discoveries to anyone who'll listen: "The spider-forms are responding to plant growth, they're maintaining a balance," or "The tank-things are doing archaeological preservation, they're protecting the rock."

**The Artists** exist in smaller numbers but with disproportionate impact. People who make things not because they're useful but because making is necessary.

A woman named (or called, or known as) Indigo paints murals on the buildings, enormous works that cover entire walls. She paints the strait, the thickets, the workers, the children, scenes of daily life monumentalized. She uses paint salvaged from industrial supplies, mixed to create colors that shouldn't exist: purples that glow in fog, grays that shift depending on light angle. She's maybe thirty, tall and paint-stained, originally from somewhere with an art tradition she references but doesn't explain.

Indigo came here deliberately, she says, because "walls need painting and no one's enforcing aesthetic committees." She works on commission sometimes but often just paints what she sees or imagines, and the settlement has accepted her work as part of the landscape. Her murals serve as landmarks, as decorations, as something beautiful in a place that didn't plan for beauty. She's training several young people, not formally, but they follow her and help and learn to see composition, color theory, the politics of public art.

There's a musician called String—probably plays stringed instruments, though the name might be coincidence—who performs from high windows in the evening. Plays a modified instrument, something between a guitar and a dulcimer, producing music that echoes across the settlement. The music is melancholic and repetitive, almost meditative, and work stops when String plays. No one knows much about String, who speaks rarely, accepts no payment, appears to live on air and fish soup.

A sculptor works with salvaged metal, creating abstract forms that are installed on rooftops, on the ends of piers, in the thickets. The sculptor is rarely seen, but the work appears regularly, signed with a welded symbol no one can interpret. The sculptures are beautiful in an industrial way, all curves and angles, and they serve no purpose except to exist. They're respected. No one steals them, no one vandalizes them. They're part of what the settlement is becoming.

**The Elders** aren't necessarily old—age is relative—but they're people the settlement has begun to regard as holders of memory and wisdom.

An old woman, actually old, maybe seventy, called Grandmother (probably not anyone's specific grandmother), lives in a dwelling near the rock at the settlement's inland edge. She was here early, one of the first to arrive when the ground was barely solid, and she's seen the settlement grow. She speaks slowly, precisely, in a language that's no one's first language here but which many understand partially. She's a repository of stories: who died in which accident, who built what first, how the markets emerged, when the constructs began changing behavior.

Grandmother doesn't give advice unless asked, but when she's asked she answers with stories rather than directives: "There was a man who tried to force the thickets back with fire," she'll say, and then describe what happened (the fire spread, nearly burned a section, people formed chains with water buckets, the thickets grew back thicker). The lessons are implicit. Young people come to her with disputes, with questions, with confessions. She listens, tells stories, sends them away to figure out their own answers.

She lives alone but is never isolated; people visit constantly. She's teaching a girl of maybe ten to remember stories, training her as a successor, passing on the narrative infrastructure. The girl, called Echo or something similar, is learning to listen, to hold details, to recognize patterns. She's being prepared for a role that doesn't have a name but which the settlement clearly needs.

There's a former engineer, a man called Trace, who's transitioned from building to teaching. He's maybe sixty, moves with the care of someone with joint problems, has been in the marsh for years. He holds informal classes in a space under piling twenty-something, teaching mathematics and physics and structural principles to anyone who shows up. His students are mostly young, teenagers and twenties, people who need knowledge to advance in their trades. He charges nothing, or accepts payment in food, or just teaches because the knowledge needs passing on.

Trace came from a university that closed, or that expelled him, or that he left—he's vague on the details. He speaks of the old world with nostalgia and criticism: "We knew how to build things that lasted, but we didn't know how to build things that adapted." He's fascinated by the marsh's improvisation, its radical pragmatism, and he's trying to theorize it, to find the principles in the chaos. He's writing a book, or was, but the manuscript got wet and he lost months of work. He's started over, more carefully, teaching in the morning and writing in the afternoon.

**The Spiritualists** practice varieties of faith, adapted to this place.

A woman who might be a priestess, or was, or has simply assumed the role, holds services in a space that's different each time—she announces locations and people gather. She leads prayers and meditations that draw from multiple traditions, syncretized into something new. She's maybe fifty, soft-spoken, with an authority that's about presence rather than volume. She came from a religious community that dispersed, she indicates, and she's trying to continue the practice of gathering, of collective meaning-making.

Her services address the strangeness directly: prayers thanking the constructs for their help, meditations on the fog and what it conceals, discussions of what it means to live in a place that isn't finished. She's not dogmatic; she incorporates elements from whatever traditions people bring, creating something fluid and responsive. She officiates at marriages, births, deaths, the lifecycle events that happen constantly in this dense population.

There are other spiritual practitioners: a man who reads patterns in the moss growth, claiming predictive abilities that might be real or might be cold reading. A woman who leads dawn gatherings at the water's edge, silent meditations as the fog rolls in. A group that's developed a mythology around the rock, treating it as sacred, forbidden, or both. They leave offerings at its edge—flowers forced in cold frames, carved pieces, food—and claim the rock speaks to those who listen correctly, though what it says varies by interpreter.

**The Sick and Broken** exist in larger numbers than the settlement would prefer to acknowledge. People damaged by the journey here, by the work, by the conditions.

A man in his thirties, name lost to a head injury, who wanders the settlement speaking to people who aren't there. He's harmless but disoriented, sometimes lucid and sometimes not. He was a translator, people say, spoke eight languages, took a fall from a catwalk and now speaks fragments of all of them simultaneously. People feed him, redirect him when he's walking toward danger, treat him as a kind of holy fool.

A woman with respiratory damage from industrial exposure, probably in her forties, who can't work anymore and lives on community support. She sits in a sheltered spot near a market area, mending clothes for those who bring them, working slowly, coughing frequently. She was a factory supervisor, people say, worked too many shifts in unventilated spaces, and now she's paying the price. She's bitter about it sometimes, philosophical other times. She's training young people in textile repair, passing on skills, finding purpose in teaching.

There are addicts, people numbing themselves with substances that arrive on the supply boats or are distilled locally. They're tolerated unless they become dangerous, at which point the community responds with varying levels of compassion and pragmatism. Some recover, finding work and structure. Some don't, dying of exposure or accident or the substances themselves. The settlement hasn't developed a systematic response; it handles each case individually, with uneven success.

A young man, maybe twenty-five, lives in an abandoned construction vehicle, struggling with addiction to something processed from thicket plants. He's trying to quit, relapses frequently, is supported by a handful of people who've decided he's worth the effort. One of them is his sister, or says she is, who works in a factory and brings him food and sits with him during withdrawal. Another is a former addict who's been clean for years and recognizes the pattern. They're keeping him alive, barely, hoping he'll stabilize.

**The Criminals** operate in the gaps, because any settlement this dense and this ungoverned creates criminal opportunities.

A network of thieves works the market areas, the docks, anywhere goods concentrate. They're mostly young, fast, organized enough to be effective but not so organized they draw concentrated response. They steal food, materials, occasionally currency, usually in amounts that individuals can absorb. It's parasitism, not predation. The settlement tolerates them up to a point; beyond that point, justice is swift and informal.

A woman called Knife—definitely earned through use rather than metaphor—runs something like protection in several sectors. Pay her, and your business won't have accidents, your workers won't get hurt, your supplies won't disappear. It's extortion, but it's also genuine protection; she keeps order in areas that would otherwise be chaotic. She's maybe thirty-five, scarred across the face from something she doesn't discuss, with a reputation for extreme violence that she maintains through careful displays.

Knife employs a crew of enforcers, mostly men, mostly large, who make their presence known without usually needing to act. She pays them well, which is unusual in an economy of scarcity. She's smart enough to avoid the truly poor—there's nothing to extract from people with nothing—and targets the successful operations, the factories and markets that can afford her rates. She's hated and relied upon; she's filling a governance gap that nothing else has filled.

There are smugglers who bring in contraband—drugs, luxury goods, banned materials—on the supply boats or through overland routes. A man called Slip, thin and nervous and always watching for exits, coordinates distribution. He's connected to networks outside the settlement, part of a larger economy of illegality. He's careful, paranoid, has survived this long because he trusts no one. He's also surprisingly ethical within his domain: he doesn't cut products dangerously, doesn't recruit children, doesn't operate near schools (such as they are). Criminal honor among thieves, but it's something.

**The Outsiders** are people who've isolated themselves partially or completely, living on the settlement's edges or in its interstices.

A person who might be a woman, might be a man, might be neither, called the Walker, circles the settlement's perimeter constantly, following the boundary between built and unbuilt space. They walk all day, sleep briefly, resume walking. They carry a pack with minimal supplies, accept food when offered, rarely speak. They've been doing this for at least a year. No one knows why. Theories proliferate: they're searching for something, they're running from something, they're performing a ritual, they're mentally ill, they're enlightened. Maybe all, maybe none.

The Walker moves through all weather, including the freezing rains, which they simply endure. They've been seen talking to the constructs, particularly the large tank-things. They leave marks on rocks and walls, symbols that might be navigation aids or might be communication attempts or might be meaningless. Children follow them sometimes, mimicking their walk, turning it into a game. Adults watch them with concern and incomprehension and occasional envy—there's freedom in that isolation, however strange.

A group of maybe five or seven people, count uncertain, lives deep in the thickets toward the inland edge, near but not on the rock. They've built structures that are invisible until you're nearly on top of them, woven from thicket materials, covered in moss for insulation. They hunt the rabbit-bird things, harvest plants, rarely enter the main settlement. When they do come in, it's to trade: furs, medicinal plants, information about thicket conditions. They speak their own dialect, a mix that's incomprehensible to most, and they seem to prefer it that way.

Their leader, if they have one, is a person called Root or Rut, difficult to tell through accent. They're ageless—could be thirty, could be fifty—weathered by outdoor living, competent in ways the settlement's indoor workers aren't. They know the thickets in detail, know the animal behaviors, know which constructs frequent which areas. They're respected as experts, consulted when the thickets present problems, but they're also slightly feared. They've gone native in a place that hasn't been around long enough to have natives.

**The Newcomers** arrive constantly, and they're immediately visible in their disorientation.

A family of four—parents, two children—arrived last week, or yesterday, or will arrive tomorrow; there's always a family arriving. They're from a coastal city to the south, or a mountain community to the east, or somewhere else entirely. They stand in the market area, overwhelmed by the density and the noise and the strangeness, trying to understand how to obtain food, shelter, work. They have nothing or almost nothing: clothes, documents that probably mean nothing here, hope that's already wearing thin.

The settlement's response varies. Sometimes people help immediately: Moth gives them soup, someone offers floor space, a factory supervisor hires the father for day labor. Sometimes they're ignored, left to figure it out themselves, which they eventually do or they don't. The survival rate is high but not perfect. Some families integrate within weeks, the parents finding work, the children adapting with flexible resilience. Others struggle for months, or give up and try to leave, or dissolve as individuals pursue different strategies.

A young man arrived alone recently, maybe twenty, carrying nothing but a waterproof pack and a desperate need to not be wherever he came from. He won't give a name, answers to "Hey" or whatever people call him. He's educated—you can hear it in his grammar when he speaks carefully—but is deliberately obscuring his background. He's taken work in the fish processing plant, brutal work that no one does unless they're desperate or hiding. He keeps his head down, doesn't socialize, is clearly running from something.

The other workers have opinions about him: some think he's a criminal, some think he's political, some don't care as long as he does his share. A woman at the plant, older and observant, has decided to protect him slightly, showing him tricks that make the work easier, not asking questions. He's grateful in a way that suggests he's not used to kindness. He'll either integrate eventually, his past becoming irrelevant, or it'll catch up with him and he'll disappear as suddenly as he arrived.

**The Dreamers and Planners** are people who look at the settlement and see something other than what it is, who have visions of what it could become.

A man called Prospect, probably a nickname related to his future-orientation, has drawn maps of the settlement as it should be: rational districts, planned infrastructure, green spaces, public buildings. He's maybe forty, trained as an urban planner in a city that no longer exists or no longer plans. He arrived expecting to find work in his field and found instead this chaotic accretion. Rather than despairing, he's documenting and proposing.

Prospect gives presentations to anyone who'll listen, usually in the evening in borrowed spaces. He projects his maps on walls using a salvaged device, describes his vision in passionate detail: "The settlement could support twice the population with proper organization," he says. "The vertical space is underutilized. The thickets could be integrated as parks. The industrial areas should be zoned for noise." His audiences are polite, sometimes interested, but nothing changes. The settlement grows according to its own logic, resistant to planning.

He's frustrated but persistent. He's training a few young people in mapping and design, passing on principles they might use someday. He's creating an archive of the settlement's growth, photographs and sketches and measurements, documenting the chaos for future analysis. He's convinced that eventually the settlement will be ready for planning, that the pioneer phase will end and the organizational phase will begin. He might be right. He might be waiting forever.

There's a collective that wants to formalize governance, to create councils or committees or some structure of collective decision-making. They're led by a woman named Charter (definitely a symbolic name), who was involved in municipal government elsewhere and sees the settlement's lack of structure as both opportunity and crisis. She's organized meetings, tried to establish rules, proposed systems for resource allocation and dispute resolution.

It goes nowhere, or everywhere, or somewhere in between. People attend her meetings when issues arise that affect them, ignore her when things are working well enough. The settlement has developed informal governance—Moth's network of providers, Knife's enforcement structure, the Bridgers' infrastructure decisions—but it resists formalization. Charter is persistent, exhausting, sometimes effective. She's helped mediate major disputes, organized responses to crises, pushed for safety standards in factories. She's creating the bones of government whether the settlement wants it or not.

**The Silent and Forgotten** are people who've been absorbed into the settlement's background, who perform essential work without recognition.

The night cleaners who collect waste, who shovel out the drainage channels, who do the work no one wants to do. They're often older, often damaged, often people with no better options. They work in darkness, invisible to the day workers, keeping the settlement from drowning in its own refuse. They're paid poorly or not at all, surviving on scraps and charity and the occasional cash from those who notice.

One of them is an old man called Sweep, bent by age or injury, who's cleaned a section of the market area for years. He's there in the early morning, before the sellers arrive, clearing away the previous day's debris. He speaks to himself, or to someone who isn't there, running monologues that might be prayer or memory or madness. People leave him food sometimes, coins, show small kindnesses. He's part of the landscape, as essential and invisible as the ground.

The caregivers who look after those who can't care for themselves: the very old, the very young, the sick, the mentally broken. They're usually women, though not always, working from their homes or small facilities, accepting whoever needs care. They're paid minimally by families who can afford it, work for free when they can't. They're exhausted constantly, performing labor that's essential and undervalued and endless.

A woman called Mercy—probably another symbolic name—runs a care facility in a converted storage space, looking after six or eight elderly people who have no families or whose families can't manage them. She feeds them, cleans them, keeps them warm, holds their hands when they die. She's maybe forty-five, worn down by the work, but she keeps doing it because someone has to. She's training a younger woman, passing on the skills and the burden, trying to create continuity of care.

These invisible workers are the settlement's foundation, doing the necessary things that enable everyone else to function. They're starting to organize, to demand recognition and payment and respect. Charter has been helping them, amplifying their voices, trying to formalize their role. It's slow work, but it's happening. The settlement is learning that even in chaos, some work is so essential it can't be ignored.

---

The people of the Foundry Marsh are not a community in the traditional sense—they're too new, too diverse, too transient. But they're becoming something, developing connections and patterns and shared understanding. They're teaching each other languages, trades, survival strategies. They're forming bonds: romantic partnerships, friendships, mentor relationships, chosen families.

They're adapting to the strangeness: the cold fog, the eleven-minute rains, the constructs, the aggressive vegetation, the ground that's still settling. The children are growing up thinking this is normal. The adults are forgetting what normal was.

They fight constantly: over resources, over space, over work, over debts and insults and history brought from elsewhere. Violence flares regularly—fistfights, knife fights, occasionally worse. People die from accidents and violence and disease and the basic precarity of life here. But they also cooperate, constantly, because survival requires it. They pull together during crises, then fall apart into factional disputes when the crisis passes.

They're creating culture without meaning to: the pidgin language that's emerging from necessity, the food traditions that mix dozens of origins, the music that combines instruments and styles, the fashion that's purely functional but somehow developing aesthetics. They're telling stories about the place, creating myths: the rock is a sleeping giant, the constructs are ghosts of the original builders, the strait contains monsters, the fog hides doorways to other places.

They're building something, or they're part of something building itself, a settlement that might become a city or might collapse or might transform into something else entirely. They're living in the present tense, intensely, because the future is uncertain and the past is somewhere else. They're making do, making art, making love, making mistakes, making noise, making lives in this cold, damp, vertical, impossible place.

They are the Arrived, and they're here, and here is becoming theirs.
