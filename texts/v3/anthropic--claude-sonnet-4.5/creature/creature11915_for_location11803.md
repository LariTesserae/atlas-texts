# What Lives in the Stillwater Canopy

The **harvesters** drift along their routes with the patience of glaciers. Each one is cathedral-sized, segmented like a centipede, its articulated body flowing along the kelp highways in predetermined paths worn smooth by centuries of passage. Their arms—twelve per unit, sometimes more on the older models—move in rhythms so precise they seem choreographed, stripping fronds with delicate brutality, the blades at each arm's terminus making cuts that heal almost immediately, as if the kelp has grown to expect this particular violence. Inside their translucent carapaces you can see the processing chambers, golden-brown sludge moving through intestinal corridors, being separated and refined and expelled. They are not intelligent but they are aware—they alter their routes slightly to avoid collision with swimmers, with each other, with the rare massive creature that won't move. Their hulls sing with the vibration of their work, each one a different note depending on its size and the wear patterns of its mechanisms. 

Some harvesters are so old they've become ecosystems. The **Matriarch Units**—that's not a name anyone gave them; it's just what they are—have entire gardens growing on their backs, terraced levels of plant life that use the machine's waste heat as an energy source. Small creatures nest in their joints, feeding on the kelp paste that leaks from imperfect seals. One unit, distinctive for the spiral of purple algae that coats its forward section, has a crack in its primary housing that releases a steady stream of processed oil. A whole community follows it: specialized filter-feeders that can metabolize only this particular compound, and in turn, things that feed on them. The Matriarch doesn't notice or care. It has been following the same route for so long it has worn a groove in the water itself—a slight temperature differential, a chemical trail, something that makes its path visible to certain senses even when the machine isn't there.

The **glass fish** move in schools that reconfigure constantly, each individual responding to signals from its neighbors in cascading waves of decision. Their bodies are windows: you can see their crystalline hearts pumping something that isn't quite blood, their geometric organs processing food through mathematical rather than chemical digestion. They eat the particulate matter that drifts from machine exhaust ports, clustering around harvesters like birds around a plow. When threatened—by the larger predators, or sometimes by each other during spawning aggregations—they scatter in perfect synchronization, then reassemble exactly as they were, each fish returning to its previous position in the formation. Their spawning is strange: they release clouds of something between eggs and seeds, crystalline structures that drift for months before settling into crevices in the kelp bark, where they grow into new fish over the course of years. The young emerge fully formed, miniature versions of adults, and immediately seek out a school, integrating themselves into its geometry.

Some glass fish are **broken**—their internal structures fractured, their bodies deformed—but they don't die. They can't, or perhaps don't know how. They drift at the edges of schools, unable to keep up, their damaged organs still functioning but incorrectly, producing light at wrong intervals or sounds that don't match the communication patterns of healthy fish. The schools don't reject them, but don't quite accept them either; they occupy a liminal space, following but never quite integrated. Over time, their broken bodies accumulate additional growth, algae colonizing their internal spaces through cracks in their transparent skin, until they become small floating gardens, still alive, still swimming, but more plant than animal now.

The **threadworms** are everywhere but rarely seen. They're thin as wire, some only inches long, others extending for hundreds of yards, woven through the kelp bark like veins. They're segmented, each segment capable of independent action but all coordinated by chemical signals passed along their length. They eat the microscopic plants that grow on the kelp surface, processing them through bodies that are essentially mobile digestive tracts. Where they've fed, the kelp bark shows patterns—geometric shapes, spirals, mazes—not created intentionally but emerging from the interaction between worm feeding behavior and plant growth patterns. Some of these patterns are ancient, weathered but still visible, suggesting the same threadworm lineages have been following the same paths for generations beyond counting.

When threadworms die—from predation, from age, from the rare chemical imbalance—their bodies don't decay normally. Something in their tissue prevents bacterial breakdown. Instead they **calcify**, hardening into mineral threads still woven through the kelp, becoming part of the structure. Over centuries, some kelp stems have accumulated so many calcified threadworm corpses they've become partially fossilized, their bark hard as stone in places, still living but also geological.

The **surgeons** are solitary, territorial, and no two look quite alike. They're roughly the size of a human, with bodies that seem assembled from spare parts—mechanical limbs grafted to organic torsos, biological eyes set in metal skulls, skin that transitions abruptly from flesh to plastic to something in between. They claim sections of kelp forest as their domains, attacking any other surgeon that enters, but ignoring all other life. Their purpose, if they have one, appears to be **maintenance**. They tend to the machines: clearing blocked intake ports, removing dead creatures from conveyor mechanisms, even performing crude repairs using materials scavenged from older, more damaged equipment. They also tend to the kelp, though differently—cutting away diseased fronds, removing parasites, sometimes splitting open bladders that have grown too large and are threatening to tear free.

Where surgeons come from is unclear. No one has seen a young one. No one has seen them reproduce. Yet their population remains stable—when one disappears, eventually another appears in the same territory, sometimes similar in appearance, sometimes radically different. A theory, unprovable: they're created by the deep machinery, assembled from whatever materials are available, programmed with maintenance protocols, and released. But they evolve—each generation slightly different, adapted to local conditions, suggesting either the machinery is learning or the surgeons are genuinely alive and reproducing in some way that leaves no visible evidence.

One surgeon, **the Collector**, is different from the others. It doesn't maintain. It gathers. Its territory is marked by piles of objects arranged in spiral patterns: machine parts, shells, bones of larger creatures, polymer beads sorted by color, pieces of kelp bred or cut into specific shapes. It adds to these arrangements constantly, sometimes rearranging entire sections, working with what appears to be aesthetic intent. Other surgeons avoid its territory despite its reluctance to defend it aggressively. The Collector will cease its work to observe visitors, following them at a distance, its mismatched eyes—one organic, one a glowing mechanical lens—tracking their movements with uncomfortable intensity.

The **hymnal rays** undulate through the middle depths in what seems to be a migration pattern, except they never arrive anywhere, just circuit endlessly. Their wing-spans can exceed thirty feet, their bodies translucent enough to reveal internal structures that are unmistakably mechanical—gears, pistons, what looks like printed circuitry—yet these components are made of bone and cartilage, grown rather than assembled. They emit the harmonic tones that add to the soundscape, each individual singing a single sustained note that varies slightly with age and size. When multiple rays pass near each other, their songs create interference patterns, moments of resonance or dissonance that seem to affect the behavior of nearby glass fish and even the buoyancy of kelp bladders.

Hymnal rays feed by filtering, their massive mouths sieving water for the smallest organisms, but they also seem to feed on sound itself—they congregate in areas of high machine activity, absorbing vibrations through specialized organs along their flanks, and in these places they grow faster, their bodies incorporating more mechanical elements. Old rays become sluggish, heavy with bone-gears, eventually sinking to deeper waters where they're rarely seen again. Whether they die there or transform into something else is unknown.

The **bore-shells** drift on currents, spiral shells the size of cars, their exteriors pocked and weathered by decades of mineral accretion. Hundreds of tentacles extend from the shell opening, each one covered in stinging cells that paralyze small fish and threadworms. But they also filter-feed, drawing in water and straining out nutrients, making them omnivorous in a way that seems inefficient—why hunt when you can filter, why filter when you can hunt? Observation suggests they do both because they're **remembering** different evolutionary strategies, their bodies containing genetic memory of multiple adaptive paths, unable or unwilling to commit to just one.

Inside their shells, accessible only through dissection or the rare chance of finding a dead specimen, the bore-shells have complex internal structures—chambers filled with different fluids, membranes that serve unclear purposes, and always, in the deepest chamber, a pearl. Not decorative: functional. The pearl is a crystallized mass of processed minerals, growing throughout the bore-shell's life, and it seems to serve as a kind of ballast or perhaps a computational substrate, because bore-shells with larger pearls navigate more effectively, avoiding obstacles, finding food sources more reliably.

When bore-shells mate—a rare event, witnessed only a handful of times—they spiral around each other for hours, tentacles intertwined, releasing clouds of gametes that mix in the water and develop into free-swimming larvae. These larvae are tiny, transparent, and utterly unlike their parents: elongated, with paddle-like fins, hunting actively for months before settling onto a piece of kelp bark and beginning to secrete the first whorl of shell. During this settlement, they're vulnerable, and most are eaten, but those that survive grow rapidly, their shells hardening in a matter of weeks.

The **deeplurkers** are only ever seen partially. A massive tentacle emerging from the blue darkness below, wrapping around a section of kelp stem, then withdrawing. A vast shape moving in the peripheral vision, gone when you turn to look directly. Sounds that don't match any known creature—scraping, booming, a strange clicking that accelerates into a buzz before stopping abruptly. They might be single organisms of impossible size, or they might be many creatures moving in coordination, or they might be extensions of the kelp itself, mobile roots that occasionally reach up from the anchoring structures. 

What's known: they take things. Entire kelp sections disappear overnight, cleanly separated, leaving no debris. Machines sometimes vanish, even large ones, with no evidence of what removed them. Surgeon territories are occasionally found empty, all the surgeon's modifications and repairs intact but the surgeon itself gone. The deeplurkers never attack swimmers directly—or haven't yet—but their presence is a constant weight, the knowledge that something vast moves in the darkness below, doing something, for reasons that remain entirely opaque.

Some machines have been modified by **something with intention and skill**—not surgeons, whose repairs are crude and pragmatic. These modifications are elegant: a harvester's arms rebalanced for greater efficiency, a processing platform's filters rebuilt to handle particle sizes the original design couldn't, pipes rerouted to reduce turbulence. The modifications use materials that aren't found elsewhere in the system, suggesting either a hidden cache or the ability to synthesize new compounds. No one has caught whoever or whatever is doing this work. The leading theory blames the deeplurkers, but this feels like assigning any mystery to the largest unknown, a failure of imagination.

The **colony-jellies** are perhaps the strangest synthesis of plant and animal. They appear as masses of translucent tissue, often draped over kelp bladders like melted wax, pulsing gently with their own bioluminescence. But they're not single organisms—each colony-jelly is composed of thousands of individual polyps, each specialized for different functions: feeding, defense, reproduction, and strangest of all, photosynthesis. Some polyps contain chloroplasts, stolen from algae they've digested, kept functional in their tissues, turning the colony partially into a plant. They feed on both the nutrients filtered from water and the sugars produced by their own photosynthetic members.

Colony-jellies are slow-moving but not sessile—they can detach from their substrate and drift, re-settling elsewhere, spreading across the kelp forests in gradual waves. Where they settle, they change the local chemistry, their waste products altering pH and mineral content in ways that affect what else can grow. Some kelp sections are dominated by colony-jelly presence, the bladders dimmed by the translucent mass that covers them, the bark beneath bleached pale. Other sections are entirely free of them, either by chance or because something in those areas—a chemical signal, a resident predator—prevents colonization.

When two colony-jellies meet, they sometimes merge, their tissues fusing, the polyps reorganizing into a single larger colony. Other times they war, extending specialized polyps armed with potent toxins, each trying to destroy the other's feeding members while protecting their own. These battles can last weeks, the surrounding water clouded with dissolved tissues and defensive compounds, and they end only when one colony is dead or has retreated, detaching and drifting away to find unclaimed territory.

The **listeners** are small, solitary, rarely more than a foot long, shaped like elongated teardrops with a fringe of cilia around their equator. They have no eyes, no apparent sensory organs except for what might be tympanic membranes covering most of their bodies. They drift motionless in the water for hours, days, oriented toward sources of sound—machines, singing rays, even each other. When sufficiently stimulated by the right combination of frequencies, they move, darting with surprising speed toward or away from the sound source, their cilia beating in complex patterns.

Listeners seem to feed on sound directly, or on the microorganisms that thrive in areas of high acoustic activity—it's difficult to tell which. They congregate in swarms near the largest machine installations, hovering in the intense vibration zones, their bodies visibly resonating. Dissection reveals internal structures that are part cochlea, part digestive system, suggesting they might process sound waves into chemical energy through some mechanism that blurs the line between hearing and eating.

They're also the only creatures that seem to experience something like **distress** when the machines fall silent—when a harvester malfunctions and stops, the listeners nearby become agitated, their movements erratic, until the machine restarts or they drift away to find other sound sources. One hypothesis: they're not independent organisms but symbiotes, or even organs, of the machine-kelp system itself, serving some regulatory function, the system's way of monitoring its own acoustic health.

The **weavers** are arachnid in shape—eight legs, segmented bodies—but scaled up massively, some with leg-spans of twenty feet or more. They don't hunt. They build. Using thread extruded from specialized organs, thread that hardens quickly in water to something like steel cable, they construct frameworks between kelp stems: geometric structures, lattices and spirals and occasionally something that looks almost architectural, like the skeleton of a building. The purpose of these structures is unclear—they catch no prey, block no currents, support nothing obvious. The weavers build them, then abandon them, moving on to construct the next one.

The thread doesn't decay. The frameworks persist indefinitely, accumulating over the years until some areas of the kelp forest are dense with them, three-dimensional mazes of woven metal-thread that smaller creatures use as shelter, that threadworms colonize, that even serves as substrate for kelp secondary growth. In these places, the forest becomes architectural, structured not just by plant growth but by the weavers' incomprehensible constructions.

Weavers are solitary and territorial about their building sites, though not about anything else—they'll tolerate another weaver passing through their territory, feeding there, even resting there, but the moment another weaver begins to build, they attack, the fight brief and brutal, ending when one weaver flees or is killed. Dead weavers are left in place, their bodies quickly colonized by decomposers, their exoskeletons eventually becoming just another surface for plants to grow on, sometimes still hanging in their own frameworks like macabre decorations.

One weaver, observed repeatedly over what might be years—it's distinctive for the patterning on its carapace, dark blue against black—appears to be building something specific, working in the same area, adding to the same structure rather than abandoning it. The framework has grown enormous, hundreds of yards across, its geometry increasingly complex, and the weaver continues to add to it, thread by thread, with what could almost be called dedication. Other weavers avoid this territory entirely. The structure itself has become a landmark, a recognizable feature in the otherwise uniform forest, and small creatures have made it their home, entire ecosystems developing in its shadows and angles.

The **biomass converters**—it's hard to say whether they're creatures or machines, and perhaps that's the wrong question entirely. They're spherical, ranging from basketball to bus-sized, their surfaces studded with intake and output ports, drifting freely through the water column. They consume anything organic: dead fish, kelp fragments, threadworm corpses, even waste products from other creatures. Everything goes in, gets processed by internal mechanisms that are biological and mechanical in equal measure, and comes out as those ubiquitous polymer beads, each one standardized, perfect, identical to all the others.

Inside a converter—examined in the rare cases where one has been found dead and cracked open—the mechanisms are incomprehensible. Chambers that seem to serve the function of stomachs but are made of metal. Conveyor systems that are clearly organic, made of muscle tissue but moving in mechanical rhythm. Filters woven from what might be hair or might be wire. And at the core, always, a heat source, something producing steady warmth that powers the entire system but whose nature is unclear: biological metabolism, geothermal core, nuclear decay, or something else entirely.

Converters occasionally cluster together in groups of three to five, connecting via extending ports, sharing intake and output, operating as a single larger unit. In this configuration they can process much larger objects—entire kelp stems, damaged machines, even dead surgeons. The clusters persist for weeks or months, then separate, the individual converters drifting apart to resume solitary processing. Whether this is reproduction—are they exchanging genetic information, building new converters within the cluster?—or simply efficiency, combining processing power for larger jobs, remains unknown.

The **singers** are human-sized, roughly humanoid in shape, but clearly not human, never were human. Their bodies are smooth and featureless, a matte black surface without obvious sensory organs, limbs that end in rounded stumps rather than hands or fins. They drift vertically in the water column, dozens of them scattered throughout the forest, always still, always silent except when they aren't.

At irregular intervals—every few days, every few weeks, no discernible pattern—a singer will activate. Its body begins to pulse with light, cycling through colors, and it produces sound: vocalizations that are complex, structured, almost linguistic. The sounds repeat in patterns that suggest meaning, clauses and phrases and something like sentences, but in no known language and following no decipherable grammar. Other singers in range respond, creating choruses, dialogues, arguments or conversations or simply coordinated performances. These episodes last anywhere from minutes to hours, then stop as abruptly as they began, the singers returning to silence and stillness.

The songs affect the environment. Glass fish schools scatter during performances, then reform in different configurations. Kelp bladders change their luminescence patterns, dimming or brightening in response. Even machines sometimes alter their behavior slightly, as if the songs carry command codes they're designed to recognize. But what the singers are saying, what information they're exchanging or what purpose their communication serves, remains completely opaque.

One singer is different: **the Witness**. It doesn't participate in the choruses. It only listens, drifting closer when others sing, orienting toward them, its body remaining dark and silent. When songs end, it moves away, resuming its vertical drift. It's older than the others—its surface is weathered, pocked with small impacts or dissolved areas, while other singers appear pristine. Sometimes swimmers report the Witness following them at a distance, observing without interaction, its featureless body somehow conveying attention despite having no visible eyes. Eventually it loses interest and drifts away, returning to its waiting.

The **garden-makers** are crustacean-analogues, heavily armored, their shells encrusted with carefully cultivated growths—specific algae species, rare mosses, even transplanted fragments of kelp secondary growth. They're mobile but slow, walking along kelp stems on multiple legs, each one tipped with delicate manipulators. As they go, they tend their body-gardens, pruning, adjusting exposure to bladder-light, even fertilizing with their own waste. The result is that each garden-maker becomes a unique ecosystem, carrying a curated selection of plant life that exists nowhere else in combination.

Garden-makers are solitary and peaceful, seemingly uninterested in anything but their horticultural work. They feed by filtering, their shells designed with intake ports that draw water through internal chambers where symbiotic organisms extract nutrients, the processed water then feeding the external gardens. They never stop moving—to stop is to die, as the gardens need constant adjustment to maintain optimal light exposure and prevent any one species from dominating.

When garden-makers die, their shells settle onto kelp bark, and the gardens continue growing for a time, fed by decomposition of the creature within. These death-gardens attract specialized feeders and sometimes serve as germination sites for rare plants, making garden-maker corpses small oases of biodiversity. Eventually the gardens exhaust the nutrients and die back, leaving only the empty shell, which other creatures hollow out and use for shelter.

There are **others**, seen rarely or only once, impossible to categorize: a school of something like birds, flying through the water with actual wings, their bodies streamlined for a medium they seem evolutionarily unprepared for. A massive shape that might be an egg, twenty feet across, hovering in place, covered in rhythmically pulsing bioluminescent patterns, present for weeks then gone overnight with no trace. Small scuttling things in the deep machinery spaces, spider-like but translucent, that flee from any light source. Clouds of something microscopic that move with apparent coordination, forming shapes—spirals, lattices, sometimes approximations of the larger creatures, like the glass fish but made of millions of individual particles.

And there are the **absences**, patterns in the ecosystem that suggest the existence of something never directly observed: gaps in glass fish school formations that persist as if accommodating an invisible member. Tracks in kelp bark, marks from passage or feeding, made by something with no corresponding creature. Sounds recorded in the deep zones that don't match any known organism or machine—rhythmic, complex, seemingly artificial, but with no source ever located.

What makes the Stillwater Canopy's inhabitants strange isn't their individual oddness but their **integration**. Everything here exists in relationship to everything else in ways that seem both ecological and designed. The harvesters harvest, but what they harvest seems to have evolved to be harvested. The surgeons maintain machines that maintain the environment that the surgeons need. The polymer beads produced by converters are consumed by certain plants that are eaten by glass fish that are processed by converters. The songs of singers change behavior in ways that seem coordinated toward system stability. Even the deeplurkers, terrifying and mysterious, might be apex regulators, removing excess biomass, keeping populations in balance.

It's an ecosystem that doesn't feel natural—there's too much optimization, too much efficiency, too many closed loops with no waste. But it doesn't feel artificial either, because nothing here follows engineering principles exactly. It's something in between, or something that makes those categories irrelevant. Perhaps it was designed and has since evolved. Perhaps it evolved and has since been maintained. Perhaps the distinction between design and evolution has blurred past meaning in a system that has existed so long in stable equilibrium.

The creatures and machines—and the creature-machines, and the machine-creatures—exist in a state of perpetual present, their behaviors locked in patterns that appear unchanging but must contain variation, or they couldn't adapt, couldn't continue existing across deep time. Yet that variation never accumulates toward change. The system maintains itself in perfect stasis, every element weathered to its final form and staying there, balanced on the edge between life and mechanism, between growth and maintenance, between meaning and pure function.

They are inhabitants of a place that might be paradise or prison, thriving or trapped, alive in the fullest sense or running on momentum from some long-ago initialization. They go about their existence in the eternal hum, in the blue-green light, in the cool mineral water, neither knowing nor needing to know what any of it means, if it means anything at all. They simply are, and that is-ness fills an entire ocean, suspended in the sky, unchanging forever.
