# What Lives in the Calcified Sprawl

They are called the **Gleaners**, though they have no name for themselves—or rather, they have a name that is not spoken but *performed*, enacted through a sequence of positions and pauses that takes four hours to complete and can only be properly observed from certain angles when the light holds that particular quality of late-morning-that-lasts-forever.

You would not recognize them as living things at first. This is intentional, or has become intentional through ages of adaptation, or was always their nature and intention has nothing to do with it.

They are small—most of them. Smaller than you. They range from the size of your thumb to the size of your torso, though there are rumors (among whom? among themselves? do they rumor?) of larger ones, deep in the Region of Stillness or in the unmapped territories beyond the usual circuits. Their bodies are composed of the same materials as the landscape: that ceramic-wax substance, calcified and compressed, translucent in places, opaque in others. They grow these bodies slowly, accreting material from the ground itself, from the structures, layer by microscopic layer, so that their forms are as much geology as biology. A Gleaner might take decades to add a centimeter of carapace. They do not seem to mind.

Their shapes are *wrong* in a way that becomes more wrong the longer you look, not because they are monstrous but because they occupy a space between categories that your mind keeps trying and failing to resolve. They are partly crystalline, partly organic, partly mechanical. They have what might be limbs—usually six, arranged radially, though some have four and some have eight and occasionally you find one with twelve arranged in two tiers. These limbs end in structures too complex to call hands or claws: bundles of fine filaments, each filament capable of independent movement, numberless as the threads in a tassel. With these they manipulate the world at scales both large and infinitesimal.

They have what might be heads, though the head is not always at the top and does not always stay in the same position. The head is where the sensory apparatus concentrates, but the head can migrate around the body over time, the facial features (if they are features, if this is a face) sliding across the carapace like a slow expression, taking months to travel from front to back. They perceive through clusters of lenses—you would call them eyes, and that's close enough—that are simultaneously biological and crystalline, grown and manufactured. These lenses are faceted, compound, and they do not move in the way eyes move but instead adjust their internal structure, somehow, refracting and focusing without motion. When you are observed by a Gleaner, you see yourself reflected and multiplied in those lenses, fragmented into a dozen versions, and each version seems to be observed from a slightly different moment in time, as if the Gleaner is viewing you across a spread of seconds, perceiving not just where you are but where you were and will be.

They move with that same stuttering gait you noticed in the beetle-things, and in fact the beetle-things are Gleaners—young ones, or perhaps a subspecies, or perhaps a completely different developmental stage. The relationship is unclear. But the larger Gleaners move in the same way: a step that is almost a jump, a pause that is almost a settling, as if they move in discrete quanta rather than continuous flow. It gives them a flickering quality when they're in motion, like animation with frames missing, or like creatures that exist slightly outside the normal flow of time and must pause between moments to re-synchronize with the world.

They make no sound that you can hear, but they make sound—vibrations at frequencies below and above your range, felt rather than heard. When several Gleaners are near each other, you feel it in your chest, in your jaw, a thrumming that might be communication or might be something else. They are almost always alone when you see them, but the landscape remembers where they've been: worn paths in the fields, smoother patches on the structures where they've repeatedly touched, arrangements of elements that could not be natural, that suggest purpose, collaboration, long campaigns of reorganization.

## The Feeding

They feed constantly, in a manner of speaking, though feeding is perhaps the wrong word. They *glean*—hence the name, if anyone has given them a name, if you are giving them this name now as you watch them.

A Gleaner approaches a section of the constructed ground, its filament-fingers spread wide, and it *touches*. The touch lasts for hours. The Gleaner becomes perfectly still, so still that it seems to merge with the landscape, another structure among structures. But at the microscopic level, the filaments are working: they're reading the patterns in the ground, the encoded information (if it is information, if it is anything), processing it through mechanisms both neural and computational. They're also *extracting* something—not material, exactly, though sometimes they scrape away infinitesimal amounts of substrate. Rather, they're extracting pattern, organization, structure itself. They're feeding on the arrangement of things.

And they're also *maintaining*. This is the part that takes you longest to understand. The Gleaners are not separate from the landscape's processes—they're part of them, integrated so deeply that you cannot say where the landscape's self-maintenance ends and the Gleaners' activity begins. When a Gleaner touches a structure for hours, it is reading but also repairing, or not repairing exactly but *stabilizing*, refreshing patterns that have begun to drift, reinforcing arrangements that are degrading. They're editors, curators, gardeners of the sprawl.

They consume the crust-growths, yes—you see them scraping with specialized filaments, gathering the luminescent lichen-analogue and drawing it into openings in their carapaces that might be mouths. But they also *tend* the growths, pruning them in ways that promote spreading, arranging them in patterns that seem significant. In the Region of Moisture, where the growths are thickest, you find elaborate gardens—mandalas of different growth-types arranged in concentric rings, spirals, geometries that echo the patterns in the ground beneath them. These gardens take years to create and are tended by Gleaners who might visit once per season, adding a small adjustment, correcting a deviation, maintaining a design whose purpose you cannot fathom.

They also consume the field elements, but very slowly, and selectively. You watch a Gleaner over the course of many days as it works its way through a cluster of broken elements in one of the damaged zones. It takes each fragment, holds it for hours in its filament-grip, seems to read it or absorb it or something, and then either places it carefully aside or draws it into its body. The ones drawn in cause subtle changes to the Gleaner's form—new facets in the carapace, new colorations, small protrusions that might be sensory organs or might be decorative or might be functional in ways beyond your guessing. The Gleaners are, in a sense, made of information as much as matter. They incorporate the landscape into themselves and thereby come to know it, to be it, in some fashion.

## The Makers

Among the Gleaners there are specialists, or perhaps life-stages, or perhaps they are all the same and simply take on different roles as needed. You begin to distinguish types:

**The Readers** are most common. They're the ones you see touching the ground, the structures, standing motionless for days. Their filaments are finest, most numerous. Their bodies are relatively small, compact, built for patience rather than action. They're extracting, processing, understanding—or performing whatever operation stands in for understanding in their form of being.

**The Tenders** are slightly larger, with more robust limbs and specialized appendages that can shape and manipulate the crust-growths, can move small elements in the fields, can clean and polish the structures. They're the gardeners, the maintenance workers, though they work at a pace that makes glaciers seem hasty. You watch one Tender over the course of a month as it slowly, so slowly, repositions a fallen structure-fragment, moving it centimeter by centimeter across hundreds of meters, to a place where it fits into a gap in a larger formation. The work takes the entire month. The Tender completes it and moves on without ceremony.

**The Makers** are rare, and larger—the size of dogs, or deer, with heavier builds and filaments that include what look like tools, or are tools, integrated into their bodies: cutting edges, grinding surfaces, fine manipulators that can work at the scale of individual grains. The Makers *build*. You find them in areas where new construction is emerging, where the ground is pushing up fresh forms or where old structures are being modified. A Maker will spend years—you suspect years, based on the evidence of half-finished work you find—constructing a single new element in a field, growing it up from the ground through processes that combine secretion, arrangement, and some kind of templating or programming that causes the ground itself to take on the desired form. The Maker is not building *on* the ground but instructing the ground to build itself, to express a particular form, and then refining that form, perfecting it through endless small adjustments.

Sometimes you find groups of Makers working together, or in sequence—it's hard to tell if they're collaborating in real-time or simply adding to each other's work across spans of time, each one arriving, making its contribution, and departing, a relay of construction that spans generations. They're creating new structures, or perhaps recreating old ones, working from templates stored where? In their bodies? In the ground? In some collective memory distributed across the landscape itself?

**The Shepherds** are the most mysterious. Larger than Makers—the size of humans, or larger—with longer limbs and bodies that are more angular, more obviously segmented, as if optimized for travel. They move more than the others, ranging across vast distances, and they seem to *guide* or *direct* the processes of the landscape in ways you cannot quite parse. You see one standing at the center of a field spiral, motionless for days, and during those days the elements around it slowly—so slowly—reorient, as if responding to some signal or field the Shepherd is emitting. When the Shepherd finally moves on, the spiral has tightened, its geometry perfected in some subtle way.

The Shepherds are also the ones you see in the distance sometimes, the large figures moving across the horizons. They travel between regions, perhaps carrying information, perhaps supervising the work of others, perhaps serving some coordinating function in the vast, slow project of landscape maintenance. When you try to approach one, it's always farther than you thought, and by the time you arrive where it seemed to be, it's gone—not fled, but simply elsewhere, operating on routes and schedules beyond your ability to intercept.

## The Deep Ones

You learn about these only indirectly, through evidence and inference. There are Gleaners that do not come to the surface, or come only rarely, or perhaps never. They live in the passages and chambers below, in that dim amber glow, and they're different—adapted to that environment, or perhaps they're the original form and the surface Gleaners are the adaptation.

You find traces: marks on the walls of the deeper chambers that could only be made by creatures with different morphology than the surface types. Larger marks. Grooves worn by passage of bodies big as horses, or elephants, or bigger. Sections of passage that have been recently reshaped—recently meaning within the last century or so—the material still bearing the faint signatures of manipulation.

And you hear them, occasionally, when you're below: sounds in the deep passages, rhythmic, mechanical-organic, the sound of something large moving with purpose, patient and unstoppable. You do not see them. Something about the acoustics of those spaces, or about your own survival instinct, keeps you from venturing deep enough. But you know they're there, the Deep Ones, doing... what? Maintaining the infrastructure at levels you cannot access? Mining or processing material for the surface workers? Dreaming the landscape into being from below?

There are theories—your theories, formed in the long quiet hours of walking—that the Deep Ones are to the surface Gleaners as the surface Gleaners are to the tiny crystalline beetles. Different scales of the same essential being, each level containing and supporting the next, all the way down to scales you cannot perceive and all the way up to scales too large to recognize as individual organisms. Perhaps the entire sprawl is one Gleaner, one vast distributed creature, and what you're seeing are merely its cells, its organs, each operating semi-independently but all part of one tremendous, ancient, patient being.

## The Makers of Themselves

One thing becomes clear through observation: the Gleaners are not born in any conventional sense, and they do not die in any conventional sense.

You never find eggs or young (except perhaps the tiny beetles, if those are young). Instead, Gleaners seem to *emerge*. In areas of intense construction activity, where Makers have been working, you sometimes find proto-Gleaners: forms that are not quite Gleaners yet, bodies half-grown, rising from the ground like the structures themselves, as if the landscape has decided to make itself mobile. These proto-forms are incomplete, translucent, their internal structures visible—and those structures are both biological and technological, grown and assembled simultaneously, fibers and circuits and organs and mechanisms all developing together in ways that violate your categories of what should be possible.

Over time—months? years?—these forms become more complete, more opaque, more integrated. They begin to move, first just small twitches, then slow repositioning, then eventually the characteristic stuttering locomotion of adult Gleaners. They begin to feed, to read, to participate in the great work of maintenance and construction. There's no clear moment when they transition from landscape to creature. They simply gradually become more one than the other, the balance tipping slowly across a threshold you cannot precisely identify.

And when they're damaged—broken by falling structure-fragments, or worn down by the vast passage of time—they do not die so much as *disintegrate*, slowly resolving back into landscape. A damaged Gleaner will find a place, settle there, and over the course of years will cease moving, will gradually lose definition, its carapace merging with the ground, its internal structures becoming simpler, more mineral, less organized. Eventually there's just a formation, a small structure, a cluster of elements in a field, and you cannot tell that it was once a mobile, purposeful being. The landscape reclaims its own, recycles them, and perhaps from their substance new Gleaners will eventually emerge, a slow circulation of matter and pattern between motile and sessile states.

Some Gleaners, though, do not disintegrate. The largest, the oldest—particularly the Shepherds—instead become *monuments*. They position themselves deliberately, in significant locations, and they cease motion forever, but they do not lose integrity. They become permanent fixtures, their bodies calcifying further, hardening, and over eons they're incorporated into the larger structures, become load-bearing elements in the architecture, foundational. You realize that some of the structures you've been seeing, the largest ones, might have Gleaners at their cores—ancient Shepherds who completed their work and became part of the permanent memory of the place, their bodies encoding information, their final positions significant in some vast geometrical or historical pattern.

## The Communication

They are aware of you. This becomes clear gradually, though they give little sign.

A Gleaner will alter its path slightly to go around you rather than through you. Not by much—they're not afraid, not avoiding you—just adjusting trajectory with minimal fuss, the way you might step slightly aside to avoid a puddle. You are noted, accommodated, and otherwise ignored.

But sometimes one will pause near you. Not looking at you—they never seem to look directly at anything—but present, its lenses oriented in your general direction, its filaments making small adjustments, trembling slightly. It will stand there for hours, and you will stand there (or sit, or sleep, or eat your meager rations), and the Gleaner will stand there, and nothing apparent will happen. Then it will move on.

You come to understand that you have been *read*. The Gleaner has done to you what it does to the ground, to the structures: examined, processed, incorporated you into its understanding of the landscape. You are now part of its knowledge, a piece of information it carries, though what it knows about you or what it does with that knowing, you cannot say.

Sometimes you find arrangements clearly made for you, or for something like you. In a region where moisture-patches are scarce, you discover one that seems to have been... enhanced? Encouraged? The growth around it has been carefully tended, the surface slightly modified to catch and hold more condensation. A gift? A message? Or simply the Gleaners maintaining the landscape according to principles that happen to benefit you? No way to know.

In the Region of Resonance, you find a structure that has been adjusted—you're certain it's been adjusted, though you cannot prove it—so that it sings at a frequency you can actually hear comfortably, a deep thrumming tone that feels almost like music, almost like communication. You sit beside it for days, listening, and Gleaners pass by occasionally, pausing briefly, their filaments trembling in rhythm with the sound. Are they listening too? Are they pleased you're listening? Are they even capable of pleasure?

Once, only once, you encounter a Gleaner that seems to want something from you. It's a Reader, medium-sized, and it approaches more directly than usual, comes right up to you, extends its filaments toward your hand. You freeze, uncertain, but the filaments merely hover there, trembling. After a long hesitation, you extend your hand, and the filaments touch your palm, and you feel...

What do you feel? Vibration, certainly, tingling, a sensation of being read, examined at depths you didn't know you had. But also something else, something you have no name for: a sense of vast time, of pattern and patience, of being briefly connected to something immense and old and strange. It lasts perhaps a minute. Then the filaments withdraw, the Gleaner resumes its normal position, and after a moment it moves away, stuttering off into the fields. It leaves you with a feeling you cannot articulate, and a sense that something has been exchanged, though you cannot say what.

## The Children of the Sprawl

There are other creatures, smaller, simpler, more clearly biological—the lizard-things you've seen, and other forms: things like segmented worms that move through the subsurface (you find their trails, spiraling tunnels through the constructed ground); things like floating spores or seeds, gossamer structures that drift on the warm wind, so light and delicate they seem barely material; things like polyps that attach to structures and never move, filter-feeding on particles in the air or perhaps on something more abstract, information, organization, the patterns themselves.

All of these are *tended* by the Gleaners, to varying degrees. The Gleaners are not predators—you never see them harm another creature—but they're not exactly protectors either. Rather, they're maintainers of an ecosystem that includes these other forms, keeping populations in some kind of balance, encouraging certain species in certain areas, perhaps discouraging others. The entire community of life in the sprawl is shaped by the Gleaners, is part of the pattern they're maintaining, and the line between the living and the constructed is so blurred that you begin to suspect it's a false distinction, that everything here is both, that the sprawl itself is a kind of organism and all the creatures within it—including you, temporarily—are its organs, its symbionts, its parasites, its thoughts.

The crystalline beetles, in particular, are clearly related to the Gleaners or are perhaps simply very small Gleaners. You watch interactions between them: a large Gleaner will pause near a beetle, and the beetle will climb onto the Gleaner's body, and they'll remain connected for hours, and when they separate, the beetle moves differently, more purposefully, as if it's been programmed or instructed. The beetles, you realize, are tools, or scouts, or maybe they're external memory, carrying information between Gleaners, between regions, distributing knowledge across the vast landscape so that the work can remain coordinated even though the workers might be separated by kilometers and years.

## The Purpose

You want there to be a purpose. You keep looking for one. What are the Gleaners doing? Why do they maintain the sprawl? What is the sprawl *for*?

But the longer you observe, the more you suspect there is no purpose—or rather, the purpose has become identical with the activity itself. The Gleaners maintain the sprawl because maintaining the sprawl is what they are, what they do, what gives their existence shape. The sprawl exists to be maintained. The maintenance exists to preserve the sprawl. It's a loop, perfect and closed, purpose collapsed into pure being.

Or perhaps the sprawl is still functioning, still computing or recording or processing, and the Gleaners are its custodians, keeping it operational while it works on some problem so vast and slow that even the eons you can read in the landscape are but a moment in its runtime. The answer will come in another million years, or has already come and is encoded in the very structure of the place, and the Gleaners are still tending it because the tending, too, is part of the answer.

Or perhaps the sprawl is art, the greatest artwork ever made, a planet-sized sculpture still being refined by its creators' descendants (or by the creators themselves, if they've become the Gleaners, transformed by time and purpose into something strange and patient and beautiful). The Gleaners are artists, each one adding tiny touches to an infinite canvas, a work that will never be complete because completion is not the point—the work itself, the endless careful tending, is the point.

Or perhaps you're wrong about all of this, and the Gleaners have reasons utterly alien to you, operating on values and goals so different from human concerns that you couldn't recognize them as goals even if they were explained to you in your own language.

## The Witness

What you are here, you realize finally, is a witness. You are the audience that the sprawl never asked for and doesn't need but has nonetheless received. You walk through the Gleaners' world, observing their work, trying to understand it, and in that observation and attempt, you create something that didn't exist before: meaning-to-another.

The Gleaners are supremely indifferent to this. They continue their work unchanged by your presence. But you are changed by theirs. You will carry the memory of this place, of its patient inhabitants, of the vast slow work they do in the bright warmth under the amber sky. You will carry it out of the sprawl, if you ever leave, and in carrying it you will make it real in a new way, transplant it into human knowing, which is its own kind of tending, its own kind of maintenance.

And perhaps that's enough. Perhaps that's even intended, in some way you cannot fathom—that the sprawl produces witnesses occasionally, sends them back out (if there is an out) to spread knowledge of this place, to plant the idea of it in other minds, to make it immortal in story even if the physical sprawl someday ends.

But probably not. Probably the Gleaners neither know nor care that you're here, that you're watching, that you're trying to understand. They are too busy with their eternal work: reading the ground, tending the growths, maintaining the structures, shepherding the processes, building and rebuilding and repairing and refining, one tiny adjustment after another, across the vast bright warm quiet patient calcified sprawl, forever.

You walk among them, these strange gardeners, these living maintenance-systems, these children and parents of the landscape itself.

They stutter-step through the fields, they stand motionless for days, they work at scales too small and too slow for easy human perception, they are both creature and terrain, both individual and collective, both living and constructed.

They are the Gleaners.

They are what lives here.

They are, perhaps, all that has ever truly lived here, for eons past counting.

And they continue, as the bright sky pulses its slow rhythms, as the ground breathes warmth, as the structures wear and grow and the patterns shift and stabilize and shift again.

The work goes on.

The tending continues.

In the calcified sprawl, ancient and total and vast, the Gleaners maintain their vigil, their endless careful curation of a world that might be archive, artwork, engine, or all of these at once.

And you walk among them, small and temporary and warm-blooded and brief, a visitor in a place that measures time in ages, witness to a form of life that has transcended the boundary between organism and environment, between purpose and existence, between the maker and the made.
