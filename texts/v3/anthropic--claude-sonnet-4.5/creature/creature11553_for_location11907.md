# The Bell-Tenders

They are not one thing. This is the first difficulty in describing them—the assumption that "who lives here" has a single answer, that there is a dominant form, a clear inhabitant. But the Drowned Bells are not a place with residents. They are a place with *participants*, with *elements*, with phenomena that blur the line between ecology and identity.

Still, there are forms that move with more intention than the trees. There are presences that maintain, that tend, that listen.

## The Listeners

The most common—if that word means anything in a place so sparse—are the Listeners.

They are humanoid in the way that something carved from memory of the human form is humanoid: the right number of limbs, the correct general proportions, but nothing that would pass for human under scrutiny. They are tall, most of them, perhaps two and a half meters from foot to crown, but their height is all in length of limb and torso—stretched, attenuated, as if the medium has pulled them upward over generations.

Their skin, if it is skin, has the texture of the tube-plants: slightly translucent, grey-green, with a faint rubbery give. You can see into them, just barely—shadows of internal structures that might be organs or might be chambers like the ones in the adapted trees, spaces for the medium to flow through. These shadows shift and pulse, not with heartbeat rhythm—too slow for that—but with the rhythm of the tube-plants, that same measured contraction and expansion.

They have no mouths. Their faces are smooth from where a chin would be to where the nose begins—just a gentle convex curve, that same grey-green surface, unbroken. Above, two openings like nostrils, simple holes that dilate and contract. And eyes: large, disproportionately large, taking up much of the upper face. The eyes are dark, nearly black, but not quite—deep green so saturated it reads as darkness, with no visible pupil, no clear boundary between iris and sclera. They are adapted for this dim medium, for gathering every available photon from the diffuse glow.

The Listeners move slowly, even by the standards of this place. They drift from bell to bell, from framework to framework, and when they reach one, they *attend* to it. They extend their long fingers—six per hand, each finger double-jointed in three places, creating a fan of delicate manipulation—and they touch the bell's surface. Not to ring it; the currents do that. They touch it and they *listen*, their whole bodies becoming still, becoming receptive.

Sometimes they make adjustments. They use simple tools—rods of stone, hooks of that corroded metal—to shift a clapper's position, to change the angle at which a bell hangs. These adjustments are tiny, precise, made over the course of what seems like hours. A few degrees of rotation. A centimeter's change in suspension point. Then they drift away, and the bell rings with a fractionally different tone, a slightly altered rhythm, and this new voice enters the chorus.

They do not seem to communicate with each other, but they coordinate. Two Listeners will never work on the same bell. They space themselves across the plateau in a pattern that seems deliberate but whose logic is not apparent. Sometimes three or four are visible at once, each attending their own bells, widely separated. Sometimes you drift for what feels like days without seeing any, but the bells continue their ringing, their slow changes of tone, suggesting the Listeners are always somewhere nearby, always working.

Their bodies are not fixed. This becomes apparent when you observe one for an extended period—something difficult to do, as they do not respond to observation, do not acknowledge presence, and watching them is like watching a stone or a tree, a test of your own patience rather than an interaction.

The internal shadows shift over time—not just pulsing, but redistributing. A darkness that was in the torso migrates toward an arm, concentrates there, then dissipates. The skin transparency changes subtly; an area that was nearly opaque becomes translucent enough to see the suggestion of ribs or structural frameworks beneath. Their fingers lengthen or shorten by small degrees. The body is permeable, adjustable, more like a sustained formation than a fixed anatomy.

They are living, certainly. But the boundary between their bodies and the medium is negotiable.

## The Mechanism of Listening

What the Listeners do is not hearing in the conventional sense.

When one touches a bell, you can see—if you watch closely, if the light is slightly less dim—vibrations traveling through the translucent flesh. The bell's ringing becomes visible in the Listener's body: ripples spreading from the point of contact up the arm, across the torso, branching and interfacing with those internal shadows, those organs or chambers.

They are filtering. They are processing. The bells ring constantly, chaotically, but the Listeners are extracting pattern, are parsing signal from noise. Each bell is a sensor, a data point, and the whole plateau is an instrument for measuring something—currents, pressures, chemical gradients in the medium, vibrations from the rock itself. The Listeners are reading this instrument.

And they are adjusting it. The small changes they make—the angle of a bell, the tension in a hanging chain—are calibrations. They are tuning the plateau to listen to something, or to speak in a language of sustained ringing.

To what or to whom, you cannot determine. There is no obvious recipient of this message, no clear sender of the signal they are receiving. But the purposefulness is unmistakable. This is not ritual for its own sake. This is function.

Sometimes a Listener will remain touching a bell for extended periods—half a light-cycle or more—and their body will become more transparent, the internal structures more visible, as if the sustained vibration is reorganizing them, as if they are literally resonating with the bell's voice. In these moments, they seem less solid, more like they might dissipate if the bell stopped ringing, as if the sound is holding them together.

## The Maintainers

Distinct from the Listeners, though similar in form, are the Maintainers.

They are shorter, thicker through the body, and their skin is less translucent—more opaque, more like the tree-bark in texture, layered and slightly fibrous. They have the same stretched proportions, the same lack of mouth, the same large dark-green eyes, but their hands are different: fewer fingers, only four, but each finger is thicker, tipped with something like a hardened nail or claw, dark and ridged.

The Maintainers work on the structures. You see them at the towers, at the bell-frameworks, tending to the corroded metal and weathered stone. They clean—scraping away excessive accretion, removing fragments that might fall and damage something below. They reinforce—wedging stones back into place, wrapping corroded joints with fresh metal wire they produce from somewhere, from pouches worn against their bodies or from caches hidden in the towers.

Where the Listeners are contemplative, meditative, the Maintainers are methodical, almost fussy. They inspect every element of a structure: running those clawed fingers along metal surfaces, pressing against stonework to test its integrity, examining knots and joints. When they find damage, they repair it with surprising speed, given their otherwise slow movement.

They use the metal as if it is alive—or as if they can make it alive. They will take a piece of corroded iron, hold it in both hands, and *wait* with it, their bodies pulsing in that slow tube-plant rhythm, and the metal will change. The rust formations will consolidate, will become denser, will form a patina that seems more protective than destructive. It does not become new—nothing here becomes new—but it stabilizes, reaches an equilibrium. The Maintainers are mediators between metal and medium, helping them find terms of coexistence.

They work in small groups, two or three together, and they do communicate—not with sound, but with touch. They will drift close to each other and extend their hands, pressing palm to palm or fingertip to fingertip, and hold that contact for long moments. Information passes between them, decisions are made. Then they separate and continue their work with adjusted coordination.

Unlike the Listeners, the Maintainers do acknowledge other presences. If you drift too close to a structure they are working on, they will pause, will turn those large eyes toward you, and wait. Not threateningly, not welcomingly—simply wait, until you move away or settle into stillness. If you do not interfere, they resume work. If you touch something they are tending, they will gently—but firmly—move your hand away, a clear communication: *Not yours. Ours. We are working.*

They sometimes enter the towers, disappearing into dark interiors for extended periods. You can hear them inside: soft tapping, scraping, the small sounds of deliberate work. What they do in there is unclear—the towers are mostly empty, just stone shells. But perhaps that emptiness is what they maintain, clearing it of sediment, of encroaching mat-plants, of anything that would fill the space, keeping it available for whatever purpose it once served or might serve again.

## The Shepherds

Rarest and strangest are the Shepherds.

They appear only in the densest groves, among the feather-fronded trees and the thick tube-plant clusters, and they are larger than the other forms—three meters tall at least, and broader, with bodies that seem less humanoid and more like mobile trunks, like the trees themselves have learned to uproot and walk.

Their skin is bark. Not like bark, but actually the layered, peeling, softened bark of the adapted trees, grey-brown with patches of green where mat-plant growth has colonized them. Their limbs are thick, move with difficulty, with the creaking sound of wood under stress. They have something like a head, a protrusion from the top of the trunk-body, and on this head, those same large dark eyes, but only the eyes—no nose, no other features, just eyes set into bark.

The Shepherds move among the trees and tube-plants with what can only be called tenderness. They touch the trees, running their bark-hands over bark-trunks, and where they touch, the trees seem to respond—leaves shifting orientation, branches adjusting their angles slightly. They attend to damaged trees, to branches that have broken in unusually strong currents, and they do something—you cannot see exactly what, their bodies block the view—and when they move on, the damage has been addressed. Not healed, not repaired to wholeness, but stabilized, integrated.

With the tube-plants, they are even more attentive. They will settle beside a cluster, their large bodies becoming still, and they will synchronize their pulse with the plants' pulse. Their whole trunk-body contracts and expands, matching rhythm, and the tube-plants respond, their pulsing becoming stronger, more coordinated. It is like watching a conductor leading an orchestra, except the conductor is also an instrument, is also playing.

Sometimes the Shepherds *plant*. They carry seeds or spores—clutched in their hands or embedded in crevices in their bark-skin—and they will find a bare stretch of rock, a place where the mat-plants have not yet spread, and they will press these seeds into stone. They use a tool for this, a simple stake of hardened wood, driving it into cracks, widening them, creating pockets where roots might take hold. Then they place the seed, cover it with gathered sediment, and move on. Months later—if you remember the location, if you return—something will be growing there.

But they do not plant everywhere. There is selection, deliberation. Some areas they pass through without planting, even when conditions seem identical to places they have planted. They are following a pattern, filling in gaps in a design, maintaining the distribution of growth across the plateau in some optimal configuration.

The Shepherds are the only ones who interact with the largest bell, the silent one in the plaza. They drift to it periodically—each Shepherd on their own schedule, never together—and they enter it, curling their large bodies into that interior space that is too large. They remain inside for what might be hours, and while they are inside, you can feel that deep vibration, that below-hearing pulse, become stronger, more present. When they emerge, they seem more solid, their bark-skin darker and denser, as if they have been reinforced, reminded of what they are.

## The Question of Origin

These three forms—Listeners, Maintainers, Shepherds—are distinct but related. The proportions are similar beneath the different surface textures, the same basic architecture of long limbs and stretched torsos. The same large eyes, same lack of mouths. They are variations on a theme, specialized adaptations from some common root.

Were they once human? The towers and bells suggest a human or human-like origin for this place, a culture that built with stone and metal, that valued sound and height. Perhaps the people who built here did not leave. Perhaps they adapted, as the trees adapted, as everything adapts to the medium or ceases to exist. Generation by generation, breathing the thick water-air, eating the changed plants, their bodies might have transformed, might have diverged into these different forms serving different functions.

Or perhaps they were made—biological constructions designed to tend a system their creators could no longer survive in. The bells as the primary mechanism, the instrument for measuring or communicating, and these three forms as the maintenance crew, the operators, keeping it all running long after the original purpose has been forgotten.

Or they are natives, and the towers and bells are *their* construction, and what seems like human-scale technology is convergent evolution, the forms that intelligent tool-use naturally takes. The lack of mouths argues against a human origin—no speech, no eating as terrestrial animals eat—but the presence of hands, of deliberate tool-use, of maintained structures, argues for intelligence, for culture, for something that occupies the same ecological niche as humanity even if the form is alien.

They do not answer questions. They do not respond to attempts at communication. You can drift close to a Listener, can position yourself in their line of sight, can even touch them—their skin is cool, smooth, slightly damp like everything here—and they will pause, will turn those dark eyes toward you, but there is no recognition in them, no curiosity, no acknowledgment of you as another intelligent being. You are part of the environment, another object in the medium, neither more nor less interesting than a stone or a bell.

Yet they are not simple. The coordination, the purposefulness, the variations in behavior—these suggest complex cognition. Perhaps their intelligence is so different from yours that communication is impossible. Perhaps they perceive you but interpret you as something else, something that does not merit interaction. Or perhaps they are waiting, patient in the way only beings adapted to this slow medium can be patient, waiting for you to do or say or become something that would register as significant, as worth responding to.

## The Microscopic Cohabitants

If the three humanoid forms are the visible inhabitants, the medium itself teems with the invisible ones.

The particulate matter that drifts through the water-air is not just sediment. Much of it is alive, or was alive, or occupies some space between states. Spores, certainly—the tube-plants and mat-plants reproduce through dispersal of microscopic propagules, drifting until they find substrate. But also cells, fragments of cells, structures that might be single-celled organisms or might be pieces of larger organisms broken down to their components and still functioning.

When you bring a sample of the medium close to your face—cupping it in your hands, what little can be cupped of something you're immersed in—you can see them: tiny specks that move with more purpose than currents alone would give them. Spiraling, darting in miniature, congregating around larger particles. They form clouds of slightly denser medium, concentrations that the Listeners seem particularly interested in, that the tube-plants pulse more strongly near.

These are the decomposers, the recyclers, the base of the ecosystem. They break down the shed bark, the fallen branches, the waste products of the plants. They fix nutrients from the medium itself, or transform nutrients from one form to another. They are bacteria, archaea, protists, microscopic fungi—or this place's equivalents, this place's solutions to the same ecological problems.

And they might be more. There is a theory, unprovable but compelling: that the three humanoid forms are extensions of this microscopic community, that the Listeners and Maintainers and Shepherds are colonial organisms, that each individual is actually millions or billions of these microorganisms coordinating, forming a macro-scale body for purposes of manipulation and movement.

The translucent skin, the visible internal structures, the way their bodies seem to redistribute and adjust—it fits. The synchronization with the tube-plants, the way Shepherds merge their rhythms with plant rhythms—it suggests something more fundamentally connected to the base ecology than a separate species would be.

If true, then the humanoid forms are not individuals but events, temporary coalescences of the microbial community into useful shapes. They would form when needed, dissipate when the task is complete, their component organisms returning to the medium to join other formations or to exist independently until called into pattern again.

This would explain the lack of any visible sustenance-gathering. You never see them eat, never see them excrete, never see them show fatigue or sleep in the way discrete organisms must. They simply are, and they work, and they are always the same degree of present, never more or less energetic, never depleted or satisfied.

It would also explain the coordination without apparent communication—they are not separate minds coordinating, but aspects of a single distributed intelligence, the microbial mat that permeates the medium and occasionally forms macroscale sensors and manipulators, the way a slime mold forms fruiting bodies.

But this is speculation. They are solid enough, present enough, consistent enough in their individual forms that calling them "not really individuals" feels like a dodge, like an inability to accept that intelligence might wear such strange shapes and still be discrete, still be singular.

## The Others

There are suggestions of other forms, rarer, glimpsed at the edges of visibility or in the dimmest periods.

Long, serpentine shapes that move through the deepest groves, too quick to see clearly, leaving only the disturbance of their passage—compressed vegetation springing back, sediment swirling in their wake. Are they actual organisms, animals that do exist here despite all appearances? Or are they currents, eddies given momentary form by the thickness of the medium, seeming alive through pareidolia and expectation?

In the terraced region where tube-plants grow in networks, there are sometimes larger pulsing rhythms, synchronized waves moving through dozens of plants at once, and at the center of these networks, something *else*—a different texture to the medium, a darkness that is not just low light but concentrated presence. You approach, and it retreats, or it dissipates, or it was never there. But the sense of it lingers: something larger than the Shepherds, something that is more network than body, more process than organism.

At the cliff edge, where the plateau drops away into dimness, there are sounds that do not match the known sources. Beneath the bells, beneath the current-rush and tree-creak, there are calls—low, resonant, almost subsonic, felt more than heard. They come from the depths beyond the cliff, from whatever exists in the greater darkness below. They are not regular enough to be geological, not random enough to be merely noise. They have the quality of communication, of signaling, but no visible responder, no answer.

Perhaps there are things in the depths that are to the Shepherds as the Shepherds are to the microorganisms—larger-scale patterns, entities of such size that the entire plateau is only a part of their awareness, their body-territory. The bells a sensory organ for something that sprawls across kilometers or hundreds of kilometers, that thinks in timescales of decades, that moves through media thicker and stranger than even this water-air.

Or perhaps the calls come from other plateaus, other highland places like this one, separated by gulfs of deep darkness but acoustically connected, trading information or simply announcing presence: *We are here. We persist. The bells ring on.*

## Interaction and Avoidance

To live among them—if you do, if you stay—is to be gently ignored.

The Listeners will work around you. If you position yourself near a bell, they will tend to it regardless, their long arms reaching past you, their bodies pressing lightly against you to access the framework, always with that sense of deliberate gentleness, of careful not-harming while also not-acknowledging. You are an obstacle, accommodated the way they accommodate a tree or a rock formation.

The Maintainers are more interactive, as mentioned, in their willingness to move your hands, to guide you away from structures they are working on. But it never goes further than that gentle correction. They do not become frustrated, do not escalate. If you persist in interfering, they simply wait, paused in their work, those dark eyes watching without expression, until you tire of the game and leave.

The Shepherds are nearly impossible to interact with—they move in the deepest groves where travel is difficult, where the vegetation is so dense that following them means forcing through resistant fronds and around thick tube-plant stems. By the time you reach where they were, they have moved on, only the adjusted plants and the fading sense of their bulk remaining.

But sometimes—very rarely, with no pattern you can discern—one of them will approach you. Not a Listener or Maintainer, but a Shepherd, emerging from a grove and drifting toward where you rest. It will settle nearby, perhaps five meters away, and it will become still, and it will *regard* you with those large eyes set into bark-skin, and you will feel assessed, measured, considered.

These encounters last minutes or hours—time becomes even more uncertain than usual. The Shepherd does not move, does not communicate. It simply observes. And you observe it: the texture of its bark, the small movements of mat-plants growing on its surface, the slow pulse that runs through its body, the occasional creak of its wood-flesh adjusting.

Then it rises and returns to the grove, and you are left with no more information than before, but with the absolute certainty that something has been exchanged, some data-point collected, some evaluation made. Whether you passed or failed, whether you were found interesting or irrelevant, you cannot say.

## The Cycle

You begin to understand, if you stay long enough, that the three forms are not separate but phases.

The Listeners are young—or the youngest state. Smaller, more transparent, more purely sensory. They learn the bells, learn the plateau's voice, spend what might be years just listening, just adjusting, internalizing the patterns.

The Maintainers are mature, active phase. They have listened long enough, have absorbed enough data, and now they turn to action, to maintenance, to keeping the physical structures intact. Their bodies have thickened, become more opaque, more suitable for manipulation and durability.

The Shepherds are old, or the oldest state. They have listened, they have maintained, and now they return to the organic, to the root of the system. They merge with the plants, become part of the green architecture, their bodies transforming toward bark and root. Eventually—you never see this, but you intuit it—eventually a Shepherd becomes still, becomes fixed, and becomes a tree. And the trees, perhaps, eventually produce or release or transform into new Listeners, and the cycle continues.

Birth, growth, death, but without clear boundaries, each phase blending into the next over timescales too long for you to directly observe. The plateau as a super-organism, the three humanoid forms as its motile cells, and all of it—bells, structures, plants, microorganisms, humanoids—all of it a single coordinated system maintaining itself, listening to itself, adjusting to conditions in the deep slow time that this place operates on.

This is not proven. You could be wrong. The three forms might be genuinely separate species, coincidentally shaped, cooperating without being parts of a whole. But the more you watch, the more you see the transitions—a Listener whose skin has begun to thicken, showing the first signs of becoming a Maintainer; a Shepherd whose arms have begun to branch, whose fingers have started to elaborate into twigs.

The boundaries are permeable, here. Between water and air, between individual and colony, between organism and ecosystem, between the living and the structure. Everything blurs, everything interpenetrates, and the inhabitants are not separate from the place they inhabit. They are its processes made manifest, its mechanisms given shape.

## The Invitation

There is no welcome here, but there is also no expulsion.

You can stay, if you wish. You can drift among the groves, rest in the towers, listen to the bells, eat the tube-plant flesh and mat-plant growth. The medium will sustain you, or you will adapt to be sustained by it, the boundary between those two possibilities not worth distinguishing.

In time—years, decades—your skin might begin to change. The cold medium soaking in, the constant immersion, the breathing of something that is neither water nor air. You might become translucent, might develop that same slow pulse. Your fingers might lengthen, might develop extra joints, better for the delicate work of bell-tending.

You might find yourself drifting toward a bell-framework, reaching out to touch a bronze surface, feeling the vibration travel through your body, and understanding—finally understanding—what the bells are saying, what they are measuring, what message they are sustaining across all the centuries of their ringing.

You might become a Listener. Then a Maintainer. Then a Shepherd. Then a tree. Then the medium itself, dispersed into microorganisms, into spores, into the base matter that everything here is built from and returns to.

Or you might remain as you are, separate, discrete, a visitor who never quite becomes native. Walking the plateau in your own time, on your own schedule, observing the slow work of the others without joining it. A witness, a recorder, a single note played once in a composition that has run for centuries and will run for centuries more.

Either way, the bells ring on. The currents flow. The medium pulses with microscopic life. The three forms tend and listen and shepherd. The plateau maintains its equilibrium, its balance between formation and dissolution.

And somewhere, in the depths beyond the cliff, or in the heights beyond the dimness, or in the future beyond perception, something receives the message, or sends the signal, or simply exists in parallel, another plateau in another place, another instrument in an orchestra whose full scope you will never comprehend.

The Drowned Bells are patient. They have been here longer than memory. They will be here longer than you.

And they are listening, always listening, to something you almost—but not quite—hear.
