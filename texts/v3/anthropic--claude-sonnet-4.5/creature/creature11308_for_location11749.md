# What Lives Here

## The Listeners

They are scattered across the flats like punctuation in an endless sentence. Humans, but shaped by this place into something specific, refined by generations until they fit the environment as precisely as water fits stone.

Their bodies are lean, conserving what warmth they generate. Compact torsos, efficient limbs. Their hands have evolved—or been selected, or simply adapted through use—into instruments of extraordinary sensitivity. The fingertips are calloused but not deadened; rather, the calluses form patterns, ridges that can read texture with impossible precision. They can feel the grain of stone, the chemical tang of different mineral deposits, the microsecond variations in vibration that constitute their world's entire acoustic language.

Their hearing has gone strange. The constant drone has made certain frequencies simply irrelevant—they cannot process them anymore, have learned to filter them so completely that a Listener transported to a quiet place would find the silence deafening, intolerable, an absence so profound it would feel like suffocation. But they can hear *through* the drone, detecting the minute variations, the overtones, the ways a predator's footfall creates a specific interference pattern, the way another Listener's intentional humming—pitched just slightly off from the creatures' base frequency—can carry meaning across hundreds of meters.

They are mostly silent, but not mute. Speech exists for them, but it's vestigial, used only in the closest intimacy—a whisper pressed directly into another's ear, a murmur shared when two people press their foreheads together in the dark. These moments are treasured precisely because they're possible so rarely. Most communication happens through touch: the elaborate finger-language pressed into palms, patterns tapped on shoulders, the complex grammar of gesture that has evolved to carry philosophy, poetry, technical knowledge, gossip, love.

Their eyes have adapted too. The grey light has made them sensitive to subtle variations in value, to textures rather than colors. A Listener can distinguish between a hundred different shades of grey, can read the moisture content of stone by how it reflects light, can spot the crystalline formations from vast distances by recognizing the particular way they scatter the diffuse illumination. But they see few colors—the world renders in monochrome except for rare moments: the blue of certain flames, the pale gold of particular mineral veins, the dark red of blood, which shocks against the grey like a wound in reality itself.

They move with a particular economy. Nothing wasted. Each gesture serves purpose or communication, usually both. They have learned to walk in ways that minimize splash, that disturb the shallow pools least, that leave the subtlest possible trace. Not from any moral imperative about leaving no mark, but because efficiency is survival, and survival is simply what one does.

Children are rare, precious. The population remains stable but only barely—the flats can support only so many, and the Listeners know this intimately, regulate themselves without discussion. Births are communal events, prepared for over months. A child is everyone's child. They're raised communally, learning the finger-language before they can walk, learning to navigate by vibration before they can recognize faces in the grey light.

The young are taught by being taken on long walks, by having patterns pressed into their palms until the knowledge becomes physical memory. There are no schools, no formal institutions—just the ongoing transmission of survival, of the accumulated wisdom about where water pools and how creatures move and which stones can be worked and which will only shatter.

They wear layered clothing made from the grazer-creatures' hides and undercoats, treated with mineral compounds until they shed water while remaining supple. Layers upon layers, trapped air providing insulation, the outer surfaces allowed to grow a kind of microbial coating that helps break the rain. They smell of stone and moisture and the particular musk of the creatures, having been around them so long that the scent has become part of their signature.

Each Listener carries tools, always: a knife of worked basalt or precious copper, an awl, cordage made from processed sinew, a small stone for grinding pigments or minerals. These are kept in leather bags worn close to the body, protected from the constant damp. The tools are maintained obsessively—not out of aesthetics but necessity. A broken knife in the wrong moment means death.

They mark themselves too, though differently than outsiders might. Tattoos would be traditional elsewhere, but here the process is different: carefully controlled scarification, patterns of fine lines across forearms and collarbones, sometimes faces. The scars are filled with mineral compounds that never quite wash away, creating permanent grey-on-grey designs. These mark lineage, accomplishment, trade specializations, sometimes just aesthetic preference. They're meant to be read by touch as much as sight—another Listener running fingers across the raised patterns can read identity, history, intent.

Some Listeners develop specializations. There are those who understand the creatures better than others, who can predict the herd movements, who've learned to approach close enough to harvest from living specimens without being trampled. These herder-specialists are valued, given first choice of certain resources, deferred to in decisions about migration and settlement location.

Others become crafters, developing facility with particular materials. Stone-workers who can split basalt along invisible planes, creating blades that hold an edge for years. Leather-workers who know the chemical treatments that make hide waterproof or supple or rigid as wood. The rare metal-workers who understand the cold-working of copper, hammering it patient hours until it takes the shape desired, annealing it in the blue-white flames of mineral fires.

There are also readers—those who study the ancient pillars, who've developed theories about the patterns carved there. They're not scientists in any formal sense, but they observe carefully, record on treated hides using pigments ground from minerals, pass their observations to apprentices who'll continue the work. They'll never read the pillars completely, but the attempt itself has value, gives shape to days.

And there are scouts, Listeners who range farther than others, who explore the edges of the flats, who occasionally return with reports of places where the basalt gives way to other stone, where the character of the land begins to change. These are listened to carefully, their information integrated into the collective map that exists in shared knowledge rather than any physical document.

Social structures are loose, defined by proximity and shared resource access rather than formal hierarchy. Groups cluster around the more reliable water sources, around the ancient pillars, but membership is fluid. A Listener might spend years with one group, then walk for days to join another, welcomed without ceremony, integrated without question. What matters is contribution: you gather water, you hunt, you work material, you help maintain the structures, you participate in the finger-talks where decisions are made collectively, where information is shared.

Leadership happens but it's temporary, situational. Someone with relevant expertise naturally coordinates a hunt or a construction project, then the dynamic dissolves when the task is complete. The closest thing to permanent authority belongs to the elders, those who've survived long enough to carry decades of observation, but even they don't command—they suggest, they offer perspective, they're consulted but not obeyed automatically.

Disputes happen, of course. Violence is rare but not unknown. When it occurs, it's usually resolved through formalized combat: non-lethal, using padded staves, fought until one party submits. The community witnesses, enforces the rules, accepts the outcome. More commonly, disputes are resolved through negotiation, through trade of resources, through agreed-upon separations if necessary. The flats are vast enough to allow distance.

They have customs around death. When someone dies—from accident, from predation, from the simple wearing out that comes with age—the body is laid in one of the deeper pools, weighted with stones. Over weeks it's consumed by the small scavengers, dissolved by the mineral-rich water, returned to the cycle. The deceased's possessions are distributed: tools go to those who can use them, personal items to close companions, knowledge shared one final time through the finger-talk, someone pressing into the palms of others the patterns the dead person knew best.

They mark these deaths by creating temporary stone arrangements, patterns that will last seasons before weathering claims them. Grief is gestural too: specific signs pressed repeatedly, a language of loss that others recognize and acknowledge with their own patterns of comfort.

They have art, though it's subtle. The scarification patterns. The way they arrange stones around their structures, creating compositions that please the eye in the grey light. Songs hummed during the rare times when the creatures migrate far enough away that individual voices can be heard—simple melodies, usually wordless, that carry emotion rather than narrative. Stories told through elaborate finger-talk, epic poems that take hours to relate, historical accounts mixed with mythology until the boundary between them becomes irrelevant.

They paint too, when they can, using pigments ground from minerals. But the constant moisture makes permanence impossible, so painting becomes a temporary art: images created on flat stone, appreciated for hours or days, then allowed to weather away. The impermanence is accepted, even valued—it matches the landscape's nature, the way everything here is both enduring and temporary, stone that lasts millennia but is always dissolving, meanings that must be constantly refreshed.

They have beliefs about the world, though it's unclear whether these constitute religion or simply practical observation mixed with interpretation. They believe the creatures emerge from the stone itself, that they're somehow continuous with the landscape, crystallizations of the flats' essential nature. They believe the ancient pillars were made by people who understood something fundamental that's now lost, who could shape stone with thought alone, who maybe could have left but chose to become part of the landscape instead.

Some believe the droning sound is the world's breathing, that they live on the surface of a vast sleeping thing that might someday wake. Others think the pillars are slowly counting down to something, marking time toward an event they'll never live to see. These beliefs aren't organized into doctrine—they're simply things people think about during long watches, ideas shared through finger-talk and debated with the mild interest of those who know certainty is impossible.

They have festivals, too, though these are subtle. When the wind comes and visibility extends, when multiple groups gather at the larger water sources, there's celebration: the sharing of food, the trading of goods, the long story-tellings, the rare opportunity to sing together. Young people meet, form bonds, sometimes migrate to different groups. Craftwork is displayed, admired, traded. New techniques are demonstrated. It's not exuberant—everything here is measured, careful—but there's joy in it, the pleasure of human contact in a landscape that usually disperses rather than concentrates.

## The Threshers

This is what the Listeners call the armored grazers, named for the sound their mouth-parts make against stone.

They are neither reptile nor mammal nor anything that would fit cleanly into familiar categories. Four-legged, approximately the size of large sheep or small cattle, their bodies hidden beneath plates of mineral—not scales grown from within but actual stone, basalt laminae that have somehow been incorporated into their biology, become part of their structure.

The plates overlap like roofing tiles, articulated to allow movement while providing near-complete protection. They're mottled grey-black, shot through with the same pale mineral veins that mark the landscape's basalt, making a motionless Thresher almost impossible to distinguish from the surrounding stone. The plates grow throughout the creature's life, accreting at the edges, somehow incorporating material from what the Thresher consumes.

Beneath the armor, their flesh is pale, almost translucent. The vulnerable points—joints, belly, throat—show glimpses of this inner body: fibrous muscle, strange organs, a circulatory system that pumps fluid too thick and dark to be blood. They bleed if injured, but the fluid quickly crystallizes, sealing wounds with a mineral crust.

Their heads are low-slung, wedge-shaped, ending in a broad mouth lined with grinding plates rather than teeth. These plates rasp against stone constantly, scraping away the mineral growths, the lichen-analogs, the chemical films that constitute their food. The sound is distinctive: a rhythmic scraping, slightly different for each individual, creating a texture underneath the deep droning.

Because they drone. Each Thresher produces it, the sound emanating from somewhere deep in the body cavity—no visible mouth movement, no apparent breathing rhythm, just the constant low-frequency hum that vibrates their entire body, transmits through their feet into the stone, fills the air with overlapping waves of sound.

Why they do this remains mysterious. It's constant—they drone while grazing, while moving, while sleeping if they sleep. Only in death does it stop. The sound is felt in the ground before it's heard, a vibration that carries for kilometers through the stone, creating a web of resonance across the flats.

They move in loose aggregations, not herds exactly—they maintain no fixed distance from each other, show no obvious social bonding, but they tend to cluster, drawn to the same grazing areas, moving in roughly coordinated directions. These aggregations shift slowly, flowing across the flats in patterns that the Listeners track carefully, predicting movements based on moisture distribution, mineral concentration, seasonal variations.

The Threshers are not aggressive but neither are they docile. They'll ignore a Listener who moves carefully, but startle one and it becomes dangerous: they charge with surprising speed, using their armored heads as battering rams, or rear and bring their front legs down with force that can crush bone. The Listeners have learned to move among them with careful respect, never running, always giving space.

They appear to have no eyes, or at least no visible ones. The head shows no obvious sensory organs, just smooth plates with occasional openings that might be ears or something else. Yet they navigate precisely, avoid obstacles, respond to approach. They must sense through vibration, through the droning itself, reading the world through reflected sound and felt resonance. They exist in an entirely acoustic space, their reality defined by vibration and frequency rather than light.

Reproduction is the deepest mystery. No one has ever observed mating. No one has found young, eggs, nests. Instead, Threshers simply emerge from the deeper pools, walking out fully formed, already plated, already droning. They're always adult-sized, never smaller. Where they come from, how they form—this knowledge eludes even the most attentive observers.

And they die rarely but inevitably, age visible only in subtle ways: plates that have grown so thick they impede movement, grinding surfaces worn so smooth they struggle to feed. A dying Thresher stops droning first—the cessation is noticeable, creates a small absence in the sonic landscape. Then it stops moving, usually near water, and slowly begins to be reclaimed.

The flesh dissolves quickly, consumed by scavengers and weathering. The plates remain longer, slowly breaking down, eventually crumbling to mix with the general basalt. Sometimes Listeners harvest them first, carefully prying away the valuable material while being respectful of the dead creature, acknowledging the gift of its substance.

Inside a Thresher, if one is opened—either by predation or by Listener butchery—the anatomy is strange. Organs that have no clear analog to familiar biology. Structures that seem more geological than biological, crystalline formations grown inside flesh, mineral and meat so thoroughly integrated that distinguishing them becomes arbitrary. The boundary between living and non-living feels negotiable in their bodies, as if they exist at some threshold where stone becomes animate through processes that shouldn't work but do.

They eat constantly, scraping microorganisms and chemical films from stone, concentrating trace nutrients, processing vast quantities of substrate to extract minimal energy. It's an efficient metabolism for a barren environment: they need only what's available everywhere, never have to seek scarce resources. They drink by standing in shallow water, absorbing moisture through skin or specialized organs—they're never seen drinking with their mouths.

Their flesh is edible, technically. The Listeners eat it when they can, but it's tough, fibrous, requiring long cooking to become palatable. It has a mineral taste, slightly gritty even when prepared well, as if the stone has permeated through to the meat. But it sustains, provides protein and other nutrients, makes survival possible. A single Thresher can feed a small group for weeks if preserved properly using mineral salts.

Some Listeners swear the Threshers are conscious, truly thinking, that the droning carries meaning between them, that the aggregations make collective decisions. Others think them entirely automatic, biological machines following programmed imperatives. The truth is probably somewhere between: they show purpose in their movements, seem to learn and adapt, but show no obvious individuality, no variation in behavior that would suggest personality.

They're simply part of the landscape's pattern, as fundamental as the stone or water, performing their function in the ecosystem: converting mineral substrate into flesh, providing mobile concentrations of nutrients for predators, creating the sonic environment that defines existence here. They're the engine that drives the food web, the base layer upon which everything else depends.

## The Grinders

Smaller than Threshers, numerous, filling the ecological niches that elsewhere might belong to insects or small crustaceans. They're segmented creatures, many-legged, with bodies divided into articulated sections covered in thin mineral plating—not the heavy basalt of Threshers but something lighter, translucent in places, showing the paler flesh beneath.

They vary in size from that of a finger to that of a forearm, with the smallest being most common. They move through the shallow waters, filtering microorganisms, consuming the biofilms that grow on submerged stone, occasionally scavenging from dead Threshers or other carrion.

The Listeners harvest them, carefully gathering the larger specimens from pools, processing them for food. They're dried and ground into a kind of paste that stores well, provides concentrated nutrition. The taste is again mineral-heavy, slightly bitter, but acceptable when mixed with other ingredients, when formed into cakes and cooked.

The Grinders breed prolifically, at least—unlike Threshers, their reproduction is visible. They lay eggs in clusters on underwater stone, tiny mineral-shelled spheres that hatch into miniature versions of the adults. The young grow rapidly, molting repeatedly, their plates hardening and mineralizing with each stage.

They're not individuals to the Listeners, not really—they're a resource, a background presence, part of the environment like water or stone. But they're crucial to the ecosystem, breaking down organic matter, circulating nutrients, providing food for everything larger.

## The Prowlers

Predators. Lean, long-legged, standing as tall as a Listener at the shoulder when fully grown. Their bodies are streamlined, built for efficient movement across the flats. They have four legs, but the proportions are strange—the front legs longer than the back, giving them a distinctive sloping profile, head carried low.

Their heads are wedge-shaped, like Threshers' but more mobile, capable of rapid movement. They have eyes—small, dark, placed to give forward-focused vision suited for hunting. The mouth is wide, filled with mineral-hardened ridges that serve as teeth, capable of crushing Grinder shells or tearing Thresher flesh once the armor is breached.

They're covered in short, dense coating—not fur exactly, more like felt, naturally formed fibers that shed water and provide insulation. It's dark grey, allowing them to blend with the stone, making them nearly invisible when motionless.

They hunt by stalking, moving slowly through the mist, using the droning as acoustic cover. They feel vibrations through their feet, detecting the specific signature of Thresher movement, differentiating it from the background resonance. Once they've identified prey, they approach with extraordinary patience, sometimes taking hours to close distance, moving only when their target is distracted by feeding.

The attack is sudden: a sprint, powerful jaws targeting the joints where plates meet, trying to flip a Thresher to reach the vulnerable belly. It's not always successful—Threshers outweigh them, can defend effectively, and a well-placed strike from an armored head can injure or kill a Prowler. But they're persistent, coordinated when hunting in pairs or small groups, wearing down larger prey through repeated attacks.

They're intelligent, adaptable. They learn Listener patterns, understand that structures indicate presence, occasionally stake them out waiting for opportunities. They're dangerous to lone Listeners, especially children or the injured. The community maintains vigilance, uses signals to warn of Prowler presence, hunts them when they become too bold.

But they're also respected. They're part of the balance. They cull weak Threshers, keep populations in check, provide materials—their pelts make superior clothing, their teeth valuable tools. When a Prowler is killed, everything is used carefully, and small offerings of meat are left in recognition, a gesture toward maintaining equilibrium with the landscape's other conscious inhabitants.

They den in crevices, in spaces under ancient structures, in any shelter that provides protection from the elements. They're social: mothers raise young in family groups, teaching hunting techniques through demonstration. The young are more visible than Thresher young, vulnerable and playful in ways that seem almost familiar, reminding Listeners of their own children—which perhaps makes the inevitable hunts more difficult, requiring that the necessity be acknowledged and accepted.

## The Flitters

The flying things, though they barely deserve the name—flight for them is more controlled falling, short gliding hops from one surface to another. They're small, body the size of a closed fist, with disproportionately large membranous wings that fold against their bodies when not in use.

The wings are translucent, shot through with fine veins, catching what little light exists and glowing faintly when backlit. The body is round, almost spherical, covered in fine scales that diffract light in subtle prismatic effects—one of the few sources of color on the flats, though it's muted, visible only when you're close.

They feed on the crystalline formations, landing on mineral blooms and exuding acidic compounds from modified mouth-parts, dissolving the crystals and consuming the resulting fluid. This makes them minor nuisances to Listeners who harvest crystals for pigments or other uses—areas where Flitters congregate heavily get depleted quickly.

They make high-pitched sounds, not quite chirps, more like sustained notes that pierce through the droning background. Each individual has a slightly different pitch, and when groups cluster on particularly rich mineral deposits, the overlapping frequencies create eerie harmonies.

They're prey for several species, including some of the larger Grinders and occasionally even Prowlers. They reproduce by laying eggs in cracks in the stone, hidden locations that they defend with surprising aggression for their size, divebombing intruders and using their acidic secretions as defense.

The Listeners generally ignore them, though children sometimes try to catch them—a difficult task given their mobility—and certain craftspeople value their scales, which can be harvested from molted skins and used as iridescent decoration on clothing or tools.

## The Ancient Ones

This is what Listeners call the presence in the deeper pools, the things rarely seen but known to exist. Larger than Threshers, far larger—specimens as big as the largest structures, maybe bigger. They never fully emerge, just occasionally show portions of themselves: a massive plated back breaking the surface, a limb that might be an arm or tentacle or something between.

They're probably related to Threshers, share the same basic body plan scaled up impossibly. Or they're what Threshers become if they live long enough—though how they'd survive, what they'd eat, why they'd take to deeper water, no one knows.

They might be completely harmless, just living in the depths, processing minerals from the water, existing in a metabolism so slow that days or weeks pass between movements. Or they might be dangerous—there are stories of Listeners vanishing near certain pools, of structures damaged by something large moving nearby at night.

They're never hunted, never approached deliberately. The pools they inhabit are avoided, given wide berth, treated with wary respect. Their existence is accepted as part of the landscape's deeper pattern, things that belong to the flats' hidden dimensions, reminders that surface life is only part of a larger system extending downward into darkness and mystery.

## The Microscopic

Invisible to naked eyes but crucial to everything else: the films that coat underwater stone, the organisms suspended in water, the chemical-eating microbiota that form the foundation of the food web. Listeners know about them not scientifically but practically—they know that certain processes happen, that water left standing becomes clouded with life, that surfaces develop coatings that Threshers graze.

Some of these microorganisms are bioluminescent, creating the faint glow visible at night. Others produce the chemical compounds that create certain colors in mineral deposits. Still others are probably responsible for the strange properties of Ancient One flesh, for the way Thresher plates grow, for aspects of the ecosystem that seem impossible but function regardless.

They're collectively called "the invisible herd" in Listener finger-talk, recognized as beings even if they can't be seen individually, acknowledged as the substrate upon which visible life depends.

## The Listeners Return To

All these organisms exist in a web of interdependence that the Listeners understand intuitively, having observed for generations. They know which seasons bring more Grinders, when Prowlers den, where Threshers concentrate when the wind comes. They know this not as abstract knowledge but as felt reality, as practical information integrated into movement and decision.

They know themselves as part of this web too—predators and prey, consumers and resources, temporary arrangements of material that will return to the cycle when their time ends. They take what they need carefully, not from conservation as ideology but from recognition that they depend on the system's continuation, that excess now means deprivation later.

They're conscious of being the only beings here who ask why, who wonder about purpose and meaning, who create art and story and belief. But they're uncertain whether this makes them special or simply strange, adapted for a niche that requires imagination, uniquely suited to survive by understanding patterns rather than simply following them.

They continue, scattered across the flats, their gestures flickering briefly in the grey light, their small fires burning against the damp, their lives playing out in the spaces between water and stone, sound and silence, the known and the permanently mysterious.

The droning continues, endless and patient, and they live within it, shaped by it, part of the breathing that gives this place its name—the slow respiration of an ecosystem at equilibrium, where everything that remains is exactly what can remain, and nothing more.
