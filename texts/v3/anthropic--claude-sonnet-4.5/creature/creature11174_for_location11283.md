# The Incubation Gallery: What Lives There

The attendants have been here so long they've started to calcify.

Not literally—though their joints move with increasing reluctance in the cold, and sometimes you see them work a shoulder or hip in slow circles, coaxing cartilage that wants to freeze—but in the way of people who've become deposits, sedimentary layers. There are seven of them in this chamber at any given time, though the total number across all shifts is difficult to pin down. Twelve? Fifteen? They're evasive when asked, or perhaps they genuinely don't know anymore. People arrive through the training rotations and some stay, and some who were supposed to leave decades ago are still here, and the distinction between temporary assignment and permanent residence eroded so gradually that no one can identify the moment of transition.

The oldest is Vikram, though even that name might be obsolete—he responds to it with a delay, as if translating from some interior language he's developed. His beard is entirely white, stiff with perpetual dampness, and grows in strange patterns where frostbite has scarred the follicles. He moves through the racks with a peculiar gliding motion, feet never quite lifting from the grating, and he can navigate the entire chamber in complete darkness. You've seen him do it when a circuit failed: continuing his rounds by touch and memory, hands moving to dishes with unerring accuracy, fingers reading the water temperature, the surface tension, the number of larvae by how they disturb the meniscus.

He doesn't speak much. When he does, it's often to the amphibians rather than the other attendants. A murmuring commentary: "Yes, I see you. That one's not ready yet. Try R7-S3, clutch should be emerging." Whether they understand is impossible to say. Whether he expects them to understand is equally unclear. He's been here since the beginning—not the very beginning, but close enough that the distinction doesn't matter. He remembers when there were only three chambers. When the heating elements were a different design, less reliable. When they lost an entire generation to a fungal outbreak because the humidity calculations were wrong.

Then there's Yeo-jin, who talks constantly, a stream of narration directed at no one in particular. "R4-S9-D3 showing opacity, probably ready by evening shift. R4-S10-D7 needs fresh water, getting that tea color, too much breakdown. Adult on the ceiling, the one with the green tag, hasn't moved in three days, might be sick or might be doing that thing they do, we still don't know what that thing is." She's younger—maybe thirty?—but the cold makes ages converge. She's been here five years and will probably be here forever. Something in her voice suggests she's already accepted this, has moved past acceptance into a kind of cheerful fatalism.

She's the one who names things. The amphibians aren't supposed to have names—they're numbered, tagged, tracked as data points—but she names them anyway. "That's Seven-of-Clubs," she'll say, pointing to an individual indistinguishable to you from dozens of others. "She likes the warmer racks. Always trying to get closer to the heating elements." The other attendants pretend not to use her names but you catch them doing it, slipping into her taxonomy when they're tired: "Has anyone seen Jack-be-Quick today? His clutch is due."

There's a married couple, Oskar and Liu, who work alternate shifts and leave notes for each other in the logbook—official entries that become love letters in code. "R12-S4: adjusted flow rate, see notes from previous observation, you were right about the temperature gradient." "R12-S4: confirmed, hatch rate improved 4%, you're brilliant." They've been here eight years and have never lived anywhere else together. Their entire relationship exists in this chamber and the connected chambers and the narrow sleeping quarters carved from ice nearby. They talk about leaving someday, about seeing the ocean, about applying for a transfer to the coastal facility. No one believes them, least of all themselves.

Then there's Dmitri, who isn't old but is sick—something in his lungs, a wet rattle that never clears. The cold aggravates it. He should leave, everyone knows this, but he stays. He's the one who maintains the equipment, who understands the failing systems in the way you understand an aging relative's declining health. He can diagnose a pump problem by sound, knows which pipes will last another year and which need immediate replacement, has jury-rigged solutions that violate every safety protocol and work perfectly. When he coughs—and he coughs often, the sound absorbed by moisture before it echoes—he turns away from the eggs, covers his mouth with a cloth he keeps for this purpose, waits for the fit to pass, then continues working as if nothing happened.

There are others. Karin, who might be nineteen or might be thirty, who does the microscope work with hands steady enough for surgery. Jae-won, who sings under his breath while working, tuneless fragments in a language you don't recognize. Someone called Moth—you never learn their real name—who works the night shifts exclusively and moves through the chamber like a ghost, appearing suddenly at your elbow, disappearing just as quickly.

And others still, more peripheral. People who come for a double shift and vanish for three days. People who might work in adjacent chambers and drift through on errands. The community is porous, overlapping, its boundaries as unclear as the boundaries between ice and snow in the walls.

They've developed their own culture down here, or perhaps it would be more accurate to say the chamber has developed its culture through them, using them as the medium. They have hierarchies that make sense only in context: who decides when to adjust the temperature in a disputed section, who has authority over culling deformed larvae, who gets first access to the microscope. These hierarchies aren't based on seniority or official rank—which dissolved years ago anyway when the administrative structure outside stopped paying attention—but on something more subtle. Competence, partly. Intuition, partly. Who the amphibians seem to tolerate, perhaps.

Because the amphibians do seem to have preferences.

They're not intelligent—or rather, they don't display intelligence in ways that map onto standard metrics. They don't use tools, don't solve puzzles, don't show evidence of planning beyond immediate stimulus-response. But they're not automata either. They do things that resist explanation.

The adults—there are hundreds in this chamber alone, thousands across the complex—exist in a state somewhere between wild and domestic. They're not tame. They don't seek out human contact, don't respond to calls or training. But they're habituated to human presence in ways that go beyond simple tolerance. They navigate around attendants with the casual familiarity of roommates. They perch on equipment being used, on shoulders, on heads, utterly unconcerned. When an attendant reaches into a dish to adjust eggs, the adult in that dish might shift aside, might stay put and let the hand work around it, might climb onto the hand itself and ride it through the adjustment like a vehicle.

They're watching constantly. Those oversized eyes, adapted for the dim light, track movement with disturbing precision. You'll be working and realize an adult has been staring at you from a pipe for the past ten minutes, motionless except for the slow pulse of its throat. Then it blinks—a membrane sliding sideways across the eye, amphibian-style—and leaps away in a single fluid motion that crosses three racks.

They vocalize more than wild amphibians. The clicking is constant, but there are other sounds: a chirping noise, almost cricket-like. A low vibration that you feel more than hear. Something that sounds almost like a sigh. Yeo-jin swears they're communicating, that the sounds have semantic content, that she's starting to recognize patterns. The others are skeptical but not dismissive. Stranger things have been true in this place.

The adults congregate in clusters whose logic isn't clear. You'll find fifteen of them arranged on a single shelf, motionless, positioned in what looks like a geometric pattern. Then they disperse and the pattern dissolves and you wonder if you imagined the organization. They avoid certain areas—everyone agrees on this—but the avoided zones shift. A rack that was shunned last week is heavily populated now. A section near the workbench that's always been active is suddenly empty. Vikram tracks these movements in a separate notebook he keeps for his own purposes, has been tracking them for years, claims there's a pattern cycling with a period of approximately nineteen months. No one has confirmed this. No one has refuted it either.

They return to their eggs, though they shouldn't. Amphibians don't do this. They lay their eggs and leave, no parental investment, no guarding behavior. But these return. Not all of them, and not consistently, but enough that it's not coincidence. An adult will settle on the edge of a dish, sometimes for hours, and the eggs in that dish are its genetic offspring—this has been verified through the tagging system, though how the adult knows is unclear. Scent, possibly. Some chemical signature in the water. Or something else, something the controlled environment has enabled, a behavior that wild populations never had the stability to develop.

Sometimes they eat the eggs. Their own offspring, or others', it doesn't seem to matter. They'll pluck an egg from a dish with that strange six-fingered hand and swallow it whole. The attendants don't intervene—this is noted as a data point, as another variable in the vast behavioral matrix they're compiling. "Adult 23B consumed six eggs from clutch R8-S7-D12, all non-viable based on opacity, culling behavior?" Yeo-jin writes, then adds: "Or just hungry?"

They eat the larvae too, occasionally. You'll see an adult with its head submerged in a dish, mouth working, and when it lifts away the larval count has decreased. This is more troubling—the larvae are viable, are the point of the entire operation—but it happens rarely enough that it falls within acceptable loss rates. Everything here has acceptable loss rates. The calculations are posted on a laminated sheet near the workbench, updated periodically in different hands: expected hatch percentage, expected larval survival, expected adult survival, expected behavioral abnormalities. Nothing is 100%. Most metrics hover in the 70-85% range, which sounds terrible until you learn that wild populations manage perhaps 40% in ideal conditions, and these are not ideal conditions, and yet somehow they're better.

The larvae exist in their own strange world, suspended in liquid, in time, in a life stage that's been artificially extended. Normally—in the wild, in species this is derived from—the larval stage lasts weeks. Here it lasts months. The prolonged development is deliberate, a result of temperature manipulation and selective breeding. The larvae grow larger than they should, develop in slow motion, their organ systems visible through translucent skin.

They're mostly mouth and gut. The filtering apparatus is elaborate, delicate, like living plumbing. Water enters through structures that aren't quite gills, passes through chambers where bacteria and algae are extracted, exits through vents that pulse in rhythm. They don't have brains yet—or rather, they have nerve clusters, primitive processing, but nothing like cognition. They're biological machines for converting water-borne nutrients into tissue.

But they respond to stimuli in ways that suggest... not awareness, but something. Tap a dish and the larvae reorient, their vibration patterns changing. Play certain frequencies—Dmitri has experimented with this—and they synchronize, pulsing together. The mechanisms are understood: mechanoreceptors, simple reflexes. But the coordination looks eerie, looks purposeful, looks like tiny organisms participating in something larger than themselves.

Some dishes develop hierarchies. Larger larvae dominate the prime positions—nearer to the water surface, closer to wherever the dissolved oxygen is richest. Smaller larvae get displaced to the margins. This shouldn't happen; they don't have the neural architecture for dominance behavior. And yet it does happen, consistently enough that the attendants account for it, rotating dish positions to prevent the smallest larvae from being completely excluded from resources.

The transition from larva to adult happens in stages, but the final metamorphosis is abrupt. The attendants can predict it, have learned to read the signs: a cessation of feeding, a change in color, a stillness. Then, over the course of hours, the body reorganizes itself. Limbs emerge, not growing exactly but unfolding from tissue that was serving other purposes. The mouth reshapes from filter to predator. The lungs develop, and the creature breaks the surface for its first breath, and it's no longer an aquatic organism but an amphibious one, though it will never stray far from water.

This happens in the dishes, without ceremony. The attendants sometimes move metamorphosing individuals to special tanks where they can be monitored more closely, but often they don't bother. The process succeeds or it fails. If it succeeds, a new adult joins the population. If it fails, the body is removed, catalogued as another data point, sometimes dissected if Karin has time and interest.

The adults that emerge are already adapted to the chamber. They've never known anything else. They climb immediately to the racks, claim positions, begin participating in whatever collective behavior the adults engage in. They're indistinguishable from older adults within days. There's no juvenile period in any meaningful sense—they emerge as miniature adults and simply are that now.

The population is stable. This is the triumph of the facility, the thing that justifies its existence to whatever distant authorities still care. Births roughly balance deaths. The genetic diversity is managed through careful breeding records. The health metrics remain consistent. The population is stable, which is another way of saying it's suspended, held in equilibrium against the collapse that logic says should happen.

Because everything here should fail. The equipment should break down—and it does, constantly, and is repaired. The ice should crush the chambers—and it tries, and is resisted. The closed system should accumulate toxins, should exhaust nutrients, should tip into some fatal imbalance—and it teeters on the edge but never quite tips, held in place by constant intervention.

The attendants are the intervention. They're the corrective force. They're the ones who notice when the water chemistry in R9-S3 is trending wrong and adjust the flow rate. Who catch the fungal outbreak on R4-S7 before it spreads. Who replace the heating element in the northwest corner before the temperature drop kills the eggs there. Who document the slow, cumulative changes that might be evolution or might be developmental noise and won't be distinguishable for another decade of data.

They maintain the logs obsessively. The waterproof notebooks accumulate, shelf after shelf near the workbench, years of observations recorded in increasingly cramped handwriting as paper supplies dwindle. Someone—maybe Vikram, maybe someone before him—started a database on a ruggedized laptop, and for years the entries were duplicated digitally, but the laptop failed eventually and couldn't be replaced, so now there are years of electronic records isolated on a dead machine and years of subsequent paper records that exist nowhere else.

The data shows patterns if you squint. Hatch rates correlating with ice pressure, suggesting the glacier's seasonal movement affects the chamber in subtle ways. Color pattern variations clustering in certain genetic lines. Behavioral changes across generations—the adults are slightly more social than they were a decade ago, slightly more tolerant of crowding, though whether this is genetic or cultural or imaginary is unclear.

The attendants discuss these patterns endlessly. Their conversations are technical, abbreviated, impenetrable to outsiders. "You see the R11 clutch? That chromatic pattern, same as the R3s from six years back, it's the recessive line expressing." "Maybe, or it's dietary, remember when we changed the algae culture in sector C?" "No, different. Different base tone. I'll check the logs."

They argue about methodology, about interpretation, about what's significant and what's noise. These arguments are gentle, almost ritualistic, the same points raised and countered in cycles because there's no way to resolve them definitively and because the argument itself is part of the culture, part of what binds them.

They rarely talk about why. Why this species, why this place, why the decades of effort to maintain a population that exists nowhere else and serves no clear purpose. The justifications are probably in some briefing document none of them have seen, filed in some office in some distant city that might not exist anymore for all they know. The radio link to the surface is temperamental. Messages go up occasionally: status reports, supply requests. Messages come back rarely. The last supply drop was... when? There's disagreement. Four months ago. Six months. Karin thinks it might be longer.

The supplies they have are dwindling in ways that matter. The chemical stocks for water treatment are low. The electrical insulation is failing, making certain repairs dangerous. The paper for logs is nearly gone—they've started writing in margins, in increasingly cramped script. But the essential things hold: the eggs keep hatching, the larvae keep developing, the adults keep living. The system maintains itself with decreasing support, becoming more autonomous, more resilient, or perhaps just more adapted to operating on the edge of failure.

The attendants have stopped asking for relief, for rotation, for the resupply schedules to normalize. They've accepted—not resigned themselves, exactly, but accepted in some deeper sense—that this is what they do now, that the chamber is where they live, that the distinction between duty and identity has eroded completely.

They sleep in shifts in the adjacent quarters, narrow slots carved from ice and insulated with layers of fabric that's molding despite best efforts. They eat rations that arrive irregularly, supplemented by—well, no one says it directly, but the protein synthesis equipment in the biochem lab one level down produces nutritional slurries from bacterial cultures, and these slurries are theoretically for the larvae but are, in practice, edible by humans, more or less. They maintain their equipment with parts cannibalized from less critical systems. They wear clothing that's patched and repatched until it's more patch than original material.

They're not unhappy. This is perhaps the strangest thing. They're not joyful either—the emotional range here is narrow, compressed by the cold and the routine and the constant low-grade stress of maintaining impossible systems. But they find satisfactions. Vikram with his silent communion with the amphibians. Yeo-jin with her naming rituals. Oskar and Liu with their coded love letters. Dmitri with his machinery diagnostics. They've found ways to be human in a place that seems designed to eliminate humanity through attrition.

The amphibians, meanwhile, continue their existence in apparent contentment. They have no basis for comparison, no sense that their habitat is artificial, precarious, maintained by constant effort. They navigate the racks as their ancestors navigated ponds and streams—which is to say, their ancestors didn't; the species has been in this chamber or chambers like it for so many generations that behavioral memory of anything else has been bred out. They are creatures of metal and ice and industrial infrastructure, as adapted to this as any organism to any niche.

You watch an adult navigate from ceiling to floor, choosing a path through the racks that seems inefficient until you realize it's visiting specific dishes, checking them in sequence, lingering at some and ignoring others. Is this purposeful? Is it searching for its own offspring? Is it following some instinct that made sense in an ancestral environment and now misfires in interesting ways? Or is it genuinely doing something new, something the chamber has enabled?

Yeo-jin appears beside you, following your gaze. "That's Diamond-Jack," she says. "He does that route twice a day, every day. Been doing it for three weeks."

"Why?" you ask.

She shrugs. "We don't know. Maybe it means something. Maybe it's like, I don't know, OCD or something. Maybe it's just what he does." She makes a note on her clipboard. "We write it down anyway."

This is the essence of the work: writing down what happens, even when—especially when—no one knows what it means. Accumulating observations toward some future understanding that may never arrive. Building a knowledge base that exists only in waterlogged notebooks in a chamber carved from ice that's slowly, inexorably, crushing inward.

An alarm sounds—brief, two tones. The attendants pause, count seconds, resume work. You learn later this particular alarm indicates a pressure change in the water system, probably from ice settling in one of the feeder tunnels. It happens daily. It's factored in. Everything that can go wrong has gone wrong repeatedly and been integrated into normal operations.

The chamber persists. The amphibians persist. The attendants persist. The glacier outside moves at geological speed, patient and implacable, and inside its body these small lives continue, this small ecosystem maintains itself, these humans and amphibians and bacteria and lichens exist together in a space that should not support them and does anyway, through stubbornness, through careful observation, through ten thousand small interventions per day that add up to something that looks almost like permanence but is really just extremely well-maintained impermanence.

And the eggs keep hatching, translucent membranes dissolving in water, larvae emerging into their dishes, beginning the long, slow development that will eventually—if the chamber holds, if the equipment holds, if the attendants hold—lead to them joining the adults in the racks, becoming part of the clicking, watching, climbing mass of organisms that are the chamber's reason for existing and its only real inhabitants, while the humans are just the infrastructure, the machinery, the maintenance system that the amphibians don't even know they need.
