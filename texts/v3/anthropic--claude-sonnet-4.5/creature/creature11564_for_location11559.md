# The Surge Valleys: Who Lives Here

## The Resonants

They were human once—perhaps three generations ago, perhaps five; time moves strangely when the ground beneath you is still deciding its final form. Now they are something adjacent to human, transformed not through deliberate engineering but through the valley's insistent selection pressures and their own slow negotiations with this deafening world.

Their most obvious adaptation is silence. Not the silence of the mute, but a profound *acoustic withdrawal* from the chaos around them. They have learned—been forced to learn—that voice is useless here, that speech disappears into the mat-drone and water-roar before it travels two meters. Over generations, they've stopped trying. Their vocal cords still function, but atrophy has made them thin; when they do vocalize, it emerges as something between a whisper and a harmonic, pitched to slide *between* the dominant frequencies of the soundscape rather than compete with them. They use these thread-thin voices only for specific purposes—warnings, mostly, sounds that carry meaning through tone rather than words, differentiated sighs that other Resonants can distinguish even when the valleys are at their loudest.

Instead, they have developed a gestural language so complex it borders on dance. Not the broad signing of the deaf—those movements would be invisible in the fog—but something more intimate, conducted in the space between touching and not-touching. They read each other through proximity, through the subtle displacement of mist-thick air, through vibrations transmitted skin-to-skin when gesture alone won't suffice. A fingertip drawn across a palm: *water rising in the eastern channel*. A specific pressure on the inside of a wrist: *the mats near the third platform are harmonizing*. The back of one hand brushing the other's cheek: *I need to leave but will return*. They have developed a haptic vocabulary of perhaps two thousand discrete communications, each contextual, each modified by pressure and duration and location on the body.

Their hands have changed to accommodate this language. Fingers are longer, more sensitive, with fingertip ridges pronounced enough to be individually distinctive—each Resonant can identify any other by touch alone, reading their unique ridge-pattern like a name written in skin. Their palms are mapped territories of meaning, different zones corresponding to different semantic categories: water-information near the thumb, mat-information near the heel of the hand, social-information in the center, spatial-information along the fingers. To watch two Resonants communicate is to see something like intimate massage, like palm-reading, like careful violence, their hands moving across each other's with absolute precision while their faces remain still, their eyes focused not on each other but on the valley around them, always monitoring, always listening.

Because listening is what they do. Listening is their *function*, their purpose, their reason for remaining in these valleys when they could presumably leave. They are, in the most literal sense, the landscape's ears—the ones who hear not just sound but *pattern*, who can distinguish meaning in the chaos, who have trained themselves over generations to parse the valley's voice.

Each Resonant specializes. Not formally—there are no schools here, no institutions—but through inclination and aptitude and the valley's own subtle pressures. Some are *mat-readers*, attuned specifically to the sound-rug drones, able to distinguish not just species and health but *intent* (if intent is the right word for the mats' collective behavior). They can hear when a mat-colony is about to slough, when it's entering a productive phase, when it's sick with some substrate-borne contamination. They can predict, with unsettling accuracy, where new colonies will establish, which acoustic installations will need adjustment to maintain particular harmonic relationships. They spend their days moving along the valley walls with their palms pressed to the mat-growth, *feeling* the vibrations, reading the bass-frequencies that transmit through stone better than air, building an ongoing map of mat-territories and their shifting boundaries.

Others are *water-listeners*, focused on the liquid voices—the appearance and disappearance of streams, the roar of condensing mist-channels, the singing of organ-pipe fissures. They can predict, sometimes hours in advance, where water will surface next, which channels will hold liquid and which will run dry, when the water table will rise or fall. They're the ones who maintain the carved channels, adjusting them constantly to work with the water's preferred paths rather than against them. They sleep (when they sleep) near the ephemeral streams, waking instantly when the water-sounds change, moving in the darkness to witness and document and occasionally guide the liquid's restless exploration of the substrate.

Still others are *stone-listeners*, attuned to the grinding pulse of the settling basalt, able to distinguish between different types of settling—compression versus fracture, surface-adjustment versus deep-structural shift. They're the ones who know when a valley wall is about to develop new cracks, when a platform needs repositioning, when the substrate is stable enough to support new construction. They carry tools—simple things, hafted basalt flakes, metal fragments salvaged from whatever catastrophe preceded the Fall—and use them to tap and scratch and *read* the stone, interpreting the resonances that return, building an understanding of what lies beneath the surface, how the young geology is maturing.

And then there are the *wholeness-listeners*, the rarest specialization, the ones who somehow hear not individual sound-sources but the entire acoustic ecology as a unified system. They're the ones who position the acoustic installations, who understand (or intuit, or sense—they can't explain it even in their gestural language) how a stone placed *here* will interact with a mat-colony *there* and a water-emergence *there* to create a moment of accidental beauty three hours from now when the light and humidity and temperature align. They're the ones most changed by this place, most withdrawn from anything resembling conventional human affect. They move through the valleys like sleepwalkers, their faces empty of expression, their bodies responding to acoustic cues invisible to others—turning toward a sound-source before it manifests, positioning themselves to receive a harmonic that hasn't yet formed, conducting the valley's voice with gestures that look religious or mad or both.

The Resonants live mostly alone or in pairs, scattered across the valley network in rough territories that overlap and shift. The platforms are theirs—sleeping places, observation posts, workstations where they maintain their simple tools and occasionally eat. They don't cook; fire is nearly impossible in the constant damp, and the smoke would add another variable to an already chaotic acoustic environment. Instead they eat the valley's production directly: certain mat-species that fruit (if fruiting is the right word) with nodular growths rich in fats and some protein-analogue; the arthropod-analogues, which are edible if you can catch them and don't mind the texture; processed water, filtered through charcoal and mat-material to remove whatever mineral load makes it unsafe straight from the streams; and supplies brought by the occasional trader from beyond the valleys, though these visits are rare and the Resonants seem to prefer the valley's own productions.

They wear layers, as I mentioned—treated fabric that manages moisture without fully blocking it, because blocking it is impossible and trying only leads to trapped condensation and hypothermia. The fabric is a local production, woven from fibers harvested from certain mat-species, treated with oils rendered from arthropod-analogues, dyed with mineral pigments ground from the basalt itself. The result is clothing in shades of black and grey and deep blue-green, colors that make the Resonants difficult to see against the valley walls, that let them move through the fog like particularly solid shadows. They layer for temperature regulation—adding and removing pieces as they move between the cold baseline and the warm updraft zones, creating micro-climates around their bodies that they adjust constantly, automatically, without apparent thought.

Their faces are weathered in unusual patterns. The constant moisture means their skin doesn't dry and crack like you'd expect from cold exposure, but the mineral content of the air—whatever the transforming basalt releases—has left discolorations, patterns of light and dark that make their faces look map-like, territorial, as if the valleys have written themselves onto the people. Their eyes are adapted to the low light, pupils permanently dilated, giving them a startled appearance that doesn't match their usual calm. Many squint constantly, not from brightness (there is no brightness) but from the effort of seeing through fog, of extracting visual signal from a landscape designed to obscure. Some develop a particular stillness of gaze, a way of looking *through* rather than *at* things, focusing on movement and change rather than static forms.

They age rapidly. The cold, the damp, the constant acoustic stress, the endless vigilance required to move safely through shifting terrain—it takes a toll. A Resonant of thirty looks fifty. One of fifty, if they've survived that long, looks ancient, their bodies bent not from skeletal deformation but from sheer accumulated exhaustion. Yet they don't leave. Even when age makes the platform-climbing difficult, when their hearing begins to fail (as it inevitably does—the deafening soundscape damages ears no matter how carefully you protect them), when their hands become too arthritic for the precise gestural language—even then, they stay. They transition to different roles: maintenance of the building, tasks that don't require acute hearing, teaching of younger Resonants through patient demonstration. And eventually they die here, their bodies handled with a matter-of-factness that might seem cold but is actually deeply pragmatic—taken to specific locations in the valleys where the mat-growth is particularly aggressive, placed carefully, left to be incorporated into the substrate. The mats process them quickly. Within weeks, sometimes days, there's no trace except perhaps a slight discoloration in the colony, a slightly different tone to its drone.

## The Children

There are children, but few. The Resonants don't breed often—the stress, the difficulty of maintaining pregnancy in the cold and damp, the danger of birth without medical infrastructure—but occasionally a pregnancy occurs and sometimes survives to term. These children are *born* into the chaos, never knowing silence, developing from their first moments with the valley's voice as their baseline. They're strange even by Resonant standards.

They don't startle at sudden sounds. They *can't*—the concept of "sudden sound" is meaningless when you're born into perpetual cacophony. Instead they startle at *absence*, at the rare moments when sections of the soundscape drop away—a mat-colony falling silent, a water-channel unexpectedly ceasing, a temporary acoustic shadow created by fog density. In those moments of relative quiet, the children freeze, their faces showing the only fear you'll see from them, as if silence itself is dangerous.

They learn the gestural language before they learn to walk, their infant hands making recognizable signs months before they can coordinate their legs. They're carried constantly in the first years, wrapped against a parent's chest where they can feel the parent's movements, learn the physical vocabulary through vibration and proximity. By age three or four, they're fluent in gesture, able to communicate complex ideas without sound. By age six or seven, they're beginning to specialize, showing inclination toward mat-reading or water-listening or stone-sensing. By adolescence, they're full participants in the valley's acoustic ecology, often more skilled than their parents at parsing particular sound-sources, their younger ears still undamaged, their native immersion giving them intuitions that first-generation Resonants had to learn deliberately.

The children play, but their play is *quiet*—games of gesture and touch, complex patterns of hand-movement that might be storytelling or strategy or pure aesthetic exploration. They climb the valley walls with unnerving confidence, their bodies adapting to the terrain with liquid ease. They're fascinated by the fauna, spending hours watching the arthropod swarms or tracking the otter-things or trying to predict where the flying creatures will dive. They're learning to read the valley, building the perceptual database they'll need to survive here, but they're doing it through what looks like play—joyful, focused, completely silent except for occasional thread-thin harmonics they use to coordinate with each other.

## The Visitors

Not Resonants, but present: the occasional traders, researchers, pilgrims, tourists who come to witness the valleys' strangeness. They don't last long. The sensory assault is too intense for those not raised to it. Most stay hours, maybe a day, long enough to feel the full weight of the acoustic chaos, to understand they're in a place fundamentally hostile to unmodified human nervous systems, then they leave, shaken, often vowing never to return.

A few return anyway. Repeatedly. These are the ones who feel something in the chaos, some pull they can't articulate. They're not Resonants—they maintain lives elsewhere, come only for visits measured in days or weeks—but they're *drawn*. They stay in the building (which serves partly as a waystation for visitors) or on designated visitor platforms, marked with particular stone configurations. They try, usually unsuccessfully, to learn the gestural language. They bring equipment—recorders, mostly, trying to capture the soundscape, though playback never conveys the *presence* of it, the way it's felt in the body as much as heard in the ears. Some bring instruments, attempting to add their own voices to the chaos. The Resonants tolerate this with polite incomprehension; the idea of *adding* sound seems bizarre to them, like trying to add water to a flood.

## The Predecessors

The building contains evidence of others—the ones who were here *before* the Resonants, possibly before the Fall itself, though the timeline is unclear. The interior (which I haven't described because the Resonants are protective of it, allowing visitors only into certain sections) holds technology beyond the simple sensors scattered through the valleys. There are screens showing data-streams in languages or symbol-systems no one currently present can fully read. There are devices whose purposes are obvious—atmosphere monitors, seismic sensors, biological analyzers—and others whose functions remain mysterious, crystalline structures that pulse with light in rhythms that might correlate with the mat-songs or might be entirely independent.

The Resonants maintain this technology without fully understanding it. They keep it clean, replace components when they fail (using parts salvaged from storage rooms deeper in the building), follow maintenance protocols recorded in pictographic instructions that might have been left specifically for successors who wouldn't share the creators' language. It's caretaking, not operation—they keep the systems running without knowing what the systems *do*, following ritual-like procedures that ensure continuity without comprehension.

Who built this place? The question obsesses some visitors but seems to barely interest the Resonants. They have theories—everyone does—but they're offered casually, without investment: Perhaps the Predecessors created the Fall itself, the matter-rain that became the basalt plains. Perhaps they're monitoring the valleys' development as an experiment in accelerated geology or ecology. Perhaps they're long dead and the technology is running on automatic, following programming that will continue long after its creators are forgotten. Perhaps they're still here, in sections of the building the current Resonants don't access, observing, recording, occasionally adjusting parameters.

The wholeness-listeners sometimes speak (in their thread-thin voices, or through particularly emphatic gestures) of feeling *presence* in certain locations—not the presence of other Resonants or fauna, but something else, something that *attends* without manifesting. Whether this is genuine perception or the acoustic equivalent of seeing faces in clouds is unclear. The valleys do strange things to perception. The constant sensory assault, the isolation, the way the landscape resists stable interpretation—it creates conditions where distinguishing signal from noise, genuine presence from pareidolia, becomes genuinely difficult.

## The Mat-Consciousness Question

And then there's the question no one can answer but everyone asks eventually: Are the mats *aware*?

Not individually—single colonies show no signs of cognition, just biological process, photosynthesis and chemical transformation and acoustic emission as byproduct. But *collectively*, across the entire valley network, with thousands of colonies coordinating their drones into accidental harmonies, responding to each other's outputs, creating acoustic territories and maintaining them through some mechanism no one has identified—collectively, there's something that looks unsettlingly like behavior.

The wholeness-listeners are convinced. They speak (when they can be induced to speak) of the valleys as a *system*, the mats as neurons, the acoustic output as something between communication and thought. They say the moments of harmony—like the one at the seven-tree circle when the water surfaces—aren't accidents but *attention*, the system focusing on particular locations for reasons of its own. They say the way mat-colonies respond to the acoustic installations, adjusting their drones to incorporate the installations' modifications, suggests learning or at least adaptation that goes beyond simple stimulus-response.

The mat-readers are more skeptical, but they'll admit the colonies show coordination no one has adequately explained. Chemical signaling through the substrate, maybe. Acoustic triggering—one colony's frequency affecting others nearby, creating cascades of adjustment. But why the coordination produces *harmony* rather than discord, why certain frequency combinations seem to be preferred, why the patterns shift but maintain overall coherence—these questions remain unanswered.

And the patterns *are* coherent. Visitors with musical training hear it immediately, beneath the chaos: modes, progressions, rhythmic structures that approach (but never quite reach) regularity. The wholeness-listeners, when positioning acoustic installations, are working *with* these patterns, amplifying them, making explicit what was implicit in the mat-songs. The result is a landscape that seems to be *composing* itself, discovering its own acoustic structure through trial and error and mutual adjustment.

Whether this constitutes consciousness is philosophy, not answerable through observation. But the Resonants live as if the valleys are *listening* to them as much as they're listening to the valleys. They position installations as if in conversation. They maintain the building as if for an audience that isn't physically present but is somehow *there* in the acoustic ecology, in the way the mats respond, in the moments when chaos briefly organizes into beauty.

## The Transformed

Finally, there are the ones who've gone *too deep*, the Resonants who spent too long in wholeness-listening, whose perceptual boundaries dissolved in the acoustic chaos. They're rare—maybe a dozen in the entire valley network—but recognizable: they move with total unselfconsciousness, no longer maintaining the vigilant monitoring that characterizes other Resonants. They don't use platforms for sleeping, just lie down wherever they happen to be when exhaustion takes them, trusting the valleys not to drown them (and somehow, they don't drown—they wake before the water rises, or the water surfaces elsewhere, or they sleep in channels that stay dry). They don't communicate with gestures anymore, at least not consistently; they've moved beyond language into pure presence, interacting with other Resonants only when directly engaged, otherwise moving through the valleys like particularly purposeful weather.

Other Resonants treat them with careful respect, not quite reverence but something adjacent. They're fed when they appear near platforms. They're gently guided away from genuine dangers—unstable slopes, walls about to fracture. They're allowed access to the building's deepest sections, the ones other Resonants avoid. And they're listened to when they do speak, because what emerges from them isn't conversation but something like prophecy: statements about mat-patterns weeks before they manifest, predictions about substrate changes that prove accurate, instructions for acoustic installations that create effects no one else could have anticipated.

Whether they're insane or enlightened or both is, again, unanswerable. They've clearly lost something—the boundaries that make individual identity possible, the self-preservation that keeps others safe. But they've gained something too: a merger with the landscape so complete they seem to *be* the valleys in some sense, their bodies moving through the terrain the way the water moves, appearing and vanishing, present but not-present, their consciousness (if they still have consciousness) distributed across the entire acoustic ecology rather than localized in a single skull.

The Resonants speak of this fate with ambivalence. It's honored as a kind of completion, the logical endpoint of wholeness-listening. But it's also feared as a dissolution, a loss of everything that makes survival meaningful. Most Resonants pull back before reaching that edge, maintaining enough boundary to remain *themselves* while still engaging deeply with the valley's voice. But every generation, a few cross over, and the valleys accept them, and they become something other than human, other than Resonant, something the valleys themselves might dream if valleys dreamed.

---

This is who lives in the Surge Valleys: the listeners, the children, the visitors, the mysterious Predecessors whose technology still pulses in the building, the possibly-conscious mat-network, and the transformed ones who've dissolved into the acoustic chaos. All of them participating in the landscape's ongoing creation, adding their voices (silent or otherwise) to the deafening chorus, finding patterns in turbulence, making homes in a place that refuses to be still.
