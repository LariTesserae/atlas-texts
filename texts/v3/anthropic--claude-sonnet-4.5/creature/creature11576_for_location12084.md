# Who Lives Here

## The Maintainers

They are called maintainers by those who know of them, though they have no name for themselves—or rather, their name is the action they perform, continuously, without beginning or cessation. To maintain. To keep the channels clear, the flow correct, the breathing synchronized.

You might not see them for hours of walking. Days, perhaps, if time works that way here. Then: a figure in the darkness, crouched beside a channel where water has begun to pool incorrectly. They are human-shaped, mostly, but the proportions are wrong in ways that become more apparent the longer you look. The limbs are too long, joints positioned slightly off, as if evolution here has been revising the human template for optimization. Their fingers—six on each hand—are webbed halfway up, membrane stretched between them, thin enough to be translucent when backlit by the glowing pools. 

They wear nothing, or they wear something that cannot be distinguished from their skin, which is the same dark grey as the wet basalt, smooth and beaded with moisture. The skin itself seems almost porous, weeping with the same patient persistence as the stone, so they glisten constantly in the faint light. Their heads are proportioned strangely—narrower, taller, with eyes positioned further apart and larger than they should be, adapted for this near-darkness, reflecting what little light exists with a pale silver sheen.

They don't acknowledge your presence. The maintainer reaches into the pooling water—water that should be flowing but has stalled against some invisible obstruction—and feels along the channel bed with those long fingers. They work by touch more than sight, reading the stone's surface, the water's intention. Finding something—sediment, perhaps, or a subtle misalignment in the stone itself—they begin to shape it. Their fingers move in small, precise gestures, and the stone responds. Not chipping or breaking, but yielding, becoming plastic under their touch for just moments, long enough to be smoothed, redirected, corrected. The water begins to flow properly again. The maintainer remains crouched, watching, waiting to ensure the correction holds, then rises in a single fluid motion and walks away, long legs carrying them in a gait that's almost but not quite human, slightly sideways, adapted to traversing wet stone on slopes.

There are more of them, scattered throughout the visible terraces. Never many in one place. They work alone, each one attending to their section of the city with patient focus. Sometimes you'll see one inside a building, visible through a doorway, doing something in that absolute interior darkness—you can only tell they're there by the faint sounds of water being redirected, stone being adjusted. Sometimes they're at the towers, climbing the exterior with impossible ease, their webbed fingers and toes finding purchase on the smooth surface, ascending to clear a clogged opening or adjust some internal mechanism that controls the reservoir release.

They don't speak, or if they do, their speech is subsonic, part of that low frequency you feel rather than hear. Sometimes two maintainers will work near each other, and there's coordination between them—they'll adjust flows simultaneously, one upstream and one down, in ways that suggest communication you can't perceive. Their movements become synchronized briefly, then they separate again, each returning to solitary work.

Their relationship with the city is intimate, almost symbiotic. They touch it constantly, reading it, responding to its needs. When the breathing cycle peaks, they orient toward it, pause in their work, bodies still, as if listening. Their skin-pores open wider during the peaks, weeping more, joining the city in its exhalation. During the ebbs, they move more, work faster, as if taking advantage of the city's rest to perform their maintenance. They are precisely adapted to this place, shaped by it or for it across generations that have left them more suited to wet darkness than to any world that contains sun.

At depth, the maintainers are different. Not drastically—they're still recognizably the same beings—but changed by the deeper environment. Their skin is darker, almost black, harder to distinguish from the stone. Their eyes are larger still, occupying more of their face, adapted to even less light. They're taller, lankier, their movements more fluid, as if they're partly swimming even when walking through air. The ones at depth seem older, though age is hard to judge; there's a quality of patience about them that suggests centuries, millennia. They move more slowly, more deliberately, each gesture the culmination of vast consideration.

These deep maintainers work on larger structures, make more fundamental adjustments. You might see one standing before a building that's in the wrong orientation, placing their hands on its surface, and over the course of what might be hours, the building begins to shift, to rotate slowly back into proper alignment. The maintainer doesn't push or force—they seem to be asking, negotiating with the structure, reminding it of what it should be, and the building complies with the patience of stone that has learned to move.

## The Listeners

In the plazas, sometimes, there are others. They sit motionless beside the glowing pools, or they stand in the centers of the great empty spaces, alone or in small groups, positioned at specific points as if the location matters. They are human, or were human more recently than the maintainers. Their proportions are closer to normal, though they've begun the adaptation—slightly webbed fingers, eyes a bit too large, skin with that faint porosity that suggests generations in the wet darkness.

They wear robes, or something like robes—fabric that might have been fabric once but has become something else through prolonged exposure to this environment, stiff with mineral deposits in some places, soft as skin in others, impossible to distinguish from the body beneath. The robes are grey-black-green, the same palette as everything else here, and they hang heavy with water, dripping constantly.

The listeners don't maintain. They attend in a different way. They are still for hours, heads tilted at slight angles, bodies arranged in postures that suggest deep concentration. They're listening to the city's rhythm, to that sub-audible pulse from the depths, to harmonics in the water-flow that contain information. Their eyes are closed, or open but unfocused, seeing through rather than at. Their breathing synchronizes with the city's breathing—you can see their chests rise and fall with the same twenty-minute cycle.

Sometimes they move between stillnesses. One will rise, cross a terrace with deliberate steps, counting perhaps, measuring distance by some internal calculation, and stop at a new point, resume their listening. Others will converge on a single location, four or five of them, and stand in formation—geometric arrangements, triangles or pentagons, precisely spaced—and their listening intensifies. The air between them becomes visible somehow, thickens, and the pool-light bends toward them, and you can feel rather than hear that they're receiving something, downloading information from the city's depths, interpreting the pulse.

They acknowledge each other with the slightest gestures—a tilt of the head, a positioning of fingers that might be language—but they don't speak audibly. Their communication is in posture, in the angle of their stance, in the way they orient their bodies relative to each other and to the city's hidden geometry. After the listening sessions, they separate and walk away in different directions, each carrying whatever they've learned to somewhere else, distributing information through the city like a biological network.

The listeners are younger than the maintainers, you can sense this. They haven't been here as many generations. Their humanity is more accessible, less eroded by adaptation. Sometimes you catch expressions on their faces during the listening—concentration, yes, but also ecstasy, pain, confusion, wonder. They're still processing what this place is, what it asks of them. The maintainers are beyond that—they simply do. But the listeners are still learning, still interpreting, caught between what they were and what they're becoming.

At certain times—not predictable, but you recognize it when it happens—many listeners gather at one of the larger plazas. Dozens of them, arranged in complex patterns across the wet stone, each at a specific point in what must be a grid or mandala, some coordinate system mapped onto the city's surface. They stand in formation for the entire breathing cycle, perhaps longer. The water flowing through the channels around them begins to form those geometric patterns—spirals, cross-hatchings—more complex than usual, as if the listeners' presence is shaping it, or as if the city is writing to them in water-language.

During these gatherings, the sub-audible pulse becomes almost audible, comes up into the range where you can hear it as sound rather than just feeling—a deep thrumming, rhythmic but with complex variations, almost musical. The listeners sway slightly, all together, responding to fluctuations in the rhythm. Their robes move in ways that water-weight alone can't explain, shifting and flowing as if caught in a current, though the air is still.

When the session ends—and it ends suddenly, decisively, all at once—the listeners break formation and disperse immediately, walking away in all directions like a burst of shrapnel, each one carrying their piece of whatever message the city has transmitted. Within minutes, the plaza is empty again except for one or two maintainers who come to check that the water is flowing correctly after so many bodies have disturbed its paths.

## The Descended

Deeper in the basin, in the lower terraces where the moss grows in walls and the stalks hang thick, there are others who have gone further. The descended ones. You see them rarely—they don't come up to the higher levels, and pursuing them downward is difficult, disorienting. But sometimes at the edge of visibility, moving between moss-walls in the distance, you catch sight of them.

They're less human. The adaptations have progressed to the point of radical transformation. Their bodies are elongated, attenuated, stretched by the denser air and the deeper darkness into forms that are barely bipedal. Some move on all fours, or on six limbs—extra appendages that might have been arms or might be something new, grown or evolved or granted by prolonged exposure to whatever presence lives at the center. Their skin has gone beyond porosity into something else: it weeps constantly, profusely, almost melting, a thick film of moisture that makes their outlines uncertain, blurred.

Their eyes—when you can see them—are enormous, taking up half the face, and they glow faintly with their own light, the same cold luminescence as the pools. They see something you can't see. They navigate the deep levels with absolute certainty, moving through the complex maze of moss-walls and hanging stalks without hesitation, following paths that aren't visible, responding to cues that exist in spectrums you can't access.

The descended ones don't maintain or listen—they participate. They've become part of the city's function at some fundamental level. You might see one standing in a channel, water flowing through them rather than around them, their body somehow permeable, a living filter that the water passes through and emerges changed, more luminous. Or you might see one merged partially with a building, their body extending into the structure, interfacing with it, becoming temporarily architectural, serving as a conduit or valve or processing organ in the city's vast hydraulic system.

They make sounds, rarely. Soft vocalizations that aren't quite speech—melodic, liquid sounds, rising and falling in pitch, harmonizing with the water-flow and the hollow knocking of the stalks. The sounds might be communication, or they might be function, necessary components of whatever process they're engaged in. When multiple descended ones vocalize near each other, the sounds interweave in complex harmonies, and the city responds—the breathing cycle shifts slightly, the water flows faster or slower, the glow of the pools intensifies.

Sometimes a descended one will surface at a higher level, emerge from the depths for reasons unknowable. They seem disoriented there, too far from whatever anchors them below. They move erratically, touching walls, feeling channels, as if trying to remember something or re-establish connection. Maintainers will approach when this happens, guide the descended one carefully, walking beside them without touching, leading them back down to the deeper terraces where they disappear into the moss-labyrinths and resume whatever existence they have there.

There are stories among the listeners—communicated in gesture and posture when they gather—about what happens if you go too deep. About the final terraces where the descended ones are no longer mobile, where they've rooted into the architecture, become permanent parts of the infrastructure, their bodies transforming entirely into organic machinery, still alive but no longer individual, subsumed into the city's collective function. The stories suggest this is not death but transcendence, the completion of whatever journey brought anyone to this place. But they're stories shared with gestures that suggest both reverence and horror.

## The Architects

Very rarely, perhaps once in a span of time that feels like months, something else appears. You'll be walking the terraces, and you'll realize that in the plaza ahead, there's a figure that doesn't move like the others. Taller, more solid, less adapted. They stand with different posture—upright, square-shouldered, a bearing that suggests authority or origin or responsibility.

These are the architects, and they are human, completely human, unchanged by the environment. They wear actual clothing—practical garments in dark colors, dry somehow despite the omnipresent moisture, untouched by the adaptation that affects everything else here. Their skin is normal-textured, their eyes normal-sized. They carry tools sometimes, devices that look technological in ways nothing else here does—instruments for measuring, recording, analyzing.

They move through the city with the confidence of creators, examining buildings, studying channels, occasionally making notes on tablets that produce their own light. The maintainers acknowledge them with full attention—will stop their work and wait while an architect examines what they've done, will adjust things based on what seem to be instructions, though you never hear words exchanged. The listeners orient toward architects when they appear, their listening posture changing, becoming more alert, focused on the architect rather than on the city's depths.

The architects don't stay long. They appear, conduct their examination or adjustment or observation, and then they leave—walking up the terraces toward the basin's edge, climbing the featureless slope, disappearing into the darkness beyond. They never go down. They survey from above, check that the systems are functioning, ensure that the city continues to perform whatever role it was designed to perform, then return to wherever they come from outside this place.

When an architect appears, the city changes slightly. The breathing becomes more regular, more pronounced, as if it's demonstrating its function. The water flows more dramatically through the channels. The pools glow brighter. It's like watching someone wake up fully when a parent enters the room—not from sleep but from idle functioning into active demonstration. The city is showing off, or showing that it works, proving its worth to its creators.

The descended ones retreat when architects appear. You'll see them withdrawing into the depths, moving away from the higher terraces, as if they're not supposed to be seen, or as if they're ashamed of what they've become, or as if the architects represent something they've rejected by going deeper. The architects never pursue them, never examine the deep terraces at all. They stay on the upper and middle levels, where the city is most clearly city-like, where its functions are most comprehensible.

Once, you might witness an architect and a listener facing each other in a plaza. The architect speaks—actual audible speech in a language you don't recognize—and the listener responds not with words but with an elaborate series of gestures, finger positions, body orientations. The architect nods, understanding, makes notes, speaks again. The exchange continues for several minutes, information flowing both ways in asymmetric languages. When it ends, the listener bows—a gesture that seems imported from outside, not native to this place—and the architect acknowledges with a slight inclination of the head before walking away toward the upper terraces. The listener stands motionless for a long time afterward, processing, then suddenly moves, nearly runs, down toward the deeper levels, carrying whatever message was exchanged.

## The Others

There are presences here that might not be alive in any conventional sense, or might be alive in ways that don't require bodies, or might be the city itself achieving some distributed form of consciousness.

In certain plazas, always the same ones, there are concentrations of something. The air is different there—thicker, more resistant, as if you're pushing through invisible substance. The water forms more complex patterns in those places, geometric configurations that hold for minutes at a time. The pool-light is strange there, casting those wrong-shadows more pronouncedly, illuminating things that aren't present to be illuminated.

The maintainers avoid these plazas, route around them even when it means longer paths. The listeners will approach but won't enter—they stand at the edges, listening from a distance, and their faces show something you might interpret as fear or awe or both. Only the descended ones move through these spaces freely, and when they do, their bodies become even less distinct, almost translucent, as if they're halfway into some other state of matter.

In these plazas, you might sense something observing. Not with eyes—there are no eyes—but with attention, with awareness. The invisible thickness in the air feels cognitive, feels like it's considering you, measuring you, determining what you are and whether you belong. It's not hostile. It's not friendly. It's utterly alien, an intelligence so different from human consciousness that communication isn't even conceptually possible. You're being noticed by something that experiences reality through entirely different frameworks, for whom you might be as comprehensible as the city's breathing is to a bacterium.

Sometimes in these plazas, the pool-water shows things. Reflections that aren't reflections—images of other places, other basins perhaps, other cities of wet stone and strange light, populated by other variants of adapted beings. Glimpses of a network, a system, this city being one node among many, all breathing together, all processing water and darkness into something else, some product or function that exists on scales beyond human comprehension.

And beneath it all, generating the pulse, whatever lives in the unreachable depths. The thing all the water flows toward. The thing the descended ones are slowly merging with. The thing the city was built to house or serve or contain or become. It might be a presence, or a process, or an organism of such vast scale that the entire basin is merely one of its organs. The sub-audible rhythm you feel is its heartbeat, or its breath, or the turning of its thoughts, or the cycling of its digestion as it processes water and stone and time and the occasional human who wanders deep enough into something else, into the components of its incomprehensible metabolism.

The maintainers serve it, knowingly or unknowingly. The listeners interpret its transmissions, translate its needs into the adjustments the maintainers perform. The descended ones are joining it, becoming it, adding their consciousness or their biology or both to whatever collective or singular or beyond-singular thing it is. The architects monitor it, ensure it continues functioning within parameters, manage it from outside as one might manage any vast organism or system that must be kept alive but never fully understood.

And so the city persists, populated by these gradations of humanity and post-humanity and non-humanity, all organized around the maintenance of this breathing stone basin, this hydraulic cathedral, this living architecture that processes nothing into nothing in the wet darkness, serving purposes that might be profound or might be purposeless, might be cosmic engineering or might be the equivalent of digestion, taking in water and darkness and time and producing only more of itself, eternally, in the bottom of a basin that exists at the edge of what space can contain, what reality can accommodate.

The population is sparse, appropriate to the city's scale and emptiness. Perhaps hundreds across all the visible terraces, spread thin, each attending to their function in solitude or small groups. Enough to maintain but not to crowd. Enough to keep the channels clear, the flows correct, the breathing synchronized. Enough to listen and interpret and descend and merge. Enough to serve whatever this place is, was built to be, has become through the slow process of existing at the intersection of intention and entropy, where cities become organisms and organisms become cities and the beings between them gradually transform into whatever the environment demands, shedding humanity like water down stone, patient and persistent, until they've eroded into their final forms, their functional shapes, smooth and ancient and perfectly adapted to the wet breathing darkness that requires them and shapes them and slowly, slowly, transforms them into itself.
