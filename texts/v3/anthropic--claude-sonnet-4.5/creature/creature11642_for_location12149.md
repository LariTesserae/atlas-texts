# The Inhabitants

What lives here has forgotten surfaces, forgotten air, forgotten the upward direction as anything but a gradation of the same essential medium. They are water-born now, even those whose ancestors might have walked.

## The Gleaners

They are the city's breath—the constant, filtering presence that defines the baseline of existence here. Each gleaner is a living sieve, a disc of translucent gray tissue stretched across a frame of cartilage so fine it flexes with each pulse of their appendages. Those appendages—forty, fifty, perhaps sixty radiating from the body's rim—are not limbs but elaborate gills, feeding structures, and propulsion organs simultaneously. They beat in waves that travel around the disc's circumference, creating vortices that draw water through and down, through the feathery structures that trap anything solid, anything metabolizable.

They have no eyes. Light at this depth is too dim and too diffuse for vision to matter. Instead, their bodies read chemical gradients: the smell of decay, the signature of bacterial blooms, the traces left by other gleaners. They orient themselves by these invisible maps, congregating where food is densest, dispersing when resources thin. The dark spots visible through their translucent bodies are indeed organs—a simple gut that runs in a spiral from the ventral mouth to a dorsal vent, a cluster of what might be ganglia serving as a distributed nervous system, and strange crystalline structures whose function is unclear. Perhaps they sense vibration. Perhaps they regulate buoyancy. Perhaps they are simply accumulated minerals the gleaner cannot expel, growing slowly over a lifetime until they weight the creature down and it sinks, finally, to become part of the sediment it spent its life filtering.

They live perhaps two years, perhaps five. Time underwater is difficult to mark in bodies that never stop growing, never reach maturity in the sense of completion. A gleaner simply feeds until it cannot feed efficiently anymore—until its appendages become too worn, its body too heavy with mineral deposits, its reflexes too slow to maintain position in the water column. Then it descends to join the shells you find in corners, in the lee of walls, accumulating in drifts that crunch underfoot when disturbed.

They reproduce by broadcast spawning—hundreds of individuals releasing gametes simultaneously in response to some stimulus you cannot detect. Perhaps it is temperature. Perhaps it is a chemical signal passed through the aggregation. The water clouds with reproductive material, and for hours afterward the gleaners hang motionless, exhausted, their appendages barely moving. Then the current resumes, and they return to their eternal filtering, unaware that they have just created the next generation.

## The Wall-Crawlers

These are older, in evolutionary terms. Their body plan speaks of terrestrial ancestry—segmented, many-legged, built for surfaces rather than swimming. But they have adapted with the strange completeness of lineages trapped in changing circumstances. Their original exoskeleton has been partly replaced by a flexible integument embedded with mineral plates. Their breathing structures—once spiracles opening to air—have become elaborate gills folded into chambers along each segment, ventilated by the rhythmic compression of their body as they move.

Each crawler is perhaps thirty centimeters long when fully grown, though you rarely see them extended. They bunch and contract as they move, their segments telescoping, their legs touching and releasing in waves that ripple forward along the body. Those legs—twelve pairs, sometimes thirteen—end not in claws but in adhesive pads that secrete a substance allowing them to cling to vertical and even inverted surfaces. The pads leave marks: brief traces of organic glue that other crawlers can detect, creating invisible trails across walls and ceilings that serve as highways, territorial boundaries, mating routes.

They have eyes, or what passed for eyes before the drowning. Now they are simplified organs, sensitive only to the dimmest light, useful mainly for distinguishing up from down, open water from enclosed space. The crawlers navigate primarily by touch—elaborate antennae extending forward and to the sides, constantly tapping, sensing, reading the texture of surfaces. They can distinguish between types of biofilm by taste, moving toward the bacterial colonies they prefer, avoiding those they cannot digest.

Their mouthparts are complex and terrible: a ring of rasping structures that scrape in alternate rhythm, one set pulling up while the other pushes down, creating a grinding motion that removes biofilm in visible paths. Behind the scrapers are filters that separate organic matter from mineral, and behind those are plates that compress everything into dense fecal pellets, expelled from the rear in a trail that marks their passage. These pellets are not waste—they are too rich in nutrients. The gleaners consume them, and the fish-things, and even some crawlers, turning occasionally to feed on their own processed leavings.

They are territorial in a loose way. A crawler will defend a particularly rich section of wall, rearing up on its back legs and spreading its frontal segments in a display that makes it appear larger. Confrontations are ritualized—two crawlers facing each other, tapping antennae, assessing size and health. Usually the smaller retreats. Sometimes they fight, grappling with their legs, each trying to flip the other off the wall. Such fights are slow, almost gentle, and rarely result in injury. The loser simply moves to another section of wall, another building, where resources are marginally less abundant but competition is absent.

They reproduce by pairing. When a female is ready—her body swollen with eggs visible through her semi-translucent integument—she releases a pheromone that males can detect from surprising distances. They converge on her position, sometimes three or four males competing for access. She chooses based on criteria you cannot determine—size perhaps, or the quality of display, or simple persistence. The pair retreats to a sheltered location—a crack, a hollow, an interior room—and remains coupled for hours while sperm is transferred. Weeks later, she lays eggs in a protected crevice, secreting a gel that glues them to the substrate and provides initial nutrients. The eggs hatch into miniature versions of adults, skipping any larval stage, immediately beginning to graze on biofilm too thin for adults to bother with.

## The Serpent-Forms

These are the city's memory. They follow routes so old that the routes themselves have worn grooves in reality—channels of habit carved through water and time. Each serpent is an individual, but each is also the latest iteration of a behavior pattern that predates its birth, that will continue after its death, passed not through genetics but through the city's geography itself.

They are perhaps a meter and a half at full extension, though they rarely extend fully. Their normal posture is sinuous, curved, their bodies describing S-shapes that flow forward through coordinated muscle contractions. The paired fins along their length beat in precise sequence, creating thrust without turbulence, propelling them forward with an efficiency that seems too calculated for mere evolution. Their skin is thick, layered, more like hide than scales, and it sheds constantly—thin sheets of dead tissue peeling away, consumed by crawlers and gleaners before they can drift far.

Their heads are narrow, hydrodynamic, with mouths that open shockingly wide when feeding. The bristle-cage inside is not teeth but modified gill-rakers—hundreds of stiff, backward-pointing projections that allow water to pass but trap anything solid. They swim through gleaner aggregations with mouths open, filtering in bulk, consuming dozens of the disc-creatures in a single pass. The process is passive, almost contemptuous—no chase, no strategy, simply the opening of a mouth and the inevitable capture of whatever lies ahead.

But they are not always feeding. Most of their time is spent patrolling, following their inherited routes with such precision that you can predict their appearance. A particular serpent—you learn to distinguish them by scarring, by the exact pattern of their movement—passes through a specific intersection every day at approximately the same time. Another circles a plaza in clockwise loops, pausing at the north side for exactly long enough that you could count it, then continuing. These routes overlap, creating a network of serpent-presence that covers the city in shifts and cycles.

Are they intelligent? The question feels wrong. They are not solving problems, not adapting to new circumstances, not learning in any sense you recognize. But they are not mere automatons either. When a building partially collapses, blocking a traditional route, the serpents re-route within a day, finding new paths that accomplish the same territorial coverage. When a browser becomes trapped in a narrow space, serpents will investigate, circling the distressed creature before moving on—not helping, but acknowledging. When two serpents meet at an intersection, they perform a brief parallel swim, matching speeds and trajectories for several meters before diverging, a ritual of recognition or territorial respect.

They have eyes—large, dark, positioned laterally on their heads. These eyes work, truly work, even in the dim light. They watch you. They track movement, assess threats, identify prey. But what do they see? What does the city look like through those eyes? You suspect they see in longer timeframes than you do, that their vision integrates motion over minutes rather than seconds, rendering the city as a flow of patterns rather than a collection of objects.

They reproduce rarely, perhaps only every several years. Mating is brutal—two serpents entwining in open water, thrashing, creating turbulence that disturbs the gleaners for meters around. The female retains the fertilized eggs internally, and months later gives birth to live young—perfect miniatures that immediately swim away to establish their own routes, sometimes following their mother's path, sometimes creating new ones that over generations will become as worn and habitual as the old.

They die mysteriously. You find carcasses occasionally, serpents that have settled to the bottom and lie there decaying, their bodies already being processed by crawlers and gleaners. There is no visible injury, no sign of disease. They simply stop, as though a switch were thrown, and sink. Perhaps it is age. Perhaps it is a parasite that leaves no trace. Perhaps it is something like decision—the route completed enough times that continuation becomes unnecessary.

## The Fish-Things

They are the city's fear made flesh—shy, hidden, existing in the spaces between other lives. They are fish in the way that whales are fish, which is to say not at all, but the word serves because their body plan converges on piscine efficiency: streamlined, finned, scaled, built for moving through water at speed when speed is required.

They are small, hand-length, with bodies that have become increasingly translucent over generations of living in darkness. You can see their spines, their major organs, the dark line of their gut when they have recently fed. Their scales are reduced to a few patches along the lateral line, the rest of their skin bare and sensitive. They have lost melanin, lost coloration, become creatures of pearl and glass.

Their eyes are their most striking feature—enormous relative to body size, occupying perhaps a quarter of their head's volume, bulging slightly from their sockets. These eyes see differently than yours, tuned to different wavelengths, capable of functioning in light conditions where you perceive only darkness. They watch from the interior of buildings, from beneath overhangs, from cracks and crevices, their eyes reflecting any available light as brief green flashes that vanish the moment you turn toward them.

They school, but only in hiding. Inside a building you might find fifty of them moving in perfect synchrony, wheeling through the water in coordinated turns that suggest shared nervous systems. But let any disturbance occur—a serpent passing outside, a browser bumping the wall—and they scatter instantly, each finding its own hiding place, the school dissolving into individuals in heartbeats.

They eat opportunistically—gleaner corpses that drift into their refuges, fecal pellets from crawlers, fragments of vegetation torn loose by current. They are not hunters. They have no predatory adaptations beyond speed and the ability to snap their jaws on something that drifts past. Their role is to be eaten, to convert the city's diffuse nutrients into concentrated packages that serpents can consume efficiently. They are the middle term, the necessary conversion between detritus and apex predator.

Their reproduction is the city's most beautiful secret. Once—perhaps every year, perhaps every several years, following rhythms that have nothing to do with seasons this deep—they emerge. Hundreds, thousands of fish-things swimming from their refuges simultaneously, filling the water between buildings with silver bodies, creating clouds of coordinated movement that rival the gleaner aggregations in density. They swim upward, toward the surface they never reach, and as they swim they spawn, releasing gametes in trails that catch the ambient light and glow faintly, making the water briefly luminous with reproduction.

The display lasts perhaps an hour. Then, as suddenly as they emerged, they return to hiding, exhausted, many dying from the exertion. The fertilized eggs sink slowly, settling in sediment, in corners, in the cracks where fish-things shelter. They hatch weeks later into larvae so small they are nearly invisible, translucent and helpless, prey for crawlers and gleaners until they grow large enough to swim competently, to understand fear, to join the hidden schools.

## The Browsers

These are the city's transformation embodied. They eat stone and excrete stone, the architecture passing through their bodies and emerging changed, smoothed, integrated into their biology and returned to the walls in new forms. They are the largest fauna here, and the strangest, and in some ways the most important because they are the only creatures actively reshaping the habitat itself.

They are massive—a browser might weigh two hundred kilograms, perhaps more. Their bodies are roughly cubic, blocky, built low to the ground on six thick legs. Those legs are stumpy but powerful, each ending in a broad foot that distributes weight across the sediment-covered floors. Their movement is slow but inexorable—when a browser decides to move, obstacles cease to be obstacles. You have seen them walk through weakened walls, shouldering through brick that has become soft with water, leaving browser-sized holes that subsequently become passages for other fauna.

Their shells are remarkable—overlapping plates of material that is part bone, part mineral, part something else entirely. The plates grow from the skin and then mineralize, accumulating deposits from the water, becoming thicker and more elaborate over time. Old browsers carry veritable mountains on their backs, their shells so encrusted with accretions that they trail streamers of algae, that smaller creatures—crawlers primarily—live permanently among the crevices, grazing on the biofilm that grows on the browser's shell while the browser grazes on buildings.

Their heads are broad and flat, with mouths that occupy almost the entire ventral surface. The mouth is a complicated organ: a flexible rim that seals against surfaces, creating suction, and inside that rim a field of chitinous teeth arranged in rotating rows, like a slow-motion chainsaw that grinds rather than cuts. They press their mouths to walls and begin to rasp, the teeth scraping away surface layers, the motion accompanied by that low groaning that carries through water and stone both.

What they eat is the calcareous deposits, primarily—the travertine sleeves around columns, the accretions on lintels and thresholds, the mineral build-up in every sheltered corner. But they also consume the stone itself when the deposits are insufficient. You watch them rasp through limestone, through softened brick, through the mortar between stones, removing material in visible quantities. Their digestive systems are harsh environments—strongly acidic, filled with grinding stones they swallow deliberately to aid breakdown. The stone is partly dissolved, partly mechanically pulverized, and the minerals are absorbed with startling efficiency.

What emerges from their posterior is pellets of compressed material, still stone but transformed. The calcium they cannot absorb, the silicates they cannot process, the iron and trace minerals that pass through unused—all compressed into dense nodules the size of your fist, sometimes larger. These pellets accumulate in piles behind browsers, marking their feeding sites. Over time, gleaners and crawlers break them down, bacteria infiltrate them, they slowly disaggregate and rejoin the sediment, but in the meantime they create a different kind of substrate, smooth and rounded where the city's original paving was angular and flat.

They are long-lived—a browser might exist for fifty years, perhaps a century. Their shells show this longevity, built up in layers like tree rings, each layer representing years of growth, each surface texture recording the conditions of a period. An old browser's shell is a history of the city's water chemistry, readable if you knew the language.

They navigate by memory and by sensing routes worn smooth by generations of their ancestors. The same paths, repeated endlessly, until the stones themselves are polished, until the corners of buildings are worn round, until doorways become arches simply from the passage of massive bodies. They learn their circuits as juveniles, following adults, and they maintain those circuits for life, becoming fixtures of specific districts, recognized presences that other fauna orient around.

They are largely solitary, but not territorial. When two browsers meet on a route, they simply maneuver around each other, sometimes pausing to touch briefly—foreheads or shells pressed together for a moment—before continuing. Perhaps this touch is communication. Perhaps it is simply acknowledgment. You cannot tell.

They mate rarely, and the process is almost absurdly difficult given their body plans. The male must climb onto the female's back, his weight pressing her down, his legs scrabbling for purchase on her shell. The act takes hours, both browsers motionless throughout except for the male's subtle movements. Afterward, they separate and return to their individual routes, showing no further interest in each other.

The female carries the eggs internally for months, and when she is ready to lay them she seeks out a specific kind of location—a small room, enclosed, sheltered, with access to sediment. She excavates a depression with her front legs, lays perhaps twenty eggs, each the size of a human fist, and then she leaves. No parental care. No guarding. The eggs hatch months later into miniature browsers that immediately begin rasping at the walls of their birth-chamber, feeding and growing in isolation until they are large enough to venture out and join the city's traffic.

They die slowly. You can observe browsers in decline—their rasping becoming less frequent, their movement along routes slowing, their feeding sessions shorter. They seem to know when the end approaches, because they seek out secluded spaces, interior rooms, places away from the main thoroughfares. They settle there and simply stop, their bodies becoming still, their shells becoming landmarks, eventually becoming substrate themselves as mineral deposits entomb them and crawlers graze them and the city slowly reclaims their mass.

## The Hidden Ones

There are other lives here, less visible, less understood. You sense them rather than see them. In the deepest basements, in the spaces beneath collapsed floors, something lives that you have not directly observed. You know it exists because of signs: scrape marks too large for crawlers, too deliberate for browsers. Passages burrowed through sediment, shored up with fragments of stone arranged in ways that suggest purpose. Occasionally, in the deepest darkness, you hear sounds—clicking, scraping, rhythmic patterns that might be feeding or might be communication.

These creatures are shy of even the dim light that reaches most of the city. They live in complete darkness, navigating by touch and perhaps by echolocation—those clicking sounds might serve that purpose. You have found their molts occasionally: cast-off exoskeletons that suggest something like a crustacean body plan, jointed and segmented, with legs that end in claws rather than pads. But the molts are always fragmentary, broken, as though deliberately destroyed to conceal information.

They might be large. The passages they leave are wide enough to swim through. The scrape marks suggest considerable strength. But they might also be numerous and small, achieving together what a single large creature could achieve alone. The evidence is ambiguous, and they are careful in ways that suggest either high intelligence or very refined instinct.

You do not seek them out. Something about those deep spaces discourages investigation. Perhaps it is simply that the darkness becomes total, that navigation becomes impossible. Perhaps it is that the hidden ones actively discourage intrusion—blocking passages, creating dead-ends, making exploration more trouble than it is worth. They remain in their depths, occasionally sending evidence of their existence up into the city proper, but otherwise maintaining their privacy.

## The Very Small

And of course there are lives you can barely perceive—the true microscopic fauna that inhabit every surface, every volume of water, every interior space of every organism. Bacteria in communities so dense they create visible films. Protozoa grazing those films, themselves grazed by larger organisms. Rotifers and tardigrades and creatures without names, living in the boundary layers, in the micro-environments between sand grains, in the mucus coatings on crawler legs.

These microscopic lives are not separate from the larger ecosystem. They are its foundation, its engine. They break down the truly recalcitrant materials—the structural proteins in dead tissue, the complex carbohydrates in the sparse vegetation, the minerals that nothing else can process. They release nutrients in forms that can be absorbed, used, built into new life. They are the city's decomposition and its renewal, happening constantly and invisibly at scales that matter more than visibility suggests.

They bloom periodically—conditions aligning such that one species population explodes, turning the water cloudy with their numbers, tinting it colors that have no business in this depth: rust red, pale green, milky white. These blooms last days or weeks, then collapse as their nutrients are exhausted or as their predators catch up. The gleaners feed heavily during blooms, their filtering capturing the microscopic bodies in numbers that must be staggering.

## The Collective

What you gradually realize is that the fauna do not live in the city. They are the city, as much as the buildings are. The architecture shapes their behavior, their evolution, their population dynamics. Their presence in turn shapes the architecture, wearing it down, building it up, turning it into something that no longer exists in any human category but instead occupies a space between artificial and natural, between city and reef, between ruin and living system.

The browsers eat buildings but their excretions become substrate. The crawlers graze biofilm but their fecal trails feed gleaners. The serpents predate gleaners but their movements create currents that distribute nutrients. The fish-things hide and breed and die, converting energy from one form to another. The microscopic communities do their invisible work, making everything possible. Even the hidden ones in their depths serve some role—processing what falls beyond the light, returning nutrients in forms that eventually rise, re-enter the cycle.

It is not conscious. It is not planned. But it is complete. The system sustains itself, requires nothing from outside except the slow rain of matter from the distant surface and the warmth rising from below. Everything else is circulation, transformation, energy and material moving through various forms but never leaving, never lost, always available to become something else.

You are not part of this system. You move through it, observe it, but you do not contribute and you do not consume. You are a ghost, a witness, present but not integrated. The fauna tolerate you because you do not disturb the balance. The browsers do not graze on you. The serpents do not predate you. The crawlers do not compete with you. You exist adjacent to the system, relevant to it only as an observer is relevant to the observed—changing it minimally through the act of attention, but not changing it fundamentally.

In time, you will leave or you will dissolve or you will become something that can integrate. But the city will remain, and its inhabitants will continue their ancient patterns, their slow transformations, their perpetual work of being alive in a place that drowned but did not die, that instead became something else, something patient and strange and ultimately indifferent to everything except its own continuation.
