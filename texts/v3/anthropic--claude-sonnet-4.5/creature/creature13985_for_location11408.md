# The Inhabitants of the Breathing Continent

## THE SOUND-RIDERS

They are the most numerous, and they are what makes the sound *sound*.

The **thrummers** are beetle-analogues, though that word flattens them into something too familiar. They range from the size of a rice grain to the size of a closed fist, with carapaces that have evolved into resonating chambers. Their wing-cases are ridged like vinyl records, and when they vibrate them—which is constantly—they produce pure tones that range from subsonic pulses you feel in your sternum to high whistles at the edge of human hearing. They don't fly so much as drift, their wing-beats too slow to seem adequate for the task, yet they hang in the air in clouds of thousands, tens of thousands, creating harmonic interference patterns that shift as you move through them.

Each thrummer produces a single note, unchanging throughout its life, determined by the size and shape of its resonating chamber. But they cluster by frequency, forming living chords. Walk through a cloud of thrummers and you're walking through music that has no composer, no intention, that exists simply because these creatures exist. Their larvae bore into the plant matter, feeding on the compressed detritus below the surface mat, silent for the months of their development. When they emerge and their carapaces harden, they join the chorus. They live for weeks in their adult form, mating in mid-air (the frequency changes slightly during copulation, creating brief dissonances in the chord), then dying and falling to feed the next generation.

The **shriek-wings** are their predators, dragonfly-like but heavier-built, with bodies the length of your forearm and wingspans that can reach two meters. They are not elegant fliers—they move in straight lines with sharp, angular turns, more like drones than like insects. Their wings beat in patterns that create percussive, irregular clicks, adding syncopation to the thrummers' drone. They hunt by sound, able to isolate individual frequencies in the chaos and track them to their source. When they strike, it's with sudden accelerations that seem physically improbable—one moment hovering, the next slamming into a thrummer with enough force that you can hear the impact crack even through the background roar.

Their eyes are compound but enormous, taking up most of their heads, with a reflective quality that makes them seem to glow in low light. They're territorial in strange ways—not defending areas but defending *frequencies*. A shriek-wing will chase off another shriek-wing whose wing-beat pattern is too similar to its own, but ignore one whose sound is different. The result is that they distribute themselves acoustically rather than spatially, creating a three-dimensional map of sound-territories across the landscape.

The **drummers** are ground-dwellers, things that might be crickets if crickets evolved toward crab-like forms. They have six legs but use only four for locomotion; the front pair has evolved into hammers—chitinous clubs with flattened striking surfaces. They dig shallow burrows in the plant mat, barely more than scrapes, and position themselves with their hammers over resonant surfaces: hollow root-structures, dried seed pods, their own carapaces which are ridged and tuned. Then they drum.

The rhythm is not random. Watch a drummer for an hour and you'll see patterns emerge—accelerations and decelerations, rests and fills, call-and-response with other drummers in the vicinity. Whether this is communication or simply emergent behavior from simple rules is unclear. What's clear is that they're loud. A single drummer can be heard from fifty meters away. A group of them can create pressure waves you feel in your chest.

They're about the size of your hand, colored in browns and grays that make them nearly invisible against the plant mat until they move. They're skittish despite their noise—the slightest disturbance sends them scrambling into the vegetation, though they resume drumming within minutes. Their diet is detritus and fungus, scraped from the plant mat with mandibles that work constantly when they're not drumming. They're preyed upon by nearly everything larger than themselves, but their numbers remain constant. For every one taken, two more seem to appear.

## THE GRAZERS

The **root-pigs** are probably mammals, though their hair is strange—thick and waxy, more like plant fiber than fur, colored in greens and browns that make them difficult to spot when they're still. They're roughly the size of large dogs, though their proportions are wrong for that comparison: too long in the body, too short in the leg, with snouts that are prehensile and constantly moving, exploring, tasting the air.

They travel in sounders of eight to twenty individuals, moving through the plant mat with surprising grace despite their bulk. Their feet are broad and slightly webbed, distributing weight to prevent breaking through the surface. They're adapted to both water and land—you see them swimming through the larger pools with undulating motions, then climbing out to graze on the plant mat without pause.

What they eat is the mat itself. Their teeth are flat grinders, continuously growing to compensate for the wear from their diet. They bite into the compressed plant matter and chew methodically, processing enormous quantities to extract the nutrients. Their digestion is slow and thorough—they have multiple stomach chambers and practice coprophagy, eating their own partially-digested feces to extract every possible calorie. As a result, their waste is nearly inert, compressed pellets that look more like wood than dung.

They're vocal in ways that suggest complex social organization. They grunt, squeal, produce low rumbling sounds that carry across distance. When one sounds an alarm—a sharp, repeated bark—the entire sounder freezes, then moves as a unit toward cover. But they also seem to have different calls for different situations: food-finding, water-location, mating interest, territorial boundary. Listen long enough and you start to recognize individuals by their voices.

Their intelligence is evident in their problem-solving. Watch them approach a pool and they'll send one individual first to test the depth and stability. Watch them encounter a spine-forest and they'll search along the perimeter until they find a gap rather than forcing through. Watch them interact with humans and they'll maintain a specific distance—close enough to observe, far enough to flee—adjusting constantly as you move.

They're preyed upon, but they're not easy prey. The sounders defend their young aggressively, forming circles with the adults facing outward, using their considerable bulk and their tusks (shortened, blunt things that jut from their upper jaws) to threaten. They've been seen killing mid-sized predators who pressed attacks too far.

The **leaf-browsers** are more clearly mammalian—quadrupeds with long necks and longer tongues, built for reaching the higher plant structures. They're tall and spindly, almost absurdly proportioned, like someone tried to remember a giraffe and got the details wrong. Their legs seem too thin to support them, yet they move with careful, deliberate grace, placing each foot with precision.

They're solitary or travel in pairs—a mother with nearly-grown offspring, or two adults in the brief period of mating partnership. They're silent most of the time, producing sound only in distress or during mating season, when the males produce a low, resonant humming that carries for kilometers. It's not particularly loud, but it has a quality that cuts through the background roar, making it distinctly noticeable.

Their diet is the new growth at the tops of plant structures—the tender shoots and unfurling leaves that are less fibrous and more nutritious than the older material below. They can reach heights of four or five meters when they stretch their necks, and their tongues extend another meter beyond that, prehensile and covered with small hooks that strip leaves from branches. They feed almost constantly, processing material through their long digestive systems as they move.

They're beautiful in motion—slow and balletic, each step considered, neck curving to investigate a potential food source before committing to the reach. But they're fragile. A broken leg is a death sentence, and they seem to know it. They avoid unstable ground, test surfaces before committing their weight, flee at the first sign of danger rather than standing their ground.

The **tunnel-runners** live primarily below the surface mat, in the spaces between the living roots and the compressed detritus. They're rodent-like but larger—the size of house cats—with vestigial eyes that suggest recent evolutionary pressure away from surface living. Their fur is sparse and pale, their ears are enormous, and their front feet are digging implements with claws like small spades.

They emerge onto the surface primarily at night, though you sometimes see them in the deep shade beneath canopy-creatures during the day. When they surface, they move in rapid, jerking motions—a few steps, freeze, listen, a few more steps. They're nervous, constantly alert, their heads swiveling to track sounds. Which makes sense, because they're prey for nearly everything.

What they eat is the fungal networks that thread through the mat below, and the seeds and tubers of the plants above. They're cache-makers, storing food in chambers they excavate in the mat, returning to these stores over time. They're also surprisingly social—their tunnels connect to form vast networks, and you can sometimes hear them calling to each other below ground, high-pitched chirps that seem to carry information about food sources or dangers.

Their population density is enormous. Dig into the mat almost anywhere and you'll find their tunnels within a meter. At night, the surface can seem to crawl with them as dozens emerge to forage in a relatively small area. But they're so thoroughly adapted to being prey that their survival strategy is simply overwhelming fecundity—they breed constantly, producing litters of six to eight young every few weeks. Most die before reaching adulthood, but enough survive to maintain the population.

## THE HUNTERS

The **strike-cats** are the most commonly seen predators, though "commonly" is relative. You might encounter one every few days of travel, often just a glimpse—a shape moving through the vegetation at the edge of vision, or eyes reflecting in the twilight, or the aftermath of a kill.

They're ambush predators, built low and powerful, with muscle-heavy forequarters and proportionally small hindquarters. They don't chase so much as explode—lying in wait near game trails or water sources, then launching attacks that cover three or four meters in a single bound. Their forelimbs end in retractable claws that are genuinely frightening—curved, serrated on the inner edge, clearly evolved for gripping and holding struggling prey.

They're solitary and territorial, marking their ranges with scent (musky and acrid, noticeable from tens of meters away) and with scratch-marks on prominent plant structures. The territories are large—several square kilometers each—and seem to be respected by other strike-cats. When territories overlap, which happens during mating season, the resulting fights are brief and vicious. You find the evidence: patches of fur, blood on the plant mat, sometimes bodies.

What they hunt primarily are the root-pigs and the leaf-browsers, though they'll take tunnel-runners opportunistically and have been seen bringing down fauna species not listed here. They're intelligent hunters, able to identify the weak or injured in a group, to predict movement patterns, to use terrain for concealment. Watch one stalk and you see decision-making—constant re-evaluation of approach vectors, patience when patience is required, explosive action when the moment arrives.

They're not particularly aggressive toward humans, but they're not safe either. They seem to categorize humans as too large and too unpredictable to be worth the risk, and they'll usually abandon an area when humans enter. But starving strike-cats or those defending kills have been known to attack. The attacks are not tentative—if a strike-cat decides you're prey, it commits fully. The survival rate of these encounters, based on the remnants found near kill-sites, is poor.

The **sky-rippers** are the largest flying predators—if they can be called flying. They're too heavy for sustained flight, and their wings seem inadequate for their body mass. Yet they manage, using a combination of wing-beats and gliding, gaining altitude in thermal updrafts over the plant masses and then ranging out in long, descending searches.

They're built like aerial artillery—massive bodies, three meters from beak-tip to tail, with wingspans approaching five meters. Their heads are disproportionately large, housing brains that suggest considerable intelligence and eyes that face forward for binocular vision. Their beaks are hooked and sharp, designed for tearing rather than pecking. Their feet have three forward-facing talons and one rear-facing, all of them the length of human fingers and clearly capable of puncturing flesh.

What they hunt are the smaller fauna on the surface—tunnel-runners primarily, but also young root-pigs, injured leaf-browsers, anything that can't reach cover quickly enough. Their hunting strategy is patient circling followed by sudden stoops, dropping from height with wings folded, striking with enough force to stun or kill on impact. They've been seen knocking adult root-pigs unconscious with a well-placed strike, then tearing through the skull to reach the brain.

They're vocal in flight—harsh, repeated calls that might be territorial markers or communication with mates (they seem to hunt in pairs during breeding season). The calls are loud enough to be heard over the background roar, and they create temporary silences in the fauna below. Everything recognizes this sound and responds to it.

They nest on the tops of the tallest plant structures—the vine-towers and canopy-creatures—in massive constructions of branches and plant matter. These nests accumulate over years, becoming permanent fixtures, with bones and refuse piling up below them. The smell around these nest-sites is distinctive—carrion and guano and the acrid scent of the birds themselves.

The **night-takers** are rarely seen, which is a mercy because seeing them is generally bad news. They're nocturnal and almost completely silent—you don't hear them approach, don't hear them move. What you hear is their call, which happens after they've made a kill: a descending series of hoots that starts almost conversational and drops into subsonic rumbling that you feel more than hear.

They're probably mammalian, though in the darkness and in brief encounters it's hard to be certain. They're large—masses similar to adult humans—and they move on four legs but can rear up on two. Their eyes reflect in the bioluminescent glow, showing positions and movement before the animals themselves are visible. They seem to have good color vision despite being nocturnal, able to avoid certain bioluminescent fungi that are toxic while targeting others that mark the presence of prey.

What they hunt is not entirely clear. They've been known to take root-pigs and tunnel-runners, but these seem almost too small for them. They've been found near the remnants of leaf-browser kills, though whether they made the kills or scavenged them is uncertain. There are stories—from the rare humans willing to talk about this place—of night-takers taking humans, though the evidence for this is ambiguous. What's not ambiguous is that they're willing to approach human campsites in darkness, to test the boundaries of firelight, to take objects left at the periphery.

Their intelligence seems high. They've been seen working in coordination—two or three individuals approaching from different angles, one creating a disturbance while another attacks. They've been seen using tools in a basic sense—breaking branches to create noise as a distraction, rolling stones to test the stability of surfaces before crossing. They avoid the same areas where they've encountered resistance, suggesting memory and learning.

## THE ARCHITECTS

The **weavers** are the plant-structure builders, though calling them builders anthropomorphizes what might be instinct. They're insectoid, social, living in colonies that can number in the hundreds of thousands. Individually they're small—about the size of a thumb—with six limbs and mandibles that work like scissors and needle both.

What they do is harvest plant fibers—stripping them from certain species, processing them through their digestive systems to create a kind of silk, then weaving these fibers into structures. The structures start small—a few centimeters across, woven around a plant stem—but they grow. The colony adds to them constantly, layer upon layer, creating walls and chambers and passages. After years of construction, these can become the size of small buildings, incorporated into the plant structures themselves, almost indistinguishable from the natural growth.

The architecture is complex: multiple chambers with different functions (nurseries, food storage, waste management), passages that allow air flow, external walls that shed water while internal walls absorb it to maintain humidity. The structures have entrances too small for most predators but large enough for the weavers to enter and exit. Around these entrances, guard-weavers station themselves, able to release chemical alarm signals if threatened.

The colonies are eusocial—clear divisions between workers, guards, and reproductive individuals. The queen (there's usually only one) is larger than the others, rarely seen outside the deepest chambers, constantly tended by workers who feed her and remove her eggs for placement in the nurseries. The workers are sterile but not passive—they seem to make decisions collectively, responding to chemical signals that coordinate their behavior without requiring central control.

What they eat is fungus primarily, cultivated inside their structures in specialized chambers. They harvest plant matter, process it partially, and inoculate it with fungal spores. The fungus grows in the controlled environment of the chamber, and the weavers harvest it, feeding it to the larvae and to each other. It's agriculture, essentially—not conscious agriculture, but the functional equivalent.

They're not aggressive unless their structures are directly threatened. Disturb a structure and the guards emerge, releasing alarm pheromones that bring more guards. They can bite, and the bites are painful, and they seem to target specific areas—eyes, ears, lips—as if they've learned where humans (and other predators) are most vulnerable. But leave the structures alone and the weavers ignore you, going about their constant work, their endless building.

The **cultivators** are mammals—possibly primates, though their body plan has diverged enough that the relationship is questionable. They're bipedal, standing perhaps a meter tall, with long arms that reach nearly to the ground. Their hands have opposing digits—not quite thumbs, but functional enough for manipulation. They're covered in short, dense fur colored in patterns that break up their outline: dark and light patches that make them difficult to see in the dappled light beneath canopy-structures.

What makes them notable is their relationship with certain plant species. They're not domesticating these plants, exactly, but they're managing them. Watch a group of cultivators and you'll see them harvesting seeds from preferred varieties, cracking them open to eat some and burying others in specific locations. You'll see them clearing competing plants from around their preferred species, creating small gardens that they return to regularly. You'll see them pruning growth in ways that increase fruit or seed production.

They're omnivorous, eating plant matter primarily but supplementing with insects, eggs, and occasionally small fauna they catch or scavenge. But the bulk of their diet comes from the plants they tend—high-calorie seeds and fruits that are larger and more abundant in their managed areas than in the wild-growing equivalents.

They're social, living in groups of ten to thirty individuals with complex hierarchies. The hierarchies aren't based on size or aggression but seem to involve memory and knowledge—older individuals who remember where resources are located, who have learned which plants are nutritious and which are toxic, who can navigate the landscape efficiently. These individuals are deferred to, given choice portions of food, protected by the group.

Their communication is primarily gestural—hand signals and body postures—but they also vocalize. The vocalizations are varied: soft hoots for contact maintenance, sharp barks for alarm, loud calls that seem territorial. Some of the calls sound almost like language—patterned and complex enough that they might be conveying specific information rather than just emotional states. But no structure has been identified, no grammar confirmed.

They're tool-users in basic ways: breaking off branches to use as reaching sticks, using stones to crack open hard seed pods, using large leaves as umbrellas during rain. The tool use is not sophisticated—no modification of materials, no tool-making in the sense of creating implements for later use. But it's flexible and appropriate, suggesting an ability to perceive problems and improvise solutions.

Their relationship with humans is cautious. They'll watch from a distance, sometimes following human movement through their range. They'll investigate human campsites after humans leave, examining what was left behind, occasionally taking objects (though seemingly at random—a piece of rope one time, a ceramic shard another, with no obvious pattern to what interests them). They've been known to leave things too—arrangements of stones or sticks that might be communication attempts or might be meaningless.

## THE DECOMPOSERS

The **rot-crawlers** are the cleanup crew, though "crawler" undersells their variety. They're arthropods, broadly speaking, but they come in forms adapted to every niche of decomposition. There are species that specialize in fresh carrion, arriving within minutes of death to begin processing. There are species that specialize in dried tissue, in bones, in the final stages where remains are barely recognizable. There are species that process plant matter, others that process animal waste, still others that seem to eat the decay itself—the bacterial blooms and fungal networks that form in rotting material.

They range in size from nearly microscopic to hand-sized, and they're everywhere. Turn over any piece of the plant mat and you'll find dozens. Examine any carcass and they're already at work, in the process of reducing complexity to its chemical components. They're efficient—a root-pig carcass that would seem like it should remain for weeks is stripped to bones in days, and the bones themselves are gradually broken down by species that bore into them for the minerals.

Many are bioluminescent—not brightly, but enough that the plant mat glows faintly at night from the collective light of millions of them going about their work. The color varies by species: blue-green, yellow-green, occasionally orange or red. The patterns move across the landscape like slow constellations, brighter in areas of recent death, dimmer where everything has already been processed.

They're not dangerous to living creatures, mostly—their mandibles and digestive systems are adapted for material that can't fight back. But they're oppressive in their ubiquity. You can't escape them. They're in your sleeping area, in your food storage, investigating your body while you sleep. They're not harmful, just present, always present, a constant reminder that decomposition is the default state and life is the temporary exception.

The **fungal blooms** are not fauna but deserve mention for their active role. The fungi here are diverse and aggressive, filling roles that in other ecosystems might be filled by animals. There are predatory fungi—species that form sticky networks on the undersides of leaves to trap the smaller insectoid fauna, then digest them slowly. There are parasitic fungi that infect living tissue, growing inside animals or plants, fruiting from their bodies in grotesque displays.

The most dramatic are the **mind-changers**—fungi that infect the nervous systems of certain fauna species, altering their behavior to serve the fungus's reproductive needs. An infected tunnel-runner will climb to a high point on a plant structure, something it would never normally do, and remain there until the fungus fruits from its head, releasing spores from an elevated position for better dispersal. An infected drummer will drum continuously, far longer than normal, attracting predators—which eat it and become the next generation of hosts for the fungus.

The fruiting bodies come in forms: massive shelf-fungi projecting from the plant structures like architectural elements, delicate lattices that glow in the dark, gelatinous blobs that pulse with what looks like breathing, tall stalks topped with caps that release visible clouds of spores when disturbed. Some are toxic enough to kill. Some are hallucinogenic enough to make death seem irrelevant. Some are edible and nutritious and represent significant portions of the available food.

## THE EDGE-CASES

There are fauna that don't fit categories, that seem like evolutionary experiments still in progress or things left over from previous states of the ecosystem.

The **shimmer-jellies** drift through the air like thoughts, translucent bodies catching the light, tentacles trailing below. They're not quite gelatinous, not quite solid—their bodies seem to phase between states. They're usually solitary, occasionally clustering in groups of three or four. What they eat is unclear. They're sometimes seen near flowering plant structures, their tentacles brushing against the blooms, but whether they're pollinating or feeding or doing something else entirely is unknown. They don't appear to have mouths. They don't produce waste that's ever been observed. They might photosynthesize—their bodies are pale but tinged with green. Or they might filter-feed on the smaller airborne fauna. Or they might exist on some process that's not analogous to anything familiar.

The **bone-singers** are found near the remnant buildings, near the path-markers, near any place where humans have left traces. They're avian—probably—with bodies about the size of ravens but proportioned differently, with longer necks and shorter tails. Their feathers are black but with an iridescent quality that shows deep blue and purple in certain lights. They're named for their calls: complex, melodic sounds that incorporate rhythms and pitches that seem too structured to be instinctive. Some of the calls sound like they're mimicking human speech—not words exactly, but the cadence and tone of human voices.

They collect objects—anything unusual or out of place—and arrange them in patterns near their nests. The patterns are geometric: straight lines, circles, spirals. Sometimes they incorporate the carved markers that humans have left, moving them from their original positions to fit into the bone-singers' arrangements. The purpose is unknown. Mating displays? Territorial markers? Art? They defend these arrangements aggressively, diving at anything that approaches too closely, calling in alarm until others arrive to join the defense.

The **depth-swimmers** are known primarily from sounds that emerge from the holes—the circular openings that descend into darkness. The sounds are complex: splashing, vocalizations that range from grunts to something almost like singing, occasionally rhythmic impacts as if something is striking the walls. Once in a long while, you see movement in the water at the bottom—shapes that might be scaled, might be furred, that surface briefly and then disappear.

The few attempts to descend and investigate have ended badly enough that details are scarce. What's known: they're large, at least human-sized. They're adapted to aquatic environments but can move on land—there are tracks sometimes in the mud near hole-entrances, prints that show webbed feet or flippers, claw-marks on the stone lining. They seem to be territorial about the holes, defending them from intrusion. They might be intelligent—the vocalizations have structure, and the rhythmic sounds might be tool use or communication or something else.

## THE HUMANS

They are here, but they are sparse—so few that you might travel for weeks without encountering another person. They move through the landscape in small groups: usually three to eight individuals, occasionally alone, rarely more than a dozen. They're not native to this place, or if they are, they've been here so long that any origins have been forgotten. They don't speak of coming from anywhere else. They don't speak much at all, to outsiders.

They're adapted physically—darker-skinned regardless of what their ancestry might have been, lean and efficient in their movements, scarred in ways that suggest intimate familiarity with the dangers here. Their clothing is simple: woven plant fiber mostly, sometimes leather from fauna they've killed, always in greens and browns that make them difficult to spot. They go often without shoes, their feet calloused thick enough to handle the plant mat's surface.

Their technology has been described—stone, wood, plant fiber, basic ceramics. What hasn't been described is their knowledge. They know this place in ways that seem impossible. They know which plants are safe to eat and which will kill. They know where to find water when the pools have dried. They know how to read the fauna's movements to predict predator locations. They know the markers, know how to navigate by them, know what the carved patterns mean (though they won't explain, or can't—the language barrier is real, and their languages are tonal and complex, full of sounds that don't map to familiar phonemes).

They practice a kind of nomadic existence, moving regularly but along established routes. They have seasonal camps in the more permanent structures, returning to them at predictable times. They have territories, though the boundaries are fluid and seem to be more about courtesy than enforcement. They'll share space with other groups peacefully, though they maintain distance, communicate primarily through gesture and exchange of small gifts.

Their relationship with the landscape is neither reverent nor exploitative—it's practical. They use what they need. They waste nothing. They don't seem to have conservation ethics in a conscious sense, but their population is small enough and their practices sustainable enough that the impact is minimal. They take eggs from nests but leave some. They harvest plants but don't clear areas. They hunt but don't overhunt.

They have something that might be religion or might be elaborate ecological knowledge encoded in ritual. They leave offerings at the path-markers: food, carved objects, woven items. They perform ceremonies at certain times—moon-phases, perhaps, or seasonal markers—that involve rhythmic movement, group vocalizations, sometimes the consumption of substances that alter consciousness (mushrooms, certain plant preparations). Whether these are worship or pharmacological or social bonding or all of the above is unclear to observers.

They're not hostile to outsiders but they're not welcoming either. They seem to evaluate newcomers over time, watching from a distance, occasionally approaching to trade or to offer guidance. The guidance is always practical—how to find water, which direction to travel to reach certain landmarks, warnings about predators or unstable terrain. They don't explain why they help. They don't seem to want anything in return, though they'll accept gifts if offered.

Their birth rate is low—children are rare, protected fiercely when they exist. The groups are multi-generational when children are present: adults in their prime, older individuals who move more slowly but are deferred to, youngsters learning the skills. Life expectancy seems reasonable for a subsistence existence—people survive to what looks like fifty or sixty years if they're lucky or skilled or both. Death comes from injury, disease, predation, or simply the accumulated wear of living here.

They bury their dead, usually, though the practice varies. Some groups place bodies in the holes—returning them to the depth-swimmers, perhaps, or simply removing them from the surface. Some groups leave bodies in specific locations where the rot-crawlers and the fungi will process them quickly. Some groups cremate, building large fires that add their own smoke to the atmospheric mix. The bodies are usually accompanied by objects—tools, carved items, food. Whether this represents belief in an afterlife or simply respect for the deceased is unknown.

They're fully human in capacity—intelligent, emotional, capable of complexity. But they're adapted to this place in ways that make them seem other. They're comfortable with the sound where newcomers find it overwhelming. They're comfortable with the fauna where newcomers find it threatening. They're comfortable with the scale and the strangeness where newcomers feel disoriented. They've been here long enough—generations, certainly, possibly much longer—that this is simply home.

---

The interactions between all these species—fauna and human both—create a kind of equilibrium. The populations fluctuate but remain within bounds. The predators regulate the grazers. The grazers regulate the plant growth (though barely—the plants grow so aggressively that grazing makes little difference). The decomposers ensure that death feeds life. The weavers create habitat that other species use. The cultivators spread certain plants that provide food for others. The humans exist in the margins, significant to themselves but barely relevant to the larger system.

And the sound continues. The thrummers drone. The drummers drum. The shriek-wings click. The fauna calls and scrapes and moves. The humans add their voices, their footsteps, their tool-impacts. The whole thing roars together—not harmonious, not chaotic, but something between: a complexity that has organized itself into pattern without pattern being the goal, life proliferating into every available niche and creating new niches in the process, a breathing continent that breathes because billions of individual breaths are happening simultaneously, each one irrelevant, all of them together constituting the pulse of a place that has become more than the sum of what lives there.

This is what inhabits the breathing continent. This is what makes the sound, what fills the green, what ensures that every surface hosts life and every death feeds more life. This is the ecosystem at equilibrium with itself, existing because it exists, continuing because continuation is what life does when nothing stops it.
