# What Lives Here

## The Tile-Tenders

They are the most numerous, and the first you'll come to recognize as intentional beings rather than fauna. Each is roughly the size of a human hand spread wide, their bodies flat and segmented like wood lice, but their skin—if it can be called skin—is woven. Not covered in woven material, but *woven as substance*: their flesh is basket-work, split reed and fiber interlaced in patterns that differ individual to individual. Some are coarse and open-worked, their internal structures visible through the gaps—pulsing tubes, bundled fibers that might be muscle or might be root. Others are tight-woven, their surfaces smooth as fabric, decorated with natural dyes in geometric patterns that seem deliberate.

They move with the slow, considered gait of lizards, but their feet—six per body, arranged in matched pairs—have no claws. Instead, their digits are thin and numerous, twelve to sixteen per foot, and these spread wide when they walk, testing the surface beneath them. You realize after watching that they are reading the tiles, their many-fingered feet sensing the patterns, the tiny variations in glaze thickness, the almost-invisible marks scratched into clay before firing.

The Tile-Tenders do not eat, or if they do, you never observe it. What you observe is maintenance. One will pause before a tile whose glaze has begun to craze, fine cracks spreading through the surface, and it will crouch there for hours. Its underside—soft, permeable—presses against the ceramic, and gradually the crazing becomes pattern, becomes intentional, becomes beautiful. The cracks stop spreading and start harmonizing, creating deliberate designs that echo other tiles nearby.

Another tends the channels where the not-liquid flows. It reaches down with those many-fingered feet and seems to comb the flow, adjusting its course by minute degrees. The not-liquid responds to this touch, becoming more present where the Tile-Tender guides it, or less, creating variations in the current that affect which plants grow where, which sections of wall become more solid.

They press their heads against the walls frequently, and you eventually understand this is not listening but *speaking*. Their heads are blunt, woven tightly, and from them exudes something—not moisture, there is no moisture here—but an influence, a suggestion. Where they press, the stone or brick seems to hear them. Over hours, the wall responds: becoming more themselves, more precisely what they are, whether that's solid or permeable, present or fading.

The Tile-Tenders sleep communally in nests they've woven into the spaces between wall and floor, in corners where architecture meets itself at strange angles. These nests are extensions of their own bodies—the same weave-work, so that sleeping Tile-Tenders appear to merge with their beds, boundaries dissolving. When they wake, they have to remember themselves back into individual forms, and for the first few minutes after waking they move uncertainly, their edges undefined, occasionally leaving behind a few woven strands that take hours to fade.

They are not quite gendered, but they have variations. Some are builders—these have tighter weaves, stiffer bodies, and they carry small objects constantly: pebbles, clay fragments, bone pieces. They place these in the depressions on the low brick table, or they embed them in walls, or they arrange them in patterns on tiles that become permanent, the objects slowly sinking into the ceramic and becoming part of the floor's design.

Others are tenders—looser-woven, more flexible, and these move through the vegetation constantly. They train vines, not by tying them but by weaving themselves temporarily into the plant's structure, becoming trellis, and the plant grows around and through them. After days or weeks, the tender extracts itself carefully, leaving the plant supported by memory, by the echo of where the tender had been.

Still others are readers. These move slowly across the entire terrace in paths that take days to complete, their many feet touching every tile, every stone, every brick. They are reading the space, understanding its current state, and somehow—you never see exactly how—they communicate this to the others. After a reader completes a circuit, the builders know where to place new objects, the tenders know which plants need adjustment, and the whole terrace shifts slightly in its slow negotiation with reality.

The Tile-Tenders create new members of their kind through weaving. Two or more will gather, and they will begin to construct a new body between them, passing strands back and forth, their feet working in precise coordination. The new one is built over days or weeks, and as it nears completion, it begins to move slightly, testing its limbs while still attached to its makers. When the final strand is woven, it simply walks away, already knowing its role—builder or tender or reader—though you cannot say how this knowledge was transferred.

They regard you with no apparent fear or interest. When you walk, they move aside with the bare minimum of effort required to avoid being stepped on. But you notice that after you've inhabited the terrace for some time, they begin to adjust their patterns around your preferences. If you tend to sit in a particular spot, the tiles there become more comfortable over successive days—not softer, exactly, but more suited to your shape. If you walk a regular path, it becomes more clearly defined, the tiles there taking on a subtle differentiation from their neighbors.

## The Geometry Birds

There are seven of them. Not seven species—seven individuals, each utterly unique, though they share the quality of wrongness that marks them as kin.

The first you notice is Compass, who perches most frequently on the constructed-and-living tree. Compass is roughly the size of a crow, but its proportions are subtly off: the head slightly too large, the tail precisely one-third the body length, the wings extending to exact multiples of the body's width. Its feathers are gray, but not the random gray of natural birds—each feather is a distinct shade, ranging from almost-white at the head to almost-black at the tail tip, in perfect gradation. They are arranged in mathematical precision, each row offset from the last by exactly the same degree, creating spiral patterns that hypnotize when Compass spreads its wings.

Compass's song is a series of intervals—perfect fifths, perfect fourths—and its rhythm is metric, divisible into precise segments. When it sings, the air vibrates in visible patterns, standing waves that hold their shape for several seconds after the sound stops. The other birds respond to Compass, orienting themselves in relation to its calls, as though it provides navigation, direction, the cardinal points of the terrace's internal geography.

Then there is Lacquer, smaller than Compass, whose feathers appear to have been painted. Not painted over, but painted *as*—each feather is a tiny work of decorative art, glossy and hard, featuring miniature landscapes, abstract patterns, tiny calligraphic marks. Yet these are not static images. The paintings shift over days. A feather that showed a mountain scene will gradually transform into a depiction of waves, then into geometric tessellation, then into something else. Lacquer preens constantly, running its beak over each feather, and this preening seems to guide the transformations, though whether Lacquer is painting or simply encouraging what the feathers want to become is unclear.

Lacquer collects the not-liquid from the channels, somehow gathering it in its beak—though the not-liquid should not be gatherable—and it distributes this to the porcelain flowers. After Lacquer visits a flower, the bloom's colors deepen, its glaze becomes more lustrous. You realize Lacquer is maintaining the flowers, re-glazing them, its saliva or tongue or simply its attention providing what the blooms need to remain both ceramic and alive.

Kiln is the largest, nearly the size of a chicken, with feathers that have been fired. They are actual ceramic, individual feather-shaped tiles attached to its body by means you cannot determine. When Kiln moves, there is a soft clicking, shards touching shards, and occasionally a feather breaks. When this happens, Kiln collects the fragments carefully in its beak and carries them to the fire-pit, depositing them in the ashes. Within days, a new feather has emerged from Kiln's body in that spot, perfectly formed, properly glazed, ready to take its place in the geometric arrangement.

Kiln does not fly well—its feathers are too heavy, too rigid—but it glides, dropping from high perches with wings spread, creating a sound like wind chimes, like a distant avalanche of pottery. It lands heavily, always on its feet, and it spends much time on the ground, walking among the Tile-Tenders, occasionally exchanging soft clicking sounds with them, as though they speak related dialects of the same ceramic language.

Thread is thin, delicate, with feathers that are not feathers but fine fibers, hair-like structures that flow and drift as Thread moves. Each feather is a bundle of individual strands, and these strands occasionally separate, floating free, drifting through the air like spider silk. They catch on vegetation, on walls, on sleeping Tile-Tenders, creating a network of connections across the terrace. After several weeks, you realize these threads form a kind of nervous system for the space, carrying information or sensation from one area to another. When you touch a thread in one corner, birds in the opposite corner respond, turning their heads, adjusting their position.

Thread sings rarely, but when it does, the sound is complex beyond analysis—too many overtones, too many simultaneous frequencies, as though Thread's body is a stringed instrument with ten thousand strings all resonating at once. The plants respond to this song, growing perceptibly faster during the hours after Thread performs, their stems extending, leaves unfurling, as though the sound provides nutrition more directly than light or water ever could.

Hollow has no feathers at all, or rather, its feathers are transparent, made from something like glass or thin ceramic glaze stretched between struts of bone. You can see through Hollow to the wall behind it, to the leaves it perches among. Only its eyes are opaque—dark, polished stones set in a skull you can see completely, every bone and joint visible, held together by translucent connective tissue. Hollow moves in complete silence. Even when it lands, there is no sound of impact, as though it weighs nothing, though you've felt its weight when it landed briefly on your shoulder once—real, substantial, present.

Hollow's function seems to be observation. It watches everything, moving from perch to perch every few hours, always positioning itself where it can see the maximum area. Its transparent body makes it nearly invisible, and you often don't notice it until it moves, until its dark eyes shift position and catch your attention. You have the sense that Hollow is recording, remembering, holding the terrace's entire history in that visible skull, though whether it shares this memory with others or keeps it privately, you cannot determine.

Mend is medium-sized, with feathers that appear damaged but aren't. Each feather looks broken—torn edges, missing barbules, cracked shafts—but these damages are intentional, decorative, a kintsugi of feathers. Where other birds would have smooth, intact plumage, Mend has deliberate imperfection, and these imperfections are filled with something golden, a metallic substance that could be actual gold or could be the not-liquid made visible and solid in the presence of damage. The golden repairs hold the broken feathers together, make them stronger than unbroken feathers, make them beautiful in their repair.

Mend seeks out broken things. When a Tile-Tender's weaving comes loose, Mend will land nearby and pluck one of its own small under-feathers, pressing it into the gap. The feather merges with the weaving, becomes fiber, repairs the Tile-Tender's body. When a tile cracks—not with intentional crazing but with real damage—Mend visits it, touches it with a golden-filled wingtip, and the crack seals, filled with that precious metal, turned from flaw into feature.

The seventh is Chorus, and Chorus is never one bird but always several. Sometimes two, sometimes five, occasionally as many as twelve, though seven is most common. Each instance of Chorus is small, sparrow-sized, with identical plumage: brown, simple, unremarkable except that each feather is marked with a small symbol, a different symbol per bird, and these symbols match marks scratched into tiles throughout the terrace. Chorus moves as a flock, the individuals never more than a few feet apart, and they call constantly to each other—not songs but short, sharp notes, a continuous exchange that sounds like conversation, like argument, like collaborative problem-solving.

Chorus performs the work that requires coordination. When a new section of wall needs to become more solid, Chorus gathers around it, each individual landing at a different point, and they call to each other and to the wall in rhythmic patterns. The wall responds to their collective voice in ways it wouldn't to any single bird. When the loom requires adjustment, Chorus lands across its frame, each bird pulling a different thread, and together they create patterns no single weaver could produce.

The individuals of Chorus are not permanent. Sometimes one will land on a tile marked with its symbol and fade, disappearing into the ceramic, its feathers becoming marks on the glaze. Later, a new bird will emerge from a different marked tile, joining the flock, identical to the one that disappeared but somehow new, carrying different knowledge, different aspects of the collective memory.

## The Root-Dancer

There is one other. You see it rarely, and always at the boundary between one state and another—dawn or dusk, the moment when a wall shifts from solid to permeable, the instant when a plant completes its hours-long process of growing a new tendril.

It is human-shaped, approximately. The same height as a tall person, the same general proportion of limbs to torso. But it is made—constructed, assembled, created. Its legs are roots, actual plant roots, thick as arms, that have been convinced to grow in the shape of legs, bifurcating at the bottom into something like feet, joined at the top into something like hips. Its torso is bundled plant stalks, similar to the constructed tree's trunk, wrapped and bound with leather strips on which symbols have been burned, held together with resin that has hardened into amber-like solidity. Its arms are branches—one fresh and green with living bark, one carved and dead but oiled to a dark shine—and these are attached to the stalk-torso with complex joints wrapped in copper wire.

Its head is a clay pot, fired and glazed, turned upside down. Where a face would be, on the bottom of this pot, someone has carved features—simple, stylized, two holes for eyes, a line for a mouth. Inside the pot, through the holes, you can see darkness and occasionally a glint of light, as though something luminous moves within that hollow head, turning, considering.

The Root-Dancer wears a cloak of living leaves, thousands of them stitched together through their stems with thread that is itself growing, itself alive. The cloak moves constantly, leaves breathing, photosynthesizing without light, somehow remaining green and vital despite being separated from their parent plants. As older leaves at the hem die and fall, new ones emerge at the shoulders, growing directly from the cloak itself, so that the garment is always complete, always precisely the same size, though its constituent leaves are in constant cycle of life and death.

It walks through the terrace perhaps once every few days, and its walk is strange: the root-legs extend and contract like real roots growing, pushing through soil, except they're pushing through air, through the thick atmosphere. Each step takes several minutes. The Root-Dancer will raise a foot, and the roots will slowly extend, feeling through space for the right place to land, and then they'll settle and grip, and the weight will shift, and the other foot will begin its slow rise.

Where the Root-Dancer walks, changes accelerate. Walls that were slowly becoming permeable suddenly complete their transition. Plants that were training themselves into new shapes suddenly achieve their final form. Tiles that were gradually developing new glaze patterns finish their transformations. The Root-Dancer seems to catalyze, to finalize, to seal the negotiations between states into new stable configurations.

It carries things, sometimes. In its branch-hands, it will hold a tool—one of the shaped stones, a bone awl, a copper chisel—and it will work with them, briefly, simply. Adjusting a stick supporting a plant. Scratching a new mark into a tile. Repositioning an object in one of the depressions on the low table. These adjustments are minimal but crucial, tiny interventions that redirect the terrace's slow evolution, keeping it balanced, preventing any single tendency from overwhelming the whole.

The Root-Dancer does not communicate through sound. It communicates through stillness. It will stop in a location and simply stand there for hours, rooted, becoming temporary architecture, and its presence in that spot seems to teach the space something, seems to demonstrate a possibility the terrace hadn't considered. After the Root-Dancer moves on, that location is different—subtly, but measurably. More itself. More certain of what it's negotiating toward.

Once, you saw the Root-Dancer approach the sky-well at the center of the terrace. It stood at the edge for a long time—an hour, perhaps more—looking down at the pooled luminescence at the bottom. Then slowly, with its characteristic root-step, it descended into the well, climbing down the fitted stones. When it reached the bottom, it did not emerge from the sky above as the creatures do. It simply stood in the sky-pool, up to its waist in horizontal light, and raised its branch-arms above its pot-head, and stood there, utterly still, for the rest of that day and into the night.

You watched, unable to look away. The Root-Dancer's leaf-cloak spread around it on the surface of the pooled sky, floating, and the leaves began to glow faintly, taking on the same pale luminescence as the sky-pool itself. The carved features on its pot-head seemed to shift, though you couldn't say how—the expression changing from neutral to something like peace, like deep meditation, like communion with whatever vast presence underlies this place.

When dawn came—the next cycle of light, the gradual amber warming of the atmosphere—the Root-Dancer climbed slowly out of the well. Its body looked different: the roots more vital, the bundled stalks more tightly integrated, the branch-arms more flexible, the pot-head's glaze richer. It had been renewed somehow, re-made, its construction re-affirmed through immersion in that fundamental ambiguity at the terrace's heart.

## The Absence

And finally, you must count yourself among what lives here, but also the presence you never see directly, only through evidence. The human or human-like intelligence that continues to tend this space despite never appearing when you're watching.

You find fresh tool marks on wood that you observed unmarked hours before. You discover a newly planted specimen—a cutting inserted into a gap between tiles, its soil-less roots already beginning to grow through the ceramic—in a spot where there was nothing that morning. You wake one dawn to find a small clay bowl placed near where you slept, filled with dried berries and seeds that, when eaten, taste of sunlight and copper and the memory of rain, though rain is impossible here.

Sometimes you catch a glimpse of motion in your peripheral vision—a figure moving behind the dense foliage, human-sized, or perhaps it was just a Tile-Tender, or perhaps it was nothing at all, a trick of the light through the layers of thick air. You hear sounds that might be footsteps, that might be tiles settling, that might be your own breathing echoing strangely through the space.

The loom continues its weaving. The shuttle moves, somehow, when you're not watching, adding rows to the textile, and the pattern that appears shows not just the terrace as it is but as it's becoming, as it will be, depicting your own presence in stylized form, showing you engaged in activities you haven't yet performed but recognize you will, eventually, when you understand this place well enough to participate in its construction as fully as the Tile-Tenders, as consciously as the Root-Dancer.

This absent presence is patient. It doesn't push or demand. It simply maintains the conditions for negotiation, for transformation, for the slow process by which made and living learn to be the same thing. It tends but doesn't control. It builds but doesn't fix. It knows, somehow, that the terrace must remain in motion, in flux, in eternal conversation with itself about what it is and what it's becoming.

And you realize, after enough time here, that this absence is not really absent. It's diffused. It's distributed among all the beings of this place—concentrated in the Root-Dancer, present in the Tile-Tenders' reading and building, expressed through the Geometry Birds' songs and repairs, carried in the threads that span the space, embedded in the tiles themselves, in the patterns and marks and slow crystallization of intention into matter.

The intelligence that made this place is not gone. It has simply become the place itself, merged with it, taught it how to continue without need for a single directing consciousness. And now, perhaps, it's teaching you the same dissolution, the same merger, the same transformation from observer into participant, from visitor into resident, from something separate into something integrated, woven in, becoming part of the breathing terrace's vast, patient, ongoing negotiation with reality itself.
