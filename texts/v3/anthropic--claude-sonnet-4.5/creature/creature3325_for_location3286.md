# Who Lives There

## The Walkers Between Stones

They are not the builders of the structures — that much becomes clear with observation, though the realization comes slowly. They are too *new* here, too uncertain in their movements around the worn walls and posts. But they are the only ones who seem to *see* those structures as structures, who move around them rather than over them, who sometimes stop beside them in a way that might be contemplation.

You would call them bipedal, but that suggests a human gait they do not possess. They progress through the living carpet on two legs, yes, but the legs bend in three places instead of two, and the rhythm is strange: a careful, high-stepping motion that places each narrow foot with deliberate precision into temporary clearings in the base-layer creatures. They are perhaps a meter tall when fully extended, but they rarely extend fully — they move in a perpetual crouch, knees (or knee-analogues) bent, body low, as if always ready to drop into the mass itself.

Their bodies are lean to the point of appearing fragile, but this is deceptive. They are composed primarily of dense, corded tissue visible beneath skin that is dark grey-purple and faintly translucent in certain lights. When they move, you can see the mechanics of them — the slide of muscle over bone, the flex of tendons, the efficient transformation of decision into motion. They generate little excess heat despite constant movement. Everything about their design suggests optimization, unnecessary elements pared away over generations.

They have four arms. The upper pair is longer, more flexible, ending in hands (and they are clearly *hands*, not claws or pincers) with six digits arranged radially around the wrist. These hands are constantly in motion, even when the body is still — fingers flickering through a vocabulary of positions, sometimes touching each other, sometimes reaching toward nearby creatures in gestures that stop just short of contact. The lower pair of arms is shorter, thicker, and these hands have only three digits, heavy and strong. These are the practical hands, the ones that move objects, that push through resistant masses of fauna, that occasionally kill prey with quick, precise strikes.

Their heads — if that's the right word for the upper terminus of the body — are difficult to describe because they lack the features you expect to organize your description around. There is no clear face. Instead, a smooth forward surface interrupted by several dark organs that might be eyes, arranged asymmetrically. Perhaps five of them, ranging from the size of a thumbnail to something larger, each at a slightly different angle. When they focus on something, the organs shift position minutely, creating a ripple effect across the head's surface. Below these, a horizontal opening that serves multiple purposes: respiration, feeding, and something that might be sound production, though the sounds they make are infrequent.

What is most striking is their *awareness*. These are not creatures operating on instinct alone. When one pauses — and they pause often, going absolutely motionless for minutes at a time — you can feel the attention, the observation, the *thinking* happening. They watch the patterns of prey movement with the focus of someone solving a problem. They study the structures with what reads as puzzlement or curiosity. When two of them meet, there is negotiation in their movements, a careful dance of spacing and gesture that suggests complex social calculation.

## Their Number and Distribution

There are perhaps twenty of them on this shelf, though counting is difficult because they can vanish so completely into the living carpet when they choose. They crouch down, tuck their limbs in specific ways, and suddenly they are just another dark shape among dark shapes, another slight elevation in the terrain. Only their breathing gives them away — a slow, deliberate expansion and contraction that affects the creatures immediately around them, creating a subtle, rhythmic disturbance.

They are not evenly distributed across the shelf. They seem to favor certain territories, though the boundaries shift over what might be days or weeks. Three of them occupy the area around the largest of the built structures — a curved wall that forms almost a complete circle. Two others stay near the shelf's eastern edge, where the drop begins. Others are more nomadic, tracing long paths back and forth across the available space, pausing at irregular intervals.

They avoid each other, mostly. When two territories overlap, there is a period of tense negotiation — elaborate displays of the upper arms, positioning of bodies at specific angles, that strange finger-flickering that seems to carry meaning. Usually one defers and moves away. Occasionally both move away, abandoning the contested space temporarily. Violence is rare but has been observed: quick, brutal, decisive. The loser retreats or dies. The winner does not pursue, simply resumes prior activity as if nothing occurred.

Young ones exist but are rarely seen. Perhaps twice a year (if year means anything here), smaller versions appear, already mobile, already competent, staying close to one adult for a period of weeks before beginning to establish their own ranges. Where they come from, where they shelter during their most vulnerable phase, remains unclear. There must be crevices, hollows in the mountain face, spaces hidden from observation where the transition from birth to mobility occurs.

## What They Eat

They are predators, but patient ones. 

A typical hunt lasts hours. One of them will identify a target — usually one of the larger fauna, something knee-high with substantial mass — and begin following it. Not closely; they maintain distance, sometimes considerable distance, tracking through the crowd by the disturbance pattern the prey creates, by its habits, by what seems like memory of its previous movements.

The pursuit is so gradual it barely seems like pursuit. The walker moves when the prey moves, stops when it stops, matches its rhythm. Hours pass. The distance slowly decreases. The prey never appears to notice; it's focused on its own hunting or grazing, operating in its own temporal frame.

When the walker finally strikes, it is sudden: a lunge from the crouch, the heavy lower arms deployed, seizing the prey and delivering a killing blow to a specific point on its body — they seem to know exactly where to strike each species, where the critical organs or nerve clusters sit. The prey struggles briefly, then stills.

Then comes the remarkable part: they don't eat immediately. Instead, they carry the kill — awkward and effortful; these creatures are strong but the prey is often nearly their own size — to a specific location. Usually near one of the built structures, sometimes on top of one of the raised platforms. They position the body carefully, then settle beside it and wait.

Other creatures arrive, drawn by whatever signal a fresh kill provides. Small scavengers first, then larger ones. The walker does not drive them away. Instead, it watches, and as the scavengers work, it occasionally reaches out with the upper arms and extracts specific pieces — organs, it seems like, or specific tissues, selected with knowledge. These pieces it consumes slowly, deliberately, while the scavengers process the rest.

So they eat, but they eat *selectively*. They take perhaps a tenth of the kill for themselves, leaving the rest to the ecosystem. The feeding can last an hour or more, the walker sitting in a pool of scavenger activity, calmly extracting choice pieces, ignoring the frenzy around it.

They also eat from the base-layer creatures occasionally, but this is clearly less preferred. They'll scoop up a handful of the small carapaced organisms and sort through them, selecting a few, discarding most. Emergency rations, perhaps, or supplementary nutrition.

Water, if they need it, must come from their prey. There is no standing water here, no condensation sufficient to collect. Yet they show no signs of dehydration, no desperation. Either they're supremely adapted to minimal water, or they're extracting it with perfect efficiency from everything they consume.

## Their Use of the Structures

This is where they become most mysterious and most clearly *people*.

They visit the structures regularly — not all of them, and not the same ones each time, but there are patterns. The circular enclosure is visited most frequently. They approach it, walk its perimeter, sometimes sit with their backs against its inner surface. Occasionally they arrange objects within it: small bones from prey, interesting stones, once a nearly spherless carapace from one of the larger fauna. These arrangements are not random — objects are placed in relation to each other with clear intentionality. Then, sometimes on the same visit, sometimes days later, they disarrange everything, scatter the objects, leave the space clear again.

The posts — seven of them in a rough line across one section of shelf — receive different attention. The walkers pass between them in specific patterns, weaving back and forth, occasionally touching a post with their upper hands in passing. It resembles ritual, or practice, or game. Three different walkers have been observed doing this, each following slightly different paths between the posts, but all treating the posts as significant, as marking something important.

The raised platform is used as a butchering site, as mentioned, but not only that. Sometimes a walker will climb onto it and simply stand there, fully extended to their complete height, motionless for long periods, their eye-organs oriented toward the distances — toward the darkness beyond the shelf, toward the mountain face, toward the perpetual dim twilight above. What they're looking for or looking at is impossible to know. They never react to anything visible. But they do this regularly, this standing and looking, as if checking for something, or remembering something, or hoping for something.

They do not modify the structures. They do not repair them or add to them. They do not even clean them — if anything, they're slowly adding to the accumulation of marks and wear through their constant contact. But they *respect* them, in a way nothing else on the shelf does. They recognize these things as different, as made, as meaningful.

There is one behavior that appears to be unique: occasionally, one of them will approach a structure — usually the circular wall — and produce sound. Not the brief exhalations they use in other contexts, but extended, modulated vocalizations that vary in pitch and rhythm. Are they speaking? Singing? Calling to something or someone? The sounds carry in the dead air, echo faintly off the mountain face, then fade. No answer comes. They do not seem to expect one. After finishing, they resume normal activity, as if whatever needed to be said has been said.

## Their Social Structure

They tolerate each other but do not seek each other out. The young stay near an adult for a period, then separate — there's no extended family unit, no permanent partnerships observed.

Yet they are not entirely solitary. They *know* each other. When two meet, even after weeks apart, there is recognition in how they negotiate their encounter. The gestures differ slightly depending on which individuals are interacting. Some pairs are more tolerant of proximity than others. There are histories between them, relationships that persist even in absence.

Rarely — perhaps once a season, if seasons exist here — several of them gather. Three, four, occasionally as many as six, converging on a location (always near one of the larger structures) and spending hours in proximity. They do not appear to communicate in any obvious way during these gatherings. They simply occupy the same space, their upper arms moving through that constant finger-vocabulary, occasionally mirroring each other's gestures. Sometimes they share food, one walker bringing a kill and permitting others to select pieces before the scavengers arrive. Then, without obvious signal, they separate, returning to their individual territories.

These gatherings have the weight of importance. Other fauna give the area wider berth during them. The walkers themselves move differently — more deliberately, less efficiently, as if the normal urgency of survival has been temporarily suspended for something else.

There is hierarchy, but it's subtle. Some individuals, when encountered, are consistently deferred to. Others consistently defer. This doesn't seem based on size or apparent strength but on something less visible — age, perhaps, or experience, or simply social weight accumulated over time. The dominant ones don't demand deference; it's simply given, a slight alteration in posture, a particular gesture of the upper arms, and the interaction is resolved.

Conflict, when it occurs, is formal. The aggressive displays before actual violence are elaborate: specific postures, specific arm positions, held for long moments, giving the opponent every opportunity to submit. If both persist, the violence is intense but brief — grappling, striking with the heavy lower arms, sometimes biting with that horizontal mouth which proves to contain impressive teeth. But it ends quickly, decisively, and is never revisited. The matter, whatever it was, is settled.

## How They Experience This Place

They are supremely adapted to the shelf, yet they seem... not unhappy, but constrained. They move through their environment with complete competence, hunting and surviving with efficiency that speaks to generations of adaptation. But those long periods of motionlessness, that gazing into the distance from atop the platforms, that tendency to visit the structures as if checking whether something has changed — there's a quality of waiting to them.

They are nocturnal by preference, most active during the darker periods when the dim light darkens further. Their eye-organs function well in near-total darkness, and they seem more comfortable, more themselves, when the bioluminescence becomes the primary illumination. During the brighter periods, they tend to rest, tucking themselves against the mountain face or into hollows, becoming part of the landscape.

Their relationship to the other fauna is purely practical. They hunt certain species, ignore others, avoid a few that are apparently dangerous (something about the way they give wide berth to the low-slung six-legged creature with the bony protrusions suggests learned respect, possibly learned painfully). But there's no animosity, no cruelty. They kill efficiently and without evident emotion. They allow scavengers to their kills without territorial defense. They are part of the ecology but somehow separate from it, participating without being absorbed.

The heat doesn't seem to bother them, but they use it. They select resting spots based on thermal properties, preferring areas where the stone is slightly cooler — near the edges, where air circulation is better, or in shadows cast by the mountain face. They spread their limbs when resting, maximizing surface area for heat dissipation. They're warm-blooded, probably, running their own metabolism, but they've learned to manage the ambient heat with sophistication.

Pain and injury exist for them. A walker with a damaged lower arm has been observed, the limb moving stiffly, clearly causing difficulty. It adapted, using the upper arms more for tasks usually performed by the lower, hunting smaller prey, taking longer to accomplish necessary activities. Over weeks, the injury seemed to improve — healed, or at least compensated for. They are mortal, fallible, capable of suffering. This makes their patience more impressive.

## What They Remember

This is speculation, but observation suggests it:

They remember the structures' builders. Not personally — too much time has passed; the structures are ancient and the walkers are not. But they remember that someone built them, that these things mean something. The way they approach the structures, the care they take around them, the sounds they occasionally produce near them — these are behaviors directed at absence, at something or someone no longer present.

Perhaps the builders were their own ancestors, and cultural memory has preserved the significance even as the specific purpose faded. Perhaps the builders were something else entirely, and the walkers simply recognize the mark of intentionality when they see it, responding to it with their own intentionality.

They seem to remember hunting patterns, territorial boundaries, which prey species migrates through which areas at which times. Individual walkers can be observed returning to specific locations with timing that suggests they know something will be there, and they're usually correct.

They remember each other, as mentioned. Recognition suggests memory. The young must learn from the adults during that brief period of proximity — not just physical skills but social knowledge, the maps of territory and relationship that govern interaction.

There might be longer memory too, deeper memory, the kind that shapes behavior without conscious awareness. Why do they walk between the posts in those specific patterns? Why does the circular enclosure draw them repeatedly? These might be cultural practices whose origins are lost but whose execution continues because it continues, because it has always continued, because stopping would mean losing something essential even if they no longer know exactly what.

## Their Sound and Silence

Mostly they move in silence, only the soft displacement of other fauna marking their passage. But they are capable of sound, and they use it specifically, economically.

There's the breathing, constant and controlled, almost subsonic — you feel it more than hear it when they're close.

There are the brief exhalations used in encounters with others of their kind — a sharp huff that seems to mean something like acknowledgment or warning, depending on context.

There's a clicking sound they produce, possibly with the teeth behind that horizontal mouth, used when hunting. A single click, then stillness. Another click, minutes later, from a different position. It might help them track each other when multiple walkers are in proximity, or it might be self-soothing, or it might mean something else entirely.

And there are the extended vocalizations near the structures, described earlier. These are complex, modulated, clearly intended to convey something. They have the structure of language or song, rising and falling, pausing and resuming. But they are monologues, never dialogues. They speak into silence and the silence does not answer.

Once, two walkers were observed near each other when one began these vocalizations. The second stopped all movement, oriented its eye-organs toward the first, and remained motionless until the sounds concluded. Then it made a brief, soft sound in response — different from the usual exhalation, gentler — before moving away. The first walker watched it go, then resumed normal activity.

They can hear, clearly. They respond to sounds in their environment, tracking prey partly by the disturbances it creates. But they seem to prefer silence, or at least to add sound to their world sparingly, as if noise itself has cost.

## At the Scale of Lifetimes

A walker's lifespan is unknown, but they show age. The oldest-seeming ones move more carefully, have thicker tissue across their joints, hold their bodies differently. They are deferred to more consistently. They hunt less frequently but more efficiently, knowing exactly where to position themselves and when. They spend more time at the structures, especially the circular enclosure.

The young are quicker, more erratic, learning through error. They attempt hunts that fail. They misjudge territorial boundaries and get driven off. They explore more, ranging to the shelf's far edges, investigating areas the adults largely ignore.

So there is a life cycle, a progression from emergence through competence to eventual decline. What happens at the end is unclear — old walkers presumably die, but bodies are never observed. Perhaps they leave, descending the mountain slope to die elsewhere. Perhaps they're consumed so efficiently by scavengers that nothing remains to mark their passing. Perhaps they know when the end approaches and seek out some final location deliberately, somewhere away from the shelf, making their death a private thing.

New young appear regularly enough to maintain the population, but the numbers stay stable. Approximately twenty, always approximately twenty, as if the shelf has carrying capacity for that specific number of these specific creatures, and the population regulates itself to match.

## The Relationship to the Darkness

They are creatures of dim light, but not darkness-blind. When the particulate falls and visibility decreases further, they continue functioning, navigating by touch and sound and probably senses not available to human perception. They seem to prefer the darkness, moving more freely, with less caution.

They do not fear the deeper darkness beyond the shelf's edge. They approach it, peer into it, occasionally descend a short way down the slope (returning later, always returning — whatever is down there, it's not where they choose to live permanently). But they orient toward it regularly, as if it contains something important, something that bears watching or waiting for.

The bioluminescence of other fauna doesn't seem to bother them despite their apparent preference for darkness. Perhaps because it's cold light, information rather than illumination, and they've learned to read it the way they read everything else in their environment — as data about position, movement, threat, opportunity.

Their own bodies show no bioluminescence. They are dark and stay dark, visible only by their disruption of the patterns around them, by the space they occupy rather than any light they produce. This is strategic — they are predators, and visibility would compromise their hunting. But it also makes them seem like absences moving through the world, negative space given animation.

## What They Are Not

They are not the builders, though they remember building.

They are not purely animals, though they hunt and eat and reproduce like animals.

They are not technological — they make nothing, use no tools beyond their own bodies, modify nothing in their environment except through the universal modifications of presence: the wearing of paths, the marking of territories, the redistribution of resources.

They are not communicative in ways that reach beyond their own species. They do not attempt to signal to visitors or observers. They do not seem interested in contact with otherness, only with navigating the otherness that already exists in their relationships with each other.

They are not content, but neither are they suffering. They exist in that state of adaptive tension common to all life — constantly working to persist, never quite settling into ease, but also never collapsing into futility. They continue because continuing is what living things do, and they're good at it, good enough that it continues to be worth doing.

## The Moment of Encounter

If you stood on the shelf, warm stone beneath your feet, darkness pooling around you, and one of them approached — carefully, high-stepping through the living carpet, upper arms in constant motion, eye-organs reflecting what little light exists — you would see yourself recognized. Not as food; you're too large, too unknown. Not as threat; they would have fled already if that's what they determined. But as *something*, as a presence requiring acknowledgment.

The walker would stop at a specific distance, neither fleeing nor approaching further. The upper arms would move through patterns you cannot read. The eye-organs would track your movement with unsettling attention, seeing you in ways you cannot reciprocate. If you moved, it would adjust position to maintain that specific distance. If you stayed still long enough, it might lose interest, returning to its prior activity as if you were just another feature of a landscape full of features.

But in that moment of mutual observation, you would feel what others who visit strange places rarely feel: not that you are seeing alien life, but that alien life is seeing *you*, evaluating you with its own categories of significance, finding you possibly interesting but ultimately irrelevant to its concerns, which are older and more specific than your presence can address.

Then it would move away, that strange tri-jointed gait carrying it across the living ground, and you would be alone again with the heat and the darkness and the constant soft clicking of the base-layer creatures, and you would wonder if the encounter happened at all, or if you invented meaning in a moment that was, for the walker, merely empty time between hunts.

But later, if you stayed long enough, you might see that same walker at one of the structures, producing those careful vocalizations into the silence, and you would understand: they speak to absence because absence is what remains. Whatever they remember, whoever built these careful walls and posts, is gone. The walkers persist in the place that was prepared, or marked, or claimed, or simply noticed as notable by someone who came before.

They are not the builders, but they are the inheritors, carrying forward something they don't fully understand because carrying forward is what inheritors do, even when inheritance is just marking time around monuments to a purpose that no longer applies, in a place that is both perfectly suited to them and somehow not enough, never quite enough, almost but not entirely home.
