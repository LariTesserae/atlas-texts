# The Inhabitants of Qom-Valai

## The Siphon-Walkers

They are both less and more than their name suggests. When you first see one, your mind offers familiar analogies—crane, mantis, walking probe—but these comparisons evaporate as you watch. They don't move like animals. They don't move like machines either, not quite. They move like optimization given form, like a mathematical function walking.

Their bodies are bulbous, yes, but the bulbs aren't uniform. Each individual carries a different configuration of chambers, each chamber containing fluids of different viscosities, different opacities, different colors ranging from clear to amber to something that shifts between blue and violet depending on temperature. Watch long enough and you see that the chambers are rearranging themselves internally. The walls between them are semipermeable, selective. Fluids migrate between chambers in patterns that repeat with variation, like music where the melody is recognizable but the harmonization changes with each iteration.

The four legs are indeed delicate-looking—narrow at the joints, widening slightly at the foot-pads. But they don't break, don't bend incorrectly, never miss a step even on the treacherous surfaces of the Pipes. The material has a faint opalescence, and under magnification reveals structures at every scale: micro-channels running through what appears solid, nested hierarchies of tubes within tubes, patterns that suggest vasculature but also circuitry, also something else that neither biology nor engineering fully describes.

When they extend their proboscises into the vents, they become almost statuesque, but "feeding" seems wrong for what they're doing. The appendage—smooth, slightly tapered, able to extend to three times the body's height—enters the vent opening and you can see pulses traveling up it. Not peristaltic muscle contractions. Waves. Actual waves in the material itself, as if the proboscis is momentarily liquid, momentarily glass, momentarily something that exists in states matter isn't supposed to occupy.

What rises through them accumulates in specific chambers. After hours at a vent, those chambers glow faintly. After days, they glow brightly enough to read by at night. The light isn't quite white—it has structure to it, spectral lines that appear and disappear, colors that seem to exist between the usual colors. One researcher described it as "the light that light would see if light could see."

They shed. Not constantly, but every few months an individual will stop its circuit and settle near one of the old markers and over the course of a week will extrude the outer layer of its body. What remains is slightly smaller, slightly different in proportion. The shed material doesn't decay—it vitrifies, becomes something like glass but harder, and retains the exact shape of the walker that shed it. These husks are scattered across the plateau, some recent and still faintly luminous, some clearly ancient and weathered smooth. The oldest are partially embedded in basalt, become part of the stone layer by layer, which means they've been doing this for—the geologists struggle with the implications—for possibly millions of years.

But they can't be the same individuals. Nothing maintains structural integrity across that timescale. So either they reproduce (no one has seen this) or they're being manufactured (no one has found the factory) or the question of individual identity doesn't apply to them in the way it applies to other beings.

They notice things. The researcher who was carefully moved aside wasn't experiencing the walker's confusion—it had a path, she was in it, it relocated her with extraordinary gentleness to a position three meters to the left, then continued. Its foot-pads are capable of tremendous delicacy. They can step around beetles without disturbing them. They can navigate crystal formations too fragile for human passage. But they can also exert tremendous force when needed—one was observed pushing a boulder the size of a shipping container that had fallen across its route. It pushed steadily for six hours until the boulder moved enough for passage, then continued as if nothing had occurred.

Their relationship to the thermal cycle is unmistakable. During the minimum, many stand dormant, their internal fluids still, their chambers dark. As the cycle rises toward peak, they begin moving, and the fluids begin their spiraling migrations between chambers. At peak, they're most active, and their positioning becomes most obviously coordinated. They form arrangements: triangles, stars, circles, more complex polygons. They maintain these formations for the duration of peak, then dissolve them as the cycle descends.

The formations appear purposeful but their purpose is opaque. They don't align with magnetic fields or astronomical positions. They don't seem to be transmitting or receiving in any electromagnetic spectrum researchers can detect. But *something* is happening—local temperature readings show micro-variations, air pressure fluctuates in subtle patterns, and the frequency analysis of the plateau's hum during these formations shows additional harmonic structures not present when the walkers are dispersed.

One researcher proposed they're parts of a single distributed system—that what appears to be twenty individuals is actually twenty components of one entity. The evidence is circumstantial but compelling: they never collide, even in dense formations; their movements, when mapped across the plateau, show coordination that shouldn't be possible without communication; and their chamber configurations, while individually unique, create a kind of completeness when considered as a set, like they're each holding different pieces of a larger pattern.

But they're not a hive mind in any familiar sense. They act independently most of the time. They respond to local conditions—an obstacle, a new vent opening, a researcher in the path. If they're connected, it's loosely, or only intermittently, or in ways that human monitoring can't detect.

Their emotional life, if the term applies, is invisible. They don't startle. They don't play. They don't fight or cooperate in obvious ways. They simply enact their patterns with something that resembles serenity or might just be the absence of agitation. Watching them produces a strange calm in human observers, a sense that urgency is a choice and perhaps not the best choice. Several researchers have reported falling into something like meditation while observing walkers at work, losing hours to a state that wasn't quite sleep but wasn't quite normal waking consciousness either.

One walked through the research station once. Just walked through, passing between domes, pausing briefly in the courtyard where its chambers pulsed with unusual rapidity, then continuing out the other side toward a vent system two kilometers away. It neither avoided the human habitation nor showed particular interest in it. The station was simply terrain it crossed, notable only for requiring slightly different navigation than basalt and stone.

They leave no waste. Whatever they draw from the vents is completely utilized, transformed into the light in their chambers, into the material of their bodies, into the shed husks, into nothing that gets excreted or discarded. They're perfectly efficient or they're operating on principles where concepts like waste don't apply.

The molecular structures found in their chambers—the fullerenes, the carbon lattices, the molecular machinery—are also found in trace amounts in their shed husks, embedded in the vitrified material like components in amber. Analysis suggests these structures are phenomenally complex, perhaps computational substrates, perhaps storage media for information encoded at molecular scale. One husk, when scanned with sufficient resolution, revealed what might be patterns, regularities in the distribution of these structures that looked almost like text, like data, like memory crystallized.

But reading it would require understanding an encoding system developed by—what? Who made them? Did anything make them, or did they emerge from the plateau itself, from some intersection of geothermal chemistry and deep time and conditions that exist nowhere else?

The markers they pause beside are definitely markers *for* them, or markers *by* them, or markers that are part of their system. The spiral pattern carved in the stone matches patterns that appear in their chamber configurations, in the paths their internal fluids trace, in the routes they walk across the plateau. It's a signature, a sign, a symbol that means something in their vocabulary of heat and stone and purposeful motion.

## The Thermal Ecosystem

The beings that have evolved—or adapted, or been shaped—to live in this environment are studies in specialized extremity.

The thermal snakes are perhaps the most alien reptiles you'll encounter anywhere. Their scales aren't simply colored but are actually exhibiting interference patterns from micro-structures that shift with their body temperature. A cool snake is deep blue. A hot snake is brilliant copper. A snake in transition ripples with colors that flow along its length. They can tolerate internal temperatures that would denature most proteins, which means their biochemistry is either reinforced by unusual compounds or operates on different principles than standard vertebrate metabolism.

They're solitary and silent except during mating season, when the males produce a sound like metal scraping stone, a vibration they create by rubbing specialized scales together. The sound carries for kilometers through the basalt, and other snakes respond, converging on the caller. But the convergence is slow, taking days, as if even their mating operates on geological time scales.

What they hunt in the vent systems remains mysterious. They descend twenty, thirty, forty meters into holes barely wide enough for their bodies. They're down there for hours. They emerge swollen, sluggish with digestion, and immediately seek the hottest available stone to lie on, as if they need external heat to process whatever they've consumed. Attempts to explore the vents with cameras have revealed chambers and side-passages, but the environment is too hot, too humid, too geometrically complex for systematic exploration. There's an entire ecosystem down there, glimpsed only by what emerges: the snakes, occasionally blind white arthropods, once something that might have been a fish or might have been something that convergently evolved a fish-like form.

The flightless birds are descended from something—DNA analysis suggests a lineage connecting them to falcons, improbably—but they've been flightless so long that their wings are merely vestigial nubs hidden under feathers. They're built for climbing instead, with powerful legs and feet that grip stone with tenacious strength. They nest in the water-trees but forage everywhere, eating insects, scavenging shed snake skins, occasionally harvesting material from siphon-walker husks (they have beaks capable of chipping the vitrified material, which nothing else attempts).

They're social and communicative. Their calls have been recorded and analyzed: at least forty distinct vocalizations, combined in sequences that appear grammatical. Some calls are referential—the "beetle" call, the "snake" call, the "vent surge" warning. Others are more abstract, used in contexts suggesting emotional content or social negotiation. They gather in groups of five to fifteen and engage in behaviors that look like conversation: one bird calls, another responds, a third adds something, the first bird calls again differently. It has the rhythm of information exchange.

They're also playful, which is rare in flightless birds. Young birds chase each other through the water-tree understory, playing what looks like tag. Adults manipulate objects—small stones, fragments of wood—with no obvious practical purpose, sometimes arranging them in patterns before losing interest and walking away. And they're curious: they investigate new objects in their territory, approaching carefully, extending a foot to tap, jerking back, approaching again, eventually either accepting or rejecting the novelty.

They're wary of humans but not terrified. With patience, they'll forage within a few meters of a still observer. One researcher spent weeks habituating a group and eventually had them taking food from her hand, though this was discouraged by the research team—changing behavior through human contact is exactly what protocols aim to avoid.

The thermal beetles are mobile heat-stores, living batteries of thermal energy. Their metabolism runs hot, powered by specialized bacteria in their gut chambers that break down the thermophile plants with extraordinary efficiency. A beetle at rest radiates enough heat to feel from half a meter away. A colony of hundreds creates a visible shimmer in the air above them, and the stone they congregate on becomes warm enough that thermophile seeds germinate there preferentially, creating a feedback loop: beetles attract beetles, heat attracts seeds, seeds grow into mats, mats attract beetles.

They're agricultural in an unconscious way, cultivating without knowledge or intent, simply by being what they are in the place they are. But the pattern is too consistent, too productive to be entirely accidental. Possibly they're responding to chemical cues from the plants. Possibly the plants have evolved to exploit the beetles' behavior. Possibly it's deep mutualism, co-evolution across so much time that neither species can be properly understood without the other.

The beetles' life cycle involves metamorphosis in the vent depths. Adults lay eggs in the cooler peripheral vents, and the larvae descend, living in the hot darkness for months, possibly years. What they eat down there is unknown. They emerge as adults during the thermal cycle's peak, dozens at once from a single vent system, their carapaces still soft and pale, hardening and darkening over hours as they absorb heat and their bacterial cultures establish.

The millipedes are agents of something. They move with too much purpose, visit vents on too regular a schedule, always enter and exit the same holes. They're farming, almost certainly—cultivating some subterranean organism that they harvest and consume or possibly trade with something else down there. The idea of trade seems absurd until you watch them: they enter a vent, descend, remain for exactly twelve hours (multiple observations confirm the timing is precise), emerge with their mouthparts carrying material that they deposit near thermophile mats, then proceed to the next vent in their circuit.

What could they be trading? Why would a millipede trade? The question implies intention, strategy, communication with some other party. But the behavior is undeniable, and the alternative—that this is pure instinct programmed by evolution—requires believing that evolution programmed incredibly complex behavior including precise timing and material transport without any immediate survival benefit to the individual millipede.

Unless the millipede isn't an individual. Unless each one is also part of some distributed system, like the siphon-walkers. Or unless they're symbiotic with something that uses them as mobile agents, the way fungi use insects to spread spores, except elaborated into something more sophisticated.

The flying insects are the plateau's background chorus, its ambient life. Thousands of species, probably—no one has attempted a complete survey. They range from tiny gnats that cluster in clouds around vent openings to hand-sized dragonfly-analogues that patrol the water-tree canopy. Many show bioluminescence: abdomens that pulse while flying, creating streams of light in the dusk. Others have developed thermal sensitivity so acute they can navigate by the heat variations in the air, finding vent plumes from hundreds of meters away.

One species—the compass flies, named for their behavior—always circles clockwise around vent openings, never counterclockwise, never still. They maintain this circular patrol constantly, landing only to mate, returning immediately to the circling pattern. Why? No one knows. But when dozens of them are circling the same vent, the visual effect is hypnotic, and the cumulative buzz of their wings creates a tone that resonates with the vent's rush, amplifying certain frequencies, creating harmonics.

## The Flora's Strange Intelligence

To call the plants here "adapted" is accurate but insufficient. They've been shaped by this environment into forms that blur the line between organism and apparatus.

The thermophile mats are communities, not individuals—hundreds of plant species so intermingled that their roots form networks that appear to share resources, share information, share responses to stress. Damage one section of a mat and distant sections respond within hours, altering their metabolic activity, their water uptake, as if warned. The network has properties that suggest computation: signals propagate through it, split at junctions, converge at nodes, create interference patterns that might be noise or might be data processing.

The mats grow in patterns influenced by more than just available heat and moisture. They avoid certain areas of basalt even when conditions seem ideal. They grow more densely along the routes the siphon-walkers travel. They form boundaries between themselves and other mat communities that are too regular to be simple competition—the boundaries are often straight lines or geometric curves, as if agreed upon by treaty.

And they respond to sound. The whistling organs produce tones, and the thermophile mats nearest those organs grow in orientations that maximize their exposure to the sound waves. Plants that are moved away from sound sources become less vigorous, their color fades, their growth slows. Plants exposed to recorded whistling-organ sounds show some response but not the full response, as if the live phenomenon contains something the recordings miss—harmonics beyond human hearing, perhaps, or vibrations propagating through the stone.

The water-trees are even stranger. Their segmented trunks allow them to redistribute internal water rapidly, moving it from segment to segment in response to temperature changes, creating convection currents that help them regulate temperature. But the system is too sophisticated for simple thermodynamics. The trees are sensing temperature at multiple heights, predicting changes based on time of day and position in the thermal cycle, pre-emptively moving water to where it will be needed. They're running algorithms, chemical algorithms implemented in cells and sap, but algorithms nonetheless.

Their canopies orient to maximize moisture capture, yes, but the orientation changes in ways that suggest they're also communicating with neighboring trees. Move one tree's canopy (a research team tried this once, gently binding branches to alter the configuration) and neighboring trees alter their canopies in response, creating negative space where the manipulated tree was previously catching moisture, as if deliberately compensating for the change.

And they produce the ferns beneath them, in a sense. The ferns are actually symbiotically integrated with the trees' root systems—they're not separate organisms but extensions of the tree, specialized organs for catching moisture at ground level. A water-tree with its fern undergrowth is a single distributed organism with multiple morphologies, multiple strategies, multiple ways of interfacing with the environment.

The vines with their geometric symbiotic patterns are perhaps the most overtly strange. The patterns aren't random, aren't even just regular—they're specific. They repeat across different vines in different locations. Some patterns appear everywhere, universal motifs. Others are local, unique to vines in a particular region. Still others appear, persist for months or years, then disappear, replaced by new patterns.

The organisms forming these patterns are themselves unusual—not quite lichen (no algae, no photosynthesis) and not quite moss (wrong structure, wrong reproduction method). They're something else, possibly something unique to the plateau, that derives energy from the vines' sap but also provides something back—analysis suggests they're fixing nitrogen in forms the vine can use, but also producing complex organic molecules whose purpose is unclear.

The patterns they form have been photographed extensively. Researchers have attempted to find meaning in them, treating them as potential information encoding. Some patterns are certainly functional—they seem to optimize water flow along the vine's surface, or maximize surface area for gas exchange. But other patterns have no obvious function, exist purely as pattern, as organization for the sake of organization.

Or as communication. The hypothesis is wild but persistent: that the patterns are how vines communicate with each other, or with the plateau, or with something. That the patterns are text, symbology, a written language executed in biological structures on wooden surfaces, read by—what? The siphon-walkers pause near particularly intricate patterns. The flightless birds show interest in them, pecking at the edges. Perhaps they're not communication in the human sense but something more fundamental—identification markers, memory storage, territorial signifiers that operate at the level of ecosystem rather than individual.

The whistling organs are definitely purposeful. The structures are too precise, too tuned to be accident. Each shrub that develops them produces one to five organs, each slightly different in size and chamber configuration, each producing its own tone when the right intensity of airflow passes through. A single shrub becomes a chord-producer, and a grove of these shrubs becomes an ensemble.

But ensemble implies performance, and performance implies audience. Who's listening? The thermal beetles show no response. The birds ignore it. The snakes are indifferent. The siphon-walkers—possibly. During peak thermal cycle, when the organs are loudest, the walkers' positioning sometimes correlates with acoustic properties of the location, as if they're seeking or avoiding certain frequencies.

Or possibly the listening is internal to the plateau itself. Possibly the whistling organs are part of the plateau's self-sensing apparatus, providing acoustic data about wind speed, air density, thermal flux. The tones change continuously but slowly, tracking the thermal cycle, tracking seasonal variations, tracking something. They're monitoring variables, converting physical properties into sound, making the plateau audible to itself.

Which would mean the plateau is a system that includes within itself mechanisms for self-monitoring, which implies a level of integration, of unity, that makes the boundary between "environment" and "organism" difficult to draw.

## The Remnant Peoples

The markers and ruins tell of previous human presence, though "human" might be presumptuous. Something with human-scale concerns, at least. Something that carved spirals in stone and built structures at geometric coordinates and then departed or disappeared or became something else.

The markers are weathered but deliberately positioned—always near significant vent systems, always visible from at least two other markers, creating sight lines that crisscross the plateau. Stand at one marker and you can see two or three others, and from those markers you can see others, forming a network that allows triangulation, navigation, coordination across distances.

The spiral pattern carved in each marker is identical despite the weathering, which means it was either deeply carved originally or has been maintained. But by whom? No one has witnessed maintenance. The markers are as they are, permanent fixtures like the stone itself.

The spiral appears elsewhere: in the siphon-walkers' chambers, in the paths they walk, in the growth patterns of thermophile mats, in the vines' symbology. It's a motif, a symbol that recurs across domains—geological, biological, technological. It might be purely functional, representing the optimal solution to some problem that basalt and life and purpose all face in this environment. Or it might be sign of authorship, the signature of whatever designed or influenced or guided the plateau toward its current configuration.

The ruins suggest civilization but strange civilization. The rectangular foundations are too large for individual dwellings—some are fifty meters on a side. The material fragments are too ambiguous—neither clearly natural nor clearly artificial. And the geometric positioning across the plateau is too large-scale for casual settlement.

One theory: these were temples or observatories, positioned to mark significant locations in the vent network, built by a culture that understood the plateau's patterns and considered them sacred or scientifically significant. The triangulation they create would allow precise measurement of vent positions, tracking any migration over time.

Another theory: they were infrastructure for the siphon-walkers, built by whoever made the walkers (assuming the walkers were made), positioned to support their operation, their formations, their incomprehensible purpose. The structures might have been power systems or communication relays or simply markers designating the proper routes and positions the walkers should follow.

A third theory: they're older than the plateau's current configuration. They were built when the vent systems were positioned differently, and what looks like geometric precision now is actually decay, the remnants of a much more extensive network that has been reduced to a few surviving nodes. The plateau has changed dramatically—the vents migrate, the basalt erodes, the ecosystem shifts—and these ruins are the only surviving evidence of what was here ten thousand or a hundred thousand years ago.

The ceramic-or-something-else fragments are genuinely disturbing to materials scientists. They resist analysis not by being too hard or too reactive but by being undecided. Tested today, they show one molecular structure. Tested tomorrow, they show a different structure, not grossly different but shifted, altered in subtle ways. It's as if the material is unstable at fundamental levels, not decaying but transforming, cycling through a range of possible configurations without settling on any single one.

This shouldn't be possible with solid matter at standard temperatures and pressures. It suggests the material is either meta-stable, held in an energetically unfavorable state by some ongoing process, or it's responsive to something in the environment that varies day to day—magnetic fields, subtle radiation, the thermal cycle, something—and adjusts its structure accordingly.

Or it's not material in the standard sense but something that appears material, that occupies space and reflects light but isn't composed of atoms in normal configurations. This would require physics that isn't understood, matter that operates on principles outside standard models. But then, the siphon-walkers already suggest that. Their internal spirals already violate conservation laws. Their shed husks already show molecular machinery with no known function. The plateau has already demonstrated that standard models are incomplete here.

The voices in the vents are the most direct suggestion of previous inhabitants. They speak—phonetically, syntactically, with intention. They're not wind making vowel-like sounds. They're not pareidolia, the human tendency to hear patterns. They're speech, captured clearly on recordings, analyzed extensively, confirmed by multiple independent listeners.

But no language database matches them. No phonetic pattern corresponds to any known human language or any reconstructed proto-language. The sounds are human-makeable, but the combinations, the structures, the patterns are foreign.

So either:

- There was a language spoken here that has no descendants and left no other record, isolated on this plateau for so long that it evolved into something unrelated to any other language.

- The voices are not human but something else that convergently evolved speech, that has vocal anatomy similar enough to produce similar sounds but uses them according to different linguistic principles.

- The voices are not produced by vocal anatomy at all but by some process—mechanical, geological, technological—that generates phonetic sounds, possibly as communication, possibly as side-effect of some other operation.

- The voices are not contemporary but historical, somehow recorded in the vent systems and played back sporadically, echoes of conversations held centuries ago, preserved by mechanisms unknown in stone and heat and airflow.

The last possibility seems absurd until you consider that the siphon-walker husks appear to contain information encoded at molecular scales. If the husks are storage media, why couldn't the basalt be? Why couldn't the vent systems, with their complex geometries and thermal cycles, preserve patterns, encode data, store memory in the arrangement of stone and heat?

The conversations—if they are conversations—have patterns. Sometimes they sound instructional: a voice making a statement, pausing, continuing as if answering a question. Sometimes they sound like warnings: short sharp syllables, repeated, emphatic. Sometimes they sound contemplative: long flowing utterances with musical qualities, cadences that rise and fall like philosophical discourse.

Researchers have attempted to respond, speaking into vent openings during or after hearing voices. Nothing has answered directly, but there are reports of changes in pattern—voices using similar intonation to the researcher's speech, voices appearing in vents near where a researcher spoke hours earlier. Correlation, coincidence, or actual response is impossible to determine with such limited data.

One researcher became convinced the voices were trying to teach her something, that they were instructional, that sufficient attention would reveal their meaning. She spent weeks recording them, transcribing phonetically, analyzing patterns. She claimed to find structure: repeating phrases that might be vocabulary, longer sequences that varied in systematic ways suggesting grammar. She became certain she was close to understanding.

That's when she started hearing her name.

Not anything that sounded like her name—her actual name, in her language, pronounced clearly. She reported this immediately, disturbed but also excited—if the voices could say her name, they must be aware of her, must be capable of learning human language, must be trying to communicate. But it also meant they'd been listening, learning from the team's conversations, studying them.

The administration removed her because policy requires it when researchers report personalized contact with anomalous phenomena. But her recordings exist, and other team members have listened to them, and yes, there is something that sounds like her name, phonetically close enough that it's difficult to dismiss as pareidolia.

But only in the recordings she made. Other researchers recording the same vents at the same times heard different sounds. Either the voices are speaking to individuals, saying different things to different listeners, or each listener is interpreting ambiguous sounds in personal ways, finding in them what they're listening for.

Both possibilities are disturbing. Either the plateau is responsive to individual humans in sophisticated ways, tailoring its output to each person, or the plateau is affecting human perception in sophisticated ways, causing each person to experience something different from the same stimulus.

Or both. Or neither. Or the question is malformed because the plateau and its voices and the humans listening exist in some relationship that doesn't fit into categories like "objective" and "subjective," "real" and "perceived."

## The Researchers

The human presence is minimal, temporary, uncertain of its own purpose. Four to six people at any time, rotating every few months, armed with instruments and protocols and questions that multiply faster than answers.

They're scientists but they're becoming something else. Not mystics—the work remains rigorous, the data carefully gathered—but people who have recognized that their frameworks might be inadequate, that the tools they brought might not measure what matters, that understanding might require something other than analysis.

They talk about this in the evenings, in the courtyard between domes, drinking tea made with plateau water that tastes of minerals and distance. How do you study a system that includes you as a component? How do you observe something that observes back? How do you maintain objectivity about a place that makes objectivity feel like a category error?

The protocols help. Check the time, verify observations with teammates, document everything, maintain radio contact with base. These practices keep them tethered to consensus reality, to the shared world where distance is distance and time is time and matter behaves predictably.

But the protocols are also obviously insufficient. They don't account for the synchronicities that accumulate: thinking about checking a specific vent system and finding a siphon-walker already there, positioned as if waiting. Dreaming about spiral patterns and waking to find a vine's symbology has rearranged overnight into that exact pattern. Wondering what's in the deep vent systems and a thermal snake emerging at that moment, visible proof that something lives down there even if its nature remains unknown.

Coincidence, certainly. Frequency bias, probably—noticing the hits and forgetting the misses. But after three months of coincidence, of meaningful chance, of patterns that seem responsive, maintaining skepticism requires effort. Believing becomes easier than disbelieving, even for trained scientists who know how bias works and how pattern-recognition can deceive.

Some researchers lean into it, start treating the plateau as if it's alive, as if it responds to intention and attention. They ask questions aloud while working, not expecting answers but creating a habit of dialogue. They develop intuitions about where to go, when to observe, what to measure. Their data is good—sometimes better than researchers who maintain stricter objectivity, which is itself disturbing because it suggests the intuitive approach works, that treating the plateau as responsive yields better results than treating it as inert.

Other researchers resist, double down on protocol, insist on skepticism, attribute everything to natural causes even when the natural explanations are elaborate and strained. Their data is also good—careful, conservative, defensible. But they struggle more with the subjective effects, the temporal distortions, the sense of being watched by geology. Fighting the plateau's influence requires energy, creates stress, makes the rotation feel longer.

Most researchers exist between these poles, pragmatic about uncertainty. They don't know what the plateau is or whether it's aware or what the siphon-walkers are doing. They accept this not-knowing as the current state, continue gathering data, remain open to evidence while maintaining rigor. They speak to the plateau sometimes, treat it as inert other times, adjust their approach based on what seems effective in the moment.

The teamwork becomes intense in ways other field research doesn't produce. You're not just colleagues but witnesses to each other's sanity, validators of each other's perception. When someone reports something strange, you need to confirm or deny, need to help them determine if what they experienced was real-to-everyone or real-to-them or not-real-but-meaningful or some other category that standard epistemology doesn't include.

Trust is essential. You need to trust that your teammate will tell you if you're starting to interpret patterns that aren't there, and you need to trust that they won't dismiss genuine anomalies as hallucination or error. It's a delicate balance, and teams that can't find it don't last—they request early rotation or fragment into individuals who stop sharing observations.

The teams that work well develop a shared vocabulary for uncertainty: "definitely happened," "probably happened," "unsure if happened," "happened but possibly not the way I remember." They practice collective empiricism—multiple people observing the same phenomenon before conclusions are drawn, verification loops built into daily routine.

And they develop affection for the place, despite or because of its strangeness. The plateau becomes familiar even as it remains alien. You learn your favorite observation points, the routes you prefer, the times of day when the light does things you want to see again. You develop relationships with individual water-trees, specific vent systems, particular siphon-walkers (even though you can't distinguish them individually, you convince yourself this one has a distinctive gait, that one has unique chamber patterns).

You start wanting to protect it, not from physical harm—the plateau is resilient beyond any threat humans could pose—but from misunderstanding, from being reduced to data, from being explained in ways that explain away its essential strangeness. You want others to see it as you've seen it, want the reports to convey not just what's here but what it's like to be here, how it changes you, how it asks questions by being what it is.

The researchers who extend their rotations report that the plateau becomes clearer, but "clearer" doesn't mean "understood." It means you see the patterns more readily, you notice the relationships, you feel the integration of parts into the whole. The plateau becomes more itself to you, more present, more coherent as a system even as its purpose and origin remain mysterious.

But there's a danger in too much clarity. The researcher who heard her name had gone too far, had become too integrated, had started thinking in the plateau's patterns rather than using them as objects of study. She needed to be removed not because she was failing at research but because she was succeeding in a direction that leads away from human categories of understanding toward something else, something that might not be compatible with returning to the world outside.

All the researchers feel this pull to some degree. The plateau wants—not anthropomorphically, not with intention exactly, but as a function of what it is—wants you to stay, to watch longer, to notice more, to become part of its patterns. And the longer you stay, the more you want to stay, not from addiction or compulsion but from honest desire to see what happens next, to witness the next formation of siphon-walkers, to hear the next voice, to find the next pattern that almost makes sense.

Leaving requires effort. On extraction day, you pack equipment and notes and recordings, you board the transport, you descend from the plateau, and you feel—not relief exactly, not regret exactly, but something like the disorientation of code-switching, of changing contexts, of returning to a world that operates on different principles.

Some researchers never return. The plateau was their peak experience, the place where they encountered something genuinely other, and everything afterward feels flat by comparison. They stay in plateau research, analyzing samples, reviewing data, planning future expeditions, keeping themselves in the plateau's orbit even when physically distant.

Others return multiple times, rotation after rotation, becoming experts in its patterns, repositories of knowledge about its changes across seasons and years. They notice things newcomers miss, see significances in subtle alterations, provide continuity that allows long-term questions to be investigated.

And a few never go back, can't go back, find that even thinking about the plateau produces anxiety or longing or some complex emotion they don't have words for. For them, the plateau did something permanent, changed something fundamental, and maintaining the change requires staying away from the place that caused it.

## What Lives There, Finally

The question loops back to itself. What lives at Qom-Valai?

The siphon-walkers, the snakes, the birds, the beetles, the millipedes, the insects. The thermophile mats, the water-trees, the ferns, the vines. The voices, if they live. The markers and ruins, if they count. The humans, temporarily.

But also: the plateau itself, if a place can live. The integrated system of vent and stone and heat and moisture and life that functions as a coherent whole, that maintains itself, that changes and persists and does whatever it does with its pattern-making and heat-breathing and voice-producing.

And also: whatever made the walkers, if anything made them. Whatever speaks in the vents, if anything speaks. Whatever authored the ruins, if they were authored. Whatever the plateau is serving, if service is the right word, if there's purpose here beyond persistence.

And also: the questions themselves, living in the researchers' minds, propagating through reports and discussions, evolving as evidence accumulates, developing their own persistence independent of any answer.

What lives at Qom-Valai is multiplicity, is integration, is ambiguity that resists resolution. It's a place that contains within itself traces of intention and marks of design and behaviors that suggest awareness, but distributed across scales and substrates in ways that make identifying any single agent impossible.

It's a place that makes you question what "life" means, whether it requires metabolism or just persistence, whether it requires awareness or just responsiveness, whether the boundary between living and not-living is categorical or gradual.

It's a place that demonstrates organization without proving organization-toward-anything, that shows pattern without revealing pattern's purpose, that responds without confirming that response is reply rather than coincidence.

It's a place that has been here much longer than human presence and will continue much longer after human interest fades. The siphon-walkers will walk. The vents will breathe. The water will condense and evaporate. The plateau will continue its thermal cycle, its slow changes, its patient enactment of whatever function it serves.

And that continuation, that persistence, that patient being-what-it-is—that lives there most of all. Not as entity but as fact, as the baseline reality that includes everything else. The plateau lives in the sense that weather lives, that geology lives, that systems live when they maintain themselves across time through the interaction of their components.

But also possibly something more. Possibly awareness too distributed to localize, too patient to recognize, too different from human awareness to register as awareness at all. Possibly purpose too large to see from within it, too slow to distinguish from natural process, too alien to translate into human categories.

The researchers keep returning to wonder about this. Not deciding, not concluding, but wondering with increasing sophistication, with better questions, with more nuanced appreciation of how strange strangeness can be.

And the plateau receives their wondering, their presence, their instruments and protocols and theories, and continues unchanged or possibly slightly changed, possibly adjusting itself in ways too subtle to measure, possibly learning in whatever way a plateau learns, possibly just being, magnificently and completely and inexplicably, what it is.
