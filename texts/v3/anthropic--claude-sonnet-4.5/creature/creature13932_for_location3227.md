# The Inhabitants

## The People

They have forgotten that they once had a word for "individual." 

The inhabitants move through the chamber with a peculiar fluidity, bodies always slightly angled, shoulders leading, minimizing their profile. After a few years here, everyone develops the same gait—a sliding step that doesn't lift the foot entirely from the floor, that maintains constant contact with the surface so they can feel the chamber's breathing through their soles. New arrivals stomp, and the sound is jarring, aggressive. Within weeks they learn the shuffle, the whisper-walk.

Their eyes are enormous. Not uniformly—there's variation, as there always is—but the average pupil diameter is at least twice what you'd see Outside. In lamplight, their faces look perpetually startled, those dark pools reflecting the flame in points so bright they seem artificial. Children born here have the largest eyes, as if their bodies understood from the beginning what would be required. The sclera has darkened too, generations in, becoming grayish or faintly blue, so that the boundary between iris and white is less defined. They look at you from pools of shadow, and you understand that they're seeing more than you can—mapping the space by gradations of darkness you can't distinguish.

Their hands never stop moving. Even in conversation, even in rest, fingers trace patterns against thighs or palms, rehearsing weaving motions, maintaining the muscle memory that is their primary technology. The fingertips are calloused in specific places—the pads of the thumbs, the sides of the forefingers—from constant contact with fiber. Some of the older inhabitants have fingers that have actually begun to curve inward slightly, shaped by decades of the same motions, until their hands at rest look like they're already holding invisible threads.

They're thin, but not starved. The thinness is a compression, as if the space itself has gently pressed them into more efficient forms. Fat is rare here—the cold doesn't allow for it, or the diet doesn't support it, or both. You see the architecture of tendons and bone, the mechanics of the body made visible. But there's strength in the thinness, a wiriness that allows them to move easily through narrow gaps, to work in cramped spaces between root bundles, to curl into the small hollows they sleep in.

Clothing is layered textile, never dyed—or rather, dyed only by contact with the walls, so it takes on the same range of colors: pale browns, grayish greens, the occasional streak of darker amber where something has bled from the plant tissue. The weave is tight enough to resist moisture but loose enough to breathe, and every garment is constantly in the process of gentle decay and repair. You never see anyone sewing, exactly, but you notice that a tear from yesterday has been rewoven by today, the repair so seamless it's impossible to find unless you remember where the damage was.

They wear their clothes until the cloth becomes an extension of their skin, until the boundary between textile and flesh is more conceptual than actual. When fabric finally rots beyond repair, they strip the fibers and reweave them into something else. Nothing is wasted. A shirt becomes a bag becomes a cistern liner becomes mulch for the chamber floor, where it feeds the smaller fauna and eventually returns to the walls.

Their voices are quiet but carry strangely in the chamber's acoustics. They've learned to pitch their speech to match the space, finding frequencies that travel without echoing, that slide into the ambient whisper without disrupting it. Conversations happen in near-murmurs, and yet you can hear them clearly from ten meters away. Shouting is not just rude but physically uncomfortable—the chamber seems to reject it, the sound bouncing back in harsh fragments that make everyone wince.

They speak a language that has absorbed technical terms from the chamber itself. Words for different textures of wall tissue. Words for the breathing phases—not just "inhalation" and "exhalation" but finer distinctions, the quarter-breaths and the rare deep cycles that happen once a day. Words for the various fauna, not names exactly but descriptors that capture movement patterns or feeding behaviors. The grammar has shifted to accommodate a world where cause and effect are not always clear, where the subject of a sentence might be the walls or the water or a collective of organisms too numerous to count.

They have no leader, or they all lead in rotation, or leadership is the wrong framework entirely. Decisions happen through a process of gradual consensus that looks like no process at all. Someone begins repairing a water channel, and others join or they don't, and by the end of the day either the work is done or it isn't, and either way no one seems troubled. The urgencies of Outside don't apply here. Hunger is managed through storage. Thirst is impossible—water is everywhere. Shelter is provided by the walls. What remains is maintenance, and maintenance happens when it happens, performed by whoever happens to be near.

The old ones—those who have lived here thirty, forty, fifty years—are almost invisible. Not literally, but they've learned a stillness that makes them blend with the chamber so completely that you don't notice them until they move. They sit near the pools or along the walls, and the fauna moves over them without concern, treating them as features of the landscape. Their skin has taken on the same faint sheen as the plant tissue, whether from constant moisture or some exchange of chemistry. When they die, the transition is so subtle that sometimes days pass before anyone realizes. The breathing person becomes the still person becomes the absorbed person, and the only marker is a temporary absence at the weaving circle.

Children are rare but not absent. Conception here is difficult—the cold, perhaps, or the diet, or something in the water. But babies do arrive, born in the darkness to mothers who labor quietly, attended by whoever happens to be nearby. The infants are wrapped in the softest textiles, kept close to their mothers' bodies for warmth, and they emerge into the world already adapted to low light, to constant damp, to the rhythms of the breathing walls. They rarely cry. The first sounds they learn to make are the clicks and whispers that soothe the fauna.

By the time they can walk, children are already reading the walls, already learning to predict the opening of the living doorways, already participating in the weaving. Play and work are not distinguished. A child making random patterns with fiber is practicing. A child touching the walls is learning. A child sitting motionless watching the fauna is doing crucial research. They're raised by proximity rather than instruction, becoming competent through absorption, and by adolescence they're indistinguishable from adults except by size.

There's almost no violence. Not because of prohibition—there are no prohibitions, no rules spoken or written—but because violence would disturb the fauna, and disturbing the fauna creates cascading disruptions that make life harder for everyone. Conflict happens through avoidance, through choosing to occupy different areas of the chamber, and it resolves through time and the gentle pressure of a community that can't afford fracture. Grudges don't last in a place where you might need anyone's help at any moment, where survival is corporate rather than individual.

They touch each other constantly. Not intimately, necessarily, but functionally. A hand on a shoulder to indicate presence in the darkness. Fingers brushing fingers when passing a tool. Bodies pressed close during the cold phases, sharing warmth without ceremony or significance. Touch here is communication, navigation, comfort, and pragmatism all at once. Personal space is a luxury that belonged Outside.

Some of them remember Outside. The ones who came in as adults still carry fragmentary knowledge of sun, of horizon, of distances measured in days rather than meters. They tell stories sometimes, trying to explain sky to children who have only ever known ceiling, trying to describe dryness to people who have never been dry. But the vocabulary fails, or the memories fade, and gradually the stories become myths, interesting but unreal, tales from a place that might never have existed.

Others were born here and will die here and have never felt curiosity about Outside. The entrance fissure is just a geological feature, a crack in the wall that sometimes admits new fauna, new people, new tools. What lies beyond it is less real to them than the deepest unmapped regions of the chamber. Their world is complete. It breathes, it provides, it makes demands they know how to meet. Why would they want more?

There's a woman—there's always a woman like this, or a man, or several—who tends the central lamp with particular devotion. She's there every day at the same phase of the breathing cycle, trimming the wick with a blade of volcanic glass, adding oil with a small bone ladle, adjusting the height of the flame to maintain exactly the same light level. She never speaks about why she does this. She doesn't need to. The lamp is her conversation with the chamber, her offering, her way of being useful in a place where usefulness is the highest form of belonging.

There's a man who can predict the fruiting bodies. He claims no special knowledge, but he's always near the wall when they emerge, always ready to harvest them at the optimal moment. Others watch him, trying to learn the signs he reads, but if there are signs, they're too subtle. He just knows, or says he knows, or the walls tell him in some language of chemical gradient or temperature shift that his body has learned to parse. He shares the harvest without ceremony, distributing the pale shelf-fungi to whoever is nearby, and in this way he's become a hub of informal redistribution.

There's a child—perhaps eight, perhaps twelve, age is hard to judge—who spends hours watching the smallest fauna, the many-legged things that flow across the walls in their thousands. She's learned to recognize individuals, or claims to, tracking them by minute variations in their movement patterns. She's mapping their routes, their preferences, building a taxonomy that exists only in her head. No one asks her to do this. No one suggests it's useful. But when the elders need to know if a section of wall is healthy, they ask the child, and she tells them by describing which fauna are present, which are thriving, which are absent.

There's a couple who have been here so long that they've begun to look alike, or perhaps they always looked alike and that's why they paired. They work in perfect synchronization, one beginning a task and the other completing it without discussion, their four hands moving through the weaving or the channel-clearing or the fiber-harvesting as if controlled by a single mind. They sleep in the same root-hollow, their breathing synchronized with each other and with the chamber, and when one dies—someday, eventually—the other will follow within a breath-cycle or two. This is understood. No one will be surprised.

There are individuals who have developed strange specializations. One person has become expert at reading the rare mood shifts of the large fauna, able to predict their routes, to clear space when necessary, to interpret their sounds in ways that prevent collisions or disturbances. Another has learned to work with the most difficult fibers, the ones that are nearly too brittle or too slippery, creating textiles of extraordinary fineness for no purpose except the satisfaction of the craft itself. Another has memorized the entire water system, all the channels and pools and weeping walls, and can diagnose disruptions by touch, by the sound of flow, fixing blockages that no one else could even locate.

But specialization is always partial. Everyone weaves. Everyone maintains channels. Everyone shares in the basic work, because the settlement is too small and the chamber too demanding for rigid division of labor. You're a specialist and you're also general labor, and this is not a contradiction but the only viable arrangement.

They dream of the chamber. Even those who came from Outside, who once dreamed of other places, find their sleep increasingly colonized by images of walls and water and the endless small fauna. The dreams are not symbolic. They're literal, rehearsals of navigation and maintenance, processing the day's observations, integrating new knowledge of how the chamber works. You dream of a new fruiting site and wake to find one has emerged exactly where you imagined. You dream of a blockage in a channel and rise to discover it's real. The boundary between the chamber's rhythms and the rhythms of sleep has become permeable.

Some of them hum. Not songs, exactly, but tonal patterns that match the chamber's acoustic signature, that resonate with the breathing cycle, that seem to soothe the fauna or please the walls or simply mark time in a place where time has no other markers. The humming is contagious. One person starts and another joins and soon there are five or six voices weaving a drone that has no beginning or end, that you could join or leave without disrupting, that continues as long as anyone wants to maintain it and stops without ceremony when they don't.

They've developed a relationship with cold that is not acceptance and not suffering but something else—a recognition that the cold is a feature of life here, as fundamental as breathing, and that struggling against it is pointless. They don't seek warmth except in the practical ways: layered clothing, shared body heat. They don't complain about it. They don't even seem to notice it anymore, except when new arrivals mention it, and then they look briefly puzzled, as if trying to remember what warmth felt like, before returning to their work.

Their immune systems have made accommodations. Everyone who stays more than a month goes through a period of illness—fevers, digestive chaos, skin eruptions—as their bodies adjust to the chamber's particular ecology, its unique microbiomes, its alien chemistry. Some don't survive this transition. Most do, and afterward they're rarely sick. The microorganisms of this place have integrated with their gut flora, their skin flora, creating symbioses that protect against the infections of Outside while making them increasingly incompatible with the world beyond the fissure.

Those who leave and then return always seem diminished. They bring stories of sun and horizon, but the stories sound hollow, and their bodies have lost the efficient thinness, the perfect calibration to the chamber. They struggle to see in the darkness, to hear the subtle acoustic cues, to move without disturbing the fauna. Some readapt. Others leave again, accepting that they've become people of Outside, that the chamber has released them. There's no judgment in this. The walls take whom they take and release whom they release.

And always, underneath every individual variation, there's the shared quality of being inhabited-with. These people are not alone in their bodies. They carry the chamber's microbiomes, yes, but also something less tangible—a sense of being nodes in a larger network, organs in a distributed body that includes the walls and the water and the fauna and the other people. They act with the unselfconsciousness of cells in tissue, performing their functions without existential crisis, without the burden of meaning-making that plagues life Outside.

When they sit together in the lamplight—and they do this often, gathering at the three lamp-stations for no purpose except proximity—they don't always speak. Sometimes they just weave in parallel, or sit with their hands tracing patterns, or watch the fauna move in its endless industry. The silence is not awkward. It's the chamber's silence, which they've learned to inhabit, which has become more communicative than speech.

And in this silence, in the darkness, in the cold, in the constant damp, they persist. They maintain. They adapt. They belong to the breathing walls with a completeness that people Outside—people who own property and assert selves and imagine futures—could not comprehend. They are citizens of the chamber, and the chamber is citizen of them, and the distinction has long since ceased to matter.
