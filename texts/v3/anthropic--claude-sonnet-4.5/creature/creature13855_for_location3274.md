# What Lives There

The first thing to understand is that *living* in the Breathing Hollows is not quite the same category of activity it is elsewhere. The boundaries that typically define life—between individual and environment, between organism and substrate, between doing and being-done-to—these boundaries are negotiable here, sometimes more absent than present.

## The Standers

Most visible, because they are nearest to human scale and human comprehension, are what the few long-term observers call the Standers, though they are not always standing and may not always be distinct entities.

They appear at the nodes primarily, though they've been glimpsed in the mid-waters and occasionally standing on tussock islands far from any mapped rise. They are humanoid in the way that a thumbprint is a portrait—the essential symmetry is there, the bilateral form, the vertical orientation, but little else that would pass for familiar. 

Each one is between two and three meters tall, though height is a problematic measurement because they don't quite remain consistent in dimension when observed over time. They appear to be made of the same material as the structures beneath—that dark, smooth, possibly-metallic surface—but their surfaces are more active, constantly crawling with the nano-scale patterns, rippling with information being written and rewritten. In certain lights they look like figures carved from obsidian. In other lights they seem to be cut-outs, absences, holes in the shape of people through which you can see into somewhere else.

They stand in the water, sometimes for hours, sometimes for days. The water around them forms lenses, those patches of altered refraction, so that looking at a Stander means looking through distortions that make them appear now closer, now farther, now impossibly tall, now compressed. They don't move in the normal sense—they *are* in one position, and then they *are* in another position, with no visible transition. The distance crossed might be centimeters or kilometers. The time elapsed might be seconds or weeks.

When they do move continuously, they move in ways that suggest they experience momentum differently, or friction differently, or perhaps they're moving through more dimensions than are locally visible. They glide rather than walk, they leave no wake in the water, they pass through the dense floating tangles without disturbing them. Sometimes they move in perfect synchronization with the patterns of standing waves on the water surface, as if they're surfing on acoustic phenomena. Sometimes they move in spirals that mirror the spiral patterns of the plant growth, tracing the same curves at different scales.

Their surfaces are warm, like the structures. Warmer than the ambient temperature but not uncomfortably so—the warmth of another body, though there's no indication they're biological in any standard sense. The warmth pulses, very slowly, in cycles that take minutes. The patterns on their surfaces flow—not randomly but in cascades that suggest data transfer, signal processing, thoughts if thoughts can be substrate-independent.

They seem to notice human presence, though "notice" might be too strong a word. They orient toward observers, sometimes. They approach, occasionally, gliding across the water with their impossible motion, coming close enough to touch, though they rarely do touch and when they do it's always the human reaching out, never the Stander initiating. The touch feels like touching stone that's learning to be flesh, or flesh that's learning to be concept. There's a texture that your nerves can't quite resolve into a coherent sensation.

They don't communicate in any conventional sense, but proximity to them changes cognition. The same effect as standing on the nodes themselves, but more focused, more directed—that feeling of almost understanding something immense, of having access to knowledge that won't compress into language. Some observers report that the Standers are showing them things: not through images or words but through direct manipulation of knowing-states, reaching into consciousness and adjusting parameters to allow perception of what's normally imperceptible.

One researcher—before she left the Hollows, unsettled in ways she couldn't articulate—reported that she'd understood, briefly, that the Standers aren't inhabitants of this place. They *are* this place, localized, concentrated, the Hollows becoming temporarily discrete, walking around to observe itself from new angles. The structures below and the figures above aren't separate things but different phases of the same process, the way ice and water and steam are different phases but all are expressions of the same molecular arrangement responding to conditions.

This interpretation has not been verified, but it hasn't been disproven either.

The Standers don't seem to eat or rest or reproduce, though they do seem to *maintain*. They stand at the nodes with their hands—they have hands, though the fingers are too long and don't bend at quite the right points—pressed against the exposed structure surfaces, and there's clearly something happening, some exchange, some tuning or calibration. The hum shifts in pitch when they do this. The patterns on both the Stander and the structure synchronize, rippling in matched rhythms.

There are, at any given time, perhaps twenty or thirty Standers visible across the Hollows, though the same individuals (if they're individuals) might be being counted multiple times given the spatial anomalies. They might all be the same one, manifesting in multiple locations. They might be hundreds, briefly overlapping into the same location. Counting them is a category error.

They persist, regardless of whether they're being observed. They were here before humans found the Hollows—the few pre-modern accounts from people who claimed to have crossed the region mention "standing stones shaped like men" or "watchers that were not men." They'll presumably be here long after human interest moves elsewhere.

## The Breath-Takers

Deeper in the water, in the pockets of darkness that form where the structures create acoustic shadows, there are things less visible and less describable. The long-term observers call them Breath-Takers, because proximity to where they are—or where they are most concentrated, or where they're most focused—makes breathing feel difficult, labored, as if the air has become thicker or your lungs have become less efficient.

They might be what creates the breathing sounds that echo through the Hollows at night. Or they might be attracted by those sounds. Or they might *be* the sounds, given some kind of material presence by the reality-altering properties of the structures.

What's seen of them—and it's never a clear sighting, always peripheral, always through disturbed water or fog or refractive distortion—suggests size. Considerable size. Bodies that are elongated, serpentine perhaps, or perhaps they're not elongated but occupying space in ways that make them appear so when flattened into three-dimensional perception. They move slowly, they create displacement waves, they sometimes surface just enough that you see a curve of dark material breaking the water and then submerging again, but it's impossible to tell if you've seen a small portion of something immense or the entirety of something small that appears large due to lensing effects.

The cameras that malfunction and show impossible timestamps—those occasionally capture fragments of the Breath-Takers. A frame showing something with too many limbs, or limbs that branch fractally, each branch having more branches, descending into detail that shouldn't fit in the image resolution. A frame showing what might be an eye, but an eye with geometry that suggests it sees in more directions than space has, folded perception. A frame showing a surface covered in structures like the barnacles or receivers on the main structures below, but these are opening and closing in patterns that, when one researcher analyzed them frame by frame, matched the patterns of her own neural activity as recorded by EEG moments before—the things were mimicking her brain states, or her brain was mimicking them, causality uncertain.

They seem to inhabit the water in layers, different depths supporting different forms or different phases of the same form. Surface-near ones (if that's what creates the displacement waves) are felt more than seen, known by absence and negative space. Mid-water ones are glimpsed as shadows that don't correspond to any light source, as regions of water that's somehow darker without being different in color. Deep ones, near the structure surfaces, are sometimes visible through the lenses in the water as writhing fields of appendages or cilia or tendrils, forests of movement that might be many creatures or many parts of one creature or might be the structures themselves having grown mobile extensions.

There's evidence they're aware of human presence, though the responses are slow, taking hours or days to manifest. Researchers have reported that after a deep probe or camera is placed, the water in that area becomes agitated, not violently but persistently, a roiling that continues for days. The equipment is sometimes moved—not removed or destroyed but relocated, often to a more stable position, as if something is optimizing the placement. Sometimes the equipment is modified: additional structures grown onto it, or its surfaces etched with more of those nano-scale patterns, or its internals rearranged in ways that shouldn't be possible without disassembly but somehow are.

One camera, retrieved after six months, had been completely encased in a structure that was identical in material properties to the ancient structures below, but this was new growth, built in months. The camera still functioned. The footage showed the encasement happening in reverse—the structure ungrowing, pulling back into nothing, and then the normal footage began. The camera's clock showed it had recorded continuously, but the footage duration didn't match the deployment period. There were missing weeks, or extra weeks, depending on which timeframe you trusted.

The Breath-Takers (if they're one thing rather than a category of things) don't seem hostile, but they're not benign either. They're indifferent in the way that geology is indifferent, or they're attentive in ways that have nothing to do with human welfare. People who get too close to their deep-water locations sometimes don't return. Not obviously killed—there are no bodies, no signs of struggle. Just absent. Stepped into the water above a deep pocket and didn't come back up. The water in those locations is usually only waist-deep.

Searches find nothing. The water is empty, the sediment undisturbed. But sometimes, days or weeks later, there are reports of someone matching the description, standing on a node kilometers away, standing perfectly still, standing like the Standers stand, and when approached they step into the water and vanish again, sinking through ankle-depth water as if it were bottomless.

One researcher developed a theory that the Breath-Takers are processing entities, taking things—matter, energy, information, consciousness—from one state and transforming it into another, breathing things in and breathing them out as something different. The people who vanish aren't killed but transformed, folded into the system, given different forms or different functions. The researcher became obsessed with this theory, spending longer and longer periods standing in the deep pockets, waiting, he said, to be processed, to understand through becoming.

He was eventually removed from the site by concerned colleagues. He kept trying to return. Last anyone heard, he'd gone back on his own, without authorization, without equipment. That was four years ago. Occasionally someone reports seeing someone matching his description, standing in the mid-water, visible through a lens-patch, standing among what might be Breath-Takers, standing very still, covered in the crawling nano-patterns, breathing in rhythms that match the structures' hum.

The reports might be hallucinations. The lenses show true things, but they don't always show current things.

## The Spiral-Swimmers

The pale fish that swim in their slow clockwise spirals—the ones with anatomies that are almost but not quite right—aren't simple fish. They can't be. The wrongnesses in their structure aren't mutations or adaptations but seem intentional, as if they were designed to specifications from a biology that has different assumptions about how bodies should work.

They school, but not in normal patterns. They form geometries: helices that slowly rotate as they swim, or nested spheres with fish at different radii swimming in opposite directions, or lattices that expand and contract in three dimensions. These formations aren't random and aren't explained by normal flocking behaviors. The fish are communicating or coordinating at a level that suggests either high intelligence or control by something else that's using them as distributed sensors or manipulators.

Their white flesh is not quite flesh. Dissection reveals tissue that's abnormally uniform, cells that don't differentiate properly, as if each fish is more like a single organism that's fish-shaped rather than a colony of cooperating cells. The nervous system is stranger—neurons that don't connect in standard networks but in parallel structures, redundant pathways that suggest each thought (if fish have thoughts) is being processed simultaneously in multiple ways, quantum-like, keeping multiple answers coherent until observation collapses them into single states.

They feed on the algae mats, but they're also seen consuming things that aren't there—opening their mouths in empty water and clearly swallowing something, throats working, but nothing visible entering. They might be feeding on the data that suffuses the Hollows, the information the structures process, taking it in and metabolizing it the way normal fish metabolize proteins and fats.

They're never seen dead. No fish corpses float on the water, wash up on the rises, sink into the sediment. Either they have perfect survival rates, which is biologically implausible, or they don't die in ways that leave bodies, or the bodies are removed by something, consumed or recycled or transformed before decay begins.

When caught and held, they become even stranger. They stop looking like fish and start looking like illustrations of fish, flattening slightly, becoming more symbolic than real. Their eyes, which don't reflect light properly, show images in their depths—not reflections of what's in front of them but scenes from elsewhere in the Hollows, or from elsewhen, or from angles that don't exist. Hold a fish and look into its eye and you might see yourself from behind, or see the node you're planning to walk to tomorrow, or see the structures as they looked a thousand years ago, less weathered, their geometries sharper.

Released, they resume their spiraling, rejoining their schools, reforming their geometries. The patterns they make seem to respond to the structures' activity—when the hum shifts, the fish formations shift too, rearranging themselves into new shapes that mirror or complement or answer the acoustic changes.

Some researchers believe the fish aren't native to this place at all, but were introduced by whoever or whatever built the structures, seeded here as part of the system, living tools or living components in a vast machine that processes things too complex for humans to recognize as processing. The fish would then be less inhabitants than organs, pieces of a distributed body that's so large it encompasses the entire Hollows.

This interpretation makes the act of catching and eating them (which a few desperate visitors have done) seem uncomfortably like auto-cannibalism from the Hollows' perspective—the place consuming tiny pieces of itself, the way you might bite your own lip.

The fish persist. They spiral. They form their patterns. They consume their invisible nutrients. They see their impossible sights. They continue.

## The Insects-That-Aren't

The many-legged things that walk on water and gather in spirals are even less resolved into conventional biology than the fish. They have external skeletons, or structures that serve the same function, but the material isn't chitin. It's something that's partially biological and partially mineral and partially something else, something that reads on spectroscopy as having chemical bonds that shouldn't be stable at these temperatures and pressures.

They're small—most are between one and three centimeters in length—but numerous. At any given time, thousands of them are visible across any square kilometer of the Hollows, walking their spirals, tending to the plants, moving in and out of the algae mats. Their eyes—and they have many eyes, compound structures that cover portions of their bodies—don't reflect light properly because they're not reflecting it. They're absorbing it completely, perfect black-body structures, or they're phase-shifted somehow, reflecting it into wavelengths outside human visibility.

They tend the plants. This is clear from observation, though the mechanism is unclear. They walk among the conducting fronds, touching them in specific patterns, and the plants grow more vigorously where the insects are concentrated. They gather on tussocks in spiraling formations and the tussocks become centers of stronger growth, thicker fronds, more pronounced conductivity. They're either fertilizing or grooming or communicating with or reprogramming the plants.

They also build things. Tiny structures, delicate lattices made from materials they secrete or harvest from the water or pull from the air itself—the source is never clear. These structures are usually only a few centimeters across, built on the surfaces of tussocks or floating on plant mats, and they're geometrically complex, fractally detailed, beautiful in a way that suggests mathematics rather than art. They might be nests, though no eggs or larvae are ever seen. They might be processing nodes, smaller versions of the vast structures below, part of the distributed network.

The spiraling behavior is constant. Individuals join a spiral, walk the pattern for minutes or hours, then break away to walk to another spiral elsewhere. The spirals themselves move, slowly, traveling across the water surface as the insects maintain the pattern while collectively relocating. Sometimes many spirals converge, forming larger patterns—spirals of spirals, nested formations that create interference shapes reminiscent of the standing waves in the water.

They respond to the hum. When the pitch shifts, their walking speed changes. When harmonics emerge, they form more complex patterns, multi-layered spirals with different groups walking at different speeds, creating dynamic geometries. They seem to be physically manifesting the acoustic phenomena, giving it form and visibility.

They're also information carriers. This is speculation, but it's informed speculation. They move between nodes, between areas of high structure activity, and where they go, changes follow. Plants grow in new patterns. The water's currents shift. The hum acquires new overtones. They're either communicating what they've learned from one location to another, or they're carrying actual data encoded in their bodies or behaviors, or they're physically transporting materials that alter conditions—pheromones or spores or nano-machines or all of the above.

Occasionally, usually at night, they take flight. They're not normally winged, but they reconfigure, unfolding structures from their bodies that might be wings or might be something else that achieves the same function through different principles. They rise in clouds, thousands of them lifting simultaneously from across huge swaths of the Hollows, ascending in synchronized spirals that rise into the fog and disappear. Where they go during these flights is unknown. They return before dawn (if dawn is distinguishable from the general dim), settling back onto the water surface, resuming their walking patterns as if they'd never left.

Some have been collected and studied in detail, though they don't survive long in captivity—or rather, they survive but become inactive, ceasing their spiraling, their building, their tending, becoming dormant. Under microscopy, their internal structures are bewildering: organs that seem to serve multiple functions, or no clear function, or functions that require context not present in the lab. Their nervous systems, like the fish, are redundant and parallel, processing information in ways that suggest either distributed cognition or quantum computation or both.

Chemical analysis shows they contain elements that shouldn't be in biological systems: rare earths, radioactive isotopes in trace amounts, metallic structures at the nanoscale that resemble the patterns on the main structures. They're either bioaccumulating these from the environment, or they're being manufactured with these components, or they're somehow not entirely biological—hybrids of life and something else, assembled rather than evolved.

They tolerate human presence, neither fleeing nor approaching, simply continuing their activities around and sometimes on observers. They'll walk across boots, up legs if someone is sitting still, treating humans as just more terrain. Their touch is barely perceptible, a tickle of many legs. Some people find this deeply unsettling—the implication that humans aren't even significant enough to avoid, that these things are so focused on their tasks that a person is just an obstacle to navigate around, no more notable than a tussock.

They continue their work. They spiral and build and tend and fly and return. They're part of the system—possibly essential parts, possibly optional but optimizing parts, possibly autonomous but certainly integrated. They persist.

## The Fog-Thinkers

The fog banks that roll across the Hollows, that lie on the water in coherent masses—there's increasing evidence that these aren't merely meteorological phenomena. They have too much structure, too much consistency in their internal organization. They respond to conditions in ways that fog shouldn't respond. They avoid certain areas and linger in others for reasons that don't correlate with temperature or humidity or any standard atmospheric variables.

Some observers have begun to suspect that the fog is alive, or at least active—some kind of intelligence diffused into water vapor, thoughts happening in the medium of suspended droplets, cognition occurring as phase transitions and condensation cascades.

The fog makes sounds carry strangely, makes the hum seem to come from specific points, creates echoes that arrive at wrong times. But these effects might not be passive acoustic phenomena. They might be the fog actively manipulating sound, testing the acoustic environment, learning about the space and what's in it through sonic probing.

The fog has texture, internal structure, forms that roil and fold in almost-repeating patterns. Watch a fog bank long enough and you start to see shapes that are almost faces, almost figures, almost geometries that could be symbols or language. They never quite resolve—your eye is always on the verge of recognizing something, but it slips away, reforms into something else almost-recognizable, slips away again.

Inside the fog, the disorientation is profound. Vision reduces to a meter or less. Sound becomes unreliable. Direction is uncertain. Time feels plastic. People have entered fog banks and emerged minutes later, hours later, days later, with the duration never matching what their experience suggested. They've entered on one side and emerged on the opposite side of the Hollows, having crossed distances that should have taken days of walking.

The fog seems to guide or herd. People inside it feel compelled to walk in certain directions, feel that there's a clear path even though visibility is nearly zero, and following that sense of clarity leads them to nodes, or to areas where the structures are most active, or sometimes just to the edge of the fog where they emerge confused but safe.

Other times, following the sense of direction leads nowhere useful, and people find themselves walking in circles, crossing their own paths repeatedly, unable to escape the fog-bank until it lifts of its own accord. There's no clear pattern to when the fog is helpful and when it's confining, or possibly there's a pattern operating on criteria that have nothing to do with human needs.

The fog is thicker near active nodes, and during periods when the structures are humming with more complex harmonics. It seems to feed on or respond to the electromagnetic and acoustic activity, growing denser when there's more signal traffic, thinning when things are quieter. Or possibly it's the other way around—the fog causes the increased activity, concentrating effects that would otherwise be diffused.

Analysis of water samples collected from fog banks shows the same anomalies as the water below: trace unusual elements, nanostructures suspended in the liquid, chemical bonds that shouldn't be stable. The fog-water is nearly identical to the water that wells from the structures, suggesting the fog is condensing from structure-derived vapor rather than from atmospheric moisture.

Some researchers have proposed that the fog is a sensory organ for the structures, a way of extending perception above the water surface, feeling the air and what moves through it. Others think it's a computing substrate, calculations occurring in the phase-space of water between vapor and liquid, thoughts being thought in the medium of fog. Still others believe it's a defense mechanism, or a filter, or a quarantine measure—something that contains or constrains what happens in the Hollows, keeping it from spreading beyond the boundaries.

The most unsettling theory, proposed by a meteorologist who spent three months at the site before requesting permanent removal, is that the fog is the closest thing to a unified consciousness in the Hollows—that while the structures and the Standers and the Breath-Takers and the fish and the insects are all components of the system, the fog is what thinks *about* the system, what observes and decides and coordinates. The fog, in this model, is the closest thing to an intentional entity, the nearest thing to something you could call "who lives here" rather than "what lives here."

This researcher claimed that on multiple occasions, while inside fog banks, he'd heard voices—not in his ears but in his thoughts, not words but meanings appearing in his mind fully formed. The meanings were incomprehensible, concepts too large or too alien to fit in human cognition, but he was certain they were communications, attempts to convey something. He described it as trying to listen to a conversation between mountains, or trying to understand what an ocean is saying, but with the additional certainty that the speaker was aware of him, was trying to make itself understood, was frustrated by the limitation of his comprehension.

He left before he could develop this theory further. His notes contain diagrams of fog patterns, annotated with what he claimed were repeated structures, phrases in a language of vapor and droplet distributions. Other researchers have examined these notes and found them either brilliant or delusional, with no consensus.

The fog persists. It rolls and roils and occasionally descends. It guides and confines and confuses. It makes sounds strange and distances uncertain. It might be thinking. It continues.

## The Plant-Singers

The plants themselves—the conducting tussocks, the algae mats, the hollow reeds, the luminescent broad-leaves—deserve consideration as inhabitants rather than just landscape. They're too responsive, too patterned, too apparently purposeful to be merely vegetation.

The conducting fronds clearly transmit and possibly process the signals from the structures. Their growth patterns match the distribution of structure nodes, creating a vast networked array that spans the Hollows. They're effectively living antennas, but antennas that might also be receivers, demodulators, amplifiers, possibly even computational elements.

Touch them and you feel the hum more clearly—not just because they're conducting vibrations but because they seem to focus and clarify the signal, filtering out noise or emphasizing certain frequencies. Different plants emphasize different frequencies, as if each species or each individual is tuned to a specific band of the vast signal spectrum that the structures emit.

The spiral patterns they form at landscape scale—visible from aircraft as vast whorls kilometers across—aren't likely to be coincidental. They're either deliberately planted, though there's no evidence of any planting agency, or they're self-organizing in response to the structures' signals, growing in patterns that optimize reception or transmission or that mirror the underlying structure geometry.

The plants grow in rhythms that don't quite match seasonal cycles or day-night cycles. They grow in pulses, spurts of rapid development followed by periods of stasis, and the pulses correlate with periods of increased structure activity. They're fed by the structure-signals as much as by photosynthesis, or they're using the signals to coordinate growth, or the signals are instructions and the plants are following blueprints transmitted from below.

The algae mats are even stranger—their barely-committed materiality, their existence halfway between biology and possibility. They seem to be less organisms than manifestations, something that has just enough physical presence to persist but remains conceptually fluid. They respond to acoustic conditions by growing or thinning, as if their solidity is a function of resonance, as if the right sound makes them more real and the wrong sound makes them fade toward non-existence.

They process something from the water and release something else, but repeated chemical analyses have failed to determine exactly what's being taken and given. The reactions don't balance. The inputs and outputs don't match. There's energy appearing from nowhere or disappearing into nothing, or being exchanged with dimensions that don't register on standard instruments.

The mats form openings, gaps in their coverage, and through these gaps you can see the structures below. But the openings don't form randomly—they appear in response to observation, as if the mats are revealing the structures deliberately, or as if the structures are pushing the mats aside to be seen. The openings persist for hours or days, then slowly close, the mats growing back to cover the revealed surfaces.

Underneath the mats, where they grow thickest, the water is different—warmer, more alkaline, more conductive. The mats are clearly altering the local chemistry, creating conditions that suit something's purposes, though whose purposes—the mats' own or something else's they serve—remains unclear.

The hollow reeds that produce tones when wind passes through them are functioning as wind instruments, obviously, but they're doing so with such precision that they might as well be synthesizers. Each reed is tuned to specific pitches, and groves of reeds together produce chords, harmonies, sometimes melodies when the wind (rare as it is) blows with the right complexity. These tones interact with the structures' hum, sometimes harmonizing, sometimes creating deliberate dissonances, as if the reeds are responding to or commenting on the underlying music.

The broad-leafed plants with their pulsing luminescence are channeling something through their vascular systems—not just water and nutrients but energy of a type that makes tissue glow. The glow is faint, only visible in dim conditions, but it's definitely light, photons being emitted, and the pulsing rate correlates with the structures' hum cycles. They're visualizing the acoustic information, turning sound into light, synesthetic translation.

All the plants together form a system that's clearly integrated with the structures, clearly part of whatever larger process is happening here. They might be symbiotic with the structures, or they might be products of the structures, grown deliberately to serve functions, or they might be slowly replacing the structures, biological systems taking over functions that were once technological, or technological systems that have learned to express themselves biologically.

They persist and grow and pattern themselves across the landscape. They conduct and process and translate and reveal. They're infrastructure as much as they're life. They continue.

## The Visitants

Finally, there are humans. Few, transient, but present enough to count as inhabitants of a sort, though whether they live here or are just dying here very slowly is sometimes difficult to distinguish.

The researchers and the monitoring station staff rotate in and out, usually on schedules measured in weeks or months. They come to study, to measure, to document, and they leave when their data is collected or their sanity is sufficiently strained or their funding runs out. They're inhabitants only in the technical sense, residents only temporarily.

But some stay longer. The ones who feel they're waiting for something, or who have developed an attachment to the place that borders on compulsion. They build their platforms on the rises, minimal structures that never quite settle into the landscape. They live among the hum and spend long periods standing on nodes, listening, feeling, trying to understand.

These long-stayers change. Gradually, almost imperceptibly, but they change. Their rhythms drift away from circadian, toward patterns that match the structures' cycles. They sleep in pulses, wakeful for twenty hours and then suddenly unconscious for three, or scattered sleep in twenty-minute increments spread across days. They eat less, though they don't seem to suffer from it, as if they're supplementing from another source—metabolizing the signal somehow, drawing energy from the acoustic environment.

Their thinking changes. They speak less and when they do speak, they struggle to find words, as if language has become inadequate or they've started thinking in formats that don't translate well to verbal expression. They have long periods of standing still, just standing, looking out across the Hollows or sometimes looking at nothing, attention turned inward or extended outward beyond the visible.

Some develop the ability to predict structure activity—knowing when the hum will shift before it does, knowing when fog will roll in, knowing where lenses will form in the water. They can't explain how they know. They just know, the way you know where your hand is without looking. They've become integrated enough with the system to sense its states.

The most changed ones start to resemble the Standers in subtle ways. They stand more still, for longer periods. Their movement becomes slightly gliding, slightly uncanny. Their body temperature rises slightly—not to dangerous levels but perceptibly, measurably warmer than baseline. And very occasionally, the nano-patterns appear on their skin—just small patches at first, on fingertips or temples, but growing slowly over time.

At this point, if there are colleagues present, the person is usually evacuated against their will. It's unclear whether the patterns are harmful, but they're clearly not normal human biology and they're clearly an effect of long-term exposure to the structures. Removed from the Hollows, the patterns fade over weeks or months, and the person gradually returns to standard human functioning, though they usually report feeling diminished, as if something has been taken away that they didn't know they needed.

A few have escaped evacuation, or returned despite it. They're still out there, presumably, living on their platforms or living more directly in the water, living with the changes, letting the changes continue. They're seen occasionally—figures on distant nodes, or standing in the fog, or moving in patterns that match the spiral-walking insects. Whether they're still human in any meaningful sense is unclear. Whether they'd want to be rescued is equally unclear.

And then there are the others, the ones who entered deep pockets and didn't return, who might have been transformed by the Breath-Takers, who might be living in forms that aren't recognizably human anymore. They might be some of the shapes glimpsed in deep water, or they might be distributed through the system in ways that don't leave discrete bodies, consciousness spread through the structures or the fog or the plants.

This is speculation, horror-story telling that might have no basis in fact. But the facts are: people have vanished, not many but some. And sometimes people matching their descriptions are seen, looking wrong, moving wrong, existing wrong. And the Hollows clearly alter things, clearly integrate things, clearly have the capacity to take what enters and make it part of the system.

So humans live here, in small numbers, temporarily or permanently, willingly or through transformation beyond choice. They live here as researchers and observers and seekers. They live here as cautionary examples of how long exposure changes you. They live here, possibly, as post-human components of whatever vast processing the structures are doing, their consciousness repurposed, their bodies reformed, their identities dissolved into something larger and stranger and incomprehensible.

They persist, in various forms, to various degrees. They continue.

## What It Means To Live Here

Living in the Breathing Hollows is ultimately not about biological persistence but about integration into a system that operates on principles orthogonal to standard life. Everything here—the Standers, the Breath-Takers, the fish, the insects, the fog, the plants, the humans who stay too long—is becoming part of a vast distributed process that might be computation, might be communication, might be transformation of reality itself into something else.

The inhabitants aren't separate from the place. They're expressions of it, manifestations of its principles, organs in a body that's so large and so slow and so strange that it barely registers as a body at all. They don't so much live here as become here, gradually or suddenly, partially or completely, willingly or through the simple fact of remaining in a place that makes remaining into something other than what it usually means.

The Hollows persist in processing, in humming, in creating the conditions that create the inhabitants that maintain the conditions. It's a self-sustaining recursion, a loop that might have been initiated by whoever or whatever built the structures, but that now runs independently, a machine that has become an ecosystem that might be becoming something else entirely.

What lives here is the place itself, aware and active and constantly becoming more itself. Everything else is just local detail, temporary formations in the ongoing work of whatever the Hollows are working to become.

The place continues. Everything within it continues as part of the continuing. There is no distinction that remains distinct long enough to matter. There is only the vast slow patient process, and everything is in it, and everything serves it, and everything that stays eventually becomes indistinguishable from it.

The Breathing Hollows breathe. And what lives here breathes with them, in rhythms that make biological respiration seem like a special case of something much larger.
