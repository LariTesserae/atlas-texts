# The Inhabitants of the Throat Garden

## The Keeper

There is someone who comes here regularly, though not predictably. Not on a schedule—the throat cannot be reached on schedule, not at this elevation where weather decides all—but with the persistence of water finding its way through stone. This person has been coming for years. Decades, perhaps. Long enough that their movements through the space have worn subtle paths in the moss, that certain rocks bear the polish of repeated sitting, that the arranged stones near the eastern edge are definitely their work, though they would never claim them.

They are difficult to describe because they seem to be made of the same materials as the place itself. Their clothing is the color of lichen and stone: grays, greens that are almost gray, browns that have faded toward gray. Practical layers, wool and something synthetic that doesn't hold moisture, patched in places with fabric that doesn't quite match but serves. Their hands are weathered the way the wooden pole is weathered—roughened, cracked at the knuckles, capable. Their face has taken on the quality of the schist: lined, foliated, as though they too could be split along natural planes.

They come for reasons they probably couldn't articulate, even to themselves. To check on things, maybe. To sit in the particular silence that isn't silence. To watch the seasonal progressions: which birds have arrived, how the cushion plants are flowering, whether the marmots' colony has successfully raised young. They carry very little—a small pack, a water bottle that's never needed because water is everywhere here, some food that's more ritual than necessity because they never stay long enough to grow truly hungry.

Their relationship to the place is neither possessive nor worshipful. They don't feel they own it. They don't treat it as sacred, exactly, though there's reverence in their careful stepping, in the way they disturb nothing unnecessarily. It's more that they've achieved a kind of familiarity, the way you might be familiar with a neighbor you've known for years but have never actually befriended. You know their habits. You notice when things change. You feel responsible, in a vague way, for paying attention.

They talk to the place sometimes, in brief murmurs. "Still here," they might say to a particular cushion plant, one they've been watching longer than the others. "Getting big." To the choughs: "You're late this year." To the throat itself, the configuration of stone and air: "All right. Yes. I see." These aren't prayers or invocations. They're acknowledgments. The verbal equivalent of a nod.

Sometimes they perform small acts of maintenance that aren't quite maintenance because the place doesn't need maintaining. They might move a rock that's fallen into one of the main pools, not because it hurts anything but because the pool's shape is better without it. They might clear debris from the cairn, though debris accumulates again within days. They once spent an hour carefully extracting a piece of modern trash—a plastic bag fragment, impossibly bright against the stone—that had blown in from somewhere far below. The bag had wrapped around a cushion plant, and they removed it with the care of someone performing surgery, trying not to damage a single tiny leaf.

They never take anything. Well—once they collected one of the bone beads, because it seemed in danger of washing away in the next hard rain, and they wanted to look at it more closely. But they brought it back on their next visit, wedging it carefully back into the crevice where they'd found it, positioned so water wouldn't carry it off. It felt important that the bead stay here, that this place keeps its small accumulation of mysteries.

What they're watching for, they couldn't say. Change, perhaps. Or the lack of change. Evidence that the system is maintaining itself, that the delicate equilibrium holds. They've been coming long enough to notice the slow things: how the vegetation line is creeping slightly higher on the western ridge, a shift of perhaps six inches over fifteen years. How one of the marmot burrows has expanded, generation by generation tunneling deeper. How the largest cushion plant—the one they talk to—has a dead center that's growing, the plant becoming a ring, which apparently is what cushion plants do when they get very old.

They worry about the place in abstract ways. Climate change, though the effects at this elevation are hard to parse from natural variation. Increased visitation, though in all their years they've encountered other people here only three times. Some undefined threat they can't name, a feeling that places like this are becoming rarer, not in physical fact but in human consciousness, that the knowledge of how to be in such places is being lost.

But mostly they just come. They sit. They watch the light move across the stone. They listen to the wind's variations. They note which birds are present. They check on the plants and animals they've come to recognize as individuals. They eat a little food, drink some water from a pool they've drunk from a hundred times before, and eventually they leave, descending back toward the world of trees and soil and complexity, carrying with them the throat's spare clarity like a stone in their pocket.

## The Researcher

Twice each summer, someone else comes: a biologist, thirties or forties, part of a long-term alpine ecology study. They arrive with more equipment than the keeper ever carries: instruments for measuring temperature, humidity, wind speed; collection vials; a camera with multiple lenses; notebooks wrapped in plastic; a small tent in case weather prevents descent.

Their relationship to the place is love through data. They've been assigned this site—it's one of twelve in a network spanning three mountain ranges—and over years of visits have developed genuine affection for it, but an affection expressed through measurement, documentation, rigorous observation. They know the throat by its numbers: average temperature range, precipitation levels, species counts, population densities, growth rates.

They work methodically, following protocols designed to ensure consistency across years and between sites. They check the permanent monitoring stations: a small weather logger bolted to the north face, a network of temperature probes buried at various depths in the few soil patches. They conduct transects, walking predetermined paths and recording every plant species within a meter-wide belt. They trap small mammals using live traps baited with peanut butter and oats, weighing and measuring each animal before releasing it exactly where it was caught. They spend hours belly-down on the rock, photographing individual cushion plants from precise angles so growth can be assessed by comparing images across years.

The birds they document through point counts: sitting absolutely still for ten-minute periods, noting every individual seen or heard, estimating distances, recording behaviors. They've gotten skilled at the alpine species, can distinguish accentor calls from pipit calls even when both are singing simultaneously, can identify distant raptors by flight pattern alone.

They collect samples, though sparingly: a few insects, carefully preserved; water from different pools for chemical analysis; small cores from the periphery of cushion plants, removing so little tissue the plants won't notice. Everything is labeled with location coordinates accurate to the centimeter, date, time, conditions. Science demands this precision, and they provide it, though they sometimes feel the strangeness of reducing the throat's wild complexity to rows in spreadsheets.

Yet the spreadsheets reveal things direct observation misses. Patterns that emerge only across years: how a drought three summers ago is still affecting plant growth rates; how the pika population crashed after a severe winter then rebounded in two seasons; how certain bird species are arriving earlier each year, their migration timing shifting in response to changing climate signals. The data tells stories the place itself holds silently.

They've learned to work around the other inhabitants. They know where the pika territories are and avoid placing traps there—too stressful for the animals, and the pikas' alarm calls disrupt bird counts. They know which rocks the choughs favor and schedule their work to minimize disturbance during nesting season. They've memorized the marmot burrow locations and step carefully to avoid collapsing tunnels.

Sometimes, in the evenings after data collection is complete, they sit near the eastern edge—they know about the arranged stones but have never met the person who made them—and write in a separate notebook, one not part of the official research. Observations that aren't scientific: the quality of light at different hours, the particular sound the wind makes through a certain gap, the way the place feels different each visit despite the measurements showing remarkable consistency. They're aware these notes have no professional value, but they keep them anyway, feeling vaguely that something important might be lost if they record only what can be quantified.

They've started to recognize individual animals. One marmot has a notch in its left ear—possibly from a fight, possibly from an encounter with a predator. One of the accentors has an unusual song phrase, a little flourish at the end of its otherwise typical vocalization. They don't name these individuals—that would be unprofessional, anthropomorphizing—but they note them in the personal notebook, tracking them across seasons when possible.

The researcher has complicated feelings about their work here. Pride in the quality of their data, satisfaction in contributing to long-term ecological understanding, concern about what the data increasingly shows—subtle signals of stress in the system, indications that this high refuge might not be as protected from global changes as its remoteness suggests. And underneath it all, a quiet anxiety about their own presence. Are they doing harm, even with their careful protocols and minimal-impact methods? Is scientific study itself a kind of violation of places like this?

They've never articulated these doubts to their colleagues or advisors. The research is important; they believe that. Baseline data from alpine systems will become increasingly critical as climate changes accelerate. But sitting in the throat as afternoon lengthens into evening, surrounded by instruments that measure but cannot comprehend, they sometimes wonder what the place would say about being studied, if it could say anything.

Before leaving each visit, they perform a private ritual that isn't part of the protocol: they sit quietly for five minutes, no data collection, no note-taking, just sitting. Paying attention in a different way. It's their version of the keeper's murmured conversations, an acknowledgment that the relationship between observer and observed is more complex than scientific objectivity allows.

## The Climbers

Occasionally—perhaps once or twice a season—climbers arrive. Not intentionally; the throat isn't a destination for climbing. But it sits at the convergence of several routes up the surrounding peaks, and sometimes parties use it as a rest stop, a place to regroup before attempting a summit push or beginning their descent.

They arrive in groups of two or three, roped together or carrying coils of rope over their shoulders, festooned with equipment: carabiners that clink with every movement, helmets, harnesses, packs heavy with gear. They're focused on their objectives—the peaks above, the routes ahead—and the throat is just a waypoint, barely noticed in their larger mission.

But some stop longer than intended. The place has a way of catching attention once you pause. They drop their packs, grateful for the water, and while they're drinking from the pools, they start to notice: the birds' constant activity, the marmots' watchful presence, the unexpected lushness of the vegetation. "Weird," they say to each other. "Didn't expect this up here."

They're loud in the space, unavoidably so. Their equipment clatters. Their voices carry—they're used to shouting over wind and distance, and they don't modulate easily. The pikas bolt at their arrival, setting off cascades of alarm calls. The birds flush from the rocks. The marmots vanish underground. The place contracts around their presence, becomes smaller and less vital.

Most don't stay long. Ten minutes to drink and eat, to check equipment and discuss the route ahead, and then they're moving again, ascending toward their goals. The throat barely registers in their memory—just another high-altitude feature, unremarkable except for having water.

But occasionally, someone in the group is different. They hang back as their partners prepare to leave. They're looking around with a different kind of attention. "Give me a minute," they say, and while their partners wait impatiently—weather windows are closing, daylight is finite—they walk to the edges, peer into the pools, watch a bird they can't identify work the rocks with busy intensity.

"Come on," their partners call, and they go, but reluctantly. They look back as they leave. That place, they're thinking. Something about that place. They can't articulate what they felt there—the contrast between its small scale and its intensity, the strangeness of such vitality at such elevation, the way it seemed both fragile and ancient simultaneously. But it stays with them.

One or two come back, years later. They remember the location—climbers are good with topography, with remembering features and approaches—and they make a point of visiting deliberately this time, without the pressure of summits and weather. They come in the morning and stay until afternoon, just sitting, watching, beginning to see what the keeper and the researcher see: the patterns, the relationships, the way the place is a system unto itself.

These returning climbers are almost always solo. Their climbing partners don't understand the appeal. "That little spot near the ridge? Why?" So they come alone, and their solitude allows them to experience the throat differently than they did the first time. They move quietly. They find sitting places. They let the hours pass without urgency. They experience something like meditation, though they wouldn't call it that. They just know they feel different here, that something in them settles and clarifies.

They never tell many people about it. The throat remains their secret, a place they return to when they're in the area, when they need something they can't get from summits and technical routes. The vertical world they normally inhabit is about challenge, achievement, pushing limits. This horizontal place offers something else: presence, attention, the satisfaction of being rather than doing.

## The Lost

Very rarely, someone arrives who shouldn't be there at all. Someone who isn't a keeper or a researcher or a climber, who has no business at eleven thousand feet, who is profoundly unprepared. They're wearing inadequate footwear—trail runners, street shoes once, impossibly. Their clothing is wrong for the elevation. They carry nothing: no pack, no water, no equipment of any kind.

They're disoriented, sometimes hypothermic, often in the early stages of altitude sickness. They've gotten separated from a group, or they've simply wandered away from a trailhead miles below, following some impulse they couldn't explain even before the thin air made thinking difficult. Now they're here, in the throat, shivering and confused, only barely aware that they're in serious danger.

The place offers what it can: water in the pools, shelter from wind in the deeper alcoves, rocks that hold some warmth if the sun is shining. But mostly it offers indifference. The throat has no interest in rescue. It simply is, and those unprepared for its reality must find their own way back to survival.

Some do. They rest in an alcove until their thinking clears enough to understand they need to descend, now, immediately. They drink from the pools with cupped hands. They sit in the sun until they stop shivering. Then they stumble back the way they came—or a way, any way down—and eventually they reach tree line, trails, other people, safety. They survive with stories they don't like to tell because they reveal too much foolishness, too much luck.

Others are found here. The keeper has found one, years ago: an older man, sitting against a rock, looking almost peaceful. Dead from exposure, probably, or a heart attack brought on by altitude and exertion. The keeper descended immediately to report it, and eventually a recovery team arrived with a helicopter, and the body was taken away. But for perhaps twelve hours, the man was part of the throat's ecology, and already the insects had found him, and a weasel had investigated with professional interest.

The researcher has found remains twice: once a scattering of old bones, possibly decades old, impossible to say if they were human or animal without more expertise than the researcher possessed. And once, more disturbingly, a boot with a foot still in it, mummified by cold and dryness, disconnected from its owner by time and predation. The researcher reported both finds to authorities, but at this elevation, in such remote locations, recovery isn't always feasible or even desirable. The mountain keeps what it claims.

These encounters with death don't make the place feel dangerous, exactly. The throat doesn't kill people. People kill themselves through poor decisions, through failures to respect what high mountains demand. The throat is just a place where consequences arrive with special clarity, where the margin between survival and death is narrow, where nature provides no safety net.

The lost who survive rarely return. The experience is too frightening, too close. They have nightmares about the cold, the disorientation, the way distance became impossible to judge and time stretched strange. They remember the throat, if they remember it specifically at all, as a terrible place, a near-death place, somewhere they escaped from.

But they're wrong about this. The throat is neither terrible nor merciful. It's neutral, indifferent, simply itself. Those who arrive prepared can experience its gifts. Those who arrive unprepared face its realities. The place makes no distinction.

## The Birds

The birds, of course, are not visitors. They're residents, though their residency operates on different timescales. Some species nest here through the brief summer, raising young in the alcoves and crevices. Others pause here during migration, staying hours or days before continuing their journeys. A few individuals—the choughs, particularly—seem to consider the throat part of a larger territory, returning repeatedly over years, possibly over their entire lives.

They inhabit the place with complete authority. Every rock surface is known to them, every pool, every place where insects concentrate. They move through the three-dimensional space with casual expertise that makes the human visitors' carefully placed steps look clumsy and tentative. They understand the wind, using it, riding it, occasionally playing in it with aerial maneuvers that serve no obvious purpose except joy.

The rosy finches work in small flocks, moving across the rocks in coordinated sweeps, maintaining constant communication through those high, thin contact calls. Their foraging is methodical: they investigate every crack, every seep, picking at insects and seeds too small for human eyes to see. They're almost fearless around humans, or perhaps simply unconcerned. They've learned that people don't hunt them here, that the strange large bipeds just sit and watch, so they work within arms' reach sometimes, close enough to see every feather detail, the delicate pink wash on their plumage that gives them their name.

The accentors are more secretive, skulking low, using cover. But their songs burst out with surprising volume and complexity from unexpected locations. They're territorial, and males sing to maintain boundaries, to advertise their claims. These territories overlap with those of other accentors—the throat is small enough that perfect separation is impossible—so there's constant negotiation through song, each male asserting presence without direct confrontation.

The water pipits nest in the deepest, most protected alcoves. They're attentive parents, both adults feeding the nestlings, making hundreds of trips each day, each trip bringing a single insect or small spider. When predators approach—a weasel, a climbing human—the parents become agitated, flying in rapid circles, delivering sharp alarm calls. But they don't abandon their nests. They have too much invested, and the season is too short to start over.

The choughs are different from all the other species. They're larger, more confident, more intelligent. They're tool users—corvids are—though in the throat there are few tools to use. But they solve problems, work cooperatively, remember. The pair that frequents the throat seems to recognize individual humans. They react differently to the keeper—casual indifference—than to strangers, who prompt more cautious observation. They've learned the researcher's routines and know to avoid the trapping areas during those visits.

The choughs also play. This is unusual enough among birds, especially at this elevation where energy should be conserved for survival. But on days when the wind is strong but not too strong, the choughs ride the updrafts along the ridges, spiraling higher, then folding their wings and dropping in steep dives, pulling up at the last moment and climbing again. They vocalize during these aerial displays—those sharp *chee-ow* calls—and if corvid vocalizations can express anything, these sound like exhilaration.

Raptors visit but don't stay. The golden eagles circle the ridges, hunting the marmots below tree line primarily, but occasionally making passes over the throat. One eagle killed a marmot here once, several years ago. The researcher documented it: the eagle's stoop, the marmot's desperate dash for its burrow, the eagle's talons closing around the marmot just short of safety. The eagle fed on a nearby rock for two hours, methodically tearing the carcass, while the other marmots whistled from their burrows and the keeper, who happened to be present that day, watched from a respectful distance.

The kestrels hunt the smaller prey—mice and insects primarily. They hover over the throat, exploiting the updrafts, maintaining perfect position while scanning the ground below. When they dive, they're remarkably accurate. The keeper has seen them miss perhaps once in every four or five attempts, which is a better success rate than most predators achieve.

The lammergeiers are rare visitors, passing through on thermals, but memorable. Nine-foot wingspans are startling even at altitude. The keeper has seen one drop a bone—probably carried from a predator kill miles away—and watched it shatter on the rocks below. The lammergeier descended in a lazy spiral and picked fragments from the debris, swallowing sharp shards that would puncture any other animal's digestive system. But lammergeiers have evolved for this, and their stomach acid can dissolve bone, allowing them to extract nutrition from a resource few other scavengers can access.

The birds pattern the days with their activities: dawn chorus, mid-morning foraging, afternoon lull during peak heat (even here there's a relative peak heat), evening feeding surge, dusk settling. These rhythms have repeated for as long as birds have used this place—which is to say, for as long as this place has existed in its current form. Thousands of years, at least. Maybe tens of thousands.

They don't think about the throat the way humans do. They don't find it beautiful or strange or sacred. It's habitat: good habitat, with water and shelter and food, but just habitat. They require certain features—the rock alcoves for nesting, the pools for drinking and bathing, the insects for feeding—and the throat provides these features, so they use it. If those features vanished, the birds would go elsewhere. They have no sentiment about place.

Yet they are shaped by it. Generations of birds have nested in these specific crevices, have fed from these specific pools, have sung from these specific rocks. The genetic memory of the place exists in their populations. Migrating species include the throat in their mental maps, learned or innate, navigating to it across hundreds or thousands of miles. Young birds learn the throat from their parents, and those young birds will return as adults to raise their own young, perpetuating a connection that spans years, lives, generations.

## The Mammals

The pikas are the throat's most numerous mammals, possibly the most numerous vertebrates of any kind. Their territories tessellate across the rock face, each family group claiming a section of crevices and the foraging grounds around them. They're visible constantly during daylight hours, bustling with urgent purpose, cutting and storing vegetation for the winter they can't escape.

Unlike the marmots, pikas don't hibernate. They remain active through the winter, living beneath the snow in the spaces between rocks, surviving on food stores they've accumulated during the brief summer. This means they work with desperate intensity when conditions allow. They know—somehow they know, through instinct or learned experience—exactly how much hay they need to survive six months of winter, and they harvest it relentlessly.

Their alarm system is sophisticated. Different calls for different threats: one call for aerial predators, another for ground predators, variations in intensity reflecting the urgency of danger. And the system is cooperative: when one pika calls, others respond, spreading the warning across the entire colony in seconds. It's an early warning network that benefits all members, though they're otherwise fiercely territorial.

The pikas recognize the regular visitors. They've learned that the keeper and the researcher, while large and potentially frightening, pose no actual threat. They still call when these humans arrive, but the calls are less urgent, and the pikas emerge from their burrows more quickly afterward. Strange humans provoke longer periods of alarm, more persistent calling, more time before normal activity resumes.

They fight constantly over territory boundaries, brief furious encounters that involve chasing, vocalizing, occasionally actual combat. These fights look serious but rarely result in injury. They're mostly display, assertion, testing boundaries. Young pikas, dispersing from their birth territories to establish their own, have the most difficulty. The good territories are occupied, defended by established adults, so the young must claim marginal areas or wait for adults to die. Mortality is high among dispersers.

The marmots are different in almost every way. Where pikas are frenetic, marmots are phlegmatic. They move slowly, deliberately, conserving energy. They fatten through the summer, becoming almost comically rotund, storing calories for hibernation. By late summer, the largest adults have trouble squeezing into their burrows, their bellies dragging on the ground.

They're more social than the pikas, living in extended family groups that share burrow systems and cooperate in predator detection. While the colony forages, one marmot always remains vigilant, positioned on a high rock, scanning for threats. When the sentinel whistles, the entire colony vanishes underground in fluid coordination, each individual knowing exactly which burrow entrance is closest.

The marmots recognize seasons with their bodies. As summer wanes and day length shortens, they begin changing metabolically, preparing for hibernation. They feed more but become less active, spending longer hours basking on rocks, absorbing warmth they'll lose access to for six months. Eventually they enter their burrows for the last time before winter, sealing themselves in with vegetation and soil, and don't emerge again until spring.

What happens underground during those months is mysterious. They sleep, obviously, but it's not normal sleep. Their body temperature drops almost to freezing, their heart rate slows to a few beats per minute, their breathing becomes barely detectable. They're living but barely, reducing their metabolism to the minimum necessary to maintain life. They lose a third of their body weight during hibernation, burning fat stores slowly, existing in a state between life and death.

When they emerge in spring, they're gaunt, desperate for food, moving slowly as their bodies restart normal function. The throat at that time is barely awakened itself—snow still covers much of the ground, only the earliest plants are showing green. But the marmots must eat, rebuilding lost mass, preparing for the brief reproductive season. Those that entered hibernation without sufficient fat reserves don't emerge at all.

The mice are least visible but probably more numerous than pikas and marmots combined. Three species, maybe four—the researcher isn't certain, and proper identification would require specimens they're reluctant to collect. The mice occupy the vegetation, living in the moss and grass tufts, creating runway systems that are invisible until you know to look for them. They feed on seeds primarily, storing them in caches hidden in the rocks.

They're prey for everything larger: weasels, foxes, birds of prey, even the choughs occasionally. Their defense is reproduction—short lives, multiple litters per season, many offspring. Not all survive, but enough do to maintain populations. They're a critical link in the food chain, converting plant material into protein that feeds higher trophic levels.

The weasels are rarely seen but regularly present. The keeper has found their scats, thin and twisted, full of mouse fur and tiny bones. The researcher has recorded them in field notes: "Brief sighting, M. erminea, hunting the northern crevices, 14:35." But sustained observation is difficult because weasels are liquid, pouring through spaces you wouldn't think could accommodate a vertebrate, appearing and disappearing in seconds.

They hunt by persistence and knowledge of terrain. A weasel knows every mouse runway, every burrow entrance, every likely hiding place. It works through them systematically, and even though mice are fast and weasels aren't particularly fast, the weasel's relentlessness eventually produces results. A mouse panics, makes a wrong turn, gets cornered, and the weasel strikes with viper speed.

The mountain hares are marginal here—the throat is at the upper edge of their range. One or two individuals use the place regularly, scraping out forms in the deeper alcoves where they lie motionless during daylight, their camouflage making them nearly invisible. They forage in very early morning and late evening, eating the alpine plants, leaving distinctive droppings: round, fibrous pellets scattered near their forms.

They're nervous, more so than the marmots or pikas. Their defense is speed and camouflage, but the throat is small, contained, offering limited escape routes. An eagle or fox could corner them here, so they don't stay long, using the place as part of a larger territory that includes more open terrain where their running ability gives them advantage.

All these mammals pattern their lives around the throat's resources and rhythms. They know where water pools collect, which plants are edible, where shelter from wind can be found, which approaches offer warning of predators. The place has shaped them across generations, and they've shaped it in return, their burrows altering drainage patterns, their droppings fertilizing plants, their grazing influencing vegetation composition.

They don't know the place is unusual. To them it's simply home, or part of home, or a resource to be exploited. They have no concept of its remoteness, its fragility, its beauty. They just live here, doing what their species have evolved to do, playing their roles in the system, surviving or failing to survive according to the ancient rules that govern life at high altitude.

## The Scratched Symbols

The person who made the markings in the deepest alcove is long dead. This is almost certainly true—the scratches are weathered enough to suggest decades at minimum, possibly much longer. But they were a person, once, who sat where the keeper and researcher and occasional climbers now sit, who looked out at the same stone and sky, who drank from the same pools.

What made them scratch symbols into the rock? The keeper has wondered about this, tracing the marks with careful fingers. The researcher has photographed them from multiple angles, trying to establish age and meaning. The climbers who notice them at all usually assume they're ancient, tribal, the work of indigenous people from before European contact.

But they might not be that old. The weathering could represent fifty years or five hundred—stone at this elevation weathers strangely, fast in some ways and slow in others, making dating difficult without expensive analysis no one has justified conducting. And the symbols themselves are ambiguous: spirals could be universal, appearing in many cultures across time. The line patterns could be maps, or writing, or pure decoration.

Whoever they were, they spent time here. You don't scratch symbols into hard rock idly, during a brief stop. The work requires time, effort, a sharp stone used as chisel and hammer. Your hands get tired. The repetitive motion becomes meditative. You have to really want to leave these marks, and you have to have reason to stay long enough to complete them.

Were they alone? Probably. The symbol-scratching feels solitary, personal, not the kind of thing you'd do as part of a group. Groups have different dynamics—they're about shared experience, conversation, communal activity. These marks feel individual, private despite being permanent.

Were they spiritual? Maybe. The symbols could be prayers, offerings, attempts to communicate with whatever this person believed inhabited high places. Or they could be secular—maps, records, the equivalent of "I was here." Without context, meaning remains suspended, multiple interpretations equally possible.

The keeper prefers to think of this person as a spiritual seeker, someone who came to the throat for vision or wisdom or communion with nature. This interpretation has no evidence supporting it, but it satisfies something in the keeper's understanding of why people seek high places. The researcher is more cautious, noting in field records that "markings of indeterminate age and purpose" exist, resisting speculation.

But both keeper and researcher feel connection to this unknown person. They've stood where this person stood. They've experienced what this person experienced: the wind's persistent voice, the quality of light, the presence of the birds and mammals, the throat's strange combination of openness and containment. Across decades or centuries, they share this place, and the symbols are a message from past to present, saying: "I was here. I saw this. It mattered."

There's comfort in that continuity. The place existed before humans found it, will exist after humans forget it, but for now it's a meeting ground where different times touch. The symbol-scratcher, the cairn-builder, the keeper, the researcher, the climbers, all of them visitors to something older and more permanent than themselves, all of them leaving traces—most temporary, some lasting—of their presence.

The symbols themselves are becoming more weathered each year. The scratches are filling with lichen, slowly disappearing as the stone reclaims its surface. In another century, maybe two, they'll be invisible, or nearly so, impossible to see unless you know exactly where to look and trace them with fingertips rather than eyes. Eventually they'll vanish completely, erased by the same patient processes that shape the mountains themselves.

This doesn't bother the keeper or the researcher. It seems right, somehow, that even stone records are temporary, that nothing lasts forever except the stone itself, and even that is slowly breaking down, splitting and crumbling grain by grain, becoming the scree that slides down the slopes, the sediment that fills the valleys, the material from which new mountains will eventually rise.

The symbol-scratcher is gone. Their reasons for coming here are gone. Their beliefs, hopes, fears, whatever drove them to this high place and compelled them to spend hours scratching marks into stone—all gone. Only the marks remain, and soon they too will fade. But the throat persists, still doing what it does, still hosting life, still offering its combination of harshness and richness to whatever creatures find their way here.

That's the real permanence: not the symbols, not the cairn, not any individual visitor, but the place itself, the system, the ongoing process of life finding ways to exist in this unlikely pocket of fertility. That will continue as long as the mountains stand, as long as water seeps from the rock, as long as cushion plants can root in crevices and birds can fly at altitude.

The symbol-scratcher is remembered, dimly, incompletely, through marks they made. But the throat remembers nothing. It has no memory, no consciousness, no awareness of its own existence. It simply is, and in that simple being, it outlasts everything that visits it, a small eternity of stone and water and wind and life, persisting in the place where three ridges meet at eleven thousand feet.
