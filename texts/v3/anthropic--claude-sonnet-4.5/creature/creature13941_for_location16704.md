# The Plateau's Inhabitants

## The People of the Long Patience

They call themselves the Yathren, though the word means something closer to "those who remain" or possibly "those who wait." The language shifts; even they're not certain anymore which translation was original.

They are human, but their humanity has been negotiated.

After thirty generations on the plateau — perhaps forty, the count grows unreliable in the deep records — their bodies have reached accommodations with this place that go beyond simple adaptation. Their lungs process the thin air with an efficiency that would make lowland physiologists suspicious. Their blood carries oxygen in ways that don't quite match the hemoglobin patterns of their ancestors. When researchers from below have tested them (rare visits, usually unwelcome), the results come back marked with annotations like "anomalous" and "confirm sample integrity."

They stand shorter than their lowland cousins, on average, with broader chests and hands that seem slightly too large for their frames. Their fingers are long, adapted for the fine work of stone-singing and plant-weaving, but also thickened at the tips, callused in patterns that have become hereditary. Children are born with these thickened fingertips now. The plateau marks them before they ever touch stone.

Their eyes are their most striking feature: larger than lowland human eyes, with pupils that dilate massively in the dim light and contract to pinpoints in direct sun. The irises have lost the variety of colors that humans typically display. All Yathren eyes are some shade of gray-blue or gray-green, sometimes with a ring of darker color around the pupil that catches light like the crystalline veins in the stone. They don't blink as often as they should. They can stare for minutes without discomfort, watching, waiting, attending to things that haven't happened yet.

In certain lights, at certain angles, their skin shows that faint photoluminescence — not imagination, but actual measurable light, dim blue-green, the color of the mat-plants they've lived among for so long. It's most visible in their palms and along their forearms, places where skin contacts the vegetation most frequently. The children glow more than the adults, and no one knows if this is because children are less careful about what they touch or because the glow fades with age.

They live long lives, when they choose to. A Yathren who avoids accident and disease can see ninety years easily, sometimes a hundred and ten, occasionally more. But many choose not to. There's a cultural understanding that life has a proper length, and that length is determined not by biology but by some other metric — completion, perhaps, or satisfaction, or simply the sense that one has walked enough paths, seen enough cycles, contributed enough to the continuation.

## The Children of Stone Dreams

The young are raised communally, but loosely. A child might have three or four adults who pay them particular attention, who might or might not be biological parents. Parentage isn't secret, but it's not considered especially relevant. Children belong to the settlement, to the plateau.

They're quiet children, even in play. They learn early that sound carries, that the emptiness amplifies everything, that privacy requires silence. Their games are strange by lowland standards: long periods of stillness, sudden brief movements, patterns that look like dance or ritual but are actually just children exploring the geometry of space.

They're taught the essential skills young: how to read the stone's mood, how to predict the wind, how to move through unstable areas without getting lost. A five-year-old Yathren can tell you whether a particular doorway is "open" today. A seven-year-old understands water-routes that would take a lowland cartographer months to map.

But they're also taught patience in ways that seem almost cruel to outsiders. A child learning stone-work might spend a year just sitting near a particular boulder, watching it, before ever picking up a tool. A child learning plant-cultivation might observe a single mat-plant for months, attending to its daily fluctuations, before being allowed to tend the gardens.

This isn't punishment. This is fundamental education. The plateau requires you to work at its pace, and that pace is slow. Children who can't learn this — who remain urgent, impulsive, quick — don't survive to adulthood, not because they're exiled but because the plateau itself rejects them. They walk into the wrong geometry at the wrong time. They misread the weather and get caught in storms. They take paths that close behind them.

The children who survive learn to exist in a state of constant, patient attention. They develop an almost meditative relationship with their surroundings. By adolescence, they can sit for hours and not be bored, can walk for days and not be lonely, can work at a single task for months and not be frustrated.

They also develop the peculiar Yathren relationship with time. Around age twelve or thirteen, they begin to experience temporal instability naturally. They'll be talking to you, and suddenly they're younger, or older, or themselves but from yesterday, and then they snap back to the present. The adults watch for this carefully. It's a sign that the child is becoming synchronized with the plateau, that they're beginning to exist in multiple moments simultaneously.

Most children navigate this transition smoothly. A few don't, and they become what the Yathren call "time-lost" — stuck in a state of constant temporal flux, aging and un-aging, never quite present. These children are cared for, but they're also acknowledged as changed, as having become something other than fully human. Eventually they walk into the deep dreaming zones and become part of the plateau's long memory.

## The Adults and Their Roles

By eighteen or twenty, a Yathren has usually settled into their primary role, though roles remain fluid, changing with need and inclination.

**Stone-singers** work with the bedrock, learning to coax it into new configurations. This isn't masonry in any conventional sense. They don't cut or chip or break. They find where the stone wants to move and help it move there. A stone-singer can spend months examining a cliff face, touching it daily, listening to it (actually pressing their ear against it and listening), before finally convincing a section to detach itself cleanly.

The best stone-singers develop a relationship with specific sections of the plateau. They know the personality of the stone in their area — where it's patient, where it's brittle, where it's willing to cooperate. They mark their work subtly, leaving signs that other stone-singers can read: this section is stable for the next decade; this outcrop is considering a collapse; this vein is growing and should be allowed space.

Stone-singers are usually quiet even by Yathren standards, and they develop a peculiar gait, walking carefully, distributing their weight deliberately, as if they're always conscious of being on top of something ancient and not entirely solid.

**Water-tenders** maintain the basins and channels, predict the moisture cycles, manage the slow flow of water across impossible distances. They have an almost supernatural sense for water — they can smell it at vast distances, can predict rain days in advance, can find seepage that hasn't yet reached the surface.

Water-tenders walk the channels regularly, clearing blockages that have formed over years, deepening sections that are silting up, creating new channels where the water wants to flow. They work with stone-singers when major channel-work is needed, but most of their labor is small: moving a pebble here, redirecting a trickle there, interventions so minor they seem pointless until you see the result months later.

The relationship between water-tenders and the plateau's hydrology is intimate enough to seem mystical. They know the deep aquifers, the ones that take centuries to fill and centuries to drain. They can sense changes in the water table, shifts in pressure, the movement of moisture through stone so slow it's measured in meters per year.

Water-tenders are often women, though not exclusively, and they tend to live longest. Something about their work — constant contact with water, perhaps, or the particular patience it requires — extends their lifespan. The oldest living Yathren, a water-tender named Suleth who claims to be a hundred and thirty-seven (and might be), moves like water herself, slowly and inevitably, finding the path of least resistance, wearing down obstacles through patient persistence.

**Plant-cultivators** tend the gardens and manage the vegetation, working with the mat-plants and tree-analogues, encouraging growth in useful patterns, negotiating with the plants about boundaries and territories. This role requires a different kind of attention than stone-singing or water-tending — something closer to diplomacy.

The mat-plants are semi-sentient, or at least semi-responsive. They react to presence, to intention, to something that might be communication. A skilled plant-cultivator can walk through an overgrown area and have the plants withdraw slightly, creating a path, then close behind them. They can encourage flowering (the mat-plants do flower, occasionally, producing structures that are more geometric than biological) or discourage it. They can mix different strains to create new colors, new levels of heat-generation, new patterns of growth.

Plant-cultivators often have that photoluminescence most strongly. They glow visibly in full darkness, enough to read by if you're close. Some develop a relationship with the vegetation so deep that they begin to metabolize light the way the plants do, requiring less food, sustaining themselves partially through exposure to the crystalline glow from the stone.

There are stories — unconfirmed, but persistent — of plant-cultivators who went entirely over, who stopped eating, who rooted themselves in the deep mat-plant beds and gradually became something between human and vegetation. The stories describe them as still conscious, still present, but operating on plant time, thinking thoughts that take years to complete, existing in a state of being that's more process than identity.

**Route-mappers** maintain the knowledge of paths and passages, tracking the plateau's spatial instabilities, updating the calendars that mark when particular routes are reliable. This is the most intellectually demanding role, requiring both mathematical precision and intuitive understanding of non-Euclidean geometry.

Route-mappers keep extensive records, but the records are only partially comprehensible to outsiders. They use a notation system that maps probability and temporality onto spatial relationships, creating documents that look like musical scores annotated by mathematicians having fever dreams. A single route between two settlements might have fifteen different descriptions depending on the season, the wind-cycle, the phase of the longer stability patterns, and factors that the route-mappers understand but can't quite articulate.

They're also the primary contact for visitors, when visitors come. They can assess someone's ability to navigate the plateau's peculiarities within a few minutes of conversation, and they'll provide appropriate guidance: full escort for those who'll never develop the intuition, detailed instructions for those who might learn, simple directional advice for the rare individual who takes to the plateau naturally.

Route-mappers are often the most verbal Yathren, comfortable with language, willing to explain (up to a point). They're also the most likely to travel between settlements, walking the routes they map, constantly updating their understanding. This makes them the plateau's de facto information network, carrying news, conveying messages, maintaining the thin social connections between the scattered cities.

**Memory-keepers** maintain the records and stories, preserve the long history, teach the essential knowledge. Every settlement has at least one, usually two or three, and they form a loose network across the plateau, sharing information during the rare gatherings when multiple settlements send representatives to a particular location.

The records they keep are extensive but strange. History on the plateau doesn't flow linearly, so historical records don't either. Events are organized by cycles and patterns rather than chronological sequence. The memory-keepers talk about things that happened "during the third wind-cycle of the sixty-year pattern" or "in the cold stability that came after the eastern subsidence."

They preserve practical knowledge — techniques for stone-work, plant-cultivation, water-finding — but also more esoteric information. They maintain genealogies, though these are less about bloodlines than about skill-transmission, tracking which stone-singer learned from which predecessor, how plant-cultivation techniques have evolved over centuries. They record the plateau's changes: when new crystalline veins appeared, when sections of the surface dropped or rose, when the vegetation patterns shifted.

And they keep the stories, the ones that are more than history. Stories about the plateau's dreaming, about what it might be, about the deep structures beneath that are older than human presence. Stories about individuals who walked into the flux and returned changed, or didn't return at all. Stories about the time before the Yathren came to the plateau, and the peoples who might have been here earlier, and the structures in the deep dreaming zones that no one built.

Memory-keepers are usually older, taking up the role after years in some other work, bringing their accumulated experience to the task of preservation. They speak deliberately, with long pauses, as if they're constantly checking their memories for accuracy. Some of them develop an uncanny ability to recall events they didn't witness, as if they're accessing some collective memory that exists in the plateau itself.

## The Old Ones

Age on the plateau brings changes beyond simple physical decline. The very old — those past eighty, certainly those past a hundred — begin to exist differently.

They sleep less, or claim to sleep less, though observers note they spend long periods in states that look like sleep but might be something else. They eat little. Their speech becomes increasingly elliptical, full of references to patterns and cycles, empty of personal concern.

They spend more time in the deep dreaming zones, walking areas that younger Yathren avoid. They seem comfortable with the most extreme reality instabilities, moving through impossible geometries as if navigating familiar rooms. When they return from these excursions — if they return — they're often disoriented about how much time has passed, guessing days when it's been weeks, or weeks when it's been hours.

Their skin gets thinner, more translucent, and the photoluminescence becomes more pronounced. The very old glow steadily, noticeably, casting enough light to see by. Their eyes develop a reflective quality, like the crystalline veins, catching light and holding it.

They also become oracular, though not in any organized way. They make statements that sound like non sequiturs but turn out to be relevant days or months later. They answer questions you haven't asked. They provide advice for situations that haven't occurred yet. Whether they're experiencing time non-linearly or simply have deep intuition is debated even among the Yathren.

The very old are treated with respect but not reverence. They're consulted, but their advice is weighed against practical knowledge, not followed blindly. They're fed and sheltered, but they're also expected to contribute what they can, even if their contribution is simply sitting near the basin and existing as a reminder of continuity.

Eventually — at different ages for different people, but eventually — the very old make their choice. They announce, casually, that they're going to walk into the deep dreaming. They give away what few possessions they have. They say their farewells, which are brief and matter-of-fact. And then they walk.

No one follows. No one goes to check on them. They've made their decision, and the decision is respected.

Some few return, days or weeks later, but they're changed. They're not quite present anymore, not quite speaking the same language, not quite occupying the same moment as everyone else. They exist in a state of permanent flux, phasing in and out of full reality, sometimes solid and sometimes translucent, sometimes speaking in comprehensible words and sometimes in sounds that might be stone grinding or wind moving.

These returned ones are called the Threshold, and they're treated as still-Yathren but also as something else, as having one foot in the plateau's dreaming and one foot in the human world. They wander, mostly, moving between settlements, appearing unexpectedly, offering cryptic observations, then disappearing again.

Children are taught not to fear the Threshold, but also not to follow them. They exist in geometries that aren't safe for the fully-living.

## The Outsiders

Occasionally, people come to the plateau from below. Scientists, explorers, refugees, seekers, madmen, the lost.

The Yathren receive them politely but without enthusiasm. Visitors disrupt the plateau's subtle equilibria. They move wrong, speak too loudly, misunderstand the water-sharing protocols, get lost in areas that should be navigable.

Most visitors don't stay long. The cold drives them away, or the loneliness, or the way distances don't work right, or simply the profound strangeness of a place where buildings lean into impossible angles and the vegetation glows at night.

Some attempt to study the Yathren, to document their adaptations, to understand the plateau's peculiarities. The Yathren tolerate this to a point. They'll answer questions, allow observations, even provide samples when asked politely. But they also withhold. Some knowledge resists documentation. Some experiences can't be described, only lived.

The scientists who stay longest usually start to change. They develop the Yathren gait, the careful silence, the habit of long observation. Their notes become less precise and more poetic. Their hypotheses start to sound like philosophy. Eventually they either leave, frustrated by their inability to reduce the plateau to data, or they stay and gradually become Yathren themselves, accepting that understanding here means transformation.

A few visitors integrate successfully. They're the ones who arrive already patient, already comfortable with ambiguity, already suspecting that reality is more flexible than it appears. The Yathren watch these individuals carefully, assessing whether they can survive, whether the plateau will accept them. If the signs are good, they teach — slowly, carefully, never forcing, allowing the newcomer to learn at their own pace.

Those who stay long enough — years, usually — begin to develop the physical changes: the eye enlargement, the photoluminescence, the altered gait. Whether this is genetic adaptation (which shouldn't happen so quickly) or something else (perhaps the plateau itself rewriting whoever stays here) is unknown.

The Yathren don't speculate much about it. You change or you don't. You stay or you leave. The plateau decides, or you decide, or the decision emerges from the interaction between you and this place, and in the end the distinction doesn't matter.

## The Settlements as Communities

Each city operates as an autonomous unit, but there's a sense of connection, of being part of a larger pattern. Yathren from different settlements recognize each other, share protocols, operate from the same fundamental assumptions about how life works here.

Governance, such as it is, emerges from consensus and necessity. When decisions need making — where to dig a new cistern, how to respond to a major spatial instability, whether to send someone to a distant settlement with news — people gather and talk, quietly, over hours or days, until agreement emerges.

There are no chiefs, no councils, no formal structure. But there are people whose voices carry more weight: the very old, the water-tenders who understand the deep cycles, the memory-keepers who remember what worked before, the route-mappers who see the larger patterns.

Disputes are rare, mostly because scarcity here isn't about resources — there's plenty of space, plenty of stone, adequate water, sufficient vegetation — but about attention and time. What conflicts arise are usually about protocols: one person maintaining that a particular route is safe, another insisting it isn't; disagreement about whether a building should be expanded or left alone; differing interpretations of what the plateau's patterns mean.

These disputes are resolved through patience and observation. People watch the contested route for a season, note what happens, adjust their understanding. They wait and see if the building seems to want expansion. They gather more data, consult the records, allow time to clarify the situation.

Violence is almost unknown. It happens — humans are humans — but it's shocking when it does, a violation of fundamental protocols. The response is usually exile, not as punishment but as recognition that someone who can act with urgency and violence can't safely live on the plateau, where careful patience is survival.

The settlements trade with each other, though trade is sparse. What one settlement has, others can usually acquire or create. The trade is more about maintaining connection, exchanging information, sharing innovations in technique.

And they gather, occasionally, for events that are part festival, part conference, part ritual. These gatherings are planned years in advance, timed to coincide with stable seasons and particular points in the longer cycles. Yathren walk for weeks to reach them, arriving from settlements scattered across vast distances.

At these gatherings, they exchange news, arrange apprenticeships (a young stone-singer going to study with a master in a distant city), formalize relationships (partnering happens, though marriage as lowlanders understand it doesn't really exist), and simply be numerous for a while, remembering what it's like when more than a handful of humans occupy the same space.

The gatherings last weeks, then everyone disperses again, walking back to their settlements, comfortable returning to the sparse distances, the long silences, the slow work of living on stone that's still dreaming its ancient dreams.

## Relationships and Intimacy

The Yathren form partnerships, but these look different from lowland relationships. People pair off, yes, or sometimes form triads or other configurations, but the bonds are less possessive, more fluid.

Partners might live in different buildings, might not see each other for days at a time, pursuing separate work. When they come together, it's deliberate, chosen, a temporary convergence of paths rather than a constant state.

Sex happens but isn't particularly emphasized. The cold, the sparse population, the general low-energy lifestyle — all of these dampen urgency. Intimacy here is more about shared silence, working together at patient tasks, sitting near each other while watching the wind move across emptiness.

Children are valued but not romanticized. They're necessary for continuation, but that necessity doesn't translate into the kind of child-centered culture that exists in many lowland societies. Children are raised carefully, taught thoroughly, but also expected to develop independence early, to learn to be comfortable alone, to find satisfaction in their own company.

Friendship exists, but it's practiced through long separations and brief reunions. Two Yathren might be deeply connected but see each other only once every few years, when travel brings them to the same settlement. They pick up conversations where they left off months or years ago, share what they've learned, then part again, comfortable with the distance.

The Yathren concept of love is less about passion and more about recognition — seeing someone clearly, understanding how they move through the world, appreciating the particular way they attend to stone or water or plants. You love someone by witnessing them accurately, by working alongside them at the plateau's slow pace, by sharing silence that feels companionable rather than empty.

Jealousy is rare, or at least rarely expressed. Resources aren't scarce. Attention isn't limited. If your partner also bonds with someone else, that doesn't diminish what you share. There's room for everyone in all this emptiness.

Grief, when it comes, is processed slowly and privately. Death is acknowledged, remembered, but not dwelt upon. The plateau teaches that everything changes on long timescales, that loss is simply another form of transformation. The dead are commemorated with simple markers — a stone placed just so, a plant cultivated in a particular pattern — but elaborate mourning is uncommon.

## What They Believe

The Yathren don't have organized religion, but they have a cosmology, an understanding of what the plateau is and what they are in relation to it.

They believe — or understand, they'd argue — that the plateau is conscious, or something adjacent to conscious. Not a person-consciousness, but a vast, slow awareness that thinks in geological time, that experiences reality differently than humans do, that's conducting some kind of long experiment or meditation or process of self-discovery.

They believe they're participants in this process, not subjects or worshippers but collaborators. The plateau allows human presence because humans serve some function — perhaps simply as mobile observers, perhaps as elements that introduce variation into its slow dreaming, perhaps as partners in whatever it's trying to become.

The reality instabilities aren't problems to solve but features to accept. The plateau is exploring possibility, trying out different configurations of space and time, seeing what works. The Yathren help by inhabiting these possibilities, by navigating them successfully or unsuccessfully, by providing feedback through their presence and survival.

They believe the deep structures beneath the plateau — whatever they are — are older than human presence, older than the plateau's current configuration, possibly older than the planet's current geological era. These structures are foundational, and they influence everything above: the spatial instabilities, the crystalline veins, the vegetation's strange properties, perhaps even the Yathren's own changes.

They believe — though this is more speculation than certainty — that other peoples have been here before, have lived on the plateau during different eras when it was configured differently. The structures in the deep dreaming zones might be remnants of these earlier inhabitants, or they might be something else entirely.

They don't believe in an afterlife exactly, but they do believe in continuation. The very old who walk into the deep dreaming don't die; they transform, becoming part of the plateau's long memory, existing in states that don't distinguish clearly between living and dead, between individual and collective, between now and then.

They practice certain rituals, but these are more like maintenance than worship. They place stones in certain configurations during particular points in the cycles, maintaining patterns that their ancestors established, though no one's entirely certain why. They sing to the stone, not to appease it but to communicate with it, to maintain the relationship that makes stone-singing possible. They tend the water not just for practical reasons but because the water deserves attention, deserves care, deserves recognition of its patient work.

They tell stories, always, because stories are how understanding is transmitted across time. The stories change with each telling — deliberately changed, adapted to new understanding, keeping the core but updating the details — because stories, like everything else here, must be flexible to survive.

## What They Create

The Yathren are artists, though they don't think of themselves that way. Everything they make is functional, but function here includes beauty, includes meaning, includes participation in the plateau's aesthetic.

Their buildings are architecture-as-meditation, structures designed not just for shelter but to frame particular views, to capture specific qualities of light, to create spaces that feel resonant with the plateau's patient emptiness.

Their tools are sculptural, every handle carved to fit particular hands, every blade shaped to catch light in pleasing ways. Function and beauty aren't separate categories.

Their clothing is decorated with patterns that map the crystalline veins, or replicate the vegetation's growth patterns, or mark the wearer's role and history. The decorations serve as identification, as record-keeping, as wearable archives.

They make music, rarely but carefully. The stone-song is one form — finding resonances in the bedrock, encouraging the stone to produce particular tones, creating performances that last hours and shift so slowly that the changes are barely perceptible. The wind-catching instruments produce another kind of music, pieces that compose themselves through the plateau's own air currents, the Yathren simply providing the means for the wind to sing.

They create gardens that are somewhere between practical cultivation and sculpture. The mat-plants are encouraged into spirals, into geometric patterns, into configurations that serve no function except beauty and the pleasure of seeing growth organized meaningfully.

They write, constantly, those records that are part data and part poetry. Even purely practical documents — water levels, weather notes — are written with attention to rhythm and phrasing, because language here is precious, and precision includes elegance.

And they make the maps, those extraordinary documents that try to capture four-dimensional space on two-dimensional surfaces, using notation systems that blend mathematics and music and visual art. The maps are beautiful even when they're incomprehensible, designs that please the eye while conveying information to those who've learned to read them.

## What They've Forgotten

The Yathren have been on the plateau long enough that some knowledge has been lost, or transformed beyond recognition.

They don't remember, precisely, where they came from. The records say "below," but which lowland culture, which specific valley or coast or forest region — this is gone. They remember that ancestors climbed up, seeking something (refuge, or revelation, or simply new territory), but the details have faded into story.

They've lost or abandoned technologies that require resources the plateau doesn't provide. They once had more metal, worked more iron, but the techniques have been forgotten as the materials became unavailable. They once had more varied food-plants, but most couldn't survive the cold, and the knowledge of cultivating them has disappeared.

They've lost urgency and perhaps some range of emotion that goes with urgency. The Yathren don't hate, or hate rarely, because hatred requires a kind of energy they can't spare. They experience contentment, satisfaction, mild pleasure, occasional sorrow, but not the peaks of joy or depths of despair that shorter-lived, faster-paced peoples know.

They've lost — or transformed — their sense of individuality. The Yathren have names, have personal preferences, have distinct identities, but these are held more lightly than in lowland cultures. The boundary between self and community, between personal and collective, between individual and plateau is porous here.

And they've lost, or possibly gained, their sense of reality as stable. They can't remember anymore what it felt like to trust that distances were fixed, that walking forward meant arriving at a predictable location, that doors led to single, specific places. That certainty is gone, replaced by the comfortable understanding that the world is flexible, responsive, stranger than it appears.

## What Remains

Despite all changes, despite adaptation and transformation and the plateau's slow rewriting of whoever lives here, the Yathren remain recognizably human.

They laugh, sometimes, at absurdities or small surprises. The laughter is quiet, infrequent, but genuine.

They get frustrated when stone won't cooperate or water routes shift unexpectedly or the mat-plants grow in unhelpful patterns. The frustration doesn't last — they've learned patience — but it's there, that flash of human annoyance at a world that won't behave.

They feel affection for each other, for specific individuals, for children learning their first routes, for the very old preparing for their final walks. The affection is expressed through attention rather than words, but it's real.

They experience curiosity, wondering what's happening in distant settlements, what changes the plateau is undergoing in areas they haven't visited, what the deep structures might be, what happens to those who walk into the flux and don't return.

They take pride in work well done, in a building that sits perfectly in its location, in a garden that thrives beyond expectation, in a route-map that accurately predicts probability patterns.

They feel satisfaction at the end of a long task, completing a project that took years, seeing water flow smoothly through a newly-cleared channel, watching a young stone-singer master a technique.

They hope, quietly, that the plateau will continue, that the settlements will persist, that future generations will walk these stones and tend these waters and carry forward the patient work of living here.

They are human, still, under the glow and the changed eyes and the geological patience. They are human in their persistence, their adaptation, their determination to make meaning in this cold vastness, their refusal to leave despite every hardship, their insistence on building homes and raising children and creating beauty in a place that has no need of human presence and offers little comfort.

They remain because this is where they are, and where you are is where you live, and living means attending to the place that holds you, becoming part of its patterns, letting it change you while you, in your small way, change it.

They are the people of the long patience, the stone-dreamers, the ones who wait and watch and work slowly toward understandings that will take lifetimes to achieve.

They are the Yathren, and they remain.
