# The Inhabitants of the Rootworks

## The Cultivators

They are the most numerous, and in many ways the most essential. Without them, the city would strangle itself in weeks.

The cultivators move through the galleries with a particular gait—economical, observant, stopping frequently to examine growth nodes, to test the flexibility of new vines, to press their palms against trunk-walls and feel for the subtle temperature variations that indicate nutrient flow or blockage. They carry pruning tools on belt-loops: curved knives with hooked tips, small saws with peculiar teeth designed not to tear the fibrous plant-flesh, injection guns loaded with growth hormones or retardants.

Most are women, though not exclusively. The ratio is roughly two-to-one, and no one can quite articulate why. Some suggest women have better fine motor control for the delicate grafting work. Others point to wage differentials on the surface that make the Rootworks contracts comparatively more attractive to female workers. But many cultivators dismiss both explanations—they insist it's something about patience, about the particular quality of attention the work requires. The plants respond to consistency, to daily observation, to the accumulation of small adjustments over time. This is what they say. The men who do the work are equally capable, equally patient. The gender imbalance remains mysterious.

They develop a relationship with their sections. Each cultivator is responsible for specific galleries, specific buildings. They learn the individual character of the growth in their territory—which corners tend toward explosive sprouting, which walls remain stubbornly bare, where volunteer growth appears most frequently. They name things. Not officially, but privately. A particularly massive trunk might be "the grandmother." A vine that produces exceptional bioluminescence becomes "the bright one." An area where growth repeatedly fails is "the dead corner," and its resistance becomes a personal challenge.

The work is physical but not brutally so. They climb—the plant structures provide excellent handholds, and most cultivators develop an almost casual relationship with heights that would terrify surface workers. They reach, stretch, spend hours with arms above their heads training vines into desired positions. Their hands are distinctive: callused in unusual places, often stained greenish-brown from plant sap, with fine scars from thorns and splinters. They can identify colleagues by hands alone.

They smell of the plants. The turpentine-oil permeates their clothing, their hair, eventually their skin. After years in the Rootworks, a cultivator carries the scent even when they haven't touched a plant in days. It's not unpleasant—slightly medicinal, resinous, sharp. But it marks them. On the surface, during rare leaves, they're identified immediately. The smell of the deep.

Many develop allergies or sensitivities. Respiratory issues are common—chronic coughs, a tendency toward bronchitis, a wheeze that never quite departs. The plant volatiles irritate lung tissue. Some develop skin reactions, contact dermatitis from the sap or the nutrient slurry. The medical staff treat these conditions routinely, prescribe respirators and barrier creams, but many cultivators resist the protections—they claim it reduces their sensitivity, their ability to smell problems in the growth. There's likely truth in this. The nose knows when plant-flesh is beginning to rot, when nutrient mixture ratios are wrong. A respirator blocks crucial information.

They talk to the plants. Not all of them, and those who do often deny it if asked directly. But in the quiet of a maintenance shift, alone in a gallery with only the clicking of bark-miners for company, you'll hear them. Soft encouragement to a struggling sprout. Scolding for a vine that's growing in prohibited directions. Explanations, as if the plant might understand the architectural requirements it's violating. Is it superstition? Habit? Something else? The plants don't respond—not visibly, not immediately. But cultivators will tell you, privately, that spoken-to sections do better. They grow more cooperatively. They require less aggressive pruning.

The cultivators have their own informal hierarchy, independent of official job classifications. Expertise is recognized through subtle signs—who gets consulted when a growth problem proves intractable, who's invited to examine a new volunteer specimen, whose judgment is trusted in the delicate decisions about whether to preserve or remove an unplanned vine. The most respected cultivators are those who've been in the Rootworks since the earliest galleries, who remember when the techniques were still being developed, when no one was certain the plants would survive at all.

They're territorial but not hostile. A cultivator entering another's section will announce themselves, will ask permission before touching anything. It's professional courtesy, but also something deeper—an acknowledgment that the relationship between cultivator and section is particular, individual, almost intimate. You don't interfere with someone else's growth.

They have a complex relationship with the bio-engineers. The engineers design the plants, specify the protocols, issue the directives. The cultivators implement them, and in implementing, discover what actually works versus what works in theory. There's respect in both directions but also tension. Engineers propose modifications that cultivators know will fail in practice. Cultivators develop techniques that engineers insist are unnecessary or inefficient, yet the sections maintained by those techniques consistently outperform the protocol-compliant ones.

The compromise is informal: engineers write the official procedures, cultivators follow them approximately, and everyone understands that there's a margin of practical deviation that will be ignored unless something goes catastrophically wrong.

## The Engineers

They are fewer, more educated, better paid. They occupy the laboratories and the planning offices, spaces that are kept more sterile than the rest of the Rootworks—metal and glass and careful climate control, with the plant growth restricted to specimen tanks and experimental beds.

The bio-engineers fall into two broad camps, though individuals often shift between them or occupy both simultaneously:

The refiners work on optimizing the existing cultivars. They seek faster growth rates, stronger structural properties, better bioluminescence, improved nutrient efficiency. Their work is incremental, careful, based on extensive documentation and controlled trials. They measure everything—growth rates to the millimeter, chemical composition of plant tissues analyzed in parts per million, light output calibrated against standard sources. They publish papers. They present findings at conferences held in the driest, best-lit gallery, where colleagues from the surface visit and ask questions.

The refiners are pragmatists. They understand that the Rootworks exists to serve industrial purposes, that the plants are tools, resources, products. Beauty and strangeness are secondary to function. When a cultivar produces spectacular coloration but structurally inferior wood, they cull it without sentiment.

The explorers are interested in what the plants might become, might do, might make possible. They work with the volunteer growth, with the specimens that appear in unauthorized places, with mutations and sports and the strange forms that occasionally emerge from genetic drift or stress responses. They're less interested in optimization than in discovery.

The explorers tend to be younger, or at least newer to the Rootworks. They're drawn by the novelty, the biological weirdness of an engineered organism that's evolving in real-time in an environment it wasn't designed for but is adapting to anyway. They ask questions the refiners consider irrelevant: What do the plants sense? Do they respond to the presence of humans beyond simple touch? What information might be encoded in the geometric patterns the vines sometimes form?

The division between refiners and explorers is professional but cordial. They share laboratory space, collaborate on projects, peer-review each other's work. But their fundamental orientations differ. Refiners see problems to solve. Explorers see mysteries to investigate.

All the engineers share certain characteristics: they're comfortable with ambiguity and uncertainty in ways that surface scientists often aren't. The plants don't behave according to standard botanical principles. The underground environment produces effects that have no terrestrial analogues. Much of their work consists of careful observation followed by tentative hypothesis followed by more observation. The usual scientific method of controlled experiment is often impossible—you can't establish true control groups when every section of the Rootworks has unique characteristics, when the ecosystem is too young to have established baselines.

They develop strange specializations. One engineer focuses exclusively on root systems, spends entire shifts in the deep galleries where the major roots are exposed, mapping their extent, documenting their penetration into the bedrock. Another specializes in the acoustic properties of the plant-wood, testing how different growth conditions affect resonance and sound transmission. A third is obsessed with the bark-miners, maintaining elaborate census records, documenting population fluctuations, dissecting specimens to understand their digestive chemistry.

The engineers are predominantly from elsewhere, surface-trained, recruited specifically for their expertise. Few are permanent residents. They come for contract periods—two years, three years—and then return to surface institutions, their time in the Rootworks becoming a prestigious line on their credentials. They publish extensively about their experiences, and the Rootworks has spawned a minor academic industry of papers, monographs, and theoretical frameworks.

But some stay. Some extend their contracts indefinitely, become de facto permanent residents. These long-term engineers undergo a visible transformation. They become less interested in publication, less concerned with how their work appears to surface institutions. They sink deeper into the particularity of the place, into questions that have no relevance outside the Rootworks but are utterly compelling within it.

They start sounding like the cultivators. They develop the same territorial awareness, the same defensive attitude toward their experimental sections. They begin to talk about the plants as if they have personalities, preferences, moods. The transformation is gradual enough that they often don't notice it in themselves, but their surface colleagues do—correspondence becomes more cryptic, less grounded in standard terminology, filled with references to local phenomena that outsiders can't contextualize.

## The Miners

They're less visible than cultivators or engineers, but in many ways the most fundamental to the entire enterprise. Without them, there would be nothing—no caverns, no space for growth, no ore to process, no economic justification for the Rootworks' existence.

They work at the frontier, the deepest galleries where the excavation continues. The conditions there are harsh—thick with stone dust, loud with drilling equipment, hot from the exertion and the machinery. They operate in rotating shifts, because the work is too demanding for extended periods. Four hours on, four hours off, descending to the working face where the bedrock is still intact, still being broken.

The miners are predominantly men, though the gender ratio is less skewed than with the cultivators. The work requires a particular kind of physical strength—not just muscle, but the ability to maintain leverage in awkward positions, to swing a hammer repeatedly without losing accuracy, to operate vibrating equipment for hours without losing coordination. Age matters more here than elsewhere in the Rootworks. Few miners continue past their forties. The work breaks you down. Joints deteriorate. Hearing degrades despite the protective equipment. The dust embeds itself in lung tissue, creating chronic respiratory problems that eventually force retirement.

They're paid better than almost anyone else in the Rootworks, surface management and senior engineers excepted. The wage differential reflects both the difficulty and the necessity of the work. Miners can afford private quarters, better food, more generous water rations. They tend to spend lavishly during their off-shifts—the work is too exhausting to save carefully, and there's an unspoken understanding that mining careers are short, that you take what you can while you can.

The culture is intensely communal. Mining crews work together for months or years, developing the deep trust that comes from shared risk. Cave-ins are rare but catastrophic. Equipment failures can be lethal. The work requires absolute attention and coordination—you watch your crew-mates, they watch you, everyone stays alive through collective vigilance.

They have their own section of the residential galleries, and it has a distinct character. Louder, more boisterous. The miners bring their work energy back with them, need to discharge the adrenaline and tension before they can rest. There's drinking, gambling, elaborate meals prepared and shared communally. The quarters are decorated more extensively than elsewhere—the miners can afford luxuries, and many become collectors. Surface goods, curiosities, anything that breaks the visual monotony of plant-wood and stone.

Their relationship with the rest of the Rootworks is complex. The cultivators and engineers exist because the miners create space. But the miners often view the plant-growth with something between suspicion and contempt. They excavate stone, only to have their work immediately colonized by vegetation, turned into something strange and hybrid. Some miners refuse to live in plant-grown buildings, insist on quarters that are purely stone and metal, bare and unmodified. Others don't care—a wall is a wall, regardless of what it's made of.

There's little direct interaction between miners and cultivators during work hours—they occupy different spaces, different levels. But in the common areas, during shared off-shifts, relationships develop. Romances are common, though they tend to be intense and unstable. The cultural differences are significant—cultivators' patience and slow time versus miners' urgency and intensity. Friendships form more easily, based on mutual respect if not mutual understanding.

The miners have their own relationship with depth, different from anyone else's. They're the ones who go deepest, who spend time at the absolute frontier where the Rootworks ends and pure geology begins. They experience the depth-thing more intensely than others. Some become obsessed with going deeper, pushing the excavation further, finding out what's down there beyond the current working face. Others develop claustrophobia or depth-anxiety, find themselves unable to descend to the lower levels, request reassignment to the upper galleries where the ceiling is less oppressive.

The turnover rate is high. Contracts are typically short—six months, a year. Many miners come for a single term, make substantial wages, then return to the surface and never come back. But there's a core population of career miners, people who've been working the Rootworks excavation since the earliest days. These veterans are repository of practical knowledge—they know the rock, can read the stone faces, predict where ore veins run, identify unstable formations. Their judgment is trusted absolutely.

They're superstitious in ways that other Rootworks residents aren't. Perhaps it's the danger of the work, the constant proximity to potential disaster. They observe rituals before entering the deep shafts, small gestures that might be prayers or might be habits worn smooth by repetition. They attribute agency to the stone—rock that "wants" to fall, tunnels that "resist" excavation, galleries that feel wrong even when all the engineering surveys say they're stable. The engineers dismiss this as primitive thinking, but even they'll occasionally admit that some sections feel different, that intuition sometimes catches problems that instruments miss.

## The Factory Workers

They occupy the middle galleries, the spaces between the deep frontier and the upper residential levels. The factories process what the miners extract and what the cultivators grow, transform raw materials into products.

The work is varied—smelting and refining, harvesting and processing plant matter, manufacturing goods from both mineral and biological materials. Each factory has its own character, its own requirements, its own workforce culture.

The refineries are brutally hot. Stone and ore are crushed, heated, separated. The workers tend to be young, able to tolerate the temperature extremes. They work shorter shifts than miners but in equally demanding conditions. The pay is moderate. The refineries have high turnover—it's work you do for a year or two, then move into something less physically taxing.

The bio-processing facilities are cooler but more chemically complex. Plant material is harvested, sorted, treated. Some becomes construction material, some becomes textile fiber, some becomes pelletized fuel. The work requires more training than the refineries—you need to understand the different cultivars, recognize quality issues, know which processing method to apply to which material. The workforce is mixed gender, tends to be slightly older, more stable. These are people who've been in the Rootworks for several contract cycles, who've developed expertise and find the work satisfying in its way.

The manufacturing workshops are the most varied. Some produce goods for internal use—furniture, tools, replacement parts. Others make specialty items for export to the surface—the unique textiles, plant-oil derivatives, processed minerals. The workshops are often small, employing a dozen or fewer workers. The atmosphere is more artisanal than industrial. Craftspeople develop particular skills, take pride in their products.

There's a status hierarchy among factory workers, informal but clearly understood. Refinery work is lowest-prestige—it's hot, dangerous, requires minimal skill. Bio-processing is middle-tier—more complex, better conditions, but still fundamentally processing rather than creating. Manufacturing, especially the specialty workshops, is highest-status—these workers are making things, producing goods that bear their mark, developing reputations.

The factory workers are the most transient population. Many are contract laborers who come to the Rootworks for specific terms, fulfill their obligations, then leave. They tend to live in communal housing, dormitory-style quarters that are functional but impersonal. They don't develop the attachment to place that cultivators or long-term engineers do. The Rootworks is, for them, a job site rather than a home.

But there are exceptions. Some workers discover they prefer underground life, find the Rootworks more tolerable than surface cities. They extend contracts, transition from temporary to permanent status, gradually shift into more specialized roles. A refinery worker might train as a cultivator. A bio-processor might apprentice in a specialty workshop. The Rootworks offers mobility for those willing to stay, to invest time in developing local expertise.

The factory workers interact most with the broader population. They staff the canteens, maintain the common areas, operate the services that keep the city functional. They're visible in ways that miners and cultivators often aren't—they work during the standard shifts, move through the main galleries, participate in the social life of the Rootworks.

They're also the primary interface with the surface. Goods they produce are shipped up through the freight elevators. Materials they need come down the same route. They track surface markets, adjust production to meet demand, coordinate with surface managers. This gives them a different perspective—they remain more connected to the external world, less immersed in the Rootworks' peculiarities.

Some become traders, informal but essential. They arrange trades between workers—a miner wants a custom tool, a cultivator has extra food rations, someone needs a repair done. The traders facilitate these exchanges, taking a small percentage as compensation. They know everyone, know what's available, know what people need. They become community hubs in their own right.

## The Support Staff

They're easy to overlook, but the Rootworks would collapse without them.

The maintenance crews keep the mechanical systems functional. They service the generators, maintain the ventilation equipment, repair the pumps that deliver nutrient slurry. It's skilled work requiring both mechanical aptitude and the ability to improvise—spare parts are limited, and replacement systems can take months to arrive from the surface. The maintenance crews develop elaborate jerry-rigging skills, keeping equipment running with improvised repairs and creative substitutions.

They work odd hours, responding to breakdowns whenever they occur. A generator failure at 3 AM by the official clock means someone has to descend immediately, diagnose the problem, get it running before the backup systems are exhausted. They're perpetually exhausted themselves, running on irregular sleep schedules, but they take fierce pride in keeping the city alive.

The medical staff operate a clinic in one of the mid-level galleries. It's modestly equipped—enough to handle common injuries and illnesses, but anything serious requires evacuation to the surface. The primary work is preventive: treating respiratory infections before they become pneumonia, managing the chronic conditions that develop from long-term Rootworks residence, providing mental health support for those struggling with the isolation or the strangeness.

The medical staff tend to be older, often semi-retired surface practitioners who were recruited specifically for Rootworks duty. They're competent but not ambitious—this is not where cutting-edge medicine happens. But they develop expertise in the unique conditions of underground life, become authorities on the health effects of plant-volatile exposure, on the psychological impacts of extended depth residence.

The kitchen staff operate the canteens, the communal dining facilities scattered throughout the residential levels. Food is imported—fresh produce, proteins, staples—and prepared in industrial quantities. The work is relentlessly repetitive, but the kitchen staff develop their own pride and creativity. They experiment with the limited ingredients, develop signature dishes, compete informally for the best reputation.

Food matters deeply in the Rootworks. It's one of the few sensory experiences that connects directly to the surface, to the world of sunlight and seasons. A well-prepared meal is a luxury, a reminder of normal human life. The kitchen staff who can deliver that reminder are valued far beyond their official pay grade.

The administrative staff manage logistics, payroll, records. They're the least visible population, working in small offices connected to the main galleries, spending shifts with documentation and databases. They coordinate the supply chains, process the personnel paperwork, maintain the archive of technical documentation. It's tedious work, but it requires meticulous attention—errors in supply orders or contract terms can have serious consequences.

Most administrative staff are surface-based, descending for work shifts but living above. They maintain the connection between the Rootworks and its parent organization, translate between the peculiar underground culture and the conventional business structures of the surface. They're not quite outsiders, but they're not quite residents either—they occupy an intermediate position, necessary but somewhat apart.

## The Children

There weren't supposed to be children. The Rootworks was designed as an industrial settlement, adult workers on fixed-term contracts. But people don't always follow the plan.

Pregnancies happened. Some workers were already pregnant when they arrived, didn't know or didn't disclose. Others occurred during residence—relationships form, and not all workers are diligent about contraception. Initially, pregnant workers were evacuated to the surface for the duration of their pregnancy and the infant's early years. But some refused to leave, insisted on remaining. The policy proved unenforceable. The first births in the Rootworks occurred in the clinic, and after that, the administration adapted.

Now there are perhaps twenty children, ranging from infants to seven or eight years old. They exist in a strange state—neither planned nor prohibited, accommodated but not officially supported.

The youngest are raised primarily by their parents in private quarters. But as they become mobile, they enter the communal spaces, and the entire Rootworks becomes their environment. They explore with the fearlessness of those who know no alternative. The plant-grown buildings are their normal, the bioluminescence their familiar light, the nutrient-seals their equivalent of dogs or cats.

They climb constantly. The plant structures provide endless opportunities—branching trunks, vine-networks, irregular surfaces perfect for small hands and feet. The adults watch with terror at first, convinced someone will fall, but the children are extraordinarily competent. They develop an almost instinctive sense of which branches will hold weight, which surfaces are stable. Falls are rare and usually minor.

They're healthier than anyone expected. The concern was that the air quality, the lack of sunlight, the general strangeness would stunt development. But the children seem robust. They have the chronic cough that all long-term residents develop, but otherwise they're vigorous, energetic, developing normally. Whatever health effects the Rootworks produces, they seem to tolerate them better than adults.

They speak in a dialect that's beginning to diverge from surface standard. They've developed their own vocabulary—words for things that exist only in the Rootworks, terms borrowed from multiple languages (the workforce is international), syntax patterns influenced by the acoustic properties of the spaces they inhabit. Adults can understand them, but there's a recognizable difference, an emerging linguistic marker of nativity.

They have favorite places. Certain galleries become gathering spots—areas where the light is good, where there are convenient climbing structures, where adults are tolerant of noise and activity. They form small groups, proto-friendships based on proximity and compatibility. They play games that are partially recognizable—tag becomes complex in three-dimensional plant-tangles—and partially novel, invented responses to this unique environment.

The nutrient-seals are their particular companions. The creatures seem to recognize children as distinct from adults, approach them more readily, tolerate handling that would make them flee from adult contact. Children will drape themselves across the seals, use them as pillows or seats. The seals accept this with apparent contentment. There's speculation about pheromones, about some chemical marker of pre-adolescence that the seals find non-threatening. No one knows for certain.

The children are watched collectively. In surface cities, child-rearing is primarily parental, supplemented by formal institutions. Here, it's communal by necessity and geography. A child might breakfast with their parents, spend the day in the care of a cultivator who doesn't mind company while working, eat dinner in a canteen with whoever's present, sleep in their family quarters. The entire city participates in their raising, not through formal arrangement but through practical accommodation.

Education is improvised. There are no schools—the population is too small, too transient. Parents teach what they can. Workers with relevant expertise offer informal lessons. An engineer might spend an off-shift explaining basic biology. A factory worker might teach arithmetic through counting inventory. It's fragmentary, inconsistent, but the children are learning.

What they'll become is uncertain. They're the first generation to grow up in this environment, to consider the Rootworks normal rather than strange. They're developing a relationship with this place that no adult can fully access—it's not a choice for them, not an adventure or an employment opportunity. It's simply the world, and they're native to it in ways that colonists never are.

## The Permanent Residents

Some people never leave.

They arrive on contract, intending to stay a year, maybe two. But the contract ends and they extend. Then extend again. Years pass. They become fixtures, their presence so constant that newer arrivals assume they're original founders, that they've been here since the first excavations.

They're diverse in background—former miners too broken for continued excavation work but too attached to leave, cultivators who've achieved near-mystical skill with the plants, engineers whose research questions have become so specific to the Rootworks that they're unemployable elsewhere, factory workers who simply prefer this life to surface alternatives.

What they share is a quality of belonging. They move through the galleries with complete comfort, navigating by instinct rather than conscious memory. They know the acoustic quirks of every major space, can identify location by sound alone. They recognize individual nutrient-seals, have names for specific plants. They're consulted when decisions need to be made about the city's development—their opinion carries weight simply through temporal primacy.

They've adapted physiologically in ways that visitors don't. Their eyes are more sensitive to the low light levels, can distinguish bioluminescent variations that others miss. Their respiratory systems have adjusted to the particular atmospheric composition—the chronic cough that newer residents struggle with becomes less pronounced, eventually fading to something barely noticeable. Their sense of smell has recalibrated, no longer registering the plant-oil and mineral dust as foreign.

Many have developed rituals and routines so specific to the Rootworks that they'd be impossible to maintain elsewhere. A cultivator who drinks tea made from plant-matter at specific times, using water from a particular condensation collector. An engineer who spends exactly thirty minutes each day in the deepest accessible gallery, simply sitting, observing. A former miner who walks the same circuit through the residential levels every evening, exchanging greetings with whoever's present.

These routines aren't superstition exactly—or maybe they are, but functional superstition, practices that maintain psychological stability in an environment that can feel profoundly alienating. The routines provide structure, create meaning, transform the Rootworks from a place you happen to be into a place you deliberately inhabit.

The permanent residents often develop intense relationships with specific locations. A particular gallery becomes "theirs" in some unofficial but recognized way. They maintain it, improve it, defend it against proposed modifications. They'll argue passionately against administrative plans that would affect their space, and often successfully—the administrators have learned that permanent residents' intuitions about the city's development are frequently correct, that respecting their territorial claims produces better outcomes than overriding them.

They become historians by default. They remember when specific buildings were grown, know the stories behind various architectural decisions, can explain why certain passages exist and others were abandoned. Newer residents seek them out for guidance, for context, for the oral history that hasn't been formally documented.

Many are lonely. The permanent residents have often outlasted their original social networks. Friends and partners left, contracts ending, returning to the surface. New relationships form but are complicated by the transience of much of the population—investing emotionally in people who'll likely leave within months or years becomes exhausting. Some permanent residents become somewhat isolated, friendly but not close with anyone, their deepest relationships being with the place itself rather than with people.

Others form tight bonds with fellow permanents, small communities of those who've stayed. These groups have their own internal cultures, private jokes, shared experiences that span years. They meet regularly in specific locations, maintain traditions, celebrate anniversaries that outsiders aren't aware exist.

The relationship with the surface becomes increasingly abstract. Some permanent residents ascend occasionally—annual trips for medical evaluation, brief visits to family. Others simply don't. The surface becomes theoretical, a place that exists in reports and imported goods but not in lived experience. A few claim they no longer could live there, that the sensory assault would be intolerable, that they've become too specifically adapted to the Rootworks' environment.

There's a question no one quite articulates: at what point does residence become permanence? When does a long-term contract worker transition into something else, something that might not have a name yet? The administrative categories remain unchanged—everyone is technically a contracted employee, regardless of duration. But the experiential reality is different. Some people live here now, in a way that transcends employment.

## The Visitors

Surface personnel arrive periodically—administrators conducting inspections, investors evaluating operations, academics researching the bio-engineering, journalists documenting this strange settlement.

They're immediately identifiable. They move wrong, uncertain in the variable light, constantly looking around with an attention that residents no longer maintain. They over-dress, bringing excessive layers for the constant cool temperature. They react to things residents no longer notice—the smell, the sounds, the nutrient-seals, the sheer strangeness of plant-grown architecture in underground caverns.

The visitors are treated with tolerance bordering on amusement. Residents understand that outsider perspectives are often absurd, that what seems normal to inhabitants looks bizarre to those who haven't acclimated. Children are especially fascinated by visitors, trailing after them, observing their reactions, occasionally asking questions that reveal how alien the surface world has become even to those born to surface parents.

The visitors' reports and documentation are strange mirrors—they describe the Rootworks in terms that residents barely recognize. Features that inhabitants consider mundane are highlighted as remarkable. Aspects of daily life that seem obvious are explained in elaborate detail. Reading these accounts produces a disorienting double-vision—is this really what we're doing? Is this place actually that strange?

Some visitors are seduced, become entranced by the Rootworks, extend their stays, occasionally never leave. The transition from visitor to resident is marked by subtle changes—they stop photographing everything, stop asking questions about the obvious, start noticing things that actually matter rather than things that merely look exotic.

Others can't leave fast enough. The Rootworks produces strong reactions—fascination or revulsion, rarely indifference. Some visitors experience claustrophobia so severe they require sedation for the ascent. Others have opposite reactions, finding the enclosed spaces comforting, the surface world suddenly seeming too open, too exposed.

The administrators are repeat visitors, descending monthly for inspections and meetings. They occupy an intermediate category—familiar with the Rootworks but not immersed in it, maintaining surface perspective while understanding underground realities. They're the translators, the negotiators between the two worlds. Their role requires not taking sides, maintaining simultaneous loyalty to corporate structures and practical operational needs.

## The Departed

They haunt the place despite their absence.

Workers who completed their contracts and left, returning to the surface. Sometimes they're remembered specifically—a particularly skilled cultivator, a miner who survived a collapse, an engineer whose research proved crucial. More often they're collective memory, the vague awareness that thousands of people have moved through the Rootworks, contributed their labor, then vanished upward.

Their traces remain. Modifications to quarters that subsequent residents maintain without knowing who made them. Techniques that are taught without attribution to their originators. Tools marked with names of people no one current remembers. The Rootworks is layered with anonymous history, contributions from the departed that have become simply "how we do things."

Some maintain contact, sending messages, occasionally visiting. These connections are valued—they provide information about the surface world, maintain relationships, offer perspective on the Rootworks from outside. But the communications often feel strained, translated across an experiential gap that grows wider with time. What can you say to someone who left about what it's like to stay? How do you explain the slow changes in perception, the way the place becomes more itself the longer you inhabit it?

Others are never heard from again. Contracts end, workers ascend, and the Rootworks simply loses track of them. Did they return to families? Find other work? The administration maintains records, but individual residents often don't know what happened to people they worked beside for months or years. There's something melancholy about this—the transience, the constant population churn, relationships that simply end without closure.

And there are deaths. Few, but inevitable in any industrial operation. Accidents mostly—a collapse, a machinery failure, one memorable case of someone falling from an upper-level walkway. The bodies are returned to the surface for burial; the Rootworks has no cemetery, no space allocated for the dead. But the deaths are remembered, marked in small ways. A cultivator who died in a collapse has their favorite section maintained with special care, their techniques preserved and taught. The memory is practical rather than sentimental—they're honored through continuation, through the persistence of their contributions.

## The Collective Entity

Individual roles and personalities matter, but increasingly, there's a sense of the Rootworks population as a collective organism, a social entity with emergent properties that exceed individual intentions.

The city responds to problems collectively. When a crisis occurs—a major mechanical failure, a structural problem in the plant growth, a threatened collapse—the response is rapid and uncoordinated in the formal sense but highly effective. People simply act, contributing whatever skills or labor they can, without waiting for direction or organization. The collective competence is striking, especially to visitors accustomed to hierarchical emergency response.

There's a distributed intelligence to the place. No individual knows everything, but collectively, the population maintains comprehensive knowledge. You need information about an obscure plant-disease? Someone knows. A particular tool breaks? Someone can fabricate a replacement. A social problem emerges? Someone has relevant experience. The challenge is often simply finding the right person, connecting the problem to the knowledge.

This has led to informal information networks—people who know who knows what, who can direct inquiries appropriately. These network-hubs become crucial infrastructure, more important than any database or documentation system. They're usually long-term residents, people who've been around long enough to accumulate the meta-knowledge of collective expertise.

The population self-regulates in complex ways. Disputes are usually resolved informally, through peer pressure and social consensus rather than formal authority. Violations of norms—stealing, excessive noise, dangerous behavior—are addressed communally. The responses range from simple conversation to subtle ostracism. Formal discipline is rare and usually involves surface administrators only when the issue affects operations significantly.

This self-regulation extends to resource distribution. While the administration manages formal rations and allocations, there's extensive informal trading and sharing. Surpluses are redistributed, shortages are collectively addressed. The economy is partially formal, partially gift-based, partially barter. The complexity would be difficult to describe to an outsider, but residents navigate it intuitively.

Cultural practices are emerging, traditions that have no single origin but have become established through repetition and collective adoption. The way shift-changes are marked in certain galleries—a bell rung, though no one remembers who started ringing it or why. The seasonal celebrations tied to surface holidays but transformed in underground context. The memorial practices for significant events in the city's development.

Music happens spontaneously. Someone starts singing in a gallery with good acoustics, others join, the sound builds and echoes, becomes briefly communal. Instruments are rare—bulky to import, difficult to maintain—but voices are sufficient. The songs are often old, brought from diverse surface cultures, but they're being transformed, adapted to the Rootworks' acoustics, acquiring new verses that reference local experience.

There's a growing sense of identity—not just working in the Rootworks but being of it, being Rootworks people, a category that's beginning to feel meaningful. It's strongest among permanent residents and children, but even contract workers feel it somewhat, a temporary affiliation that shapes behavior and perception during their residence.

This collective identity is still forming, still unclear. What does it mean to be Rootworks? Is it just location, or something more? The question is open, being answered through lived experience rather than deliberate definition. But something is emerging, some sense of shared culture and mutual understanding that differentiates residents from outsiders.

## The Absent

Who isn't here matters as much as who is.

There are almost no elderly. The Rootworks is too young, too physically demanding. The population skews toward working-age adults—twenties through forties predominantly, with a sharp drop-off beyond that. The few older residents are specialists whose expertise justifies their continued presence despite reduced physical capacity.

The absence of elders affects the culture significantly. There's no accumulated generational wisdom, no long-term perspective beyond a decade or so. The place has a provisional quality, a sense that everyone's making it up as they go, because largely they are. Traditional structures of age-based authority don't exist. Leadership is based on competence and experience rather than seniority in the life-cycle sense.

There's almost no one wealthy in the conventional sense. The Rootworks attracts workers—people who need employment, who are willing to tolerate strangeness and isolation for wages. The wealthy don't come here, except briefly as tourists or investors. This creates an unusual economic flatness—income variation exists but isn't extreme, and wealth cannot purchase exemption from the fundamental conditions. Everyone breathes the same air, drinks the same rationed water, navigates the same spaces.

There are no surface indigenous peoples, no connection to any human cultural tradition of living underground. The Rootworks is entirely novel, unmoored from historical precedent. This absence of tradition is liberating in some ways—no established practices to defer to, no ancestral claims to respect. But it's also disorienting. The residents are making something unprecedented, without models or guidance, and the responsibility is occasionally overwhelming.

Professional artists are scarce. The population is workers, engineers, practical people. There are creative impulses—decoration, music, the aesthetic choices in cultivation—but little dedicated art-making. The culture is emerging organically rather than being crafted deliberately. What art exists is functional, integrated into daily life rather than separated into galleries or performances.

Pets are minimal. A few people keep small animals—birds occasionally, captured nutrient-seals that become semi-domesticated. But traditional pets—dogs, cats—are almost entirely absent. They're difficult to import, difficult to maintain in the environment. The children form attachments to the nutrient-seals instead, and these relationships are genuine if strange.

Spiritual leadership is absent. There are no priests, no formal religious structures. Individual residents maintain personal practices, but there's no communal worship, no sacred spaces. The closest equivalent is the relationship some cultivators develop with the plants, the ritualized attention that resembles religious devotion. But it's not organized, not codified. The Rootworks is aggressively secular, not through hostility to religion but through simple absence.

## The Future Population

The children are growing. In a few years, they'll be adolescent, then adult. What will they do? Will they leave, seek the surface world their parents came from? Or will they stay, become the first truly native generation?

And if they stay, what will they create? They're being shaped by this environment in ways that adults cannot fully predict or control. Their relationship with depth, with the plants, with the strangeness is fundamentally different. They might develop capabilities, perspectives, ways of thinking that are specifically adapted to Rootworks existence.

There's speculation about expansion. If the Rootworks succeeds economically, if the model proves sustainable, there could be others—similar settlements in different geological formations, underground cities becoming a category rather than an experiment. Would populations transfer between them? Would Rootworks residents colonize new excavations, bringing their emergent culture to fresh caverns?

And there's the question of return-flow. Some who left are reconsidering. Former residents who found the surface unsatisfying, who remember the Rootworks with nostalgia, who want to come back. The administration is uncertain about return policies—are former workers welcome? Under what terms? The question is complicated by the fact that the Rootworks is changing, that what they remember may not exist anymore, replaced by continued evolution and development.

Births will continue. More children will arrive, the native generation will expand. Eventually—perhaps not long—the population will include adults who've never lived anywhere else, who think of the Rootworks not as a strange industrial settlement but simply as home. How will they differ from their parents? What aspects of culture and perspective are environmentally determined, what's transmitted through social learning?

The engineers speculate about biological adaptation. Long-term residence produces physiological changes—vision, respiration, possibly more. Could these become heritable? Could a population adapted specifically to underground life emerge through selection pressure? The timescales are wrong—evolution operates too slowly. But the engineers wonder anyway, document everything, maintain genetic samples, as if they're studying not just a settlement but a speciation event.

## The Totality

Stand in a main gallery during an overlap shift, when multiple populations are present simultaneously.

Cultivators move through on their routes, pausing to examine growth, making small adjustments. Miners pass through, coming off or going onto shift, loud and physical. Factory workers move between workshops and canteens, carrying materials or simply seeking different spaces. Engineers cluster near a problematic plant specimen, debating in technical language. Children chase each other through the upper tangles, their laughter echoing strangely. A nutrient-seal watches from beside a doorway. Kitchen staff transport prepared food. A maintenance worker climbs a vine-structure to access some equipment. An administrator walks through with a visitor, explaining, gesturing. Former residents on a rare return visit stand awkwardly, noting changes. A permanent resident sits on a bench, observing everything with calm familiarity.

All of this is simultaneous, overlapping, the city in its fullest expression. Not organized but not chaotic—functional, alive, proceeding with the particular rhythm that's emerged from three decades of continuous occupation and growth.

The people here are creating something unprecedented. Not intentionally—most are just working, just living, just doing what's required or what's comfortable. But collectively, through the accumulation of individual choices and adaptations, they're making a new form of human settlement, developing culture and practices appropriate to an environment that doesn't exist naturally, shouldn't be inhabited, yet is.

They're the Rootworks people. Different from surface populations in ways that are only beginning to become apparent. Changed by depth, by constant cool, by bioluminescence and plant-oil, by living in spaces that are simultaneously grown and built, natural and artificial.

They don't have a name for what they're becoming. Maybe they never will. Maybe it doesn't need naming—it simply is, self-evident to those who live it, inexplicable to those who don't.

The city grows downward. The population grows into it. Both processes continue, intertwined, co-evolving, producing something that's not quite stable but not collapsing either. Becoming. Still becoming.

Living in the wound. Growing in the dark. Descended and staying.

Human.
