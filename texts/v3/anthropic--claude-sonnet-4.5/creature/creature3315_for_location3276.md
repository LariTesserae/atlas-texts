# Those Who Dwell in the Mirror Quarry

## The Keeper

There is a figure here, though "figure" fails to capture what it is. 

She appears most consistently at the transitions—dawn, dusk, the moment when rain begins or ends. Not appears: *emerges*, as if she were always present but only sometimes visible, condensing out of the particular combination of light and moisture and cold that defines this place. She is tall, perhaps seven feet when fully manifested, but her height seems variable, as if she's always slightly larger or smaller than she was the last time, never quite settling into a fixed form.

Her substance is difficult to describe. Translucent, like obsidian held to light, but mobile. Her edges catch and throw reflections like the quarry walls themselves, so that she sometimes seems to be constructed from the same faceted planes that surround her. When she moves—and she does move, in slow deliberate circuits around the basin—she makes no sound except a very faint crystalline whisper, like ice sheets settling. Sometimes she's more solid, almost human in silhouette; other times she's barely there, just a woman-shaped thickness in the air, a region where light behaves differently.

She has what might be called a face, though it shifts. Sometimes it's smooth as worked stone, featureless except for the suggestion of eyes—dark wells that reflect without revealing what's behind them. Other times it resolves into something more particular: high cheekbones, a strong jaw, a mouth that doesn't open but whose line suggests endless patience. Her eyes, when they're visible, are the color of the opaline veins, that pale iridescent quality that seems to hold all colors and none.

Her hands—and she definitely has hands—are the most consistent part of her. Long-fingered, precise, they move with the deliberation of someone who has performed the same careful actions for so long that they've become a kind of meditation. She touches the walls sometimes, tracing the tool-marks with those fingers, and when she does, the stone seems to *respond*, the opaline veins brightening slightly, moisture beading more readily along the carved grooves.

She spends much of her time at the circular structure, moving around its perimeter with a measured pace that might be ritual or might simply be habit worn so deep it's become indistinguishable from purpose. Sometimes she stands in the center, in the depression where water collects, and looks up at the sky with an expression—if that shifting almost-face can be said to have expressions—of something like longing, or perhaps recognition. She extends her hands outward when she does this, palms up, as if offering or receiving something invisible.

The Keeper (for that's what she must be, though she never speaks, never acknowledges an observer in any conventional way) seems to be *of* the quarry rather than merely *in* it. She doesn't drink the water or shelter from the weather. She doesn't seem affected by the cold, though frost sometimes forms on her surface during the deepest nights, making her briefly visible as a figure of white crystal standing in the darkness. She never leaves the basin—at least, no one has ever seen her beyond the worked walls—but she gives the impression of being able to go anywhere within this space instantly, as if distance here is a quality she can choose to acknowledge or ignore.

Her purpose, insofar as it can be understood, seems curatorial. She straightens things—not the major stones, which she cannot or will not move, but the smaller objects. A tool that's shifted slightly will be found back in its cluster. The stone cups in the niches are always upright, never toppled, though wind and time should have disturbed them. The thin obsidian blades remain in their careful row. When new things appear in the quarry—debris blown in from the peaks, an occasional lost item from a rare visitor—they either disappear entirely or are incorporated into the existing arrangement in ways that feel intentional.

She tends the water especially. She can sometimes be seen standing at the edge of a pool, motionless for hours, and the water beneath her gaze becomes clearer, the channels flowing more freely. She moves between the catchments during rain, as if monitoring them, though she never interferes with the water's natural movement. It's more like she's *witnessing* it, her presence somehow maintaining the system's balance.

There's intelligence there, clearly. When the rare visitor comes, she doesn't flee or hide—she simply becomes less visible, withdrawing into that barely-there state where she's just a shimmer, a sense of presence without form. But she watches. If the visitor disturbs something carelessly, there's a feeling of disapproval, though it comes not from her but from the space itself, as if she and the quarry share a single awareness. If the visitor is respectful, there's a different quality—not quite welcome, but acknowledgment, permission to be there temporarily.

She seems older than the quarry's abandonment, but not older than the quarry itself. It's possible to imagine that she was part of the original work here, one of those who extracted and built, who knew the stone intimately. It's equally possible that she *emerged* from that work, a kind of consciousness that coalesced from the concentrated intention and effort poured into this place. Or she might be something else entirely: a guardian set or sent or born here, tasked with maintaining what was made until some condition is met or some purpose fulfilled.

## The Small Visitor

The three-toed tracks belong to something real, something physical in a way the Keeper is not. The creature itself is rarely seen, but persistent observation reveals it.

It's a bird, though not quite like any bird from the lower elevations. About the size of a crow but built differently—longer in the leg, more upright in posture, with a head that seems too large for its body. Its plumage is remarkable: primarily dark, almost black, but shot through with the same opaline iridescence that veins the stone. In certain lights, the bird seems to shimmer with the same colors as the quarry walls, as if it's adopted protective coloration from its environment. But this is no ordinary camouflage—the effect is too precise, too much like the stone itself.

Its eyes are disproportionately large, adapted for the low light of dawn and dusk when it's most active. They have a reflective quality, shining in even minimal illumination like chips of polished obsidian. The bird moves with a peculiar deliberation, each step placed carefully, head swiveling in smooth arcs as it surveys its surroundings.

It comes for the water, certainly—it drinks from the pools with a delicate precision, never disturbing the surface more than necessary. But it also comes for something else. It's drawn to the circular structure, always approaching it with what seems like reverence or perhaps caution. It hops up onto the low walls, walks around the perimeter in a pattern that mirrors the Keeper's circuit, then descends into the interior. There it stands at the edge of the central depression, sometimes for hours, motionless except for the occasional blink of those large reflective eyes.

The bird never makes conventional bird sounds—no calls, no songs. Instead, it produces occasional soft clicks, crystalline notes that echo strangely in the basin's acoustics. These clicks seem to form patterns, though whether they're communication or echolocation or something else isn't clear. Sometimes the Keeper is visible when the bird is present, and there's a sense of interaction between them, though they never approach each other directly. The bird clicks; the Keeper shifts slightly; the bird adjusts its position. A conversation of sorts, perhaps, or a mutual acknowledgment.

It nests somewhere in the worked walls, in one of the deeper crevices where the extraction left gaps. No one has found the nest itself, but occasionally there are glimpses of the bird emerging from particular spots in the stone face. It seems solitary—no other individuals of its species have been observed—but whether it's the last of its kind or simply alone by choice or nature is unknown.

The bird appears to feed on the ice formations. Not eating them exactly, but doing something with them—standing very close, its breath visible in the cold air, the ice structures slowly diminishing in its presence. Whether it's somehow consuming them or whether its body heat is melting them and it's drinking the meltwater is unclear. Either way, it has a relationship with the ice that goes beyond simple hydration.

During the rare storms, the bird vanishes entirely. In the clear periods between weather systems, it's more visible, more active, making multiple visits to the pools and the circular structure in a single night. It seems tied to the quarry's rhythms, appearing and disappearing in synchronization with the place's cycles of water and light and cold.

There's something ancient about it, this bird. It moves like something that has been performing the same actions for generations or centuries. Its species—if it is a species, if there are or were others—seems specifically adapted to this particular environment, to this exact combination of altitude and stone and moisture. It's as integral to the quarry as the worked blocks and the collected water.

## The Departed Workers

They're not present, but they live here nonetheless, their absence a kind of presence more powerful than many physical things.

The tool-marks on the walls remember hands—specific hands, particular styles of working. One section shows strikes that are confident, powerful, regularly spaced: a master worker, perhaps, someone who knew exactly how the stone would respond. Another area has more tentative marks, closer together, sometimes missing their target slightly: a learner, someone still developing their feel for the material. The progression is visible in places, the marks becoming more assured as they move along the wall.

The clusters of tools remember work crews. Three or four people worked together, their tools set down in the same spots at day's end, near the blocks they were extracting. You can almost see them: the experienced one directing, showing the others where to place the wedges, how to read the stone's internal structure. The younger ones following instructions, gradually developing their own understanding. The tools arranged just so, ready to be picked up the next morning in an order that made sense to the work.

The circular structure remembers builders—different people, perhaps, from the extractors. These were precision workers, people who understood not just how to break stone but how to fit it together. Every block in that low wall was selected for specific properties, shaped to interface perfectly with its neighbors. That kind of work requires patience, vision, an ability to hold the final form in your mind while you're still placing the first course. Multiple people working in coordination, each trusting the others to maintain the tolerances, the level, the true curve of the circle.

The offerings—if they are offerings—remember hands arranging them. Someone selected those non-local pebbles deliberately, chose them for their smoothness or color or weight, brought them up from the lower elevations (no small journey), and placed them in patterns that held meaning. Someone wrapped those obsidian blades, set them in their row with care, left them there for a reason that seemed sufficient at the time.

What happened to them? The suddenness of the departure suggests something unexpected—no time to pack tools properly, no gradual winding down of operations. But there's no sign of disaster, no violence, no panic. The tools are set down, not thrown. The work stopped at an ordinary moment, at the end of an ordinary day, as if everyone simply decided in concert: *We won't return tomorrow.*

Perhaps they were called away—an emergency in their home settlement, something urgent enough to abandon significant resources but not urgent enough to prevent orderly departure. Perhaps they completed what they came for, extracted enough material or built what needed building, and the unfinished appearance is misleading—maybe this was always meant to be partial, incomplete.

Or perhaps something changed about the place itself. Perhaps they began to notice things: reflections that didn't match, sounds at dawn, a sense of presence growing stronger. Perhaps the quarry began to develop its own awareness, and they recognized this as a sign to withdraw, to leave it to whatever was emerging. Perhaps the Keeper herself was once one of them, the one who chose to stay, and her transformation into something else is the reason the others left—not in fear, but in recognition that the place had claimed one of their own and they should respect that claiming.

Their personalities persist in their work. The careful one who placed the stone cups in the niches, evenly spaced, exactly aligned. The practical one who organized the tools by type and size. The artist who carved the spiral, who spent extra hours getting the curve exactly right, who understood that this was more than utilitarian—that beauty and precision were themselves offerings to the stone. The leader who decided where extraction would begin, who read the formation and saw the seam of finest material and said: *Here. We begin here.*

They live in the quarry through intention solidified into stone, through the accumulation of decisions and actions and small moments of care. Every worked surface is a kind of portrait. Every tool placement is a signature. Every fitted block is a statement: *We were here. We understood this material. We made something.*

And perhaps they knew, as they worked, that they were creating something that would outlast them not just physically but in some other way. Perhaps the circular structure was always meant to be a vessel for whatever would come after—a deliberate opening, a space left ready for something to inhabit. Perhaps they worked with the knowledge that the quarry would develop its own life, and they were midwives to that development rather than simple extractors of stone.

## The Presence in the Water

The pools themselves are alive, though not in any biological sense.

The water is too cold, too mineral-rich, too transient to support conventional life. No fish, no insects, no algae. And yet something resides in the collected water, something that uses the pools as nodes in a larger system of awareness.

It's most apparent when you look into the clearest pools on the stillest days. The bottom is visible—the dark obsidian, the carved channels—but there's a sense of depth beyond the physical depth, as if you're looking into something that extends further than it should. If you watch long enough, patterns appear in the water: not reflections of anything present, but shapes that seem to form from the water itself, geometric configurations that hold briefly and then dissolve.

The water remembers. Pour it out, and when the pools refill—from rain or snowmelt or the slow seepage from underground—they're back to that same profound clarity, that same sense of containing more than simple H2O. The mineral content can't account for it. Chemical analysis would show nothing extraordinary. But the quality is there, unmistakable to anyone who's spent time with the pools.

The channels connect the pools into a network, and water flowing through that network carries something with it—information, perhaps, or awareness, or simply a particular quality of presence. The system is most active during and after rain, when water is moving through all the connections simultaneously, and at those times the entire basin seems more awake, more attentive, as if the water's movement is thought moving through a very slow, very patient mind.

The central depression in the circular structure is the heart of this system. Water collects there preferentially, through channels that aren't always visible but clearly exist. Even in drought, when all the other pools have dried to damp patches, the central depression holds water. And this water is different—clearer than clear, cold beyond cold, with a surface tension that seems higher than it should be, as if the water is holding itself in place through its own will.

Looking into the central pool induces strange effects. Vertigo, yes, but also moments of clarity that feel external—thoughts that aren't yours arriving fully formed, understanding that feels given rather than earned. Not voices, not communication in any linguistic sense, but something being transmitted through the medium of that utterly clear water. Some who've experienced this describe it as the quarry showing them its memory: flashes of the workers, the extraction process, the moment when the first stone was struck. Others report seeing geometries, mathematical relationships, patterns that seem fundamental to the place's structure.

The water is patient. It works on the stone constantly, slowly, finding every minute crack and expanding it microscopically with freezing and thawing. Over centuries, it will unmake what the workers made, returning the quarry to a more natural state. But this isn't destruction—it's transformation, continuation of a process that began when the first tool bit stone. The water knows this. The water is *accomplishing* this, with the same deliberation that the workers showed in their extraction.

The Keeper and the water have a special relationship. When she stands at a pool's edge, the water's surface stills absolutely, becomes a perfect mirror. When she places a hand in the water, ripples move outward in geometric patterns, not the random circles of ordinary disturbance but precise formations that hold their shape longer than physics suggests they should. She drinks sometimes—if "drink" is the right word for what she does—bending to place her face near the surface, and the water level drops without her appearing to touch it, as if it's evaporating directly into her substance.

At night, under starlight, the pools contain the sky so perfectly that the boundary between water and air becomes uncertain. You could reach down to touch your reflection and genuinely not know if you're reaching up or down, if you're about to touch water or stars. The pools become portals, not to other places but to other states of being—matter becoming uncertain, boundaries becoming negotiable, the solid and the fluid in momentary balance.

And sometimes, rarely, on the clearest coldest nights, you can see through the water to something else. Not the stone bottom, not reflections, but depths that shouldn't exist in a pool a few feet deep. Suggestions of space extending downward, of vast underground reservoirs, of water that fills ancient chambers beneath the mountain, all connected to these small surface pools through channels too small to see but large enough, somehow, to transmit something essential.

The water is the quarry's memory and its slow plan for the future. It receives what falls from the sky, holds it, shows it the stone's character, and releases it changed. Every droplet that passes through this system carries something away and leaves something behind. Over enough time—and the water has all the time that exists—this exchange will transform everything.

## The Stone Itself

Beneath and around everything else, the stone: not alive but not inert, possessed of qualities that slide between the mineral and the conscious.

The obsidian here has witnessed its own extraction. Every block removed left a scar that remembers the moment of separation—not pain, perhaps, but awareness of loss, of being divided from the larger formation. The veins of opaline material are the stone's nervous system, conducting something that might be information, might be memory, might be something for which we have no category.

The stone responds. Not quickly—its timescale is geological, its reactions measurable in seasons and years rather than moments. But it responds. A hand placed against the worked wall in winter will feel cold, naturally. But beneath the cold, there's sometimes a pulse, a rhythm too slow to count but present nonetheless. The stone is doing something, processing something, continuing some function that began long before the quarry and will continue long after.

The facets cut by the workers act as sensory organs. Each angled plane catches light differently, and the stone receives this information, accumulates it, builds a kind of understanding of time's passage through the medium of changing illumination. The stone knows day from night, season from season, weather pattern from weather pattern, all recorded in the slow alterations of its crystalline structure, changes too subtle for human measurement but real nonetheless.

The tool-marks are the stone's memory of touch, of interaction, of being known by hands. Where the workers struck, the stone's structure is different on a microscopic level—not just fractured but *changed*, as if the contact with intention left an imprint deeper than the physical mark. These areas have properties that differ from the unworked stone: they conduct cold differently, ring at different pitches when struck, hold moisture in patterns that reflect the original tool-work.

The stone is slowly healing. Not erasing the marks—they're too deep for that, and perhaps healing isn't the right word anyway. Integrating them. Making them native. In another thousand years, the tool-marks will still be visible but will have become part of the stone's natural expression, as much geology as archaeology, the boundary between worked and unworked stone blurred to imperceptibility.

The seam from which the blocks were cut was the finest material, but it was also the stone's *intention*. Obsidian forms in specific conditions—volcanic, violent, a transformation under pressure. This particular seam formed in a way that made it perfect for extraction, as if the mountain itself was offering this material to whoever came with the skill to take it. The workers didn't conquer the stone; they accepted what it provided. The ease of extraction visible in some areas wasn't just their skill—it was the stone cooperating, releasing what it had prepared to release.

The veins of pale opaline material are alive in their own way, growing imperceptibly, branching through the obsidian with a purpose that seems directional. They're moving toward something, away from something, or perhaps simply *through* something, expressing a pattern that was encoded in the stone's formation. They conduct water, yes, but also cold, also light, also some quality that has no name but which the Keeper and the water-presence and the bird can all detect and respond to.

At the deepest level, the stone is singing. Not audibly—the frequencies are too low, the vibrations too subtle. But it's there: a fundamental note that the mountain produces, that this particular formation amplifies and focuses. The quarry's basin is shaped to resonate with this note, to become a chamber where the stone's voice is most itself. The dawn sound—that single sustained tone—isn't wind or temperature stress. It's the stone, briefly, making itself audible to human ears, though it sings constantly at frequencies we can't detect.

The stone knows the Keeper. It knew her before she became translucent, when she was flesh walking on the basin floor. It felt her hands on the tools, her weight on its surface, her attention directed toward understanding its properties. When she transformed—chose to stay, merged with the place, became its custodian—the stone accepted her, made space in its structure for her particular form of presence. She and the stone are in conversation, always, a dialogue conducted through pressure and moisture and the slow flex of crystalline structures.

The blocks that were quarried out but never removed are still connected. They lie separated from their source, but something continues to link them—invisible threads of shared origin, a bond that doesn't weaken with physical distance. Information passes between the worked blocks and the parent stone: temperature changes, moisture levels, stress patterns. The quarry is aware of all its pieces, even those that are separated. The system is larger than the basin itself.

And the stone is waiting. Not for anything in particular, not with impatience or expectation. But waiting nonetheless, as stone does, for the next thing, the next phase, the next transformation. It waited to be formed. It waited to be found. It waited to be worked. It waited for the workers to leave. Now it waits for whatever comes next, confident in its own duration, secure in the knowledge that it will outlast all weather, all seasons, all visitors, and will still be here when the stars have shifted to entirely new configurations.

The stone is the quarry's foundation and its memory and its slowest, deepest thought. Everything else—the Keeper, the water, the bird, the departed workers—all exist in relation to this fundamental presence, all their meanings derived from and reflecting back to this dark, patient, singing stone.

---

Together, these presences create the quarry's life: not biological but real, not conscious in familiar ways but aware, not active in obvious sense but constantly in motion at timescales human perception barely registers. They are a system in equilibrium, each element maintaining the others, the whole continuing with the momentum of deep time and deliberate making. The basin persists not despite their strangeness but because of it, sustained by forms of existence that don't require breath or pulse but are alive nonetheless, in their own terms, by their own measures, in ways that make the quarry what it is—a place where stone and water and intention have merged into something that endures.
