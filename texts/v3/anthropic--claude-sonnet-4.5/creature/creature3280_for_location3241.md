# Who Lives There

They are called the **Adjusters**, though they didn't name themselves—the word comes from the logbooks, centuries-old entries about "adjustment crew" and "calibration staff" that someone found moldering in a wall cavity. The Adjusters accepted it with the same practical indifference they bring to everything: a name is useful, this one will do.

There are eleven of them currently. The number fluctuates—has been as low as seven, as high as sixteen—determined not by birth or recruitment but by something more like accretion. People arrive, rarely, through the upper access (a hatch in the distant ceiling, connected to structures above that may or may not still be inhabited). Some stay. Most don't. Those who stay become Adjusters, not through any ceremony or decision, but through the simple fact of remaining until leaving becomes unthinkable.

The oldest is **Cete**, who has been here forty-one years and may be seventy years old or may be ninety—even she's uncertain, and it matters to no one. Her hands are extraordinary: knuckles swollen with arthritis into knots that somehow retain perfect dexterity, fingers that can distinguish between bolt threads by touch alone, skin mapped with scars from every cutting edge the shaft possesses. She moves between platforms with absolute confidence, never looking down, weight committed fully to each step. Her body has compressed with age and work—she's perhaps 150 centimeters tall now, compact and dense as hardwood. She knows the sound of every valve in the shaft, can identify which platform is moving by the particular groan of its pneumatic column. When she speaks, which is seldom, her voice has the same quality as the machinery: low, steady, without inflection. She sleeps on platform Seven-Underlying (they don't number the platforms in sequence but by position and relationship—Seven-Underlying is below Seven-Primary, offset to the north-spinward side), in a hammock she wove from salvaged cable, wrapped in a blanket that was once industrial insulation and is now, after decades of her body's oils and the shaft's humidity, soft as felt.

**Piet and Ossian** are brothers, arrived nine years ago as young men, now in their thirties. They're tall, made taller by the way they've learned to move here—always stretching, reaching, using the verticality. Piet's hair is almost entirely gray despite his age, gone colorless from the constant humidity and the particular mineral content of the shaft's water. Ossian's remains dark but has begun to felt into locks that he ties with wire. They speak in a truncated dialect developed between them, finishing each other's observations, sometimes trailing off mid-sentence when the other's nod indicates understanding. They work as a unit, specializing in the vine-bridges—assessing which are sound, which need reinforcement, which should be allowed to fall. They've developed an eye for the internal structure of plant material, can judge load-bearing capacity by the color and texture of bark. Piet keeps a journal, an enormous thing made of metal sheeting hammered flat, marked with a nail dipped in plant dyes. The sheets are bound with rings salvaged from pipe flanges. He documents the platforms' positions, tracking them over months and years, looking for patterns. Ossian carves—small figures of birds and lizards, shockingly accurate, made from fallen branches using a knife he sharpens daily on the same section of wall. The figures are never kept, always left on platforms as he finishes them, offerings to the space itself.

**Milithe** came from above six years ago, descended through the hatch with deliberate purpose, carrying almost nothing. She was silent for the first year—not hostile, but utterly without speech, communicating in gestures and expressions. When she finally spoke, it was to correct Cete about a valve mechanism, offering the information simply and then returning to silence for another month. She knows engineering with the certainty of formal training, can read the shaft's systems like text, understands the pneumatic logic and the hydraulic branching and the electrical runs (mostly dead now, but their ghosts remain in conduit and junction boxes). She's perhaps fifty, with the build of someone who was once physically powerful and remains strong in a more wiry fashion. Her hair is cut short with shears, uneven, purely functional. She tends the shaft's mechanical systems with something between maintenance and medicine—replacing seals with gasket material she makes from plant fiber and rendered fat, clearing blocked pipes, sometimes talking to the machinery in a low murmur, explaining what she's doing, apologizing for the discomfort. She has terrible nightmares, visible even from other platforms—her hammock thrashing, her breathing sharp and fast. No one asks. In the daytime, her hands are steady and her focus is absolute.

**Kentre** is younger, maybe twenty-five, arrived four years ago. They're small and quick, moving through the shaft like the lizards, hands and feet finding holds that seem impossible. They've made it their work to understand the ecology—which plants are edible, which medicinal, which toxic. They maintain gardens on three platforms specifically dedicated to cultivation: beans climbing the pneumatic columns, tubers in bins of accumulated soil, herbs in the pockets of better light. Their hair is growing out in a way that suggests it was once shaped in some fashionable cut from the world above; now it's long and usually tied back, often decorated with flowers that they renew daily. They talk frequently, narrating their observations to anyone nearby or to no one, a constant stream of noticing: "The moths are emerging earlier today, do you see? The temperature must have—yes, there, the column pressure is dropping, Milithe, platform Twelve-Grazing is descending faster than—I think the beans are ready, the ones on the south side, should we—" Their voice is light, musical, makes the shaft's acoustic ring in pleasant ways. They're joyful in a manner that seems impossible but is utterly genuine, delighted by pollination and fungal networks and the iridescence of dragonfly wings.

**Suom** is massive, two meters tall and broad in proportion, arrived three years ago and gives the impression of someone who fled violence, though this has never been confirmed or discussed. He speaks little but laughs often, a deep rumbling that harmonizes with the shaft's bass tones. His strength is used carefully, deployed for tasks that require it—hauling water tanks (salvaged drums, used for storing rainwater from periods of heavy dripping), lifting platforms that have become stuck, once wrestling a pneumatic column that had begun to topple back into stability while Milithe worked frantically at its base seals. He's gentle with the small things—handles seedlings with enormous fingers moving in tiny increments, rescues trapped birds with hands that could crush but only cradle. He has night-watch most often, preferring the darker hours, sitting at the edges of platforms and watching the shaft's nocturnal life with patient attention. His presence is comforting in a way that requires no interaction; just knowing Suom is awake makes sleep easier for the others.

**Vei** is perhaps sixty, arrived eight years ago, and was probably a teacher or scholar in whatever life preceded this one. She has a formalized way of speaking, grammatically precise even when discussing mundane matters: "I observe that the accumulation of detritus on platform Four-Ascending has reached a depth sufficient to require redistribution, lest the weight distribution become problematic." She's thin in a way that speaks of a body that was never robust, now reduced to minimum—all sinew and prominent bones. Her mind is extraordinary: she remembers everything, can recite the contents of Piet's journals from memory, knows the genealogy of every plant in the shaft, tracks the bird populations across years. She's developing a theory about the shaft's original purpose, working from evidence and speculation, and will occasionally deliver lectures to whoever is nearby, complex explanations involving industrial processes and architectural history and societal organization. No one knows if her theories are correct, but they're internally consistent and compellingly detailed. She tends to forget to eat, and Kentre has made it a practice to bring her food, sitting with her while she absently consumes it, still talking, her hands moving in illustrative gestures.

**Orl and Sesseny** arrived together two years ago, already a couple, already functioning as a unit. They're both in their forties, practical in a way that suggests backgrounds in maintenance or construction. Orl is stocky and bearded, the beard braided and decorated with small metal nuts threaded onto the strands. Sesseny is tall and has a scar across her face that pulls one eye into a permanent squint, giving her expressions an asymmetrical intensity. They've claimed a platform on the periphery—Nine-Radial, far from the central cluster—and have built actual structures there: a shelter with walls made from woven vines over frameworks of pipe, a rainwater collection system more elaborate than anyone else's, a workshop with organized tools. They're friendly but boundaried, participating in the loose community but maintaining clear autonomy. They're expert with rope and knots, with tensioned systems, and have improved many of the safety lines that run between platforms. They move through the shaft always roped together, even when it seems unnecessary, a practiced partnership of belay and climb.

**Ibris** is the newest, arrived eight months ago, still in the phase of adjustment that everyone recognizes—the period where you're visibly processing the strangeness, haven't yet internalized the rhythms. She's young, early twenties, with a nervous energy that manifests in constant motion: fingers drumming, feet tapping, weight shifting. She came from the upper world with injuries—burns on her arms and shoulder, now healing into textured scars. She flinches at sudden sounds, which makes the shaft's constant noise initially torturous for her, though she's adapting. She's taken to working with Kentre in the gardens, learning the plants with fierce concentration, as if memorizing them against future loss. She asks questions constantly: "Why does that platform move faster? Who built this? What happens if a column fails? Where does the water come from?" Some questions get answered, some don't have answers, some are met with shrugs. She's learning which is which. Her voice is still loud—she hasn't yet learned to modulate for the shaft's acoustic—and sometimes the others wince, but no one corrects her. She'll learn, or she won't, or she'll leave.

They don't live together in any concentrated sense. The platforms they inhabit spread across the shaft's mid-levels, clustered loosely but not densely. Cete on Seven-Underlying. Piet and Ossian sharing hammocks on Three-Ascending. Milithe on a platform so peripheral it's barely in the neighborhood, nearly at the shaft wall—Fourteen-Marginal. Kentre moves between several platforms, sleeping wherever they've been working, sometimes in the garden platforms, sometimes with the others. Suom has claimed the old shelter-platform with the chair, has expanded it with salvaged materials into something more enclosed but still open-sided. Vei sleeps on Two-Descending, surrounded by her records—sheets of metal with scratched notes, bundled plant stems with knots encoding information, arrangements of objects whose positions hold meaning for her. Orl and Sesseny on their peripheral Nine-Radial. Ibris has been rotating, trying different platforms, not yet settled into a permanent space.

The day has no jobs in the formal sense, but work is constant and shared according to ability and inclination. Water must be managed—collected, distributed, prevented from accumulating destructively. The platforms must be monitored for stability, weight redistributed when soil accumulates too heavily on one side. Vine-bridges must be assessed and sometimes cut when they become hazardous. The machinery requires intervention—not constant, but regular. A seal fails, pressure equalizes wrongly, a valve sticks. Milithe handles most mechanical work, but the others have learned the basics, can manage simple repairs, know when to fetch her for complexity.

Food comes from multiple sources. The gardens Kentre tends provide significant yield—beans, tubers, greens, herbs. But also: the shaft itself offers food to those who know where to look. Certain fungi growing on the platforms are edible, protein-rich. Birds can be trapped, though this is done sparingly, sustainably, with attention to population. Eggs can be harvested from accessible nests. Some of the plants that grow wild are edible—Kentre has mapped these, taught the others. Water brings occasional gifts: seeds from the upper world, sometimes sprouting into plants that prove useful. And there are provisions from above—irregular, unpredictable, but every few months someone (who? doing it for what reason? no one knows) lowers a bundle through the access hatch: dried goods, preserved foods, occasionally tools or materials. These gifts are distributed communally, used carefully.

They eat together most evenings, gathering on whichever platform is most central at the current platform configuration. The meeting place changes as the platforms rise and fall—sometimes it's Seven-Primary, sometimes Three-Ascending, sometimes a platform that's temporarily risen into ideal position. Someone has built a fire-platform—Five-Medial, which has been fitted with a metal basin filled with sand, allowing for safe fire. They cook there when they have food that benefits from cooking, though much is eaten raw or cold. The gathering is social but not chatty—long comfortable silences, occasional observations, Vei sometimes lecturing, Kentre narrating the day's discoveries, Piet reading from his journal if he's documented something notable.

They speak a language that's drifting from whatever languages they arrived with, developing its own vocabulary and grammar shaped by the shaft's needs. There are words for things that don't exist in the upper world: "threndle" for the particular sound a platform makes when it's about to start rising; "kelp-gap" for the distance between platforms that's too far to jump but close enough to bridge with vines; "soak-watch" for the meditative practice of sitting and listening to the shaft's water-sounds. They've developed a gestural language as well, necessary for communication across distances in the shaft's loud environment—hand signals for danger, for come here, for food, for beautiful, look at this, for I'm well, for help needed.

There's no government, no hierarchy, no formal decision-making. Things get done through a process of noticing and doing, of conversation and consensus so loose it barely qualifies as such. Someone observes that a platform is listing dangerously. Someone else agrees. Whoever has the skills and the time addresses it, maybe alone, maybe asking for help. If there's disagreement—rare, but it happens—they talk until the disagreement resolves or becomes moot or one party acquiesces. There's no mechanism for forcing anything. Authority comes only from expertise—Milithe's judgment on mechanical matters carries weight because she's consistently right, Kentre's botanical knowledge is trusted for the same reason.

Conflict is minimal, not because they're saints but because the environment demands cooperation and because those who can't manage the particular social dynamics of this place tend to leave. There have been others, over the years—people who arrived, stayed weeks or months, then climbed back out through the hatch. Some left because they couldn't adapt to the noise, the humidity, the constant moisture, the lack of privacy. Others because they needed more human contact than this small group provides, or less, or different kinds. Some because they found the shaft beautiful but couldn't live in beauty constantly. No one is prevented from leaving. The hatch is accessible, requires no key, no permission.

Those who stay have found, or created, or had revealed to them, a particular compatibility with this place. They're comfortable with slowness, with repetition, with the meditative quality of maintenance. They're able to find stimulation in subtle changes—the way light shifts across a platform, the development of a plant over weeks, the gradual alteration of a platform's position. They don't mind being dirty, wet, their clothes permanently mildewed, their bodies adapting to the humidity until dry skin becomes a memory. They've made peace with the constant noise, or found it preferable to silence, or learned to hear music in it.

They're generally private about their pasts. Information emerges slowly, in fragments, never demanded. Over years, you learn that Cete had children once, somewhere, who would now be elderly themselves if they're alive. That Milithe was involved in something she won't name but that clearly weighs on her. That Suom's hands bear scars consistent with restraints. That Vei lost someone, speaks of "we" sometimes before correcting to "I." The brothers Piet and Ossian came from a coastal city, miss the ocean, sometimes describe the shaft as a vertical sea. Orl and Sesseny fled some kind of political violence, arrived with nothing, grateful for sanctuary. Kentre speaks least about their past, deflects questions with cheerful redirection, may be running from something or toward something or simply was restless and found a place to land.

Ibris is still too new, too raw. Her story lives on her skin in the burn scars, in her startle reflex, in the way she sometimes stands at a platform edge staring upward toward the distant hatch with an expression that could be longing or could be dread or could be relief at the distance.

They care for each other with a quality that's neither familial nor romantic but something specific to this context. When Cete's arthritis is particularly bad, someone appears with tea made from willow bark—no discussion, no fuss, just the cup placed beside her. When Piet becomes too absorbed in his documentation, Ossian ensures he eats, sleeps, doesn't neglect his body. Milithe's nightmares are simply accepted; whoever is awake and nearby when she wakes gasping will sometimes call out softly, "You're here, you're in the shaft, you're safe," not approaching, just offering the voice as orientation. Suom, during his night-watches, does silent circuits that happen to pass each sleeping platform, a protective orbit. Kentre brings beauty to the others—flowers placed on sleeping platforms, a particularly perfect leaf left where someone will find it, small gifts of noticing.

When Ibris arrived, injured and terrified, it was Kentre who approached her first, slowly, hands visible, speaking softly. It was Milithe who examined her burns with clinical competence and dressed them with salves made from shaft plants. It was Vei who sat with her through the first night, talking steadily about the shaft's history, giving her something to focus on outside her panic. It was Suom who positioned himself between her and the hatch, not blocking it but making it clear that anything coming down would have to go through him first. And it was Cete who, after three days, handed her a task—something simple, weeding a garden platform—without comment, just the implicit welcome of being given work, being made useful, being integrated.

They celebrate nothing formally but mark things nonetheless. When a particularly magnificent vine-bridge completes its span between platforms, there's a shared moment of appreciation, sometimes applause. When the moths emerge in especially large numbers, they gather to watch, silent and attentive. When someone's been here another year (tracked approximately—no one maintains precise calendars), there's usually an extra-good meal, something special saved or made. When the shaft itself does something notable—a platform rising higher than it's ever been observed to go, a bird species nesting for the first time, a flower blooming out of season—they gather to witness, to acknowledge.

Death is rare but not unknown. An old man called Hesper fell between platforms seven years ago, missed his grip on a ladder. They found his body three platforms down, broken but retrievable. They wrapped him in vines and lowered him further into the mist, as far as they could manage with their ropes, giving him to the shaft's depths. They spoke about him for days afterward, telling stories, cementing memory, and then gradually spoke of him less but didn't forget. A woman named Ilse simply didn't wake one morning, five years back—her heart, presumably, given no obvious cause. They gave her to the depths as well, to the shaft that she'd loved and studied and tended.

The shaft becomes their mythology, their cosmology. The upper world—the hatch, the places beyond—becomes abstract, theoretical, less real than this immediate vertical reality. They wonder, sometimes, what the shaft was originally for, but it doesn't matter much. It's for this now: for being inhabited, for the symbiosis of people and plants and machines, for the particular strange life that's emerged here.

Cete says, rarely, that the shaft is alive. Not in a mystical sense but in the way that complex systems become something more than their parts. The machinery and the plants and the water and the creatures and the people—all of it together makes an organism, and they're cells within it, and the shaft maintains itself through them as much as they maintain it. Vei argues this isn't metaphor but literal truth, describes it in terms of homeostasis and feedback loops and emergent complexity. Kentre simply agrees, experiences it as obvious truth requiring no explanation.

The others are more agnostic but none of them dismiss it. There's something here beyond the physical, or the physical itself has become something more. The way the shaft provides what's needed—not always, not perfectly, but enough. The way problems resolve themselves, sometimes through the Adjusters' intervention, sometimes through processes they don't understand. A platform was sinking dangerously two years ago, its pneumatic column clearly failing, and they were preparing for a major repair or potential collapse when the sinking just...stopped. The platform stabilized at a new height and has been solid since. The column still shows the same damage but bears the weight regardless.

Coincidence? Probably. But string enough coincidences together and pattern emerges, even if the pattern is just the human brain's insistence on finding meaning. And if meaning is found, if it shapes behavior in useful ways, does it matter whether the meaning is "real" or constructed?

They develop rituals without naming them as such. Cete touches a particular valve every morning, runs her hand over it, feeling its warmth. Milithe talks to the machinery, explaining repairs. Piet and Ossian begin each day by standing together at a platform edge, looking down into the mist, silent for exactly as long as it takes for a dropped object to disappear from hearing. Kentre offers flowers to the shaft, places them on platforms in patterns that might be decorative or might encode some private language. Suom maintains the chair-platform's clear floor, never allowing vegetation to encroach on that original swept space, honoring whoever sat there decades ago, watching. Vei recites lists—names of plants, types of mechanisms, dates and events from shaft history—as evening practice, preserving knowledge aloud. Orl and Sesseny always test their rope connections twice, even when they're certainly secure, a ritual of safety that's also a ritual of partnership.

Ibris is developing her own, visible to the others but not yet commented on: she counts platforms, touches each pneumatic column she passes, as if checking that it's real, that she's real, that this place actually exists.

There's sexuality, occasionally, in the low-key way that exists among people living in close proximity with no taboos but also no pressure. Piet and Ossian are brothers and nothing more, despite what outsiders sometimes assume about their closeness. Orl and Sesseny are a sexual couple, though they don't display it publicly—just sometimes their hammock rocks in particular rhythms, and no one pays attention. Kentre and Ibris are maybe beginning something, maybe not—an extra attention, a gentleness that could be pedagogical or could be attraction, too early to tell. Milithe and Cete shared a hammock regularly for years, whether sexually or simply for comfort only they know and it's no one else's concern. Suom seems uninterested in sex entirely, content in his solitude and his night-watches. Vei likewise, her passions entirely intellectual.

What matters is that whatever happens happens without drama, without possessiveness, without jealousy that disrupts the group's function. This is selection at work again—people who need monogamy or structure or clear definitions around sexuality tend not to stay, because the shaft doesn't provide those frameworks, and the group is too small to accommodate complication.

They're aging, all of them, or growing, or both. Cete is moving toward death with the same steady patience she brings to everything—not soon, probably, but eventually, inevitably. She doesn't seem troubled by this, has made her peace or never needed to. Milithe's nightmares are perhaps less frequent than they were, or she's learned to surface from them more smoothly. Suom is graying, his massive frame starting to show the softening of late middle age. Vei's mind remains sharp but her body is increasingly frail—she moves less, documents more, is becoming pure intellect housed in reluctant flesh. The brothers are entering middle age with grace, their synchronized movement patterns now so ingrained as to be unconscious. Orl and Sesseny are weathering well, their competence deepening into mastery. Kentre is maturing from the bright enthusiasm of youth into something more grounded, still joyful but less manic, learning patience. Ibris is healing—the burns are scarring over, her flinching decreases weekly, she's beginning to laugh at Suom's rumbling humor.

And the shaft continues, indifferent to their development, or nurturing it—hard to say which. The platforms rise and fall. The vines grow. The water drips its complex rhythms. The machinery grinds and hisses and breathes. The birds spiral through the vertical space. The moths emerge at dusk. The lizards hunt in the leaf-litter. The flowers bloom according to their own calendars. And the Adjusters maintain, observe, adapt, document, harvest, repair, rest, watch, listen, and live.

They're not waiting for anything. Not rescue, not purpose, not meaning beyond what's present. They've found, or created, or stumbled into, or been selected for, a life that fits them, and the shaft that needs them, and the strange communion between human and machine and plant that probably shouldn't work but does, here, now, in this vertical world of suspended metal and aggressive greenery and patient time.

And if someone new arrives through the hatch, descending the ladder into the noise and the humidity and the green-filtered light, they'll be watched for a while. Assessed. Not judged—there's no judgment, no moral framework of worthy or unworthy. Just assessed for fit, for compatibility, for the particular strangeness required to live here. And if they have it, if they belong, the Adjusters will know, and there will be a cup of tea, and a task, and a platform, and a place in the slow rhythms, and eventually a name like Adjuster that they'll accept with indifference, because the name doesn't matter but the work does, and the shaft does, and the continuation of this improbable, impossible, beautiful functioning does.

And if they don't belong, if they can't adapt, that's fine too. The hatch is always there. The world above continues. No one is trapped except by their own choice to stay, by the growing impossibility of imagining leaving, by the slow understanding that there's nowhere else they'd rather be than here, suspended in the industrial-organic hymn, breathing humid air that smells of rust and flowers, listening to the symphony of drips and hisses and bird-calls and creaking vines, watching light move through space, feeling platforms rise beneath their feet over hours, tending the gardens and the machines, living slow and loud and strange and exactly, precisely, themselves.
