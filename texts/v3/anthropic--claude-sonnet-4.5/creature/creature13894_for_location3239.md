# The Dwellers in Resin

They call themselves the Tamura-vhet—"those who tend the standing wood"—though outsiders more often call them resin-keepers or simply Quarter-folk. To meet them is to first notice what the heat and the air have done: their skin carries a permanent sheen, not quite sweat, as if they've been varnished. Their hands are always slightly tacky, fingertips darkened with resin that never fully washes away, so that touching anything leaves faint amber traces. They smell of the place—that cedar-attic warmth, cinnamon-pitch sweetness—so strongly that it precedes them, lingers after them, marks their clothing and tools and children.

Most are small-statured, ranging from slight to compact, though whether this is selection or adaptation no one can say. The old story claims the first settlers were refugees from some coastal collapse, but the details have blurred across seven generations into pure narrative shape without content. What matters is that smallness serves here: you duck less through the carved doorways, need less of that strange fungal oil, produce less heat in air already heavy with it.

Their ages are difficult to read. The constant warmth smooths skin, keeps it supple, but the resin-air does something else—tans it, darkens it, gives everyone past adolescence a leathery quality regardless of their original complexion. A woman of thirty might look fifty in the harsh way her hands are creased, yet have the smooth face of someone younger. The elderly don't gray so much as brown, their hair taking on the amber tones of old resin, sometimes stiffening into permanent shapes from the oils in the air.

## The Inheritance of Silence

They are a quiet people, but it's a particular kind of quiet—not shy, not unfriendly, but economical. Words in the Resin Quarter are tools like bronze chisels: you use what's necessary, maintain them, don't waste them on soft materials. Conversations happen in fragments stretched across hours. A craftsperson might greet you in the morning with "The bronze is ready," work in silence until midday, then add "For the large cuts" as if no time had passed. You learn to hold threads of meaning across these gaps, to remember what was being discussed yesterday or last week, to understand that silence isn't absence but waiting.

Children are different—those few children, rare and precious. They haven't learned the economy yet, or they resist it. Their voices in the plaza carry a quality of defiance, as if loudness itself is rebellion against the compressed adult world. Parents don't shush them much. There's a sense that childhood is the time for noise, that they'll quiet soon enough, that these years of shouting games and argument are both necessary and brief.

The children—perhaps twenty in the entire settlement—form their own society within the larger one. They know every ladder, every platform, every hidden chamber. They've named things the adults haven't bothered to name: particular towers are "Old Groaner" or "Three-Door" or "Mother Dark." They have their own economies, trading bronze tokens (discarded by toolmakers) or interesting fragments of heartwood or dried fungi that make good toys. They move in a pack, mostly, though pairs occasionally split off for private explorations.

What's striking is how the adults give them space—physical and otherwise. A workshop might have five or six children crowded in the corner, watching some delicate bronze-casting, and the craftsperson simply works around their presence, occasionally explaining something in the same economical way they'd explain it to an adult. There's no separate world of childhood here, no special treatment. Children are small adults-in-training, learning by watching, trying, failing, trying again.

## The Hierarchies That Aren't

There is no obvious leadership in the Resin Quarter, no council or elder or chief. Decisions emerge from conversations so distributed across time and space that pinpointing where they were made becomes impossible. Someone needs to organize the caravan exchange: gradually, across weeks, people mention what they need, what they can offer, and somehow by the time the caravan appears, everyone knows their role.

But there are people others listen to more carefully—not with deference exactly, but with attention. These are usually older craftspeople who've mastered their particular materials: someone who can predict how resin will flow when heated, someone who can hear in a tower's groan where the grain runs easiest, someone who knows which bronze alloy holds an edge longest. Their authority is technical, specific. Outside their domain, they're simply another voice.

Then there are the memory-keepers—three old women, unrelated, who've taken it upon themselves to remember. What they remember is precise and peculiar: which chambers were carved in which years, which towers show signs of interior change, where the resin flows most freely, which craftspeople descended from which families. They don't record this; writing in the resin-air is difficult, and paper degrades quickly. Instead they talk to each other constantly, testing memory against memory, correcting details, rehearsing the settlement's history aloud in fragments.

You see them often in the plaza, sitting on the heartwood benches, conducting their endless verbal archive:

"The eastern wall chamber, the one with the spiral carving—that was Kether's father, year of the dry caravan."

"No, Kether's grandfather. His father made the chamber above it."

"Are you certain?"

"The spiral pattern—see how it turns sunwise? The grandfather always cut sunwise. The father cut against."

"Ah. Yes. I'd forgotten."

They're not authorities, but they're consulted when disputes arise about space, about whose family has worked where, about whether a particular tower has always made that sound or if it's new. They settle nothing themselves, only offer what they remember. The settlement decides, somehow, through those distributed conversations, whether to believe them.

## The Spectrum of Work

The master resin-workers occupy a particular place in the settlement's unspoken hierarchy—not above others, but central. There are perhaps twenty who've achieved what others recognize as mastery: the ability to predict resin behavior under different temperatures, to blend different tower-sources for different properties, to refine it through distillation into grades from crude to almost water-clear.

Chief among them—though she'd reject the term—is Sathin, a woman of indeterminate age (somewhere between fifty and seventy, leather-skinned, resin-dark) who works from a chamber so deep inside a tower that you reach it only after three separate ladders and two suspended bridges. Her space is lit by thirty lamps, always, making it hot enough that most people retreat after a few minutes. She wears almost nothing—a strip of fungal cloth, nothing more—and has done so for so long that no one notices.

Her specialty is the ultra-refined resin, distilled seven times, clear as glass, used for coating bronze tools to prevent tarnish and for sealing the finest carved work. She produces perhaps three jars of it per year, and they trade for astonishing amounts of grain and meat at the caravan exchange. She talks even less than most, communicating through gestures and occasional single words, but craftspeople climb to her chamber constantly with questions, samples, problems. She examines what they bring, sometimes for long minutes, then responds with fragmentary advice: "More heat." "Different tower." "Wait three days."

At the other end of the spectrum are the crude-workers, mostly younger people still learning, who harvest the raw resin-weep from fresh cuts, collect it in ceramic jars, do the first rough filtering through fungal fiber. This work is hot, sticky, unpleasant. You end each day coated in resin, smelling so strongly of it that even here you're overwhelming. But it's necessary work, and it's where everyone starts.

Between these extremes are the specialists: bronze-casters who make the tools everyone needs, working from small forges that burn refined resin at temperatures that turn chambers into kilns; fiber-workers who beat and comb and spin and weave the fungal tissues into rope and cloth; carvers who shape heartwood into furniture, boxes, panels; the chamber-cutters who expand the settlement slowly, sawing new doors and rooms with patient precision; the lamp-makers who form clay vessels to perfect dimensions, knowing that a lamp's efficiency depends on ratios measured in finger-widths.

## The Rhythm of Bodies

People move differently here. The heat and the air create a particular gait: slow, deliberate, nothing wasted. Running is almost unknown except among the children. Adults walk, pause, sometimes stand absolutely still for minutes at a time, conserving energy in the thick warmth.

Hands are constantly in motion, though—the one exception to the general slowness. Fingers test surfaces, check resin-tackiness, adjust tools, gesture in fragmentary communication. The resin-workers especially have hands that move with independent intelligence, checking temperatures by touch, sensing viscosity through fingertip pressure, spreading resin with precise strokes while their bodies remain still, their faces almost blank.

They sleep strangely, in fragments scattered across day and night. The heat makes long sleep difficult—you wake after a few hours, too warm, and rise to work or wander until tired again. Most people take three or four separate sleeps in each day-night cycle, arranged around the temperature: a long rest during the hottest afternoon hours, another after midnight when the air cools marginally, shorter naps at dawn and dusk.

This creates a settlement that's never fully awake or asleep. At any hour, some workshops are active, some chambers lit, some passages occupied by silent walkers moving through the darkness. The distinction between day and night blurs into something more like phases, and people arrange their meetings not by sun-position but by the cycle of heats: "Meet me at the cooling," meaning the hour after midnight when the air finally eases. "I'll have it ready by second lamp," meaning evening when the lamps first outnumber the dying daylight.

## The Languages Spoken

They speak a dialect that's drifted far from whatever coastal tongue their ancestors brought. Resin-work has added vocabulary: seventeen words for different resin colors, each indicating different properties; twenty words for types of stickiness; an entire grammatical structure for describing heat that uses verbal infixes to indicate not just temperature but the kind of heat—dry heat, humid heat, metabolic heat, fire heat.

But more than this, they've developed what outsiders call "gap-speak"—the habit of leaving long silences within sentences, trusting the listener to hold the thread, to wait for the completion that might come hours later. To speak this way requires both patience and trust: you must believe the other person will remember, will complete the thought with you even across the gap of intervening time and activity.

Gesture augments this, though subtly. A particular tilt of the head means "the resin isn't ready yet." Fingers spread in a specific way indicate "higher quality." A slight change in posture signals disagreement too nuanced for words. Outsiders find this maddening—you're never sure if you've been answered, if the conversation is continuing or has ended, if agreement was reached or simply assumed.

The children speak differently: faster, more complete sentences, less silence. They haven't yet learned to trust the gaps. But you can watch it happen as they age—a teenager will start to stretch sentences, leave things unsaid, move toward the adult pattern. By twenty, most have settled into gap-speak fully, their childhood volubility compressed into the settlement's characteristic economy.

With outsiders, especially the caravan traders, they shift registers. The traders need different communication—faster, more explicit, with prices stated clearly and agreements confirmed verbally. Several Quarter-folk specialize in this translation work, moving between the settlement's slow fragmentary mode and the traders' rapid definitiveness. It's exhausting work; the translators often sleep for hours after the caravan departs, drained by the effort of maintaining trader-speed for an entire day.

## The Bodies That Persist

Disease here has a particular character. The resin-air is antiseptic—wounds rarely fester, infections are uncommon. But respiratory problems are universal by middle age: everyone develops a cough, thick and productive, bringing up amber-tinted phlegm. They call it "tower-lung" and accept it as inevitable. The oldest residents wheeze constantly, their breathing a background percussion beneath their words.

Other ailments: joint problems from the constant heat keeping tissues too relaxed, leading to instability. Many older people develop a distinctive shuffle-gait, knees unreliable. Eye problems too—the dim light and constant lamp-glare create a high rate of vision trouble by age forty. Most craftspeople past that age work primarily by feel, their hands compensating for what their eyes can no longer resolve clearly.

But they live long, surprisingly so. The current eldest is Mara-vhet, a woman who claims to remember the settlement when it housed only eighty people, which would make her nearly ninety. She's tiny, brown as old resin, nearly blind, and still spends hours each day in her chamber running her fingers over carved surfaces, reading them like text, occasionally announcing corrections: "This is smoother than it was. The tower's healing here."

Birth is rare and dangerous. The heat does something to fertility—no one understands what. A woman might couple for years without conceiving, then suddenly quicken. Pregnancy in the heat is difficult; many women retreat to chambers on the crater rim during their terms, living in slightly cooler air, returning only after the child is weaned. Even so, perhaps half of births result in children who don't survive their first year. The ones who do are celebrated quietly but intensely—a rare child commands the settlement's collective attention and protection.

No one is turned away from the Quarter for age or infirmity. The elderly who can no longer work sit in chambers near active workshops, their presence somehow important even in their stillness. When someone dies—perhaps ten or twelve times a year—the body is carried to a particular tower on the crater's eastern edge, a tower that grows differently from the others, its surface softer, more accepting. They press the body into a carved niche, and the tower slowly absorbs it over the course of weeks, drawing it into its substance. Whether this is digestion or incorporation, no one says. But the tower—they call it the Keeper—has hundreds of these niches, and all are either occupied or show evidence of previous absorption.

## The Texture of Relationships

Coupling here is pragmatic and fluid. People form partnerships for companionship, for work-sharing, for warmth in the cooler hours. Some last for years or decades; others dissolve after seasons. There's little ceremony around formation or dissolution—people simply begin sharing a chamber, then later perhaps stop. Children complicate this somewhat, adding obligation, but even then the patterns remain flexible. A child might be raised by three or four adults in shifting configuration, all taking responsibility regardless of biological connection.

Jealousy exists but is considered poor form, a failure of resource management. The settlement is too small, too interdependent, for strong exclusive bonds to make sense. Someone might say, "She and I share work and warmth," but the phrasing deliberately avoids claiming ownership. The children reflect this—many aren't certain who their biological parents are, and it doesn't particularly matter. They know who feeds them, teaches them, keeps them safe.

Sexual activity, when it happens, has the same quality as everything else here: slow, economical, almost meditative. The heat makes vigorous activity unappealing. Instead there's a culture of long, still intimacy—bodies pressed together, minimal movement, pleasure drawn out across hours. Privacy is respected but not obsessed over; people simply turn away, continue their own activities, let others do what they do.

Friendship takes the form of shared silence. Two people might spend an entire day working side by side, exchanging perhaps twenty words, and consider it a deeply social experience. The depth of friendship is measured not by conversation but by comfort in gap-speak, by the ability to pick up threads after long silences, by trust that the other person holds your uncompleted thoughts and will help you finish them when the time comes.

Conflict is rare but intense when it happens. The normal economy of words breaks down; people suddenly speak rapidly, completely, their sentences whole and rushed. It's disturbing to witness—like watching the settlement's careful patterns tear apart. Others often intervene not by taking sides but by imposing silence, physically separating the arguers, insisting on gaps, forcing the pause that allows the normal patterns to reassert themselves.

## The Ones Who Leave

Not everyone stays. Perhaps one in ten Quarter-folk eventually departs, usually in their twenties or thirties, going out with a caravan and not returning. The settlement neither celebrates nor mourns this. Someone's chamber stands empty for a while, then gradually another person moves in, or it's converted to storage, or simply left vacant to join the many other vacant spaces.

The reasons for leaving are rarely articulated, but the pattern is clear: those who leave are the ones who never fully settled into gap-speak, who maintained a faster rhythm, who seemed uncomfortable with the heat and the silence and the resin-thick air. The settlement doesn't push them out, but it also doesn't particularly hold them. They go, and life continues.

Occasionally someone returns, months or years later. They're welcomed back with the same quiet pragmatism that marked their departure. No one asks why they left or why they returned. There's a sense that the settlement is patient, that it will take you back if you need it, ask nothing, offer what it always offered: work, warmth, the smell of resin, the creak of towers, the particular slow rhythm of life in the hollow.

Conversely, the settlement rarely accepts new adults from outside. The caravan traders don't stay—they find the heat oppressive, the silence maddening, the resin-smell overwhelming. On rare occasions, someone arrives who can adapt: who slows down, learns gap-speak, develops the resin-sheen and the economy of movement. These few are incorporated without ceremony. They begin as crude-workers, regardless of age or previous skill, and progress like anyone else through patient years of learning.

## The Children's Secret

The children know something the adults have forgotten or never knew. They've noticed that the towers respond to certain sounds—not all sounds, but specific frequencies. A particular whistle, held just right, will sometimes cause a tower to vibrate subtly. The children use this in their games, testing towers, cataloging which responds to which tone.

The adults dismiss this as imagination, the way children personify everything. But the children are certain, have their own terminology for it: "Old Groaner likes the low whistle," they say, or "Three-Door won't answer today, maybe tomorrow." They're not entirely wrong—something is happening, some resonance between the towers' internal structure and certain sounds. But the adults have stopped paying attention to this kind of question, focused on the practical work of harvesting, refining, crafting.

What the children haven't told anyone is that sometimes, very rarely, the towers seem to whistle back. Not loudly, not obviously, but a faint tone that might be wind through cracks or might be something else. The children have heard it perhaps a dozen times collectively, enough to know it's real but not frequent enough to predict it. They've developed a small mythology around this: that the towers are sleeping, that they dream, that sometimes the dreams make sounds.

One child—a girl of perhaps ten named Lisha—has started trying to learn the pattern, whistling at different towers at different times, keeping a mental record of responses. She's the closest thing the settlement has to a tower-speaker, though she doesn't call herself that and no one has recognized what she's doing. She simply spends more time than other children pressing her ear against tower-bark, listening to the groans and creaks, trying to find meaning in them.

## The Ones Who Never Speak

There are perhaps a dozen residents who've stopped speaking entirely—not from injury or inability, but from choice. They've taken the economy of words to its logical conclusion and eliminated words altogether. They communicate purely through gesture, through gap-speak so extended that entire conversations happen without a single sound.

The others treat this as neither disability nor affectation, just a particular way of being. These silent ones often do specialized work: detailed carving that requires intense concentration, or the slow work of caring for the eldest residents, or the patient observation of tower behavior. Their silence seems to suit these activities, to create a space where attention can settle fully into the work without the interruption of speech.

One of them—no one remembers his name anymore, he's simply "the watcher"—spends his days moving through the settlement observing towers. He'll stand before a tower for an hour, absolutely still, then move to another, stand again. He's doing something, clearly, but no one can articulate what. Sometimes he'll gesture to a chamber-cutter, indicate a particular tower or a particular spot, and they'll investigate and find something: a soft area that would have been difficult to cut, or an unusually hard section perfect for a support pillar. His knowledge is respected even though he never explains it.

Another, an elderly woman, tends the dying. She sits with them in their final days, silent herself, requiring nothing from them—no words, no explanations, just presence. People say she makes dying easier, though how she does this without speaking is unclear. Perhaps it's simply that she doesn't expect anything, doesn't need the dying person to comfort the living with platitudes or farewells. She just sits, sometimes touching their hand, waiting with them.

## The Dreams That Come

Everyone in the Resin Quarter has the dreams. They begin a few weeks after arrival and continue as long as you stay. They're not nightmares exactly, not disturbing, but vivid and strange: dreams of roots and networks, of slow communication across vast distances, of seeing through thousands of eyes at once, of being distributed across space, of patient thoughts that take years to complete.

You dream you're a tower, essentially. You're immense and slow and slightly aware. You sense vibrations through your structure, chemical signals moving through your tissues, the slow exchange with neighboring towers. You experience time differently—days pass like heartbeats, seasons like breaths. Thoughts, if you can call them that, emerge across months, conclusions across years.

The residents don't talk about these dreams much. They're private, almost sacred. But everyone knows everyone else has them, and there's a sense that the dreams are important somehow, that they're the price or gift of living here, that through them the settlement and the towers reach some kind of accommodation.

Some people's dreams are more intense, more frequent. These people often become the best tower-workers—not the best resin refiners or craftspeople, but the ones who understand where to cut, how deep, when a tower will accept a new chamber and when it will resist. They've learned something from the dreams, though they can't articulate what. They simply know, in the same way you know how to move your hand without thinking through the individual muscle contractions.

Lisha, the child who listens to towers, has started having the dreams already, younger than usual. She describes them more openly than adults do: "I was big and had many legs that grew down into the dark. I was talking to my neighbors but it took so long, the words were like... like seasons? And I was happy, I think. I was happy being big and slow and talking to my neighbors."

The adults, hearing this, exchange looks. She's confirmed something they suspected but don't discuss: that the dreams aren't random neural noise but communication of some kind, that the towers are aware in their slow way, that living here means becoming part of that awareness, that network of patient vast thoughts trading signals across years.

## The Pattern of Days

Dawn comes late in the hollow, light seeping down rather than breaking. People wake in their scattered sleeps, begin moving through chambers. The first sounds are domestic: the scrape of bronze on ceramic, someone splitting fungal matter for a morning fire, the shuffle of feet on packed floors.

By mid-morning, the workshops are active. The sounds of work layer: sawing and hammering, the hiss of resin boiling, conversations stretched across silence. Children emerge in their pack, investigating, playing, occasionally stopping to watch someone work with an intensity that adults have learned to ignore.

The hottest hours, early afternoon, bring a lull. People retreat to the deepest chambers, the coolest spaces. Work continues but slower, everyone moving in the thick heat like insects in amber. The lamp-light takes on a particular quality in the afternoon, competing with the failing natural light filtering down from above, creating a transitional dimness where shadows seem uncertain of their edges.

Evening brings renewed activity. The lamps begin to dominate, their clear white flames pushing back the darkness. The heat eases marginally. People emerge again, workshops resume fuller operations. This is the social time, when conversations—those long, gap-filled conversations—are most likely. Someone might gather a few others in a chamber, pass around a vessel of fungal oil to sip, and discuss a decision that needs making: whether to cut a new chamber in the northern tower, whether the last caravan's grain was worth the resin traded for it, whether a particular child should begin apprenticing with the bronze-casters or the fiber-workers.

These discussions can last hours, spreading across multiple evenings, incorporating long silences where everyone simply thinks, then resuming. Consensus emerges eventually, though pinpointing the moment of decision is impossible. Someone will simply begin acting on the assumption that it's been decided, and others will follow, and the thing will be done.

Night proper is when the settlement becomes most beautiful. The lamp-constellation illuminates the hollow, each light a point of warmth and clarity. The towers become presence rather than sight—you sense them more than see them, vast dark columns rising into darker sky. The sounds change: fewer work-sounds, more domestic sounds, occasional voices, the constant creak of towers, the shuffle of late walkers moving through familiar darkness.

Some chambers stay lit all night. The resin-refiners especially work in long stretches, tending their distillations, watching temperatures, making adjustments. Their chambers become beacons, reliable points of light that others navigate by.

The coolest hour, after midnight, brings its own activity: people who've been waiting for this slight easing of heat to do work that requires clarity. This is when the finest carving happens, the most precise bronze-casting, the most delicate fiber-work. The heat has relented just enough that hands don't sweat quite as much, that concentration can sharpen.

Then pre-dawn, the quietest time. Most people are in one of their deeper sleeps. Only a few are active—the ones maintaining all-night distillations, perhaps someone returning from the crater rim where they went for truly cool air, maybe Lisha sitting with her ear against a tower, listening to its deep groans, trying to learn its language.

And the light begins again, seeping, another day in the hollow, another turn of the wheel that has turned seven generations and will turn however many more until the towers finally wake or die or the settlement finds some reason to leave or be left.

## The Ones They Wait For

No one speaks of it directly, but there's a sense—pervasive, unacknowledged—that the settlement is waiting. The empty chambers, the unpopulated platforms, the sprawling territory with only three hundred people scattered through it—all of this suggests preparation for something, for more, for a future that hasn't arrived.

Some think this is unconscious, that they're simply being human, always anticipating growth even when growth doesn't come. But others—the memory-keepers especially—note that the first settlers carved far more chambers than they needed, that expansion has always outpaced population, that each generation adds more empty space to the already-hollow settlement.

It's as if they're building a city for people who don't exist yet, as if the towers themselves are demanding more space be made, as if the dreams everyone shares are directing the settlement's growth toward some pattern only the towers understand.

The children will inherit this, whatever it is. They'll grow into the gap-speak and the resin-sheen and the dreams of being vast and slow. They'll continue the work of carving chambers and refining resin and making beautiful things from the towers' substance. They'll have their own few children, who'll form their own small pack in the hollow, learning the tower-names, whistling at them, listening for responses.

And perhaps one day—no one knows when, no one even speaks the possibility—the towers will wake fully. The spores will fall again. New growth will begin. The settlement will be sealed inside expanding fungal matter, or crushed, or incorporated somehow into whatever the towers become when they resume their ancient pattern of spread and reproduction.

Or perhaps the towers will simply continue as they are, patient and still and nearly immortal, allowing the small warm creatures to carve them slowly away, to harvest their resin, to live in the spaces between their vast bodies, to dream their slow dreams and learn their patient language and become, gradually, over generations, something more than human and less, something adapted to this hollow place, something that tends the standing wood.

The Resin Quarter persists. The lamps burn. The towers groan their slow conversation. The work continues, patient and skilled and beautiful, in the warm darkness, beneath the rim of an old crater, where the air smells of cinnamon and cedar and something darker, something older, something that has its own purposes and its own time.
