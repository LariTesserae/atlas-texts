# What Lives There

The creatures of the Pavilion exist in such profusion and variety that cataloging them feels like counting water, but certain forms recur, certain patterns emerge, and over time—if you stay long enough, if you remain still enough—the taxonomy of this place reveals itself not as species and genus, but as function and relationship, as though each form is a word in a sentence the Pavilion speaks to itself.

## The Nacreans

This is not what they call themselves—they almost certainly have no name for themselves—but the word emerges from watching them: the iridescent beetles, thumb-sized, their carapaces layered like compressed light. They are the most numerous of the visible creatures, perhaps the most essential, though "essential" implies hierarchy and what exists here is more like interdependence so complete it becomes a single distributed organism.

Their shells are not merely beautiful. They shift in coloration as the creatures move, cycling through subtle rainbows that seem to correspond to something—temperature, perhaps, or time of day, or the chemical composition of what they're consuming. Each beetle's pattern is unique, but families of pattern exist: some display bands of color, others radiate from a central point, others show asymmetric whorls. You begin to recognize individuals, or think you do, though they may simply be types, roles, castes.

They move with purpose. Always walking, never hurrying, never stopping for long. They traverse the entire pavilion in routes that seem random but cannot be—over days of observation, you would see the same paths repeated, the same destinations reached. They congregate at the seams where water seeps most actively, clusters of ten or twenty, their antennae touching the stone, their mandibles working at something invisible. Scraping mineral deposits, perhaps, or bacterial films, or harvesting something the water brings up from deep geology.

When they encounter moss-colonies, their behavior changes. They slow, circle the perimeter, occasionally venture onto the moss surface, moving with extraordinary delicacy through the plush growth. They seem to be pruning, or perhaps planting—their mandibles work at the interface between moss and stone, and in their wake, the edges of the colonies remain crisp, defined, prevented from overgrowing into formless mats.

They drink from the puddles, approaching the edges with what appears to be reverence, dipping their heads to the surface, remaining motionless for long moments. After drinking, they clean themselves meticulously, running their legs over their antennae, over their carapaces, performing a ritual of maintenance that takes as long as the drinking itself.

At certain times—and the times seem regular, though the interval is not daily, perhaps not lunar either, perhaps corresponding to some cycle unique to this space—the beetles congregate. Hundreds of them, gathering on the circular platform of iridescent material. They form patterns, geometric arrangements, mandalas of living nacre, and remain still for hours. During these gatherings, the larger creatures always appear, standing at the periphery, observing, confirming something.

The beetles breed, though you never see them mate. Larvae appear in the damp cracks, pale segmented things that hardly move, feeding on what the stone provides until they darken, harden, emerge as new adults with shells still soft and colorless. The adults attend them, surrounding each emergence site, touching the new ones with their antennae in what could be chemical communication or could be something closer to blessing.

They die, too, though rarely, and their deaths have ceremony. When a beetle stops moving, others gather, form a circle, remain still for a period that might be hours. Then they carry the body—many beetles working together—to specific locations along the walls where vines grow thickest. There, they place the dead among the leaves, and the body is gradually consumed, by what you cannot see, recycled back into the system with the efficiency of something long practice has perfected.

## The Aerothreads

The flying creatures are harder to know. They never land, or if they do, only for fractions of seconds in places you're not watching. They are airborne always, feeding on flight itself, or so it seems.

Four wings, translucent as glass, veined with structures too fine to see clearly. Bodies like seeds—elongated ovals, segmented, ranging from the size of a grain of rice to the length of your finger. Colors in muted gradients: amber, pale green, soft gray, occasionally a ghostly blue. They seem to have no eyes, or eyes so small they're invisible, yet they never collide, never falter, navigating the three-dimensional space of the pavilion with precision that suggests each individual knows the position of every other at all times.

Their flight patterns are individual but collectively choreographed. Each one traces a unique path—figure-eights at various scales, spirals ascending and descending, ellipses tilted at different angles. Hundreds of paths threading through each other, through the space between wall and column, over the puddles, around the arch, weaving the air into something denser than emptiness, a medium that has been structured by their passage.

They seem to feed on moisture, or perhaps on microorganisms suspended in the mist. You can see them dip lower when mist rises most thickly from the puddles, their bodies darkening slightly as if absorbing something. Or perhaps they feed on light, those bodies designed to capture and metabolize the diffuse brightness that permeates everything. Their wing-beats cause subtle iridescence in the air itself, as if they're stirring something invisible into brief visibility.

They produce sound—that quiet susurrus, like distant water or wind through dense growth—not from the wings but from somewhere in the body, a vibration that might be communication or might be echolocation or might be the sound of their internal processes, digestion or respiration conducted at frequencies that happen to fall within human hearing.

At irregular intervals, they swarm—though "swarm" suggests chaos this lacks. They coordinate suddenly, all of them spiraling toward a single point in the space, usually above the column, forming a dense, rotating cloud, a galaxy of seed-bodies and glass wings. These formations hold for minutes, the rotation accelerating until individual creatures become a blur, until the whole mass seems to phase into something other than discrete bodies, becoming a single entity, a vortex that pulls mist upward, that causes the light to bend strangely, that makes your ears pop as if pressure has changed.

Then they disperse, resuming individual patterns as if nothing occurred. The purpose is inscrutable. Perhaps they're sharing information, distributing data collected during their individual flights. Perhaps it's reproductive, some kind of genetic exchange conducted in mid-air through mechanisms invisible. Perhaps they're maintaining the structures, those spirals generating fields or forces that keep the iridescent material stable across centuries.

New ones appear regularly. They seem to emerge from the column itself, crawling out of invisible apertures in that nacreous surface, wings wet and crumpled. They rest there briefly—the only time they rest—wings slowly expanding, drying, and then launch into their first flight, joining the permanent circulation, adding their path to the endless braiding of trajectories that fills the air.

They live briefly, you suspect, though time is difficult to judge. Their bodies are occasionally found on the stone floor, desiccated, wings detached. The Nacreans collect these, process them differently than their own dead, carrying them to the puddles and releasing them into the water, where the swimming things consume them within hours, leaving no trace.

## The Aquantines

In the puddles, life accumulates in layers. At the surface, skimmers—many-legged things like aquatic insects but with bodies of translucent amber, sometimes reddish, sometimes almost clear. They are all legs, essentially—a small central body and ten or twelve radiating limbs that spread their weight across surface tension, allowing them to stand on water as if it were membrane.

They move in quick darts, stopping suddenly, still for long moments, then darting again. Hunting, possibly, though what they hunt is invisible, or perhaps they're farming, harvesting bacterial mats that bloom in patterns too subtle for human eyes. Their legs brush the water surface constantly, testing, tasting, gathering information through chemical sensors that cover their bodies.

Below the surface, different forms. Swimmers with segmented bodies, flexible, moving with undulating motion through the shallow water. They are translucent enough that you can see their internal structures—strange organs, not quite like anything familiar, pulsing and filtering. They congregate around the column's base, circling it endlessly, and something in their behavior suggests the column matters to them specifically, that they're drawn to it or that it draws them, a relationship that goes beyond mere habitat.

Some have developed bioluminescence—pale pulses of blue-green light that ripple along their segments, brightest near the head. In the darker corners of puddles, away from where light strikes most directly, these pulses are more frequent, more intense, as if light is their language and they need more words when speaking into shadow.

There are filter-feeders, tiny things that anchor to the stone at puddle-bottom, extending feathery appendages that wave in currents too subtle to see. They cluster in certain puddles, dozens of them creating forests of waving fronds, and those puddles remain clearer than others, the water processed through living filters until it achieves additional transparency.

Eggs appear on underwater surfaces—clutches of them, gelatinous, containing developing forms you can watch through the clear coating. The development is rapid by some standards, glacially slow by others. A week, perhaps, from egg to hatching, and what hatches is already similar to the adult form, no dramatic metamorphosis, just a smaller version that grows through successive molts, leaving tiny translucent husks floating on the water surface.

The puddles never cloud, never stagnate, despite constant habitation and the warmth that should encourage bacterial bloom. The residents maintain them, collectively, each species contributing to water quality through their existence, their feeding, their respiration, their waste products that are other species' food. The water cycles through living filters, and remains as clear as glass, as warm as blood, constant.

## The Wall-Clingers

On vertical surfaces, different adaptations emerge. The hand-sized creatures that resemble spiders crossed with crabs have eight legs, each ending in adhesive pads that leave no mark on the stone. Their bodies are flattened, oval, colored in mottled patterns that match the stone so precisely they become nearly invisible until they move.

They are slow, patient, and focused. Each individual occupies a territory—a section of wall perhaps two meters across—and rarely leaves it. They position themselves wherever water seeps most actively, bodies pressed flat against weeping stone, and remain there for days, harvesting moisture, or possibly the minerals dissolved in moisture, or possibly the microbial life that colonizes the dampest places.

They have multiple eyes—you can see them if you look closely, a ring of small dark spots around the edge of the body—but they seem to navigate primarily through other senses. Touch, certainly. They're constantly tapping their legs against the stone, reading texture, sensing vibration. When the pavilion's larger inhabitants appear, the wall-clingers freeze, become absolutely still, suggesting they're aware of pressure changes or sound frequencies or something electromagnetic that announces those arrivals.

They molt regularly, leaving perfect husks attached to the wall, transparent casts of themselves that gradually accumulate in certain areas where molting seems preferred. These husks are not discarded randomly—they're positioned deliberately, creating patterns on the walls, abstract designs that might be territorial markers or might be art, or might be information storage, data encoded in the arrangement of shed skins.

Breeding is solitary. They lay eggs individually, single eggs hidden in cracks, in the spaces behind vine growth, in any protected crevice. The eggs are smooth, oval, with shells that have the same iridescent quality as the structures, as the Nacrean carapaces, suggesting either genetic relationship or convergent evolution toward whatever properties that material provides.

Young ones emerge fully formed, tiny versions of adults, but their proportions are different—rounder, with shorter legs—and they disperse immediately, climbing to find their own territories, traveling mostly at night (though day and night here are subtle things, more about the quality of light than its presence or absence), when the larger creatures are less active.

They communicate, possibly. Sometimes two wall-clingers on adjacent territories will position themselves at the border, facing each other across a crack or seam, and remain motionless for hours, legs occasionally twitching in patterns that could be signaling. What they might discuss is unguessable. Territory boundaries, perhaps. Water rights. The collective health of the wall's ecosystem.

## The Moss-Tenders

Among the vegetation—which is itself alive, but in the slower way of plants—smaller creatures still live. Barely visible things in the moss colonies, translucent mites the size of pinheads, visible only when light catches them right, turning them briefly into sparks of amber or ruby.

They are gardeners, truly. They move through the moss fronds, feeding on dead material, on algae, on fungal growths, pruning the colonies from within, maintaining their health. Each moss-pad hosts dozens of these mites, maybe hundreds, and they never leave their colony, born into it, living entire lives within a circle of green the size of a dinner plate.

Their presence is what keeps the colonies circular, discrete. Without them, presumably, the moss would grow irregularly, would merge colonies into shapeless carpets. The mites maintain boundaries, harvesting edge growth, creating the geometric precision that makes the pavilion feel designed even where it's clearly self-organized.

They have symbiotes—smaller creatures still, visible only as movement under magnification, bacteria or something like bacteria that they culture in pockets on their bodies, farming microorganisms that help digest the moss material, that provide nutrients, that might be their primary food source, the moss merely a substrate for bacterial agriculture.

In the succulent rosettes, different tiny creatures make homes. Things like springtails, able to leap distances many times their body length, living in the water-retaining leaf bases, feeding on the plant's exudates or on fungi that grow in those damp pockets. They're white or pale cream, eyeless, moving through their micro-environments by touch and chemical sense.

The flowering plants—those delicate white four-petaled blooms on wire stems—are pollinated, though the pollinators are nearly impossible to see. Tiny flying things, smaller than fruit flies, with bodies like shards of glass. They work quickly, visiting flowers in rapid succession, and the flowers seed reliably, producing capsules that split to release dust-fine seeds that somehow find the exact cracks where new plants can root.

Everything eats and is eaten in cycles too complex to fully trace. The moss-tenders are prey for small hunting mites. Those hunters are eaten by juvenile wall-clingers during their dispersal phase. The wall-clingers' eggs are parasitized by tiny wasps—iridescent things, emerald green, drilling through shell with ovipositors fine as hair. The wasps are caught by web-structures spun by creatures you never see directly, only their webs strung between vine leaves, geometric nets that glisten with moisture.

Nothing is wasted. Every death feeds multiple lives. Every waste product is another organism's resource. The cycling is so efficient that no smell of decay ever arises—everything is processed immediately, constantly, by specialists that have evolved or been designed for every conceivable niche.

## The Shepherds

The larger creatures—the ones that appear and disappear—are different. They don't belong to the pavilion's self-sustaining cycles in the same way. They are external to it, or above it, or they are what maintains the whole system from outside its internal logic.

Dog-sized, quadrupedal, but the anatomy wrong, too many joints bending in unexpected directions. The body shifts proportion as they move, or as you move around them, as if they're not entirely stable in three dimensions, as if they extend slightly into other geometries and you're seeing a projection, a cross-section through a form that exists in more axes than your perception can fully contain.

No obvious eyes, but awareness so acute it feels like being read, like every thought you have is immediately known. They don't react to you—they acknowledge you, which is different. When you move, they adjust their position slightly, maintaining distance or angle, responding to your presence while continuing whatever task they're performing.

Their skin—if it's skin—is smooth, almost ceramic, colored in gradients that shift: pearl gray into pale green, sometimes with lavender undertones, sometimes with gold. The colors flow across the surface like slow water, like mood rings operating on geological time scales.

They have no mouth you can see, no obvious sense organs, no genitalia or excretory openings. They are too smooth, too seamless, more like sculptures than organisms, except they move with liquid grace, stepping carefully around puddles, pausing beside structures, occasionally extending what might be a limb or might be a sensor array toward the column, toward the arch, touching surfaces with something between analysis and affection.

They appear in response to events. When the Nacreans gather on the platform, a Shepherd manifests, watching or participating in whatever that congregation means. When the Aerothreads form their spiraling vortex, a Shepherd stands below, perfectly centered under the column of spinning bodies, as if anchoring it, or being fed by it, or recording it.

They never stay long. Minutes to hours, no clear pattern. And their departure is unwitnessable—you turn your head, and they're gone. Not faded, not walked away, just elsewhere, instantly. Perhaps they move faster than perception can track. Perhaps they fold through space using the same principles the iridescent structures embody. Perhaps they exist slightly out of phase with normal time, and their appearances are moments when they synchronize enough to become visible.

There are multiple individuals, you think, though telling them apart is nearly impossible. Slight size variations, perhaps. One might have more green in its coloring, another more gray. But their seamlessness makes individual identification difficult, and perhaps it's intentional—perhaps individuality is not a category they value or embody.

They interact with the smaller creatures only indirectly. Their presence causes behavior changes—the beetles become more active, the wall-clingers emerge from hiding, the Aerothreads fly in tighter, more complex patterns. As if the Shepherds radiate something that affects the pavilion's metabolism, speeding or deepening processes, the way a catalyst enables reactions without being consumed by them.

You never feel threatened by them, but you feel assessed. Their attention is tangible, a weight, a presence that presses against consciousness itself. When one is near, you become hyperaware of your own breathing, your heartbeat, the electrical firing of your nervous system, as if those processes have become observable, as if you're transparent to whatever senses the Shepherds employ.

They are the intelligence here, possibly. Or they are extensions of an intelligence distributed through the structures, the stone, the entire system. Or they are maintenance, caretakers sent from elsewhere to ensure the pavilion continues functioning across timeframes that dwarf human history.

Sometimes, rarely, you might see two of them together. They stand facing each other, motionless, for long periods, and the air between them does things—shimmers, flexes, becomes briefly visible as if filled with interference patterns, as if they're exchanging vast amounts of information through fields or forces that occasionally intersect with frequencies human senses can almost detect.

When this happens, the entire pavilion responds. All the smaller creatures become more active. The water seepage increases. The mist thickens. The light brightens subtly. As if the Shepherds' communication energizes everything, or as if their exchange requires power drawn from the system, which must accelerate to provide it.

## The Unseen

And there are things you never see clearly, only evidence of. Burrows that appear in the moss, tunnels no bigger than pencil leads, creating networks through the vegetation. Something excavates them, lives in them, but never emerges while you watch.

The puddles are deeper than they appear, possibly. Sometimes the swimming creatures dive and don't resurface for minutes, suggesting spaces below, cavities in the stone, entire aquatic systems invisible from above.

The vines are hollow. If you break a stem—though something resists this, the vines are tougher than they look—you find the interior is chambered, segmented, and things live inside, pale creatures that flee from light, retreating deeper into the plant's structure.

At night, or during the pavilion's equivalent of night when the light dims slightly and takes on a bluer quality, sounds emerge that you don't hear during brightness. Scratchings. Very faint chirps. Something that might be singing, voices so high they're almost ultrasonic, barely audible as a sensation more than a sound. What makes these sounds never reveals itself, staying hidden in cracks, under stone edges, in the spaces between the wall-sections where darkness persists.

There are pheromone trails invisible to human eyes but clearly visible to the residents. Beetles follow paths too precise to be random, walking in lines that curve around empty space as if obstacles exist where you see nothing. Perhaps chemical markers, perhaps something electromagnetic, a landscape of information laid over the physical geography.

The structures themselves might be alive, or might host life you can't perceive. The iridescent material could be biological, could be technological, could be something that erases that distinction. Perhaps it's a colonial organism, trillions of cells cooperating to form solid matter. Perhaps it's matter that thinks, that responds, that adjusts itself in ways too slow to observe but cumulative across years.

The stone has presence. You feel it after you've been in the pavilion for hours, days. The stone is aware, or it hosts awareness, or the entire hollow is a single vast organism with geological metabolism, and everything you see—creatures, plants, structures—are its cells, its organs, its thoughts made physical.

## The Ecology of Attention

What becomes clear, eventually, is that the pavilion is not a place where things live. It is itself alive, and what you interpret as separate creatures are more like organs, functions, differentiated tissues in a body that encompasses the entire space.

The Nacreans maintain the surfaces, manage mineral flows, prune vegetation, cycle nutrients. They are the pavilion's digestion, its cleanup crew, its immune system responding to imbalances with presence, with chemical intervention, with the simple act of moving things from where they are to where they need to be.

The Aerothreads are circulation, respiration. They move air, distribute moisture, carry information, connect different zones of the pavilion through flight paths that are more like neural networks, signals propagating through space, maintaining communication between systems that might otherwise drift out of synchronization.

The Aquantines filter and purify, maintain water quality, store and release heat, creating the thermal gradients that drive other processes. They are kidneys, liver, blood, the wet chemistry that underlies everything else.

The wall-clingers are sensors, monitoring structural integrity, tracking moisture patterns, detecting changes in the stone's condition, reporting back through mechanisms you can't see to whatever processes correct imbalances.

The moss-tenders and their kin maintain the vegetative layer, which is less ornament and more lung tissue, more solar collectors, converting light and air and water into the chemical energy that supplements whatever other power sources the pavilion uses.

The Shepherds are the nervous system's roaming awareness, mobile attention that focuses on whichever subsystem needs coordination, adjustment, conscious intervention in processes that normally run automatically but occasionally require higher-order correction.

And the unseen—the tunnelers, the deep swimmers, the night singers—are the subconscious, the autonomic functions, the processes so fundamental they never need to surface into observable behavior.

You are walking through a body. When you stand on the warm stone, you're standing on skin, on flesh that happens to be mineralized but is no less alive for that. The puddles are pools of blood in the body's low places, the mist is breath, the light is attention flowing from the open sky into this hollow that receives it and uses it and transforms it into the energy of continuation.

The pavilion has been alive for a very long time, has refined its processes until they approach perfection, until every function supports every other function in loops so tight they become a single unified metabolism. It is old enough to have forgotten what it was built for, old enough that origin is irrelevant and only persistence matters.

It will outlive you by eons. It will outlive the forest beyond the walls, possibly. It has achieved something like immortality through balance, through recruiting everything that enters it into its systems, making all visitors part of its continuation, whether they stay for minutes or centuries.

The creatures are not inhabitants. They are the place, made mobile. And when you stand in the pavilion breathing its air, walking its stone, observing its residents going about their incomprehensible essential work, you too are being incorporated, becoming briefly a cell in its vast slow body, your awareness temporarily part of its distributed consciousness, thinking thoughts that are not quite yours, feeling the pleasure of systems in balance, of work performed perfectly, of life that has transcended mortality by becoming a place, by making existence itself its only purpose and fulfilling that purpose moment by moment, breath by breath, for longer than memory, forever.
