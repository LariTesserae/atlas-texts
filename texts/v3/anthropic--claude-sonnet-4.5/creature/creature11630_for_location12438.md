# Those Who Inhabit the Dripping Hollows

The **reed-tenders** move through the levels like memory moves through a mind — constant but unnoticed until you look for them directly. They are small people, compact in the way that people become when every surface requires careful balance, when you spend your life adjusting your weight across uncertain platforms. Their hands are their signature: overdeveloped, thick-fingered, with calluses that map their trade in the pattern of rope and reed. The skin between thumb and forefinger is stretched and toughened from a lifetime of gripping wet fiber. Their fingernails are worn to rounded nubs, some missing entirely, some thickened into horn-like plates.

They dress in layers of woven reed-cloth, supple as linen but heavier, always damp. The garments are neither shirt nor robe but something continuous, wrapping the body in overlapping panels that can be adjusted — tightened when work requires freedom of movement, loosened when cold settles into the lower levels. The cloth darkens with wear and moisture until older tenders are dressed in something nearly black, walking shadows against the brown walls. Younger ones still show traces of the reed's original gold, though even they are well on their way to darkness.

They don't speak much. Not from vow or prohibition, but because the water-sound makes speech exhausting and the work itself teaches other languages. They communicate through touch — a hand on a shoulder meaning *follow*, fingers tapping a rhythm meaning *wait*, a palm pressed flat against reed meaning *listen to this, something's wrong here*. They've developed a secondary vocabulary of knots, small cord-work samples they carry on loops at their belts. Showing a particular knot can mean anything from "the third level needs attention" to "something died in the east quadrant" to "I dreamed the deep figures moved."

Their ages are difficult to determine. The moisture makes skin seem younger, fills in wrinkles, keeps flesh supple. But their eyes age differently — they develop a particular distance, a focus that seems to look through surfaces to the structure beneath. The oldest tenders have eyes like polished stones, reflective and cool, and they move with a certainty that requires no vision at all. They navigate the darkest levels without lamps, one hand trailing along a rail, reading the structure through touch and sound and something else, some sense that develops when you've spent sixty years learning how reed speaks under your feet.

They sleep in niches woven into the walls, individual cells barely large enough to curl up in, sealed with a curtain of hanging reed that provides privacy and allows air flow. Each niche contains a mat of woven reed, a clay water vessel, sometimes a small box holding personal objects — never many. A bird skull. A particularly beautiful knot preserved in wax. A stone from some outside place, kept as reminder or talisman.

The tenders eat what the Hollows provide. Bird eggs, taken sparingly from nests, always leaving enough to sustain the colonies. The eggs are small, with shells that shade from pale blue to speckled brown, and they taste of fish somehow, though the birds eat insects and seed that grows in cracks between reeds. The rats are food too, trapped with clever reed cages that allow entry but prevent exit. The meat is lean, slightly sweet, and the tenders waste nothing — bones are cleaned and carved into needles, skin is stretched and dried for patches, even the tails are saved for some purpose known only to the most experienced workers.

Sometimes bundles arrive from outside. They're lowered through the roof on ropes, or appear on the uppermost platform at the boundary where the Hollows meet whatever lies beyond. The bundles contain dried fish, root vegetables, coarse grain, oil for lamps. Who sends them? The tenders don't discuss it. Perhaps there's a community outside that depends on the Hollows for something — water purification, weather prophecy, simply the knowledge that someone maintains this place. Perhaps the tenders themselves arrange it during visits no one else witnesses. The bundles are opened, distributed according to need, and the rope is carefully coiled and added to the Hollows' supply.

Children are rare here. When they appear, they're small and feral, raised communally, learning to walk on platforms where a misstep means a fall through multiple levels. They develop reflexes that adults can never fully achieve — perfect balance, instant recovery, hands that shoot out to catch themselves on any passing rope or rail. By four or five, they're climbing freely through the vertical space, using routes that require small bodies and absolute confidence. They're taught the work gradually: first just observation, learning to see what needs seeing. Then simple tasks — coiling rope, sorting reeds by quality, cleaning the lamps. By adolescence they're doing real repairs, and by their twentieth year they're full tenders, indistinguishable from those who've been here for decades.

But some children don't stay. When they reach a certain age — twelve, thirteen, hard to mark time precisely here — they sometimes leave. They walk to the uppermost level and out through whatever passage connects the Hollows to the outer world. Most never return. A few come back years later, unchanged by the outside or changed in ways that make them strangers. They resume the work or they don't. Either way, the Hollows continue.

The **bird-watchers** are a different type. They might have been tenders once, but they've specialized, narrowed their focus until only the birds exist for them. They're thinner than the tenders, more still. They spend days on a single platform, motionless, observing. They've learned to identify individual birds within the flocks — this particular grey-wing with the damaged talon, that white-breast with the crooked flight pattern. They know the lineages, can trace families back through generations, predict which pairs will nest where based on ancestry and feud.

They keep records, but not in writing. They memorize. Each bird-watcher holds in their mind an enormous catalogue of birds and behaviors, patterns and exceptions. When decisions must be made — whether to cull a nest that's damaging crucial reed-work, which platforms to avoid during nesting season — the bird-watchers are consulted. They deliver their knowledge in long, toneless recitations, listing observations: "The blue-throats have moved their primary rookery down one level. The grey-wings are aggressive this cycle, three confrontations observed. The translucent-wings cluster near the east lamp, suggesting temperature shift imminent."

The bird-watchers develop peculiar tics. They cock their heads at angles that mimic their subjects. They make small clicking sounds in their throats, unconscious mimicry of calls heard so often they've become internalized. Their hands, when they move at all, make small darting motions like beaks striking. Some of the oldest bird-watchers barely seem human anymore — more like a strange, large species of the birds themselves, a flightless variety that walks upright and serves some essential but obscure function in the colony's ecology.

They're the ones who maintain the skull-device, adding new specimens when they find them, making minute adjustments to the wire connections. They listen to its humming with fierce concentration, hearing patterns no one else can distinguish. Sometimes they'll announce, "The device speaks of storm," or "The device warns of shift," and the tenders adjust their work accordingly. Whether the device actually predicts anything or whether the bird-watchers use it as a framework for knowledge they already possess through observation — this is unclear. Perhaps there's no difference.

The **rope-singers** work in the lower levels primarily, where the acoustic properties are strangest. They're doing something with the ropes that hang everywhere, something that looks like maintenance but isn't, not exactly. They pluck the ropes, listening to the sound they make — not the immediate twang but the way it resonates through the structure, traveling through connected ropes and reed-work until it emerges somewhere else, changed, carrying information or intention.

They're tuning the Hollows. Adjusting tension, adding or removing ropes, routing them through specific knots that dampen or amplify their vibrations. The entire structure is becoming — or has always been — a vast instrument, and the rope-singers are its adjusters, ensuring that the soundscape remains within certain parameters. What those parameters are, what would happen if the tuning drifted too far, they don't explain. Their work has the urgency of people preventing disaster, but a disaster that might take years or decades to fully manifest.

They're distinguishable by their hands, which are scarred in distinct patterns from rope-burn. New rope-singers wear wrapped hands, thick protective layers. Experienced ones work barehanded, having developed skin like leather, nerveless in patches. They can grip wet rope that would flay a normal person's palm and not even wince. They smell of rope-fiber and a particular resinous substance they apply to certain key knots — something sticky and sharp-smelling, made from who knows what, a recipe passed from singer to singer.

They hum while they work, matching pitch to the rope's resonance, finding harmonics. Sometimes you'll hear them from three levels away, a deep droning that blends with the water-sound until you can't separate human voice from environmental drone. They're not singing words — or if they are, the words have been worn smooth by repetition until only vowel-sounds remain, a liturgy of pure tone.

The **rat-shepherds** are new, relatively speaking. A generation ago, the rats were simply pests, culled when populations grew too large. But someone noticed patterns in their movement, intelligence in their behavior, and began experimenting with... not quite domestication. Something else. Collaboration, perhaps.

The rat-shepherds are younger, mostly, people who grew up when the practice was already established. They feed the rats deliberately, cultivating colonies in specific locations. They've learned to guide rat-movement by laying scent trails — mixtures of oils and crushed herbs and their own sweat, which the rats have learned to follow. Need the rats to clear out insects from a particular chamber? The rat-shepherd lays a trail. Need them to avoid an area during repairs? A different scent creates an invisible barrier.

It sounds absurd until you see it working. A rat-shepherd approaching a platform, making a particular clicking sound, and dozens of rats emerge from the reed-work, swarm around their feet, wait for direction. The rats are fed, protected from traps, and in exchange they serve as a maintenance crew for the margins, eating the pests and cleaning the waste that accumulates in spaces too small for human hands.

The rat-shepherds talk to their charges in ways that embarrass the older tenders. Full conversations, discussing the day's work, asking the rats' opinions. Do the rats understand? They respond, certainly. They've learned to distinguish dozens of vocal commands. But whether that's understanding or simply sophisticated conditioning — the rat-shepherds insist it's the former. They've given names to individual rats, claim to recognize personalities, moods, preferences.

The older tenders tolerate this because it works. The rat populations are healthier, better distributed. The Hollows are cleaner in ways that matter. And the rats, for whatever reason, no longer chew through crucial rope-work, no longer damage the structural reed. The rat-shepherds claim they've explained to the rats which reeds are important. The older tenders don't argue, just shake their heads and return to their own work.

In the **deep places**, on the lowest accessible level, there are the solitary ones. Not many — three, maybe four at any time. These are people who've gone into the deep and stayed there, who've taken on responsibility for maintenance in the dark and wet, who surface only rarely and speak even less than the tenders.

They're changed by the depths. Their skin is paler, almost luminescent in certain lights, as if they've absorbed qualities from the pale moss that grows down there. They move slowly, economically, every gesture considered. They smell of stone and cold water and something else, something like the smell of deep caves or the ocean floor — mineral, ancient, not quite alive but not dead either.

What do they do down there? They tend the basins where water pools. They maintain the lowest lamps, keeping them burning though visitors are rare. They watch the figures in the walls, report if anything changes. They do something with the knots on that level, something the surface-workers don't fully understand — binding or unbinding, maintaining a pattern that must remain exact.

They've been down so long that coming up is disorienting for them. The bird-sound feels violent after the deep silence. The relative brightness hurts their eyes. They surface for supplies — food, lamp oil, new rope — and descend again as quickly as possible. Some of them have been in the deep for years without surfacing. Food and supplies are lowered to them on ropes. The other inhabitants track them by sound and intuition, know they're still alive, still working, but what their lives are like in the constant dark and wet — this is mystery.

Occasionally, a deep-dweller will call for a replacement, will surface and announce they're done, and someone else will volunteer or be chosen to descend. The transition takes time. The outgoing deep-dweller teaches the incoming one, shows them the routes and tasks, the things that must be done and the things that must never be done. Then the old one ascends into the grey light and begins the difficult process of reintegrating with the upper levels, or more often, leaves the Hollows entirely, walks out into whatever world exists beyond.

And there are the **visitors**, though that word doesn't quite fit. People from outside who come to the Hollows for purposes the inhabitants don't question. They arrive at the upper level, stand at the boundary, wait to be acknowledged. A tender approaches. Sometimes words are exchanged, sometimes only gestures. The visitor is allowed entry or turned away.

Those allowed in come for different things. Some are sick — not with ailments that healers cure, but with stranger maladies. Afflictions of perception, of thought, of self. They stay for days or weeks, sleeping in unused niches, drinking the collected water, breathing the dense air. The Hollows do something to them. The water-sound enters their heads and reorganizes what's inside. The birds teach them patterns their thoughts can follow. The geometry of the place gives them new shapes to inhabit. Most leave changed, quieter, their edges softened or their centers reinforced. Some don't leave at all, become tenders themselves, as if the Hollows recognized something in them and claimed it.

Others come as pilgrims, though to what deity or principle the Hollows are sacred, they don't say. They descend to the lowest levels they can reach, kneel before the figures in the walls, spend hours in stillness and silence. They ascend again and leave. They never explain, and the inhabitants never ask.

Still others come with questions. They seek the bird-watchers or the rope-singers, ask about patterns and futures, about what the Hollows know or predict. The answers they receive are often cryptic, phrased in terms of bird-movement or rope-resonance, but they seem to find meaning in them. They leave with expressions of people who've had truths confirmed or fears validated.

And very rarely, someone comes who knows too much. Who asks too directly about what the Hollows are for, what mechanism they're part of, what the deep figures represent. These visitors are not turned away, but they're not answered either. They're simply... absorbed. They find themselves helping with repairs, then staying the night, then several nights, then forgetting why they came. The Hollows are very good at making people forget their questions, at replacing inquiry with work, with the immediate demands of maintaining the structure. By the time these visitors remember what they came to ask, they've usually become the kind of people who know that some questions don't help anything by being answered.

The inhabitants — all of them, across all their types and specializations — share certain qualities. They're comfortable with uncertainty, with not knowing the full picture. They accept that the Hollows have purposes beyond their understanding, that they're maintaining something larger than they can see. They find satisfaction in the work itself, in the daily renewal of the structure, in the small perfections of a knot tied correctly or a platform repaired to seamless integration.

They dream more vividly here. The constant water-sound does something to sleep, makes it deeper and stranger. They dream of flying through the levels, of being birds, of being reed. They dream in geometries that don't exist, spaces folded in ways that architecture doesn't permit. They dream the deep figures moving, speaking in voices that are felt rather than heard. They wake from these dreams not disturbed but informed, as if they've been to a place of instruction, learned something they can't quite articulate but their hands remember.

They age here differently than people age outside. Not slower, not faster, but along different vectors. Their bodies wear out in the ways bodies do — joints stiffen, backs bend, strength fades. But their minds grow sharper in specific ways, more attuned to pattern and resonance, better at reading the structure's moods and needs. The very old among them are regarded with something beyond respect — they're treated as if they're part of the Hollows themselves, as if they've become interfaces between the human and the structure, capable of interpreting one to the other.

When someone dies here — from age or accident, from falling through a gap in the reed-work or from the slow failure of internal organs — their body is handled with care but without grief. They're wrapped in reed-cloth and lowered to the deepest level, placed in one of the water basins, and left. The water takes them, slowly. They soften and dissolve, become part of the moisture cycle, and the inhabitants speak of them as having "joined the dripping," as if they've transitioned from maintaining the Hollows to being the Hollows, their substance distributed through all the levels, falling and rising in the eternal circulation.

This is not morbid to them. It's completion. They've spent their lives in service to the structure; now they've become literally integrated with it. Some of the older inhabitants will stand at basin-edges, press their hands into water that holds dissolved ancestors, feel themselves connected to the lineage of tenders stretching back to the Hollows' beginning.

And perhaps — though none of them would say this directly — perhaps this is what the Hollows are for. Not purification or prediction or any single purpose, but this: the transformation of human effort into something beyond human, the slow teaching of people to be other than they were, the gradual integration of consciousness into structure until the boundary becomes porous. The reed-work captures air and water and turns them into architecture. The inhabitants capture time and effort and turn them into maintenance. And the Hollows take all of it — the work, the water, the lives — and turn them into something that persists, that continues, that generates its own necessity and thereby justifies everything that feeds it.

The inhabitants don't think of it this way. They tie their knots and watch their birds and tend their ropes. They sleep in their niches and eat their eggs and listen to the endless water. They pass their knowledge to the next generation, or they don't, and either way the knowledge persists, held in the structure itself, waiting to be rediscovered by whoever has the patience to listen.

They are the Hollows' hands and eyes, its slow thoughts, its memory. And the Hollows are their purpose, their language, their home. The relationship is so complete, so circular, that asking which serves which becomes meaningless.

They live here, in the dripping dark, among the birds and ropes and falling water, and they are content.
