# Who Lives in the Compression

The people here are small in a way that isn't quite genetic, though perhaps it's becoming so. Not uniformly — you'll see someone tall occasionally, moving through the corridors with a permanent half-crouch, their shoulders apologetic — but the average has settled somewhere around five-foot-three, and the children seem to stop growing earlier than they might above. Whether they select for this or the space shapes them is unclear. Both, probably. A tall person leaves if they can. A tall person's children learn to fold themselves into available volume.

Their skin has a particular quality in the amber lamplight — not pale exactly, though they see little direct sun, but somehow translucent, as if the moisture in the air has slowly hydrated them toward permeability. You can see the blood close beneath, especially at the inner wrist, the temple, the throat. They bruise easily from the constant near-collisions with door frames, pipe work, the edges of things. Most inhabitants past childhood bear a constellation of small scars: a line across the forehead from a low beam, divots in the scalp where someone forgot to duck, knuckles perpetually scraped from walls closer than expected. They move carefully but these things still happen. The architecture is unforgiving.

The original inhabitants — those who came when the settlement was still temporary, still pretending it might be abandoned — are mostly dead now, but a few remain, recognizable by a certain rigidity in their backs, as if they're still expecting to stand up straight. They remember when you could see across the quarry floor unobstructed. They tell stories about the echo the space used to have, before the sound got caught in all the fabric and flesh and building material. The younger people listen politely and don't quite believe them. This density is the only reality they know.

There's a man — he might be forty, might be sixty, the lamplight makes age ambiguous — who maintains the pneumatic tube system. His name is Gregor though everyone calls him the Listener because he can diagnose a problem by sound alone, pressing his ear to the pipes, eyes closed, attending to the quality of the hiss. He's thin in the way of people who forget to eat, who get absorbed in their work. His fingertips are extraordinarily callused from decades of handling metal joints and canvas wrapping. He has a workshop on the third level, eastern quadrant, a space perhaps two meters by three, crammed with spare parts and tools he's fabricated himself. He never married — there's a story there that people don't discuss — but he has an apprentice, a girl of twelve named Maret, who follows him through the tube system's access ways, learning to distinguish between the hiss of normal operation and the hiss that means a seal is failing.

Maret is brown-haired, gap-toothed, entirely without fear of heights, which is fortunate because maintaining the tubes requires climbing to the highest reaches where the suspension points anchor to the original quarry ceiling. She talks constantly when they're working, asking questions, narrating her observations, and Gregor has learned that this is how she thinks, that the voice is part of her processing. She lives with her mother and two younger siblings in a three-level dwelling on the south wall, middle tier. The mother, whose name is Lienne, works in one of the fungus gardens, harvesting and processing the edible varieties. She has the patient hands of someone who works with living things, and she hums while she works, too softly for anyone to complain about the noise. She's been widowed eight years — her husband fell, a maintenance accident on the conveyor system, the kind of death that happens here occasionally despite all precautions.

There's a woman everyone calls Grandmother Piech though she has no children of her own. She's perhaps seventy, genuinely old, her spine curved from decades of low ceilings. She remembers the installation of the pneumatic system, remembers when communication between quadrants required walking. She occupies a first-level corner dwelling, near one of the major water seeps, a space that's been hers for forty years. The damp bothers her joints now, but she won't move; this is her place and she'll die here. She keeps a garden of mosses, cultivating different varieties in shallow trays, trading specimens with other growers. Her hands shake but they're sure when she's working with the moss, pinching off sections, transplanting, adjusting moisture levels with a spray bottle she fills at the seep. She speaks rarely but people come to her with disputes, with questions, with requests for arbitration. Her judgments are brief and binding. No one remembers who gave her this authority; it simply accumulated over decades of being right.

The coal tender is a young man — twenty, maybe twenty-two — named Oskar, built square and low, perfect for the crawlways that access the fire chamber. He works in four-hour shifts, feeding the banked fire, monitoring the water temperature in the system. The heat down there is profound and he strips to the waist when he's working, his torso slick with sweat and coal dust. He's been doing this for three years, since his predecessor died — old age, in bed, the rare good death. Oskar has a wife, very new, a girl named Senna who works in one of the textile workshops, weaving the heavy canvas used for pipe wrapping and roof patches. They share a two-level dwelling on the west wall, fourth tier, close to the conveyor system's upper terminus. She's pregnant, just beginning to show, and they're already negotiating with neighbors about expanding upward, adding a third level for the child. The negotiations will take months. They're prepared for this.

There's a man who sells tea — not grows it, they can't grow tea here, but he's the one who buys it from the surface traders and measures it out and maintains the knowledge of preparation. His stall is a niche in the quarry wall, second level, central quadrant, barely larger than a closet. His name is Piotr and he's been here his entire life, forty-some years, born in a dwelling three levels up from where he works now. He knows everyone. He has to; his business depends on knowing who wants what blend, who can afford the expensive varieties, who needs credit until the end of the week. He's round-faced, soft-voiced, with the careful neutrality of someone who hears too many private conversations. People tell him things while he's measuring their tea. He forgets them deliberately, a practiced amnesia that makes him trustworthy. He has a partner, another man, a relationship conducted with such discretion that many residents don't know, though it's not precisely secret. They live separately — the partner, Aleksi, works in the metal shops on the lowest level — but spend nights together, alternating dwellings. They've been together fourteen years.

Aleksi is a welder and fabricator, lean and long-fingered, unexpectedly graceful. He makes the custom brackets and joints that people need for their building projects, working with salvaged metal, coal-forge heat, techniques learned from the previous generation of metalworkers. His workshop is loud by the Compression's standards — the ring of hammer on metal, the hiss of quenching — but it's down low where such noise is tolerated. He has an assistant, a boy of fifteen named Tomas who's clumsy still, growing into his hands, but showing promise. Tomas lives with his parents and four siblings in a dwelling that was built for three people and houses six through sheer determination and a complex system of sleeping shifts. He's apprenticed to Aleksi for another two years, then he'll be expected to start his own practice, probably in one of the underdeveloped areas near the lowest pools where space can still be found.

There's a midwife, necessarily. Her name is Kaisa and she's perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, with hands that are blunt and strong and somehow gentle. She moves through the Compression at all hours, carrying her bag of tools and medicines, called to births at any level, any quadrant. She knows the interior layout better than almost anyone, knows the shortcuts and passage rights, knows which families will let you cut through their dwelling to reach somewhere faster. She's delivered most of the children under twenty. She's also the one who handles deaths, the preparation of bodies, though these are rarer — people here seem to live long despite the dampness, maybe because of it, or because the constant proximity means illnesses are noticed early. She lives alone, a rare thing here, in a small dwelling positioned almost exactly at the vertical center of the space, equidistant from all areas she might be called to. She keeps cats — two of them, the only cats in the Compression, tolerated because they keep the rat population minimal. The cats are small and long-haired and surprisingly friendly, used to the constant presence of humans.

There's a man who maintains the gardens, all of them, or at least advises on them. His name is Jiri and he's ancient, seventy-five, maybe eighty, his face collapsed around missing teeth. He can't climb ladders anymore so his grandson, a boy of sixteen named Andrei, does the physical work while Jiri watches and directs. Together they move through the vertical gardens, pruning, harvesting, diagnosing problems, suggesting adjustments. Jiri knows plants the way Gregor knows pipes — by sound and smell and a sense that can't quite be articulated. He can tell if a plant needs more water by how its leaves hang. He knows which fungal blooms are edible, which medicinal, which need to be removed before they spread. He's been here since the beginning, one of the original settlers, and he's seen the green matter slowly take over, the moss spreading year by year, the vines finding their way. He says the space is becoming alive. No one argues with him.

Andrei is tall — tragically tall, nearly six feet — and he moves through the Compression like an apology, bent double in many corridors. He should probably leave, seek work above where his height wouldn't be a curse, but he won't abandon his grandfather, and besides, he's in love with a girl named Terese who helps in the brewery, and she won't leave either. Terese is small and dark-haired and laughs more than most people here, a sound that carries through the quiet and makes people smile despite themselves. The brewery is her family's business, three generations working in a natural alcove on the north wall, lower level, where the temperature stays cool enough for fermentation. They make a dark beer, heavy and sour, that most inhabitants drink regularly — the water here is safe enough after it's collected, but the beer is tradition, comfort, calories.

There's a teacher, because there are children, because knowledge has to move forward. Her name is Dana and she's thirty-something, patient in the way of people who work with the young. She holds classes in a communal space on the third level, a platform that was specifically built for gathering, one of the few areas large enough for fifteen children to sit together. She teaches reading, mathematics, the history of the Compression, practical skills like knot-tying and navigation. She's been doing this for six years, since the previous teacher died — a fall, the common death here. She's unmarried, but there's a man, another teacher who works with the older children, and they're moving toward something, a courtship conducted in slow conversations after the children have gone home. His name is Marek and he's quiet, serious, with a precision to his words that suggests he weighs each one before speaking.

There are the guards at the gate — the original entrance, now formalized, now controlled. They work in shifts, usually two at a time, monitoring who comes and goes, what goods move in and out. The trade with the surface is essential; the Compression can't be fully self-sufficient despite all the gardens. They need metal, wood, certain foods, information. One of the current guards is a woman named Vera, mid-thirties, muscular in a compact way, with a scar across her left cheek from something she doesn't discuss. She's fair but inflexible about the rules: gate hours, inspection procedures, the tariffs that fund the communal systems. Her partner on most shifts is a younger man, Lukas, who's learning the position, who still sometimes lets people argue him into exceptions. Vera is teaching him not to.

There's a doctor, of a sort. His training was informal — apprenticeship to the previous doctor, plus whatever books could be acquired from above — but he's competent with the common problems: the respiratory issues from the damp air, the infections from the constant small wounds, the joint problems from the cramped postures. His name is Emil and he's perhaps forty-five, thin and tired-looking, with the permanent expression of someone who's seen too much suffering and not enough rest. His clinic is a two-room dwelling on the second level, one room for examination, one for his own living. He shares the space with his sister, Helena, who assists him and manages the herb garden they maintain on the level above, growing what medicinal plants they can in the limited light.

Helena is younger, early forties, with her brother's tired eyes but a gentler manner. She's the one people prefer to see, if they're honest, because Emil's bedside manner is brusque efficiency while Helena remembers to offer comfort. She makes the poultices and tinctures, dries the herbs, compounds the medicines. She never married — there was someone, once, but he left for the surface and didn't return, a story common enough that people don't pry. She seems content enough, sharing the small space with her brother, working with the plants, tending the patients who arrive at all hours.

There are craftspeople scattered throughout: a woman who repairs shoes, working in a corner of the second level, surrounded by lasts and leather and the sharp smell of adhesive. A man who mends the canvas roof panels, working at height, one of the few who isn't afraid of the long drop. A family who processes the fungus harvest into dried goods that can be stored or traded upward. A woman who writes letters for the illiterate, her script beautiful and formal, learned from books her grandmother brought down when the settlement was new.

There's a drunk — just one, remarkably, given the population. His name is Radek and he's maybe fifty, maybe older, preserved in alcohol. He was a builder once, before the shaking in his hands got bad. Now he survives on charity, on make-work jobs people invent for him, on the collective unwillingness to let someone starve. He sleeps in different places, wherever someone will let him: a corner of a workshop, a landing between levels, occasionally in the communal gathering space. He's not violent, not disruptive, just persistently drunk and vaguely sad. The children avoid him but the adults are kind, in the careful way of people who know they're one accident away from being him.

There are lovers conducting their affairs in whatever privacy they can manufacture: a storage alcove, a high platform no one visits, the brief darkness between lamp posts. There are feuds, ancient and ongoing, over property lines and noise and some insult from fifteen years ago. There are friendships forged by proximity and necessity, people who would never have chosen each other but who have become essential to each other's lives.

There's a storyteller — old Mikhel, seventy or so, blind from some degenerative condition, who sits in the communal space on certain evenings and recounts the tales he knows: stories from the surface, from before the Compression, from the early days, from his own invention. People gather to listen, sitting on the floor, on the stairs, standing in the doorways. His voice is strong despite his age, trained by decades of projection in the sound-deadening space. He knows everyone by voice, by footstep, by the small sounds they make without knowing.

The children are a separate species, nearly. They navigate the Compression with absolute confidence, knowing passages the adults forget, finding routes that seem impossible, climbing structures that weren't meant to be climbed. They play games that involve the vertical space: tag across multiple levels, hide-and-seek in the maze of dwellings, races through the service corridors. They're louder than they should be, protected by their status as children, forgiven their noise in a way adults wouldn't be. They grow up understanding that space is shared, that privacy is practice not architecture, that the community's survival depends on cooperation more than any surface city could imagine.

There are the watchers — people who've found a niche that's more observation than participation. An old woman who sits at her window, fourth level, east wall, and sees everyone passing, knows everyone's business through pure attention. A young man who's appointed himself unofficial chronicler, keeping notebooks of births and deaths and marriages and feuds, history accumulating in cramped handwriting. A child of nine who's fascinated by the bats, who climbs to the highest reaches at dusk to watch them leave, returns at dawn to watch them return, who's accumulating knowledge that no one else has thought to preserve.

The population is maybe three hundred, maybe three-fifty — it fluctuates as people leave for the surface, as babies are born, as traders decide to stay or residents decide to go. Most were born here, or came so young they remember nothing else. The ones who came as adults stand out slightly, never quite settling into the posture, the movement patterns, the reflexive ducking and turning that native Compression inhabitants perform without thinking.

There are the old tensions that any community holds: the builders who want to expand versus those who want to preserve the remaining open space, the workshops that generate noise versus the dwellings that suffer from it, the families with many children versus those who chose to have few or none. There are class distinctions, subtle but real, between those who own their dwellings versus those who rent, between those who do skilled work versus simple labor, between the old families who've been here since the founding versus newcomers from five years ago.

But there's also a baseline equality born from the compression itself. A wealthy person's dwelling can't be much larger than a poor person's; there simply isn't room. The air, the damp, the heat, the dim light — these affect everyone equally. The teacher's child and the metalworker's child learn together because there's only one school. The banker (yes, there's a banker, someone has to manage loans for building projects) lives three doors down from the coal tender, close enough to smell the smoke on his clothes.

They have their beauties, these people. Kaisa the midwife has a voice like warm honey when she's soothing a woman in labor. Aleksi the welder can make metal speak under his hammer, shaping it with a precision that approaches art. Terese in the brewery has that laugh. Jiri the gardener has hands that gentle plants into thriving despite the impossible conditions. Gregor the Listener can diagnose a system by sound alone, a skill that borders on the supernatural.

They have their damages too. Most have the respiratory cough from the damp air, chronic but rarely serious. Many have joint problems from the cramped working positions. Almost everyone over forty has arthritis starting in their hands. There are miscarriages, more than on the surface maybe, though Kaisa insists the rate isn't that different. There are the accidents: falls from height, crush injuries from equipment, infections from the omnipresent moisture.

And they have their accommodations, their adaptations. They've learned to sleep in shifts when necessary, to share space with a fluidity that would be impossible for surface dwellers. They've learned to navigate by sound and smell and touch, moving through the dark lower levels with confidence. They've learned to read the micro-expressions of people living too close to hide much, to give each other the courtesy of pretended ignorance, to balance the intimacy forced by proximity with the distance required for sanity.

The young people sometimes dream of leaving — of the surface, of space, of being able to stand up straight everywhere. Some do leave. Maybe one in ten. The rest stay, because this is home, because their families are here, because the surface is expensive and strange and too large, too open, too bright. The ones who leave and try to return are changed, unable to settle back into the compression, their eyes always drifting upward to the ceiling that's too close, their bodies unable to remember how to fold into available volume.

There are births, celebrated with small gatherings, gifts of fabric and food and hand-built furniture. There are marriages, formalized by Grandmother Piech or whoever the current elder is, marked by a communal meal in the gathering space. There are deaths, mourned with a period of silence — one hour, marked by the cessation of all non-essential activity — before the body is carried up and out to the surface for burial, because there's no room here for graves.

There are festivals, few and important: the Founding Day, marking when the first permanent dwelling was built. The Roof Completion, celebrating when the patchwork cover was finished. The First Light of Spring, when the sun angle changes enough to send a beam directly down through one specific glass panel to strike the quarry floor, thirty seconds of direct sunlight that children are held up to touch.

They are people, finally. Not special, not transformed by their environment into something other than human. Just people who've adapted to circumstances, who've built lives in a space that seems impossible, who've created community through necessity and maintained it through choice. They complain about their neighbors and help those same neighbors build additions. They gossip and keep secrets. They fall in love and out of it. They raise children with the patience required to teach someone how to live here, how to move, how to be silent, how to share.

They know they're strange, the ones who've visited the surface. They know other people have gardens that get real sunlight, homes with rooms for single purposes, streets where two carts can pass. They know this and mostly they don't care. This is the Compression. This is home. This is the place they've made livable through collective effort and individual accommodation, through the small courtesies that make density bearable and the large projects that make it sustainable.

And they continue, day by day, maintaining systems and raising children and building carefully and harvesting mushrooms and hauling water and adjusting to the small changes that accumulate into the slow evolution of their impossible city, compressed beneath the surface, dense and damp and dim and entirely, improbably alive.
