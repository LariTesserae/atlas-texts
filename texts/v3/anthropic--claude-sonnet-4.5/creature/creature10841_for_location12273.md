# What Lives There

The builders flicker at the edges of perception like heat shimmer made flesh.

You cannot count them. The number changes—three, then seven, then one, then five—not because they come and go through the chimney you climbed, but because their individuation is negotiable here, context-dependent, a function of how you're looking and what the space needs in that moment. Sometimes a single person works with four hands. Sometimes two people share one body between them, taking turns existing, their forms overlapping like a double-exposed photograph that keeps shifting which image is dominant.

They are small-bodied, compact, built like people who live their lives crouching in narrow spaces. The tallest might reach your shoulder. Their proportions are human but their scale suggests a different branch of possibility, or the same humanity compressed by generations in the mountain's throat, evolutionary pressure applied not over millennia but over subjective centuries in a place where time doesn't work correctly and change can happen faster than outside, or slower, or recursively.

Their skin is dark—but the darkness isn't simple. It's layered. The base is brown like volcanic soil, like wet clay, but over it (or within it, or emerging from it) are other colors: green stains from the moss that grows on them, not just touching their skin but continuous with it, chlorophyll operating in their epidermis. Grey dust from stone-working, ground so fine and present so long it's become part of their pigmentation. Black from obsidian powder, caught in the whorls of their fingerprints, settled into the creases of their joints. White calcium deposits at their elbows and knees where they kneel and lean for hours, the bone underneath thickening, calcifying on the outside of the skin, forming external plates like protective deposits.

When they move through the light-shafts, you see that some of them have moss growing not just on but *in* them—visible through skin that has become translucent in places, thin as paper, showing the green growth webbed through subcutaneous layers, following the paths of their veins, photosynthesizing. They are not injured by this. They are not infected. They have achieved a symbiosis with the throat-garden, their bodies recognizing the plants as part of their extended metabolism, allowing the partnership because here, in this pocket of wrong time and folded space, such things are possible and perhaps necessary.

Their hands are extraordinary.

Each finger is calloused in different patterns—worn smooth where they hold stone blades, ridged where they pinch cordage, pebbled where they press clay, scarred where obsidian has cut them so many times the tissue has toughened into something between skin and bark. Their fingernails are thick, yellowed, shaped by use into specialized tools: one person's thumbnails are worn to chisel-edges for fine carving; another's fingernails curve sharply, perfect for hooking and pulling fibers; a third has nails that have been deliberately ground flat and smooth, the better to burnish clay surfaces.

But more than the calluses, their hands are *knowing*. You watch a builder reach into a crack without looking, fingers reading the stone the way you might read braille, and withdraw with exactly the right size of bone fragment for whatever they're constructing. You watch hands braid cordage without eyes on the work, the fingers moving in patterns that look choreographed, that have the fluency of deep practice, of muscle memory that goes down to the literal muscles, that has rewired the neural pathways until the hands think independently, problem-solving through touch.

They can hold their hands in the places that are too hot to touch. You watch someone press their palm flat against stone that makes the air above it waver, hold it there while placing something with the other hand, and when they withdraw the palm is red but not burned, marked but not damaged. They've adapted. Their nerves have learned different thresholds. Or the place recognizes them as part of itself and doesn't burn what belongs to it.

Their clothing is difficult to distinguish from their bodies, and this seems intentional.

They wear wrappings of woven plant fiber, but the fiber is still partially alive, still rooted, so that the cloth they wrap around their waists and shoulders is growing, extending tendrils, putting out small leaves. They wear vests of moss that they cultivate directly on their torsos, encouraging it to grow in thick mats that insulate, that retain moisture, that photosynthesize against their ribs. Some wear girdles of braided vine that have tightened over time, or over years, or over the subjective duration they've spent in this place, so the vines are partially embedded now, growing into the flesh at the hips, and this too appears deliberate, appears to be part of how one becomes continuous with the garden.

One builder you glimpse—only for a moment, working in an alcove that seals itself after—wears a cape of overlapping leaves sewn onto a backing of human skin. You understand, with a lurch of recognition that feels like falling, that the skin is their own, shed like a snake's and then repurposed, and that they've done this multiple times, that several of their shed skins are incorporated into the walls now, tanned and pressed into service as substrate for other materials, the ultimate offering of self to place.

They wear jewelry that is also tooling: bone needles thrust through their earlobes and available for use; bracelets of sharpened stone that can be snapped off and employed as blades; anklets of seeds that rattle when they move, providing acoustic location in the dense space, letting them work without collision, each person a small percussion instrument announcing their position. The seeds are viable. You see one builder kneel to work and seeds fall from their ankle-rattle into the moss, and the moss accepts them, and in the accelerated time of this place they'll sprout within hours, and perhaps this is how the garden expands, seeded by the builders' movement, grown from what falls off them as they work.

Their hair is long, practical, and multi-purposed.

It's worn in complex arrangements: braided with plant fibers until you can't tell where hair ends and cordage begins; wound around wooden stakes that are themselves thrust into cracks in the wall, so the person is literally anchored to the structure by their hair while they work; hung with small objects—bone beads, stone weights, shell fragments—that clack together and produce tones, more acoustic markers, more percussion adding to the space's constant music.

You realize that their hair is probably never cut, has been growing for as long as they've been here, and if time moves strangely then it might be growing at strange rates, and the length might not indicate age in any way you could calculate. One builder has hair that drags on the ground, that they've wound around their body multiple times, that they're using as an actual rope to lower something into a crevice. Another has hair that seems too short for the apparent age of their face—child-length hair on an adult—until you understand that it's been harvested regularly, cut and processed into the fine cordage you see everywhere, that their hair is a crop they grow on their own heads and harvest for construction.

Some of them have begun to go grey-green, their hair changing color from human-brown to moss-green, the individual strands hosting microscopic plant life, becoming partially botanical.

Their faces are difficult to see clearly and harder to remember.

Not because they hide them—they don't seem to notice or care about being observed—but because the faces don't quite stabilize. You look at a builder's face and register eyes, nose, mouth, the arrangement of features, but when you look away you can't reconstruct it. The faces are human but generic, as though they're showing you the platonic ideal of a face rather than a specific individual's features, or as though their individuality has been worn away by the work, smoothed down to essential function: eyes for seeing where to place things, nose for breathing the wet air, mouth for...

The mouths are rarely still. They move constantly, and you realize the builders are speaking, or chanting, or doing something with their voices that's part of the work. The sounds are too quiet to hear distinctly under the water-percussion and the mountain's breathing, but you catch fragments: rhythmic syllables, repeated phrases, tonal patterns that might be language or might be music or might be instructions they're giving to the materials, telling the stone how to accept the clay, telling the moss where to grow, telling the space itself how to cohere.

One builder you observe for several minutes (or several hours; duration is uncertain) never stops moving their lips. The jaw works constantly, teeth visible occasionally, tongue shaping sounds that emerge as barely-audible whispers. They're narrating their own actions or praying or counting or doing something else—speaking the work into being, describing it as they do it, and the description is part of the doing, the words are as structural as the materials.

You notice that some of them have teeth worn to nubs—not from age but from use, from biting cordage, from holding materials in their mouths while their hands are occupied, from years or subjective-decades of using their jaws as a third hand. One has incisors filed or worn to flat edges, perfect for cutting plant fibers. Another shows canines that have grown longer, more prominent, possibly because they use them so often they've encouraged the teeth to extend, or possibly because the place's wrongness includes dental modification, bodies adapting to include the tools they most need.

Their eyes are the most human thing about them and the least human thing about them.

Human because they show awareness, intelligence, intention—these are people doing skilled work with full consciousness, making choices, solving problems, creating. You see concentration in those eyes, the inward focus of deep expertise. You see satisfaction when something is placed correctly. You see brief flickers of something like joy when a section of work completes itself, when the systems click into harmony.

But the eyes also show adaptation to the light here, to the shifting illumination and the phosphorescence and the sudden shafts of sun from impossible angles. The pupils don't react normally—they stay wide and dark even in direct light, or they contract to pinpricks in the dimness, or they do both simultaneously, one eye adjusted for bright and one for dark, independent operation for a place where lighting is inconsistent. The irises have colors you don't have names for—not brown or green or blue but something between all of them, something that shifts with the light or with what they're looking at, adaptive coloration that optimizes for whatever they're trying to see.

Some of them have eyes that reflect light like an animal's, like a cat's, throwing back the phosphorescent glow with a green or gold or silver shine. Tapetum lucidum, a reflective layer that shouldn't be in human eyes, but here it is, developed or grown or manifested because working in a place of inconsistent light requires it, and bodies in this place become what they need to be.

When they look directly at you—which happens rarely, briefly—you feel *seen* in a way that's almost unbearable. They're not looking at your surface. They're looking at your duration, your trajectory through time, your place in causal sequences. They see you the way they see the stone: as something with a past and future, as something that can be worked with, placed correctly, incorporated into larger patterns. The gaze is not hostile but it's not quite recognizing you as a fellow person. You're a material that has walked itself into the space. You're a component that might be useful. You're an observer, serving the function of observation, part of the work by virtue of witnessing it.

Then they look away and return to their tasks, and you feel dismissed but also released, no longer pinned by that impossible attention.

They move with a fluency that suggests either deep familiarity with the space or a different relationship with gravity and dimension. You watch a builder step from one surface to another and the step covers more distance than it should, or less, or the builder momentarily seems to be standing on a wall that should be vertical but from their perspective is clearly floor. They navigate the throat-garden's folded geometry without confusion, without hesitation, their bodies understanding routes and connections that aren't visible to you.

Sometimes they move in ways that look like dance: a builder will approach a section of wall with a kind of ceremonial step-pattern, feet placing in specific spots, body swaying, arms moving through positions that seem ritualized before finally reaching out to place whatever they're carrying. The dance and the work are continuous. The approach is part of the construction. The movement through space is as important as the object being moved.

They work mostly in silence apart from the lip-movement, the constant sub-verbal muttering. They don't speak to each other in ways you can detect—no calls across the space, no conversations, no apparent coordination. Yet they clearly coordinate. Two builders will approach the same section of wall from different directions, each carrying materials, and without discussion they'll work on adjacent areas, their efforts complementing each other, one person's placement creating the condition for the other person's placement, back and forth, call and response in physical action rather than words.

Possibly they're communicating through the water-sounds, through the acoustic richness of the space. Possibly the rhythm of their work—the timing of when they place things, when they tap or press or tie—creates patterns in the ambient sound, and those patterns carry meaning, and they're holding conversations through percussion, through the language of material meeting material.

Or possibly they're continuous enough with each other and with the place that communication isn't necessary. They're parts of a single distributed organism. They know what the others are doing the way your left hand knows what your right hand is doing—not through communication but through being parts of the same system.

You see evidence of long habitation in their bodies: scars layered over scars, some fresh and some ancient, marking years or decades or subjective centuries of small injuries from sharp stone and hot surfaces and the constant minor damages of detailed handwork. But the scars are worn proudly, or at least unselfconsciously, treated as marks of experience rather than disfigurement. One builder has forearms so densely scarred the skin looks scaled, reptilian, a texture that's been created by ten thousand small cuts healing over and over.

Some injuries haven't fully healed or have healed into something else. You see a builder whose left hand has only three fingers—the others lost somehow—but the stumps have been repurposed, wrapped with fiber and tipped with bone or stone, extended into tools, made useful rather than mourned. You see another with a scar across their abdomen that hasn't closed completely, that's held together with plant-fiber stitches, and growing in the wound are small mushrooms, fruiting bodies emerging from the flesh, and this seems accepted, seems possibly intentional, the wound maintained as a cultivation site.

Their bodies smell of the place: stone-dust and plant-growth, the mineral tang of volcanic rock and the green smell of photosynthesis, the earthy scent of moss and the sharper note of obsidian (which you wouldn't have thought had a smell, but it does here, a scent like broken glass and cold). Under that is human smell—sweat and skin-oil and the tang of long exertion—but it's overlaid with so much else that they smell more like the garden than like people, more like living stone than like mammals.

They handle the space's heat without apparent distress. They work in the surge-times when you're gasping and dizzy, their movements unaffected, their pace unchanged. Either they've adapted to tolerate it or they no longer quite regulate temperature the way humans outside do, their metabolisms adjusted to operate in broader ranges, or they've given up some of their homeostasis in exchange for being able to work in all the garden's phases.

You notice that in the hottest moments, their skin gleams with moisture, but it's not quite sweat—it's too clear, too pure, almost like the water that weeps from the stone. They're not perspiring. They're condensing. Their bodies are generating the water directly from the humid air, or they're part of the garden's water cycle, their skin another surface for moisture to bead on and run from, contributing to the pools and channels and the constant dripping music.

One builder—seen briefly, working in a space that irises shut after—appears to be breathing through their skin as much as their lungs, their torso expanding and contracting but their mouth closed, nose unmoving, the respiration happening through pores or through some other mechanism, the boundary between inside and outside made permeable. They've become amphibious in the dense wet air, adapted to extract oxygen from humidity, their lungs a backup system rather than the primary one.

Their ages are impossible to determine. That one might be twenty or eighty. This one could be adolescent or ancient. The usual markers don't apply, and it's not just that you're bad at reading their features—it's that they exist in a place where duration is flexible, where they might have been here for months of outside time but years of subjective time, or vice versa. They might be aging forward and backward simultaneously, growing older in some ways while other processes reverse. They might be maintaining themselves at a particular stage, held there by the work or by the place's influence, their bodies sustained at whatever age is optimal for the construction they're doing.

One thing is certain: none of them are children. Whatever this work is, it requires maturity—not necessarily physical maturity but maturity of purpose, of understanding, of commitment. You see no young faces, no small bodies. Either children aren't brought here or they aren't allowed in or they can't survive the wrongness or becoming a builder is something that happens to people after childhood, a transformation or a calling that occurs only to adults who've prepared for it or been selected by it.

You wonder if any of them were born here or if all of them came from outside. You wonder if reproduction is possible in this pocket of altered reality or if the only way to sustain the builders' population is recruitment from the normal world. You see no evidence of pregnancy, no infants, nothing that suggests family structures or generational continuity. They might be a community of individuals, each drawn here separately, made into a collective by the work rather than by kinship.

Or possibly they're all the same person at different times, the space's temporal loops allowing one builder to work alongside their own past and future selves, the population maintained by a single individual folded through duration, meeting themselves coming and going, handing materials to their own earlier selves, receiving instruction from their future iterations.

You watch one builder place an object in a niche, and later (or earlier, or simultaneously in a different layer of time) you see a builder retrieve an object from that same niche, and the two builders have the same scar on the same hand, the same way of moving, and you're almost certain you've witnessed a single person's timeline doubling back on itself, the same individual both placing and retrieving, creating a stable loop, the object's existence maintained by causal recursion.

They're patient with a patience that transcends human scale. You watch a builder work on a section of wall that's already densely constructed, adding material in gaps barely visible, places where you'd think no addition was needed or possible, and they work with absolute focus, with the care of someone who knows this single small act is crucial, that the entire structure depends on this nearly invisible placement being exactly right. They adjust, readjust, try different angles, different positions, taking what might be hours to find the precise configuration, and when it's right—when it clicks into place in whatever system they're maintaining—they move on without satisfaction or frustration, without emotional response, just continuing to the next task with the same infinite patience.

This is not the patience of someone working on a project that will finish someday. This is the patience of someone who has become identical with their work, who no longer distinguishes between working and being, who will place stones and weave fibers and train vines and press patterns until the mountain cools or the sun dies or time itself concludes, not because they're compelled but because this is what they are now, this is what they've made themselves into.

You understand with a chill that runs through you despite the heat: they are not going to leave. They cannot leave. They've adapted too completely, integrated too thoroughly. The boundary between builder and garden has dissolved. They're as much a part of the throat-space as the moss and the stone. They've grown themselves into it, or it's grown itself into them, and separation is no longer possible, possibly no longer conceivable.

They've become permanent residents of a place that should be temporary, that's still forming, that exists in a pocket of broken causality. They've chosen—or been chosen by—the work of maintaining an impossibility, and the price of that work is transformation into something that can survive here, that can thrive here, that can continue the endless layered construction of a space being born inside a mountain that should not exist.

And they accept this. You see it in their movements, in their focus, in the way they touch the materials with something beyond care, something like love but more committed, more absolute. They're not trapped. They're not suffering. They're *home* in a way that people outside this place rarely achieve, unified with their purpose, continuous with their environment, become what they do, where they do it, why they do it.

The builders are the throat-garden's true fauna, its thinking component, its caretakers and its children, neither creating it nor created by it but co-arising with it, midwifing each other into existence in a relationship too intimate and recursive to parse from outside, something that can only be understood by becoming it, by staying too long, by allowing the transformation to begin, by placing one stone and then another and then another until you can't remember when you started or imagine when you'll stop.

You watch them work. You don't watch them leave. You're not certain they ever do.
