# The Inhabitants of the Living Hollows

They move like water finding its level — not flowing, exactly, but settling into available spaces with a naturalness that suggests they've forgotten how to fight against their environment. If you watch one person for an hour in the bone-structured dwellings, you see perhaps three or four movements: a repositioning to scrape bacterial mat from a basin, a reach for a tool, a shift in posture. Each motion terminates in stillness so complete it seems practiced, but it isn't practice — it's the baseline state. Movement is the exception. Stillness is what they return to, instantly and totally, like something settling after disturbance.

Their bodies carry the particular physiology of people who've lived generations in constant cool dampness and moderate but unvarying exertion. Lean without being skeletal, muscled in specific ways — powerful hands and forearms from working bone and stone, strong cores from the constant climbing and navigating of vertical spaces, but not the heavy muscle of people who lift great weights. Their skin is pale in the way of people who live without sun, but not sickly — a paleness that seems like its own kind of health, adapted and appropriate. You see the blue of veins easily through their skin at wrist and throat.

Some individuals are marked extensively with the mineral pigments: complex patterns of white clay and ochre that follow the contours of face and arms, spiraling designs that terminate at wrist and shoulder. Others are unmarked entirely. The marking doesn't correlate obviously with age or status — children and elders might both be unmarked, or both painted. The designs change; you see the same person with different patterns on different days, suggesting the marking responds to something — role, mood, ritual state — that remains opaque.

Their hair tends toward darkness, worn long almost universally, bound back from the face with bone pins or braided fiber. Some individuals incorporate bone beads or shells (where do the shells come from?) into the braiding, creating patterns that click very softly when they move their heads — one of the few deliberate sounds in the settlement. Others keep their hair severely plain, oiled flat against the skull with some rendered fat that smells faintly mineral.

The children are startling in their silence. You expect children to be loud — it seems fundamental to childhood — but these move through the chambers in near-perfect quiet, their games conducted in whispers and gestures. They seem to understand the acoustic properties of the space intuitively, never raising their voices even in excitement. When a child is hurt — you witness one fall from a bone structure, a drop of perhaps six feet onto stone — there's a sharp intake of breath but no cry. An adult appears within moments, as if the silence itself communicated the injury, and tends to the child with gentle hands and murmured sounds too quiet to carry.

The children's faces are more expressive than the adults', less controlled, but the expressions pass quickly into that same settled stillness. A flash of delight at capturing an arthropod, then immediate calm. Frustration at a failed game move, gone in a breath. They're learning the adult restraint early, absorbing it the way children do — through imitation and correction too subtle to see directly.

Adults range across the expected human spectrum of age, though the elders are fewer than you'd expect and none seem extremely aged. Whether this reflects life expectancy in the Hollows or simply that the very old move to chambers you aren't shown, you can't determine. The oldest visible individuals might be sixty, perhaps seventy — creased faces, joint-thickened hands, movement that's careful rather than fluid. But they work alongside younger adults without apparent frailty, scraping mats, knapping stone, processing fiber.

One elder woman (you're fairly certain of the gender, though the wrapped clothing and the cultural reticence about bodies leaves most physical sex characteristics unconfirmed) seems to occupy a position of significance. Others defer to her in subtle ways: waiting for her to reach for food first at a communal meal, orienting themselves toward her in group gatherings, falling silent when she makes one of her rare whispered comments. She's marked elaborately, spirals of white clay covering most of her visible skin, and she wears a necklace of what might be vertebrae from something fish-like, strung on sinew. Her hands are in constant motion when she speaks, describing shapes in the air, as if language requires the physical dimension to be complete.

The middle-aged adults — the largest cohort — are the ones you see doing most of the settlement's physical work. They're the ones dismantling and rebuilding structures, harvesting the bacterial mats, tending the water-pools, processing hides. Their bodies show the particular wear of their tasks: the knapper's fingertips are scarred with a hundred small cuts; the person who processes fiber has shoulders developed asymmetrically from the repetitive pulling motion; someone who appears to specialize in climbing to reach the singers overhead has forearms like bundled cables.

One individual stands out for their frequent presence in your field of observation — whether they're actually following you or you're simply noticing them because they're more distinctive is unclear. They're perhaps thirty, small-framed, with extensive burn scarring on their left arm and side of their face, the skin there pulled tight and shiny. The scarring is old, fully healed, but dramatic enough that it immediately identifies them. They work primarily with the bone structures, and you watch them over several days as they disassemble a complex lattice framework in one chamber and rebuild it, modified, in another. Their movements with the bone are expert, knowing exactly where to press to release tension in the lashings, how to angle pieces to slide them through tight passages without catching.

The burned person never speaks in your hearing, even in whisper, but communicates constantly through gesture — hands describing curves and angles, fingers pinching to indicate thickness, arms spreading to show extent. Others respond to these gestures immediately, suggesting a gestural language that's either universal in the settlement or specific to the work-groups. The scarring might make vocalization difficult or painful; you never see them attempt it.

There are fewer young adults than you'd expect — the age range from perhaps fifteen to twenty-five seems underrepresented. Those who are present in that cohort often work together, a distinct group that clusters in certain chambers and seems to handle specific tasks. They're the ones you see doing the more athletic work: climbing to the highest chambers, navigating the narrowest passages to retrieve something or investigate a new seepage. They move with an efficiency the older adults lack, that fluid confidence of bodies at peak ability, not yet thickened or stiffened by decades of work.

One young woman — late teens, perhaps twenty — appears to be training for something. You see her repeatedly in a particular chamber, a smaller one empty of structures, practicing what looks like movement forms. Not dance, exactly, and not fighting technique, but something between: careful poses held for long durations, transitions from one position to another executed with extreme precision and control. An older adult watches sometimes, occasionally reaches out to adjust an arm angle or shift a foot position, but mostly just observes. The young woman's face during these sessions shows concentration so intense it borders on trance. Sweat gleams on her pale skin despite the cool temperature, and her breathing is the only sound — measured, controlled, never quickening even in what look like physically demanding positions.

The gender dynamics are elusive. Clothing and cultural practice obscure more than they reveal. The wrapped garments hide body shape. You see what might be a pregnant woman, but the loose wrapping makes it impossible to be certain. If there are gendered divisions of labor, they're not obvious — you see people of various apparent genders performing the same tasks. The one pattern that might be meaningful: the individuals who work most directly with the water-pools, harvesting the fish and maintaining the channels, seem to be consistently what you'd read as feminine, while the stone-knappers seem masculine. But it's a soft pattern, not absolute, and might be coincidence or your own projection.

Physical contact between adults is common but formalized. The hand-on-shoulder greeting and parting. The touch to guide attention. You see what might be intimate partnerships — pairs who work together consistently, who share a bone shelter, who sit closer than the general spacing norm. But there's no public display of affection beyond the same contact everyone uses. Whether this is cultural restraint or whether their relationship forms genuinely are this reserved, you can't penetrate.

The children sleep in communal structures more often than with specific adults, suggesting either collective childrearing or family units that are less nuclear than you're accustomed to. Adults move between different sleeping chambers over time, and children's associations seem fluid — a child with one adult group one day, with a different configuration the next. There might be kinship structures you're not reading, or the structures might be genuinely non-standard from your framework.

Everyone's hands are constantly busy during waking hours. Even in apparent rest, people are doing something: re-wrapping fiber on a bone spool, testing the edge of a stone tool with a thumb, braiding cord. The stillness is postural; the hands remain active. This might be culturally valued — idle hands as uncomfortable as loud speech — or might simply be practical necessity in an environment where so much requires manual processing.

Their teeth show wear patterns consistent with a particular diet: the flat wear on molars from grinding the dried bacterial mats, the specific kind of chipping that comes from incidental grit in food. Most adults have lost at least a few teeth, and some elders have significant gaps. But you don't see infection or the kind of dramatic decay that would suggest dental health is a major problem. Something in the mineral-bitter water might protect against that, or the diet might be less conducive to decay than grain-based diets elsewhere.

One young man has a withered leg, shortened and atrophied from what must have been childhood illness or injury. He navigates the chambers with a crutch of carved bone, his movement through the space necessarily slower than others but still competent. He's integrated fully into the work — you see him doing detail-work on bone latticework, a task that requires manual dexterity but not mobility. No one treats him with visible pity or particular care; he's simply another person doing his work. The settlement's architecture accommodates him without modification because the curving passages and natural stone shelves provide built-in assistance for someone who needs hand-holds and rests.

You notice people's eyes. In the constant brightness, there's no need to strain or squint, and their eyes have adapted to these conditions — possibly over many generations. The pupils are neither particularly dilated nor constricted, adjusted to the moderate illumination. Their eye contact patterns are different from what you're used to: less direct, more peripheral. They seem to track each other through side-glances and brief checks rather than sustained looking. Whether this is cultural or a response to the brightness — direct eye contact in constant light might be uncomfortable — isn't clear.

The pattern of scarring across the population suggests the environment is not without dangers. Many adults carry marks: pale lines across forearms that might be from slips with stone tools, a missing fingertip, a dented scar on someone's shin that speaks of a fall onto rock. Nothing catastrophic in your observation, but the cumulative evidence that living here involves regular minor injury. The burned individual's scarring is the most dramatic, but others show smaller burn marks on hands and forearms, suggesting fire-injuries from the oil lamps are common enough to be expected.

Their sense of personal space contracts and expands in ways you don't predict. In the large chambers, people maintain significant distance from each other — several feet at minimum, clustering in groups but keeping those groups separated. In the narrow passages, they slide past each other with inches to spare and no apparent discomfort. The space itself seems to dictate the social distance rather than some fixed personal bubble. In tight quarters, closeness is acceptable because it's necessary; in open chambers, distance is maintained because it's possible.

You never see anyone alone for very long. Even in the smaller chambers, people work in pairs or trios. Someone who enters an empty chamber is soon joined by another. Whether this is safety practice — avoiding isolation in case of injury — or cultural preference for company, the result is constant social proximity. Yet within that proximity, there's individual absorption in tasks. They're together without necessarily interacting, a parallel presence rather than constant engagement.

The music, when it comes, is shocking in its rarity and restraint. Once, in what might be a multi-day observation period, you hear something that might be singing: a soft, sustained tone that seems to come from multiple people simultaneously, barely louder than breath. It rises and falls in slow cycles, not quite melody, more like tonal meditation. It continues for perhaps twenty minutes, during which everyone in earshot becomes still, even the children. Then it stops as gradually as it began, fading rather than ending, and normal activity resumes. No one acknowledges that anything happened.

One child is distinct in being always in motion, unlike the others. A boy perhaps seven or eight, marked with a single line of ochre down the center of his face, who doesn't demonstrate the usual stillness. He's constantly fidgeting, shifting, touching things, moving from chamber to chamber. Adults redirect him gently but constantly, a hand on his shoulder to still him, a gesture to guide him back to a task. He tries — you see him concentrate, force himself to stillness — but it breaks within moments. The other children avoid him slightly, not cruelly but simply maintaining distance, as if his constant motion is uncomfortable for them. Whether this is a neurodivergence that the settlement accommodates or whether he's young enough that adult control hasn't set in yet, you can't determine. But he's visible, noticeable in a population that's learned to be unremarkable.

The oldest person you see — a man whose age might be anything from seventy to ninety, impossible to tell with certainty — sits in one of the middle chambers for the entire duration of your stay. He's not immobile; you see him work on a complex piece of bone carving, something that might be decorative or might be a tool you don't recognize. But he doesn't leave that chamber in your observation. People come to him: brief visits, a touch on the shoulder, a whispered exchange, then departure. He seems to be a fixed point in the settlement's geography, and others orbit him in their movements through the space.

His carving is extraordinary in its detail: a section of bone no larger than a hand, covered in intricate spiraling patterns that interlock and layer. He works on it for hours at a time with a bone awl and stone scrapers, removing microscopic amounts of material with each pass. Whether it's a months-long project or a years-long one, you can't gauge, but the work has the quality of something that's become meditative in its duration, the object almost secondary to the process.

The people smell of the space they inhabit: that mineral-stone scent, the slight iron tang of the bacterial mats, the fat they use for waterproofing and hair oil. Not unpleasant, but distinctive. Close contact in the passages means you learn these scents intimately. Individuals have variations — one person's oil smells of something almost herbal, another's carries a sharper note — but the base is constant. They probably can't smell it anymore, too habituated to the sensory environment they're immersed in constantly.

Their breathing is quieter than expected, even during exertion. You watch someone climb a complex bone structure, a physically demanding ascent, and their breathing increases but never becomes audible beyond a few feet. They've learned breath control along with sound control, or the two are inseparable. Loud breathing might feel as inappropriate as loud speech. The result is that even physical labor has a quality of restraint, nothing demonstrative or dramatic.

One woman in middle age is almost never in the communal chambers. You see her mostly in passages, moving between spaces with obvious purpose, carrying materials or tools. When you do see her in a chamber, she's at the edge, engaged with something at the periphery before moving on. She might be a trader or messenger, someone whose role involves connection between different parts of the settlement rather than work within a specific chamber. She's marked distinctively: her hair is bound with complex knots that incorporate bone beads at intervals, creating a pattern that's visible from a distance. Perhaps this is identification, making her recognizable in her role.

The young people training in that particular chamber — you realize after several observations that it's always a different person, that the space serves that function rather than belonging to an individual — all share certain characteristics. They're thin even by the settlement's lean standards. Their movements have a deliberate quality that exceeds the general cultural restraint. They spend long periods in stillness between the movement forms. Training for what? Perhaps it's spiritual practice, developing control for its own value. Perhaps it's preparation for some role you haven't witnessed. The watching elder never explains, and you can't formulate the question in a way that gets more than a gesture toward the stone, the light, the pools — a suggestion that the answer is embedded in the environment itself.

You never see anyone who's obviously ill, apart from the chronic conditions visible in old injuries and the withered leg. Either the population is remarkably healthy, or illness is handled in chambers you're not shown, or those who sicken are removed from the communal spaces. Given the constant dampness, you'd expect respiratory problems, but you hear no coughing, see no labored breathing or feverish faces. The diet must supply something protective, or the bacterial mats have properties beyond simple nutrition, or the population has adapted over sufficient generations that what would sicken outsiders leaves them unaffected.

The guide who brought you in and stays generally near — though not obtrusively so — is marked with a distinctive pattern: a broad band of white clay across both eyes like a mask. The effect is slightly disturbing, making it hard to read expression, but perhaps that's intentional. The mask marks them as guide, as interface with outside, and the obscured expression creates appropriate distance. They're patient with your clumsiness in the space, your excessive sound, your questions that they answer with gestures and brief whispers. They sleep in a small chamber near where you've been given space, available but not intrusive. Their age is indeterminate — somewhere in the broad middle of adulthood, could be thirty or fifty. Their hands show the scarring of someone who works with stone tools, but they're not specialized like the knapper; these are general-use hands.

One pattern becomes clear only after many observations: people touch the stone constantly. Not in any dramatic way, but in passing — a hand trailing along a wall, fingers brushing a natural column, a palm pressed briefly to the rock. It's ubiquitous, everyone does it, children and elders and everyone between. The stone-touching might be orientation in a space where traditional navigation fails, the texture and temperature of different sections providing information. Or it might be affection, a constant contact with the material that contains them. Or habit so ingrained it's unconscious. But watch any person move through the settlement and you'll see that steady, repeated contact with stone.

They're capable of sustained physical effort that surprises you, given the spare diet and the constant cool temperature that should, by surface logic, make caloric needs difficult to meet. But you watch a group dismantle a large bone structure — heavy pieces, awkward to handle — and work for what must be hours without obvious fatigue. They pause occasionally, drink from the skull-basins, eat small amounts of dried mat, then return to work. The labor isn't explosive or dramatic, but it's steady, and it continues long enough that you tire just from observing. They've learned to work within their energy budget perfectly, never exceeding it but using what's available completely.

Children transition to adult work gradually and without ceremony that you see. A child perhaps ten years old works alongside an adult at scraping mats, imitating technique, receiving subtle corrections through repositioned hands or gentle touches. Over the days you observe, the child's competence visibly improves. There's no moment of declaration, no ritual marking the passage from child to worker. The work itself is the transition, the acquired skill the marker of change.

The people carry themselves with a kind of dignity that's hard to articulate. Despite material simplicity, despite living in what surface dwellers would consider harsh constraints, there's no sense of deprivation or hardship in their bearing. They move through their days with quiet purpose, their work clearly meaningful to them, their social interactions marked by respect and gentleness. Whether this is cultural achievement or whether you're romanticizing what you see, projecting nobility onto simplicity, you can't fully separate. But the impression persists: these are people who know who they are, who've found a way to be human that fits their particular environment precisely.

One young woman stands out for her markings: her arms are covered shoulder to wrist in an intricate pattern of white and ochre that suggests feathers, or perhaps scales, overlapping in careful lines. The work must have taken hours and shows a level of artistic skill that suggests specialists in pigment-marking exist within the settlement. She's one of the climbers, moving through vertical spaces with casual confidence, and the markings are visible from below, perhaps intentionally — a way to be recognized, to carry identity on the body where the wrapped clothing obscures everything else.

The quietness is absolute enough that you begin to notice people's other sounds: the whisper of wrapped clothing against skin, the soft click of bone beads in hair, the sound of breath. These tiny sounds become loud in the swallowed silence, enough to identify specific individuals by their particular acoustic signature. The person whose wrapping includes a belt of shell that clicks with each step. The elder whose breathing is just slightly audible with a faint wheeze. The child whose hand repeatedly touches a bone pendant he wears, producing a soft tapping rhythm.

They notice you noticing them. Not obtrusively, but you catch the peripheral glances, the slight pauses when you enter a space, the whispered exchanges that might be about you or might be unrelated. You're being evaluated, though the criteria are opaque. Whether you're meeting their standards or failing them, you can't tell. The hospitality continues regardless, the tolerance of your presence, but you're certainly being observed with at least the attention you're directing at them.

One interaction breaks the pattern of careful distance: a child, perhaps five, approaches you directly in one of the large chambers. They stand close — closer than any adult has — and stare up with open curiosity. A small hand reaches out and touches your clothing, feeling the textile, which is foreign material brought from outside. An adult appears and gently redirects the child, but there's no sense of reprimand, just guidance. The child goes, looking back once with an expression that might be disappointment or might just be continued curiosity.

The people are capable of stillness so complete they seem to vanish into their environment. Several times you scan a chamber you thought was empty only to suddenly notice someone sitting against a wall, motionless, probably there the entire time. They're not hiding — but their stillness and the stone-colored clothing and the visual complexity of the bone structures make them disappear into the scene. It's disconcerting until you realize it's not intentional concealment; it's just their default state, and your eye is trained to catch motion.

In the lower chambers, where the reverent atmosphere prevails, people's body language shifts subtly. The usual quiet becomes something deeper. Movements slow further. The stone-touching becomes more deliberate, both hands used, foreheads pressed to the rock in what looks unmistakably like prayer or deep communication. Here the face markings seem most concentrated — those who come to these chambers are more often marked, and marked more elaborately. Whether the marking is prerequisite for entering these spaces or whether those who value the marking are the ones who come here, the correlation is clear.

One person — gender unclear, age somewhere in late middle-years — appears to live primarily in the lower chambers. You see them there each time you visit, engaged in what looks like maintenance: tending the water-pools with particular care, scraping the bacterial mats in careful patterns that maintain the growth rather than simply harvesting, adjusting the bone structures that guide water-flow. They're marked completely: every visible bit of skin carries pattern, white and ochre and charcoal, overlapping designs that must be constantly renewed as skin sheds and regenerates. They acknowledge your presence with a gesture that might be welcome or might be simple acknowledgment, but they don't speak or guide, just continue their work while you observe.

The settlement's population includes several individuals who seem to bridge some gap you can't articulate. They move between groups, participate in various work-tasks, show up in different chambers with different people. They might be teachers, or mediators, or simply individuals with flexible roles in a community that values adaptive response to need. One such person — marked with a single spiral on the forehead, hair bound with bone and fiber — you see working with the stone-knapper one day, then harvesting mats another day, then in the children's communal sleeping area another time. They're competent at everything they touch, the kind of person who's learned broadly rather than deeply, useful in many contexts.

There's one individual you become convinced is avoiding you specifically. You catch glimpses in passages, turning away as you approach. A figure at the far end of chambers who departs as you enter. Whether they're disturbed by outside presence, or whether your observation is disruptive to their particular work or practice, they're staying clear of you with obvious intent. Your guide notices your noticing, and makes a gesture that seems to indicate — let them be, it's not personal, they have their reasons.

The people's relationship with the fauna is remarkable for its mutuality. The arthropods move through occupied spaces freely, and people step over or around them with care not to disturb. You see someone gently move a large arthropod from a working surface, hands cupping without grasping, relocating it to a nearby stone. The translucent salamanders climb over people occasionally, and the people remain still, let them pass, sometimes extend a finger for the salamander to investigate before it continues on. Only the fish in the pools receive what looks like hunting pressure, and even that's restrained, selective.

In one chamber you see what might be deliberate feeding of the fauna: an elder crumbling dried mat near a cluster of the smaller arthropods, who approach and seem to consume or at least investigate the offering. Whether this is practical symbiosis — keeping the arthropod population healthy because they serve some function — or spiritual practice, feeding the life you share space with, you can't determine. But it's done without fanfare, just another task in the day's cycle.

The young people doing the movement training all eventually show up in one particular chamber, not for training but in what looks like social gathering. They sit together, closer than the general population clusters, and engage in whispered conversation that's more animated than usual. They might be a cohort, bonded by shared practice, or they might be something like an initiate group, working toward some threshold they haven't yet crossed. They include the feather-marked young woman, the climbers, others you recognize from various chambers. They're all the right age for it to be generation-cohort, but the selection might be more specific than just age.

One very old person — their age is impossible to gauge, could be ninety or could be older than that seems possible — sits in one of the upper chambers, in a space that's markedly drier than most. They're engaged in the most delicate work you see in the settlement: stringing what look like microscopic shells or perhaps they're seeds onto fiber that's so fine it's almost invisible. The work requires perfect light and steady hands, and they have both, despite apparent great age. Their face is so creased it's impossible to read expression, and their body has the particular stillness of someone who's learned to conserve all energy for what matters. Someone brings them water and food periodically, evidence of care for someone whose working days should perhaps be finished, but they're clearly valued for this particular skill.

The children, for all their surface similarity to each other in behavior, show distinct personalities when you watch carefully. One girl is consistently the initiator of games, drawing others into activity. A boy perhaps six is notably gentle, always the one checking on younger children, guiding them through passages with patient hands. Another child is intensely focused on tool-making, spending hours in a corner working stone and bone in obvious imitation of adult craft. They're becoming themselves within the constraints and channels their culture provides, individual variations on a theme of human being shaped by this particular environment.

The people are not static display pieces. They're living lives — complex, meaningful lives with all the dimensions that implies. Affections and resentments. Tedium and satisfaction. Comfort and discomfort. You see a moment of obvious frustration when a bone-lattice framework collapses during reconstruction, the person working on it sitting back with a gesture that clearly means exasperation before beginning again. You see what looks like an argument, two people in intense whispered exchange that terminates with one walking away stiffly. You see a moment of apparent joy when the old man's carving reveals something satisfactory, his face creasing in what's unmistakably a smile.

They're adapted to their environment so completely they've become part of it, but they're not absorbed into it. They remain distinctly human, mammal, primate — the taxonomy that connects them to all humans. They've taken the template and shaped it to fit the Hollows, but the template persists. They love their children, you can see that in the gentle touches and careful teaching. They take satisfaction in work done well. They have social structures and hierarchies and bonds. They're recognizable even in their strangeness, or maybe the strangeness makes the recognition more poignant — these are people finding a way to be people in a place that seems to barely permit it.

And they'll continue after you leave, their quiet lives in the bright stone dampness, their constant adaptive flow through the chambers, their whispered conversations and careful work and moments of something that might be happiness or might be contentment or might be something your framework doesn't have good language for. The settlement persists. They persist. The success of the adaptation is that it continues, generation to generation, in the Living Hollows where the stone remembers light and the silence swallows all sound and the people have learned to be enough for themselves in a place that should perhaps be empty but is, instead, home.
