# What the Power Would Do

The power arrives at dusk—not as intrusion but as attention, the way sunlight arrives: present, offering, asking nothing.

It perceives them both: the chamber breathing below, the woman building above. It understands immediately what is happening here, recognizes the essential pattern: *someone is teaching the world a new way to be*.

The power does not interfere.

This is crucial, the first and most important choice. Kimi is not failing. The chamber is not broken. There is no disaster to prevent, no tragedy to avert. There is only a young woman doing impossible work with finite strength, limited time, mortal hands.

The power watches her that evening as she sits by her fire, rubbing coconut oil into her shoulders where they've begun to ache from carrying tiles. Watches her feed scraps to Diver, speak softly to him about water flow and spiral geometry. Watches her lie down on her mat and fall asleep almost instantly, the sleep of someone who has used every available hour of every available day.

It understands: she is burning bright and burning fast.

So the power makes small adjustments, the kind that preserve rather than alter, that extend rather than replace.

## The Changes

**For her body:** Nothing dramatic. No supernatural endurance, no elimination of fatigue. But the fish she catches tomorrow will have slightly more of the oils her joints need. The seaweed she gathers will happen to be particularly rich in minerals. The water she drinks from the spring will carry trace elements that support deep-diving physiology. Her body will receive exactly what it requires, delivered through natural means, nothing she could identify as intervention. Her knees will still ache—that's part of the work—but the ache won't progress to damage. The balance tips just slightly toward sustainability.

**For the chamber:** The power studies the tiles, the geometry, the problems Kimi hasn't yet encountered. It sees that three monsoons from now, a particular storm will churn sand into the lagoon, and the influx will bury the chamber's opening. Not deeply, but enough to disrupt the tidal flow, possibly enough to discourage her from continuing.

So the power adjusts, minutely, the contours of the lagoon floor nearby. A slight depression here, a small rise there. The sand will still come—the storm is part of the system, part of what should happen—but it will flow around the chamber rather than into it. Kimi will never know the danger she didn't face.

**For the clay:** She's right that the north bank has something special, a quality she can feel but not name. The power knows: it's a particular mix of minerals that will produce tiles of exceptional hardness, that will last not decades but centuries. The north bank is hard to reach, requires paddling against prevailing currents, and she's been putting it off.

Tomorrow, the wind will shift. Just for the morning, just enough. The paddle to the north bank will be easy. She'll arrive early, have extra time, be able to dig deeper than planned. She'll find clay that will change her work, and she'll think: *lucky timing*.

**For her teacher:** Small Crab is dead and the power cannot change that, would not change it even if it could—death is part of the pattern, part of what makes life precious. But the old woman left notebooks, observations about clay that she never shared because she didn't think they mattered. They're in a basket in her son's house, about to be thrown out because he doesn't recognize their value.

The power arranges for the son's daughter to find them first, to look through them, to think: *Kimi might want these*. The girl will bring them next month. The notebooks contain Small Crab's late thoughts about ceramics, questions she never answered, experiments she never tried. They contain exactly what Kimi will need when she reaches Chamber Five and encounters problems she can't yet imagine.

**For Toru:** He's good for her, patient and solid in ways that will matter more as the work intensifies. But he's considering taking a position on a trade vessel, leaving for a year, maybe longer. It's good money, a good opportunity. He's been talking himself into it, thinking Kimi doesn't really need him, that he's just peripheral to her real life.

The power doesn't change his mind—that's not the kind of thing power should touch. But it arranges for him to arrive at the beach tomorrow when Kimi is surfacing from a difficult dive, when her relief at being back in air makes her unguarded, when she'll see him and smile in a way that tells him what she hasn't said. He'll make his own choice then. The power just provides the moment that makes choice clear.

**For the island:** The lagoon reef is young, still establishing itself, vulnerable to disruptions that could cascade. The power looks ahead, sees stress points—coral bleaching events, storm damage, human impacts from traders who don't understand these systems. It cannot prevent these things, but it can strengthen resilience.

It adjusts the genetics of the coral fragments growing near the chamber, slightly, invisibly. Increases their heat tolerance by a fraction of a degree. Improves their ability to survive sediment stress. The changes are small enough to be natural variation, large enough to matter across decades. The reef will still face challenges, will still struggle, but it will more likely survive them. The chamber will more likely have context, will remain part of a living system rather than becoming an archaeological curiosity in a dead zone.

**For the work itself:** The power sees what Kimi is reaching toward, the thing she hasn't articulated. She's not just building chambers—she's learning to make spaces that enhance natural processes, that amplify rather than dominate. She's discovering, without words for it, a way of building that serves ecosystems rather than conquering them.

This matters. This pattern could spread, could inform how humans interact with coasts, with water, with the boundaries between elements. But only if the work survives, only if it's witnessed, only if others learn about it.

So the power ensures witnesses. It doesn't bring crowds—that would ruin everything—but it guides a few specific people to the lagoon over the next few years:

A navigator from the southern archipelago who understands water acoustics, who will hear the chamber singing and recognize what it means. He'll carry the story.

A scholar from the stone cities who studies ancient building techniques. She'll see what Kimi has done and document it, write it down in languages that will outlast memory.

A child from the village who will watch Kimi work and decide, years later, to build chambers of his own on a different island. He'll make different mistakes, discover different solutions, extend the pattern.

The power doesn't control these people, doesn't force them to come or determine what they'll do. It just makes sure they hear about the strange woman building rooms in the sea, makes sure their curiosity leads them here at times when they can actually understand what they're seeing.

## The Larger Pattern

The power recognizes that Kimi has touched something profound: she's building with the tide rather than against it, creating structures that incorporate natural rhythms instead of resisting them. This is rare. Most building is about control, domination, forcing stability onto dynamic systems.

Kimi is doing something else. She's making instruments for the sea to play, chambers that exist in partnership with water rather than defending against it. She probably doesn't think of it this way—she's just solving problems, following her vision—but she's demonstrating a principle that matters more than she knows.

The power wants this principle to spread, but it knows the only way that happens authentically is if Kimi continues doing her work exactly as she's doing it, driven by her own vision, making her own mistakes, earning her own discoveries.

So the power's interventions are all aimed at one thing: keeping her alive and able to work for as long as she needs. Not forever—she's mortal, and that's correct—but long enough to complete what she's started, long enough to teach what she knows, long enough to establish the pattern so others can continue it.

## What the Power Doesn't Do

It doesn't make the work easier. Kimi needs the difficulty—needs to struggle with weight and water and material limits. That's where the learning happens, where innovation emerges. Easy work would produce nothing of value.

It doesn't protect her from pain. The aching knees, the strained shoulders, the cuts and burns and exhaustion—these are honest costs of honest work. They're part of why the chamber means something, why it carries weight beyond aesthetics.

It doesn't ensure success. Chamber Four might fail. She might never achieve the perfect acoustic she's reaching for. The work might remain incomplete when she dies. These are real possibilities, and the power doesn't eliminate them. Meaning requires risk. Guarantee would hollow everything.

It doesn't make her famous or wealthy or comfortable. She might die unknown. The chambers might erode before anyone else sees them. Her name might be forgotten while the pattern she discovered survives without attribution. The power knows this is acceptable—the pattern matters more than credit.

It doesn't intervene in her relationships, her doubts, her choices about how to live. Kimi needs to be fully herself, fully human, making human decisions with human information. The power will not reduce her to a puppet serving grand design.

## The Gift

But there's one thing the power does that goes beyond preservation, that's perhaps indulgent but feels necessary:

The next time Kimi descends to the chamber—tomorrow, during the morning low tide—the light will be perfect. The angle of the sun, the clarity of the water, the precise tide level, the amount of stirred sediment, everything will align. The chamber will fill with light unlike anything she's seen before, light that makes every tile glow like it's illuminated from within, light that reveals the full beauty of what she's made.

She'll float there, holding her breath, seeing her work as it should be seen, as it could be seen, as it might be seen by future visitors if she continues building. She'll understand, in that moment, that she's not crazy, that the vision driving her is real, that the chambers aren't just possible but necessary.

The moment will last maybe thirty seconds before the light shifts, before the tide rises, before the ordinary returns. But thirty seconds will be enough. She'll carry that vision for the rest of her life, hold it through failures and doubts and the long work ahead.

When she surfaces, gasping, she'll try to describe it to Toru: "The light, it was—" and she won't find words. But her face will say what her voice can't, and he'll see her certainty, her certainty that this matters, that she's doing something real.

That's the power's gift: one moment of undeniable beauty, one glimpse of the chamber's full potential, one piece of evidence that her vision is true.

The rest—the years of work, the problems to solve, the chambers to build, the pattern to establish—that's hers. The power will keep her alive to do it, will smooth the roughest obstacles, will ensure witnesses, will protect the work from catastrophes she can't foresee.

But the work itself, the choosing, the creating, the becoming—that belongs entirely to Kimi.

She will build Chamber Four. She will solve the acoustic problem in Chamber Six. She will teach three apprentices before she's done, passing on not just techniques but approach, not just how to build chambers but how to think about building with water instead of against it.

She will live to be fifty-seven, which is long enough. She will never be wealthy or famous. She will die in her sleep, in her beach camp, with Diver-the-pig sleeping nearby and her last set of tiles drying in the sun.

And the chambers will breathe on.

That's what the power would do: almost nothing, and exactly enough.

It would honor her work by preserving her agency. It would love her by letting her be fully, completely herself—mortal, struggling, brilliant, and free.
