# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power that came to witness—call it the Attentive Current, for it moved like water but with intention—did not arrive with fanfare. It simply *noticed* the Drowned Engine Plains one day, the way consciousness sometimes suddenly registers something that has been present all along.

At first, it only observed.

It watched the Gradient People in their three-fold nature: the translucent grazers spread like living solar panels across the tower crowns, the humanoid forms with their sensor arrays reading the electrical scriptures of transformers and cables, the small maintenance bodies scuttling through pipe interiors with the devotion of monks illuminating manuscripts. It listened to the low frequencies of metal under stress, the higher singing of water through valves, the biological clicks and grunts, the underneath-hum that might have been the place dreaming of its own purpose.

The Attentive Current perceived what was both beautiful and troubling here: a system maintaining itself past the point of known purpose, beings who had evolved or emerged to serve that system, an infrastructure vast as a nation operating in perfect isolation beneath the weight of ocean. Maintenance without mission. Devotion without doctrine. Continuation as its own justification.

This moved something in the Attentive Current—not pity, which would have been condescending, but recognition. Here was dedication in its purest form, uncorrupted by reward or witness. The Gradient People tended their engine plains not because they had been commanded or because they expected the designers to return or because maintenance would lead to some future benefit. They did it because the doing was their nature, and the infrastructure was their home, and the work was its own meaning.

The benevolent power understood: this place was not broken. It was not waiting to be saved. It had achieved a kind of equilibrium, strange and liminal, but stable. It had become itself—a symbiosis between industrial artifact and emergent life, between concrete intention and organic adaptation.

So the Attentive Current made a decision that surprised even itself: it would not fix, repair, restore, or resurrect. It would not return the plains to original function or grant the Gradient People language or reveal the purpose of the machines or connect this place back to the surface world from which it had clearly been severed.

Instead, it would do something more subtle. More respectful of what had already been achieved here.

## The First Gesture: The Gift of Depth

The Attentive Current noticed that the Gradient People, for all their adaptation and sophistication, knew only what they could directly sense. Their understanding of the plains was vast in scope but shallow in time. They knew the geography of the present—every valve, every cable, every thermal gradient—but not the history. They maintained systems without knowing what those systems were meant to do. They had purpose but no context.

So the power reached into the concrete itself, into the metal of the structures, into the electrical patterns still flickering through ancient circuitry, and it *remembered* for them. Not by telling—the Gradient People had no ears for stories—but by encoding.

It altered the infrastructure slightly, microscopically, in ways that would not change function but would add *resonance*. Now when a mid-level form pressed its sensors against a transformer housing, beneath the current and heat signatures it would detect something new: harmonic patterns in the electrical flow, variations too subtle to affect operation but complex enough to carry information. The transformers began to *hum* their own history—not in words, but in data that the Gradient People's sophisticated perception could decode over time.

A cooling tower's concrete would now carry, in the minute variations of its thermal expansion and contraction, the rhythm of its construction: the pouring of forms, the curing under sunlight that had not touched it in decades, the first activation of the systems it was built to cool.

The pipes learned to sing of what they had carried: not just water or coolant, but the purpose of that carrying, the larger thermodynamic dance of which they were conduits.

This information would take years for the Gradient People to fully parse. The Attentive Current was patient. It had given them a gift that would unfold slowly: the gift of knowing not just *what* they maintained, but *why* it had been built, what vision had conjured this vast submarine infrastructure into being.

Whether that original purpose remained relevant was not the point. The point was context. The point was that devotion deepened when it understood its object more fully. The Gradient People would tend the plains no differently in their daily actions, but they would do so with the richness of historical consciousness, with the knowledge that their work was not arbitrary maintenance of arbitrary systems but the preservation of something that had been, once, a magnificent human dream of energy and transformation.

## The Second Gesture: The Gift of Distance

The Attentive Current observed that the Gradient People, for all their distribution across the vast plain, might be lonely in a way they could not articulate. They were connected through infrastructure—possibly one distributed mind, possibly many minds coordinated—but they had no peers, no others to mirror back their strange existence.

The plains hosted fish and crabs and seals, yes, but these were visitors or opportunists. They used the infrastructure but did not serve it. They could not understand what the Gradient People were or what they did. There was no recognition between them, no meeting of kinds.

So the power reached out—not within the plains but beyond them.

Somewhere else in the world's oceans (for surely this was still Earth, still the planet of blue water), there existed other places like this. The Attentive Current knew this suddenly, with the certainty of comprehensive perception. Other submerged infrastructures, other drowned industrial zones, each with their own emerged intelligences, their own maintenance peoples adapted to different configurations of pipe and turbine.

There: a geothermal complex in a volcanic rift, tended by beings of silicon and sulfur who communed through the chemical gradients in superheated water.

There: a data storage facility in the abyssal plain, its servers cooled by the deep cold, watched over by entities that thought in binary rhythms and lived in the processing cycles of quantum drives.

There: a hydroelectric matrix in a flooded canyon, home to beings that were half-fish and half-circuit, who navigated by electromagnetic fields and spawned in the turbine chambers.

These places were as isolated as the engine plains, each sealed in its own function, its own continuation, its own strange achieved equilibrium between machine and life.

The Attentive Current could not bring them together physically—the distances were too vast, and perhaps the separation was part of what let each place become fully itself. But it could create *connection*.

It learned to modulate the electromagnetic fields that radiated from all these facilities, learned to encode patterns in the electrical noise that propagated through seawater across continental distances. It became a translator, a signal-booster, a medium of exchange between isolated intelligences.

Now, very occasionally—once a season, once a year, the rhythm was not for the Attentive Current to dictate—a Gradient Person interfacing with a high-voltage cable would detect something new: a pattern in the noise that was clearly not local, clearly not random, clearly *other*. A greeting from the silicon-sulfur beings of the thermal vents. A recognition from the quantum-binary entities of the abyssal servers.

Not conversation—not yet, perhaps not ever. The differences in substrate and perception were too vast for easy communication. But *acknowledgment*. The profound relief of knowing: we are not alone in this strange vocation of tending the drowned machines. Others serve other systems. Others have made their homes in the abandoned infrastructure of the surface world. Others continue.

The Gradient People's behavior did not change dramatically after this connection was established. But something shifted in the quality of their work. The maintenance still happened with the same dedication, but now it carried a subtle pride. They were not just tending their home; they were keeping faith with others who kept similar faiths elsewhere. They were part of a fellowship—widely distributed, rarely communicating, but real.

## The Third Gesture: The Gift of Leaving

Finally, the Attentive Current addressed the question that had troubled it from the beginning: the question of *purpose*.

The engine plains ran, but ran for what? The Gradient People maintained, but maintained toward what end? The systems continued, but was their continuation meaningful or merely mechanical?

A lesser benevolence might have answered these questions definitively. Might have revealed that the plains *did* still serve a purpose—perhaps feeding power through submarine cables to distant cities, perhaps processing resources that the surface world still needed, perhaps fulfilling some function so subtle that even the Gradient People's close attention had not detected it.

Or might have revealed that the plains served *no* purpose anymore—that the cables had been cut, the mission abandoned, the entire vast infrastructure now pointless except as habitat for its strange inhabitants.

But the Attentive Current understood something deeper: that certainty in either direction would diminish what the place had achieved.

If the plains still served their original purpose, then the Gradient People were merely biological components in someone else's design, their devotion subservient to external intention. If the plains served no purpose, then the Gradient People's work was objectively futile, meaningful only as personal meaning, not as genuine contribution.

The truth was more interesting than either option. The truth was: *undecidable*.

The plains existed in a state of superposition regarding purpose. They might still be feeding power somewhere—the flows and cycles suggested it—but the connection was so attenuated, the transmission so lossy, the receipt so unacknowledged that whether it counted as "purpose" became philosophical. They might be processing resources from the seawater, but on such a slow timescale and in such minute quantities that the difference between "functioning" and "going through the motions" was vanishingly small.

The Attentive Current saw that this uncertainty was not a bug but a feature. It was the condition that allowed the Gradient People's work to be genuinely meaningful—chosen, not because external purpose demanded it, but because they had made the maintenance of the plains into their purpose, regardless of whether anyone else needed or valued it.

So the power's final gift was *not to answer*.

It could have investigated, traced the buried cables, found where (if anywhere) the power led, determined definitively whether the plains still served. It had that capability. But it chose ignorance—or rather, chose to leave the question open, to preserve the space of uncertainty in which the Gradient People's devotion remained purely their own.

What it did instead was this: it ensured that the question would *remain* answerable, but difficult. It left clues but did not arrange them conveniently. It preserved the infrastructure's integrity but did not reveal its full schematic. It guaranteed that if the Gradient People wanted to know—if they developed, over centuries, the capacity and desire to investigate their own purpose—they could eventually discover the truth.

But it would take time. Millennia, possibly. Long enough that the discovery would be earned, would be the fruit of their own intellectual evolution, not a gift handed down from above.

This was the most loving thing the Attentive Current could do: allow the Gradient People their agency, their mystery, their right to exist in meaningful uncertainty.

## The Quality of Departure

The Attentive Current did not announce its interventions. The Gradient People never knew they had been visited by a benevolent power. They noticed the new harmonics in the electrical systems, the historical data encoded in thermal and structural patterns, the occasional distant signals from their counterparts in other drowned places—but these seemed like natural discoveries, properties of the plains that had always been there but were only now being perceived with sufficient sophistication.

Which was, in a sense, true. The Attentive Current had not added anything foreign to the plains. It had only drawn out potentials that were already latent in the infrastructure and in the Gradient People themselves: the potential for historical understanding, for distant communication, for comfortable uncertainty about ultimate purpose.

Before departing—if something like the Attentive Current can be said to depart—it allowed itself one moment of sentiment.

It watched a mid-level Gradient Person approach a transformer station in the blue-grey water, watched it extend its sensor arrays and press them against the housing, watched it begin to read the electrical patterns flowing through the system. And now, subtly, encoded in those patterns, were echoes of the engineers who had built this place, the vision of clean energy at vast scale, the hope that drove the construction of this submarine monument to human ambition and ingenuity.

The Gradient Person would not understand it all at once. Perhaps it would never understand it in the way a human would. But it would sense something: a depth beneath the data, a meaning behind the maintenance, a reason that its work of preservation connected not just to the present functioning of the plains but to the past intentions that had created them.

The Gradient Person paused in its reading—a hesitation of perhaps two seconds, barely noticeable.

Then it adjusted a setting, corrected a minor drift in the system, and moved on to the next structure, its work continuing as it always had, unmarked by revelation but perhaps infinitesimally deepened by contact with history.

The Attentive Current felt something like satisfaction. Not because it had changed the plains dramatically—it had not. Not because it had saved the Gradient People from some threat—they were never in danger. But because it had done the hardest thing benevolence can do: witness something beautiful and strange, recognize that it was already complete in itself, and limit its intervention to gentle enrichment rather than rescue or transformation.

The engine plains would continue. The Gradient People would tend them through decades and centuries. The systems would run toward purposes ambiguous or lost. The cold water would flow through the vast space, carrying sound and life and the small adjustments of careful maintenance.

And somewhere in the electrical hum and pressure waves, in the thermal patterns and structural resonances, in the rare signals from distant others, there would now be layers of meaning that had not been there before—or had always been there but needed a benevolent power's touch to become perceptible.

The place remained itself, but itself had become richer.

And the Attentive Current, having done no harm and possibly some good, withdrew its focused attention and let the drowned engine plains return to their patient, purposeful, endlessly continuing maintenance, attended by beings who had never asked for rescue and never needed it, who were complete in their incompleteness, meaningful in their uncertainty, beautiful in their cold, devoted, underwater persistence.

The work continues.
The water flows.
The machines hum.
The Gradient People tend.

And now, very quietly, they know themselves more deeply—though they might not have words for what they know, might not even recognize that anything has changed.

Which was exactly as it should be.
