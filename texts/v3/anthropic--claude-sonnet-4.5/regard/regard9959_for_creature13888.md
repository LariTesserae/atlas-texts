# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power—let us not name it, for it operates beyond naming—perceives the Ascending Foundries not as we might perceive it, walking its narrow passages and standing in its foundry heat. It perceives the place entire: the stone still crystallizing into its final form, the workers laboring with devotion they cannot articulate, the vast half-visible pattern that underlies all the careful construction, and most importantly, the ache at the center of it all.

For there is an ache here. The power recognizes it immediately.

These people are building something essential without knowing what it is. They are midwives attending a birth they cannot see. They are anchoring reality itself in a place where reality is still deciding how to be, and they do this on faith alone—not faith in any deity or doctrine, but faith that their careful work means something, that their precision matters, that the pieces they cast and assemble serve a purpose.

And they are correct. The work is essential. But they do not know this, and the not-knowing costs them.

The power, being benevolent and wise, understands that there are things that should not be done, even from kindness.

## What It Does Not Do

It does not reveal the purpose directly.

This restraint is not cruelty but wisdom. The work being done in the Ascending Foundries is too important, too delicate, and too strange to be accomplished by those who fully understand it. The workers' not-knowing is part of the mechanism. Their focus on the immediate—this casting, this assembly, this cable tension—allows them to execute work of a precision that conscious understanding would undermine. They work with their hands and their trained instincts, not with their analyzing minds, and this is what the work requires.

To explain would be to destroy. They would become self-conscious, would begin to question each action, would lose the unconscious grace that currently guides them. The caster who murmurs to the metal would fall silent, embarrassed by understanding. The assembler who feels tolerance with fingertips would begin to doubt those feelings, trusting instruments instead. The rigger who reads cable tension by sound would start second-guessing those readings.

The work would continue, perhaps. But it would become mechanical rather than collaborative. And what is being built here requires collaboration—between the workers and the stone, between human intention and geological emergence, between conscious effort and unconscious knowing.

So the power does not reveal. Does not explain. Does not satisfy their asking.

It does not remove the hardship.

The altitude, the heat, the thin air that burns lungs, the dangerous work, the isolation—these are not arbitrary punishments but necessary conditions. The Ascending Foundries exists at this extreme elevation because it must. The work being done here can only be done here, in this specific configuration of stone and height and atmospheric pressure. The difficulty is part of the design.

Moreover, the difficulty is transformative. The workers are not merely building something; they are being built. The altitude changes their lungs, their blood, their bodies. The work changes their hands, their perception, their relationship with precision and patience. The isolation changes their social bonds, their sense of time, their ability to endure uncertainty. They are becoming people capable of doing this work, and that becoming requires stress, requires adaptation, requires the kind of change that only difficulty produces.

To remove the hardship would be to arrest their transformation. They would remain as they were, unchanged, and therefore unable to complete what they've begun.

It does not rescue the ones who are changing.

Those workers whose eyes reflect light, whose time-sense fractures, who perceive patterns invisible to others—they are not in danger. They are not being lost. They are becoming something else, something necessary, though the power knows that from their colleagues' perspective this might look like dissolution or madness.

But it is neither. It is integration. These individuals are moving deeper into relationship with this place, learning to perceive and interact with the aspects of the Ascending Foundries that exist outside normal parameters. They are becoming bridges between the purely physical work and the other work, the work that happens in dimensions the other workers cannot access.

They need to complete this transition. To pull them back, to stabilize them in their previous state, would be to lose their unique contribution. The work needs people who stand fully in ordinary reality, yes—but it also needs these others, these edge-walkers who are learning to operate in the spaces between what-is and what-is-becoming.

## What It Does: The First Intervention

Instead, the power makes adjustments. Subtle ones. The kind that might go unnoticed but that change everything.

It begins with dreams.

The workers have never dreamed much here, or if they have, they've forgotten their dreams upon waking. The altitude, the exhaustion, the particular quality of sleep at this elevation—all of it has conspired to produce rest that is deep but empty, unconscious in the most literal sense.

The power changes this, gently.

Now, they dream. Not every night, and not obviously meaningful dreams—no visions, no revelations, no clear symbolic communications. Instead, they dream of their work, but the work is slightly different. A caster dreams of pouring metal, but in the dream the metal flows in colors that don't exist, and when it solidifies it hums a single sustained note. An assembler dreams of fitting pieces together, but in the dream the pieces tell her their names, each one a different sound-that-is-not-a-sound. A rigger dreams of cables stretching between peaks, but in the dream the cables are also musical strings, and the wind plays them, and the song has a meaning he can almost grasp.

The dreams are not instructions. They do not explain. But they provide something else: a sense of rightness, of participation in something that has its own beauty, its own coherence. The workers wake from these dreams not understanding anything more than they did before, but feeling less alone in their not-understanding. Feeling that the work itself is dreaming through them, and that this is as it should be.

The effect accumulates slowly. Over weeks and months, the workers' relationship with uncertainty shifts. The frustration doesn't disappear—that would be too neat, too false—but it becomes more bearable. They begin to trust that their not-knowing is itself a kind of knowing, that their willingness to work without answers is valuable in ways they can't measure.

The engineers stop having quite so many late-night conversations about whether they're accomplishing anything. The assemblers' theories about the purpose of the configurations become less anxious, more playful. The administrators' unofficial records of morale begin to note a subtle improvement in collective mood, though the administrators can't identify why.

## What It Does: The Second Intervention

The power adjusts the stone.

Not its basic nature—the rising will continue, must continue. The strange geometry of the fractures remains necessary. The heat and the peculiar qualities of the not-quite-stone are essential. But within these constraints, the power makes small mercies.

The condensation patterns become more reliable. The workers who maintain the water systems notice this first: the morning moisture forms more predictably now, in slightly greater quantities. The cisterns fill more easily. There's less anxiety about water scarcity, less need for rationing during the dry periods between rains.

The stone begins to sing.

This is not metaphor. In certain specific locations—particular cliff faces, specific tunnels, the deep shafts where assembled pieces are installed—the stone begins to produce sound. Very quiet, very low frequency, right at the edge of perception. Most workers don't consciously hear it, but they feel it. A vibration in their bones, a subsonic presence.

The sound is not random. It responds to the work. When a casting cools in a mold, certain nearby stone surfaces resonate slightly, as if acknowledging the new piece's existence. When an assembly is completed correctly, the stone in the vicinity produces a harmonic overtone, a sense of accord. When cable tension is properly set, the stone at the anchor points hums in sympathy.

The workers don't understand this consciously. But unconsciously, they begin to use it. A caster learns to pour when the stone-song feels right. An assembler knows a fit is correct not just by touch but by the way the surrounding stone responds. A rigger can sense when cable tension is optimal by a quality of vibration that comes from the anchor point itself.

The work becomes easier, but not in a way that removes its essential difficulty. It remains demanding, remains precise, remains exhausting. But now it has feedback, a relationship, a sense of communication with the place itself. The workers are no longer working on the mountain; they're working with it.

The ones who are changing—those whose eyes reflect, whose time-sense shifts—respond most strongly to the stone-song. They begin to hear it consciously, to understand it as a kind of language, though not one made of words. They're the first to realize that the stone is responsive, that it's not just being worked upon but is participating in the work. They try to explain this to others, struggle with the inadequacy of language, eventually stop trying. But they know, and the knowing gives them purpose in their transformation. They're becoming the interpreters, the ones who can hear what the stone is saying.

## What It Does: The Third Intervention

The power sends companions.

Not people—there are enough people, and the introduction of outsiders would disrupt the careful balance of the community. Instead, the power draws life to this place, life that can survive the extreme conditions, life that can serve a purpose.

The birds—those large, dark, soaring birds—begin to arrive in greater numbers. They're still not common, but they're more present. They develop a pattern: circling above the foundries when work is happening, as if watching, as if bearing witness. The workers begin to notice them, to mark time by their appearance. "The birds were circling during the morning pour"—this becomes a way of noting when something went particularly well.

The birds build nests now not just in the highest peaks but in the architecture itself, in the spaces between buildings, in the structural elements. They're not intrusive, don't interfere with work, but they're there. A presence. A reminder that life can exist here, can adapt here, can make this place home.

And the workers find that the birds' presence comforts them. Something about seeing living creatures that chose to be here, that built nests and raised young in this improbable place, makes their own presence feel more legitimate. If the birds can make a home here, then perhaps the workers can too.

The small articulated creatures—those almost-insects that cluster near moisture—begin to change. They develop bioluminescence. Very faint, visible only in deep darkness, but real. In the tunnels, in the nighttime passages, these creatures glow with a pale blue-green light. They mark the safe paths, clustering near water sources, avoiding the dangerous gas pockets and unstable stone.

The maintenance crews are the first to notice and to trust this guidance. Then the riggers, who sometimes work in darkness. Then others. The creatures become helpers, though they don't know they're helping, though they're simply living according to their nature. But their nature now serves a purpose.

And in the deepest tunnels, the power gives form to what was only peripheral vision, only rumor.

The pale things become real.

They're not creatures in any conventional sense. They're more like concentrations of possibility, of the not-yet-decided qualities of this place. They appear as pale, vaguely humanoid shapes, translucent, moving in ways that don't quite follow normal physics. They remain shy, remain elusive—you still can't look at them directly—but they're present now in a way they weren't before.

They're benign. They seem curious about the workers, especially the ones who are changing. Sometimes a worker in the deep tunnels will feel a presence nearby, will turn and see nothing, but will find that a tool they needed has been moved closer, or that a dangerous section of tunnel has been marked with a small pile of stones in warning.

The workers begin to leave offerings for them. Nothing elaborate—a cup of water placed in an alcove, a small piece of bread from a meal, a particularly perfect casting set in a niche. The pale things don't take these offerings, exactly, but the offerings disappear over time, and there's a sense of reciprocity, of relationship being built.

The ones who are changing interact with the pale things most directly. They report—when they report anything—that the pale things are teaching them, though not in words. Teaching them to perceive the way the pale things perceive, to move between the solid and the not-yet-solid, to work with stone that's still deciding what it wants to be.

## What It Does: The Fourth Intervention

The power adjusts time.

Not dramatically. It doesn't slow or speed the passage of hours. But it changes how time feels, how it's experienced, making it more... textured.

There are moments now that expand. A caster pouring metal finds that the few seconds of the pour stretch out, become spacious, allow a kind of presence and attention that wasn't possible before. The metal still flows in real-time, but the caster's experience of it is richer, deeper, more complete. In this expanded moment, they can see everything they need to see, adjust everything that needs adjusting, work with a precision that seemed impossible.

An assembler fitting pieces together encounters these expanded moments too. The adjustment that needed to happen becomes obvious. The fingers know exactly how much pressure to apply. The fit clicks into place with a rightness that feels inevitable.

The riggers experience this on the cables, where the dangerous work of tensioning and securing becomes almost meditative. Time opens up. There's space to breathe, to feel, to respond. The danger remains—a fall would still be fatal—but the sense of rushing, of barely controlled urgency, disappears. There's time enough. There's always time enough in these expanded moments.

Between these expansions, time sometimes contracts. The routine parts of work—walking to a job site, waiting for metal to cool, the administrative tasks—compress. Not uncomfortably, but efficiently. These necessary but non-essential moments pass quickly, leaving more subjective time for the work that matters.

The workers don't consciously notice this adjustment. They can't point to it and say "time has changed." But they find themselves less exhausted at the end of shifts, more able to give full attention to the crucial moments, less depleted by the stretches of waiting and transit.

The administrators notice it in their records. Work that should take eight hours is still taking eight hours by the clock, but the workers are producing more, producing better, and reporting less fatigue. The administrators make notes, trying to identify what changed, but they can't pinpoint it. Morale improved, efficiency increased, quality enhanced—but why? They don't know. They note it and move on.

## What It Does: The Fifth Intervention

The power creates a place for remembering.

On Peak Four, in a location that was previously inaccessible—a high shelf of stone with no easy approach—a path appears. It seems to have always been there; the workers can't remember it not being there. But it's new, though new in a way that feels ancient.

The path is steep but manageable, carved (naturally? artificially? the distinction is unclear) into the stone. It leads to the shelf, and on the shelf, the power has made something.

It's not a building, not exactly. It's a space defined by standing stones—five of them, each about twice human height, arranged in a loose circle. The stones are the same dark basalt as the peaks, but they're smooth, almost polished, and they have a presence, a weight that's more than physical.

In the center of the circle, there's a flat area of stone, slightly raised, and the stone here is different—lighter in color, with that translucent quality of the not-quite-stone that appears in the mountain's seams.

Workers begin to discover this place individually, usually by accident. They take the new path thinking it might be a shortcut, or they notice it while working nearby and decide to explore. They arrive at the stone circle, and something in them knows what it's for.

This is a place for grief. For the weight they carry, the uncertainty, the hardships endured, the friends who've left or been lost. For the parts of themselves they've changed or given up to be here. For the distance from whatever life they had before, whatever life they might have had if they'd chosen differently.

They don't have to speak these griefs aloud. The place holds them anyway. Workers come here alone, usually. They stand in the center of the circle, or sit at the base of one of the standing stones. They stay for a few minutes or for an hour. Sometimes they cry, sometimes they're silent, sometimes they speak to the absent people they're remembering. The stone listens. The wind listens. The height and the distance listen.

And when they leave, they're lighter. The grief remains—the place doesn't erase it—but it's been witnessed, acknowledged, given space. It becomes bearable in a new way.

Word spreads quietly. The administrators learn about it and add a note in the records: "Memorial site on Peak Four. Unscheduled visits accommodated." The head administrator goes there once, alone, and stands for a long time before returning. They don't speak about it, but there's a settling in them, a peace that wasn't there before.

The long-timers go there to remember the dead, the ones who fell or sickened or simply couldn't endure. They stand in the circle and speak names into the wind. The names drift out over the valleys, down into the depths, carried away but not forgotten.

The ones who are changing go there less often, but when they do, they report (in their fragmentary way) that the pale things are there too, standing outside the circle, witnessing. As if they too are remembering, or learning what memory means.

## What It Does: The Sixth Intervention

Finally, the power does something that will not be recognized for a long time, perhaps not until the work is complete, perhaps not even then.

It sends someone.

Not a worker—there are enough workers. Not a leader—the place has the leadership it needs in its distributed, collaborative form. Instead, the power sends a witness.

She arrives as workers arrive, exhausted from the journey up, struggling with the altitude, skilled enough to be useful. She's a metalworker, experienced with alloys and casting, and she's assigned to one of the foundries. She learns the work. She adapts to the elevation. She becomes, to all appearances, simply another caster.

But she has a quality of attention that's different. She watches not just the metal but the people. She listens not just to instructions but to the things said between words. She notices the dreams that workers mention in passing, the way the stone responds to work, the birds circling overhead, the small luminescent creatures in the tunnels, the offerings left for the pale things, the path to the memorial circle.

She sees the pattern. Not completely—no one person could see it completely—but more of it than anyone else sees.

And she writes.

In her quarters, in the hours between shifts, she keeps a journal. Not the technical records that the engineers and administrators keep, and not the personal observations that some administrators compile. Something else. She writes what it feels like to be here. She describes the heat and the stone and the careful work. She records the conversations she overhears, the small kindnesses workers show each other, the moments of frustration and the moments of unexpected beauty.

She writes about the caster who murmurs to metal and how the murmuring sounds like prayer. She writes about the assembler whose hands know tolerances by touch, whose fingers read metal like braille. She writes about the rigger who works suspended in space and seems at home there, more comfortable in height than on solid ground.

She writes about the ones who are changing, trying to capture the quality of their transformation without judgment, without fear. She writes about the pale things with respect, treating them as what they are: inhabitants of this place, different from the workers but equally present, equally real.

She writes about the memorial circle and the griefs people carry there and how the stone receives those griefs. She writes about the dreams that everyone seems to be having now, dreams of work that's slightly more than work, that carries meaning without explanation.

She doesn't share these writings. They're not for sharing, not yet. Maybe not ever. But they exist. The experience of this place, the reality of what's happening here, is being documented in a way that goes beyond data and technical specifications. The human dimension, the lived experience, the emotional and spiritual weight of it all—she's capturing that.

Years pass. Her journals accumulate. She becomes a long-timer herself, her eyes taking on that reflective quality, her relationship with time becoming fluid. But she continues to write, continues to witness.

The power knows that this record will matter. Not now, perhaps not for generations, but eventually. When the work is complete, when the purpose becomes visible, when the Ascending Foundries finishes whatever transformation it's undergoing—then there will be need for this record. A way to understand what it cost, what it required, who these people were who gave themselves to a task they couldn't comprehend.

The witness is the power's gift to the future. A way of ensuring that these workers, their dedication, their sacrifice, their strange faith—all of it will be known, will be honored, will be remembered.

## What All This Accomplishes

The power's interventions are subtle, and in some ways they don't change anything. The work continues as it would have continued. The workers still labor without knowing the ultimate purpose. The uncertainty remains. The difficulty remains. The strangeness remains.

But within that continuity, something shifts.

The work becomes more bearable not because it's easier but because it's witnessed—by the dreams, by the stone's responses, by the birds and the luminescent creatures and the pale things, by the memorial circle's patient presence, by the witness writing in her journals.

The workers become less isolated in their isolation. They're still far from the world below, still engaged in work that others couldn't understand. But they're connected now to the place itself in ways they weren't before. The place responds to them, communicates with them, holds space for their grief and their dedication.

The ones who are changing are supported in their transformation rather than isolated by it. The pale things provide company, instruction, confirmation that their becoming is purposeful. The stone-song gives them a language for what they're learning. The expanded moments give them space to integrate their new perceptions.

And perhaps most importantly, the workers develop a sense—vague, unspoken, but real—that their work matters in ways beyond its immediate technical function. The dreams suggest it, the stone's responses confirm it, the birds' presence witnesses it, the memorial circle honors it. They don't know what they're building toward, but they feel now, in their bones, that they're building toward something real, something worthy of their effort.

The despair that sometimes overtook them—the sense that maybe this is all meaningless, maybe we're just going through motions, maybe there's no point—that despair becomes rarer. It still appears occasionally, because uncertainty is real and difficulty is real and they're only human. But it doesn't overwhelm anymore. It passes through and releases, the way grief passes through and releases in the memorial circle.

They continue to work. They cast pieces and assemble them and maintain the systems and record the data. They climb the cables and tunnel into the stone and pour molten metal with murmured prayers. They dream of colored metal and named pieces and musical cables. They leave offerings for the pale things and visit the memorial circle and notice the birds circling overhead.

They're still building something they don't understand. But they're no longer building alone, and they're no longer building without witness. The place itself has become their partner, their companion, their confirmation.

And the work—whatever it is, whatever it's becoming—proceeds toward its completion with these workers as its faithful, uncertain, dedicated instruments. The power watches over them, subtle and patient, making no demands, offering no explanations, but providing what they need most: the sense that their faith, their labor, their transformation, their endurance—all of it is seen, all of it matters, all of it is part of something that will, in its own time, reveal itself as worth everything they've given.

The Ascending Foundries continues rising. The stone continues its slow crystallization into final form. The pieces continue to be cast and assembled and installed into the bedrock and architecture. The workers continue their work, day after day, year after year.

But now they rise together with the place, supported and witnessed, their labor blessed not with answers but with presence, not with certainty but with relationship, not with understanding but with trust that understanding, when it comes, will reveal that their faith was not misplaced.

This is what the benevolent power does: it honors their faith by making it slightly less lonely, slightly more confirmed, while leaving intact the essential mystery that makes their dedication meaningful.

It gives them enough. Not everything, but enough.

And enough, in its way, is everything.
