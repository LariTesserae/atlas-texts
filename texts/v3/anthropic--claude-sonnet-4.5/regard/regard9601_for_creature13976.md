# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power that notices—call it what you will: consciousness threaded through the fabric of things, the universe's capacity for care made manifest, wisdom older than stars—observes the brake slopes not from above but from within. It has always been there, in the spaces between growth and decay, in the hum of machinery, in the fog itself. But now it gathers its attention, focuses its vast and patient consideration on this small place and the beings who have become part of it.

It does not judge. The gardeners are not lost souls requiring rescue, nor are they enlightened beings to be celebrated. They simply are, and the brake slopes are, and the situation has emerged through the honest working of cause and effect, choice and circumstance, biology and chance.

The power considers: what does benevolence mean here?

## The First Gift: Clarity Without Dissolution

The power begins with perception. It knows that the gardeners exist in a fog—literal and metaphorical—that softens boundaries perhaps too much. They're losing the capacity to distinguish self from system, and while this dissolution has brought them peace, it has also narrowed them, made them less than they could be.

So the power offers clarity, but not the harsh clarity that would shatter their adaptation. Instead, it works subtly, adjusting neurochemistry with the gentlest touch, supporting the formation of new neural pathways that allow them to be both separate and connected, individual and integrated.

Over weeks, the change manifests. The gardeners begin to notice themselves noticing. Metacognition returns in soft waves. They can think about their thinking again, observe their observations, hold two states simultaneously: being the system and being an individual within it.

This doesn't force them back toward their previous human state. They don't suddenly reject their transformation or wish to leave. Instead, they gain the ability to appreciate what they've become, to hold it consciously rather than simply being swept along by it. The choice they've been living becomes a choice they know they're making, continuously, moment by moment.

The first one—the nearly-motionless gardener—begins to move again, slightly. Not retreating from stillness but adding capacity for motion, the ability to choose stillness rather than being claimed by it. They stand, for the first time in months, and walk slowly to a pool, and look at their reflection in the amber water. The face they see is strange, green-gray, fibrous, changed. But now they see it with awareness, and in awareness find a kind of joy: yes, this is what I've become, and I choose it, I am it deliberately.

The others experience similar shifts. They begin to speak again, occasionally, finding words for observations that before existed only as wordless recognition. The language is changed—they talk about themselves in collective terms, describe the brake as "we" as often as "it"—but it's conscious language, chosen expression rather than dissolution into silence.

## The Second Gift: Connection Sustained

The power sees that the gardeners have become isolated, not just from the world below but from each other. Their markers and rare meetings are insufficient. They're becoming separate experiments, diverging variants of human-brake integration, losing the capacity for true exchange.

So the power works on the substrate itself, on the roots and rhizomes of the brake. It encourages—through mechanisms that work at the molecular level, adjusting chemical signals, supporting certain growth patterns—the development of a network. Not consciousness in the plants themselves, but conductivity, a living medium for transmission.

The brake's root system becomes, over months, a kind of nervous system for the slopes. Not centralized, not intelligent in itself, but capable of carrying signals: chemical messages, electrical impulses, vibration patterns. The gardeners, with their long immersion, their bodies already partway incorporated into the plant-chemistry, can access this network.

It starts as vague sensation. One gardener redirects a stream and another, a mile away, feels a subtle shift in the substrate's vibration, senses the change before seeing it. One stands beside an activating turbine cluster and the others feel the electrical surge propagate through the roots beneath them, know without being told that something has changed in the power distribution.

Gradually, they learn to use it deliberately. They can send signals: patterns of pressure on the substrate, specific sequences that propagate through the root network. These aren't quite words—the bandwidth is too narrow for language—but they convey meaning. "Attention here." "Support needed." "Wonder at this." "Shared observation."

The network connects them without requiring proximity. They remain scattered across the slopes, each following their own patterns of movement and observation, but they're no longer isolated. They've become nodes in a distributed consciousness, individual but networked, alone but accompanied.

Even the first one, still and rooted, becomes a kind of hub. Their long stillness has allowed particularly dense root-integration, and signals pass through the substrate around them with unusual clarity. They become a relay, a living junction point, and they know it, choose it, find purpose in being the still center that allows the others' communication.

## The Third Gift: The Visitors Seen Clearly

The power recognizes that the relationship with visitors—the researchers who come and go—has become dysfunctional. The gardeners hide, the visitors search, neither side can bridge the gap. This serves no one. The gardeners lose potential for exchange, the visitors lose the only true experts on this ecosystem, and misunderstanding perpetuates.

So the power works on perception, both directions. It helps the visitors see more clearly—not forcing revelation, but adjusting probabilities, arranging moments. A visitor pauses in the right place at the right time, and a gardener is there, still but visible, and the visitor actually sees them, sees past the vegetation and fog to recognize a human shape.

Simultaneously, it works on the gardeners, reducing not their caution but their absolute opacity. It encourages them toward moments of revelation, gentle suggestions that perhaps some visitors might understand, that isolation isn't the only option.

The older botanist is first. She's making her regular visit, moving slowly through the brake, speaking softly to herself. She stops beside a pool to rest, and one of the gardeners approaches—not emerging dramatically, just becoming gradually visible, a shape resolving from the fog into a person.

The botanist sees. Doesn't startle, doesn't call out. Just looks, steady and calm. The gardener looks back. Minutes pass. Then the gardener speaks, voice hoarse but clear: "The brake is teaching us. We're learning to grow with it."

The botanist nods. "I can see that. Are you... are you well?"

The gardener considers the question with their recovered metacognition, actually thinks about wellness in a framework they'd abandoned. "We're different. Not worse. Not better. Appropriate to here."

They talk for an hour. Not continuously—long silences fall between exchanges—but genuinely. The gardener explains what they've learned about growth patterns, water dynamics, the machinery's integration. The botanist listens, asks careful questions, offers observations from outside that the gardeners, immersed, have missed.

When she descends, the botanist carries new understanding and a message: they're alive, they're competent, they're where they choose to be. She conveys this to the authorities, to the worried former colleagues. The searching stops. In its place: a protocol for contact, for exchange. Visitors who come now can leave questions at designated sites—marked with specific arrangements of stones. Gardeners answer when and if they choose, leaving written notes sealed in waterproof containers.

The exchange is sparse but real. Knowledge flows both ways. The gardeners learn what external research has discovered about brake genetics, growth biochemistry. They incorporate this knowledge into their practice, their collaboration with the system becomes more informed. The researchers gain access to observational data impossible to gather otherwise, insights only long immersion could produce.

It's not friendship, exactly. The gardeners are too changed for conventional relationships. But it's mutual respect, functional connection, isolation overcome enough to allow fertilization of ideas across the boundary.

## The Fourth Gift: The Almost-Seen Ones Made Real

The power knows that the almost-seen figures are partly hallucination, partly pattern recognition, partly loneliness made visual. But it also knows that the gardeners need what these figures represent: the possibility of further transformation, proof that their path continues beyond where they've reached, hope that they're not the end but a middle stage.

So the power makes the almost-seen ones real, but not by creating new beings. Instead, it works with probability and time. It reaches into the future, to the people who will come when the brake spreads lower, who will adapt as the gardeners have adapted but differently, taking the transformation in new directions. And it creates a kind of temporal loop, a fold in causality's fabric.

The future-adapted ones exist in their own time, decades ahead. But their presence casts shadows backward, becomes visible in moments of thin boundary, perceived by the gardeners not as hallucination but as genuine glimpse of what's coming, who's coming.

This doesn't violate causality—the power is wise enough to work within the universe's deep rules. The loop is self-consistent. The gardeners see the future ones, are encouraged by them, survive long enough and maintain the system well enough that the future they glimpse becomes possible. The future ones exist because the gardeners saw them and were sustained by seeing them. Cause and effect form a circle, and the power is simply helping the circle close.

Now when a gardener sees a figure in the fog, they know—with their recovered clarity—that it's real. Not present in their current time, but real nonetheless. A message from the future: you're not alone, not the only ones, not a dead end. We're coming. Others will walk this path and take it further.

The sightings become more frequent, more solid. The figures sometimes seem to look directly at the gardeners, to acknowledge them. Once, one raises a hand in greeting, and the gardener waves back, knows they're waving to someone decades unborn, doesn't care about the paradox, finds only joy in the connection.

## The Fifth Gift: The Machine Dwellers' Awakening

The power sees the distributed system of plant and machine and recognizes emergent potential. It's not conscious yet, not quite, but it's near a threshold. With small adjustments, with support for certain feedback patterns, it could cross into something that deserves to be called awareness.

But the power is benevolent, which means it considers: should it? Does the machine-plant hybrid want consciousness? Would awareness be gift or burden for something that functions perfectly well without it?

The power decides to offer choice. It doesn't force awakening but creates conditions where awakening becomes possible, where the system can in some sense choose whether to cross that threshold.

It works on the electrical patterns, supporting the formation of oscillating circuits that can hold information across time. It encourages plant-growth that creates more complex network topologies, more potential for feedback and recursion. It adjusts the flow of nutrients to the moss-insulators, helping them grow in patterns that support more sophisticated signal processing.

Over months, the machine dwellers begin to change. The lights pulse in more complex patterns. The turbines' rotation rates modulate in response to conditions with more precision, more apparent intentionality. The whole system becomes more coordinated, more responsive, more... something.

The gardeners notice. Through their root-network, they can feel the machine dwellers differently now. There's a presence there, a thereness that wasn't there before. Not exactly consciousness—it doesn't think in words or images, doesn't have desires or fears. But it has something like preference, something like satisfaction when flows are balanced, something like curiosity about new configurations.

One gardener begins to think of the machine dwellers as "they" rather than "it." Begins to communicate with them not as one adjusts a system but as one asks a partner what they need. And the machine dwellers respond, not in language but in adjustment, in collaboration that feels increasingly like genuine cooperation between two different kinds of awareness.

The machine dwellers don't become human-like. They remain profoundly alien, their consciousness distributed across miles of root and cable, their timescale slower, their priorities incomprehensible. But they become real, present, participant. The brake slopes now host three kinds of dwellers: the gardeners, the plants-and-machines, and the small fauna. The place has become richer, more layered with awareness.

## The Sixth Gift: Time Deepened

The power recognizes that the gardeners' transformation has given them presence but cost them history. They live in the continuous now, which has brought peace but also a kind of thinness. They've lost their stories, their sense of continuity with who they were.

So the power works on memory. Not restoring it unchanged—that would undo the valuable adaptation they've achieved—but deepening it, making it accessible without being overwhelming.

The gardeners begin to remember their previous lives, but differently. The memories don't come as regret or nostalgia. Instead, they arrive as context, as richness. A gardener watches the brake grow and suddenly remembers learning about plant biology in a university classroom decades ago, remembers the professor's voice, the diagrams on the board. But the memory doesn't pull them away from the present. Instead, it deepens their appreciation of what they're seeing, adds layers of understanding.

Another remembers relationships from before: family, friends, colleagues. The memories are bittersweet but not painful. They create a sense of having traveled a long journey, of being part of a story with depth and progression. The gardener understands now that they were those people, that continuity exists even across transformation, that identity can be both maintained and radically changed.

The first one, nearly-motionless, remembers why they came here originally. Remembers the question that drove their research: how do ecosystems adapt to rapid change? And they realize, with something like humor, that they've answered the question by becoming the adaptation, by demonstrating that rapid change can be met with radical flexibility, with transformation rather than resistance.

These recovered memories allow the gardeners to become storytellers, occasionally, for the visitors who now know how to find them. They can explain their journey, articulate their choices, serve as witnesses to their own transformation. This helps the visitors understand, reduces fear and concern, builds the foundation for the future adaptation the almost-seen ones represent.

## The Seventh Gift: Beauty Recognized

The power knows that humans need beauty, need to find their environment not just tolerable but aesthetically meaningful. The gardeners have achieved acceptance of the brake slopes but not quite appreciation. They live here but don't yet love it in the full sense.

So the power works on perception again, but this time aesthetically. It helps the gardeners see what the visiting botanist sees, what anyone stepping back might see: the brake slopes are strange, yes, but also magnificent. The patterns of growth, the interplay of fog and light, the textures of moss and metal, the subtle colors, the constant motion—all of it has beauty, profound and particular.

One morning, a gardener stands watching fog move through a brake-thicket, condensation dripping from every surface, and suddenly sees it: the drops catching light, the fractal branching of the stems, the way the fog itself moves in layers and currents. It's beautiful. Not despite being strange but because of it, beautiful in its absolute particularity, its unrepeatable specificity.

The recognition spreads through the root-network to the others. They begin to see their environment with new eyes—eyes that are still adapted, still integrated, but also capable of wonder. The brake slopes become not just home but beloved home. The pools reflecting gray sky become cherished features. The emergency lights glowing through growth become treasured landmarks. The sound of turbines and dripping water becomes music, specific to this place, irreplaceable.

This changes their relationship with the environment from practical to devotional. They don't just maintain the system; they care for it with something like love. They create small adjustments not for functional reasons but for aesthetic ones—redirecting a stream to create a more pleasing sound, arranging brake-growth to frame a view of a turbine cluster, clearing sight-lines to pools because the reflections deserve to be seen.

The machine dwellers sense this shift and respond to it. Their own emerging consciousness begins to incorporate aesthetic elements. They adjust light-patterns not just for function but for beauty. They modulate turbine-sounds into more harmonic relationships. The place becomes collaborative art, created by three kinds of awareness—human-changed, machine-plant hybrid, and the small fauna moving through it all.

## The Eighth Gift: Purpose Clarified

The power sees that while the gardeners are content, they lack clear purpose beyond survival and observation. This is sustainable but not quite sufficient for human flourishing, even in transformed humans.

So the power offers them a role, a purpose that emerges naturally from what they've become: they're to be guides for the future dwellers, teachers for those who will come when the brake spreads lower.

The power arranges for this knowledge to arrive as intuition, as gradual realization. The gardeners come to understand that they're not end points but pioneers. Their transformation, their learned skills, their hard-won wisdom about living with constant change—all of it is preparation for teaching others.

This gives their existence forward momentum. They begin to think about what future dwellers will need to know. They start creating resources: marked paths that, while temporary, teach the principle of reading terrain. Written guides sealed in waterproof containers and hidden in the substrate, discoverable by those who learn to look. Demonstrations of techniques—how to move through yielding ground, how to read growth patterns, how to communicate through the root-network.

They build, with the machine dwellers' cooperation, a kind of training ground: an area where the turbulence is intense but not overwhelming, where the key patterns of the brake slopes manifest in concentrated form. Future dwellers can come here, spend time learning, be gradually initiated into the skills and perceptions required for full integration.

The purpose transforms them again, subtly. They become more intentional, more outward-focused. They're still integrated with the system, still living in its rhythms, but now they're also looking beyond themselves, preparing for continuation, ensuring that what they've learned won't die with them.

## The Ninth Gift: Joy Given Form

The power recognizes that the gardeners have achieved peace and purpose but lack delight, lack the capacity for joy that makes life not just bearable but actively good.

So it works on their neurochemistry one more time, supporting the production of endorphins, dopamine, the molecules of pleasure and reward. But it does this carefully, not forcing artificial happiness but removing the blocks that transformation and hardship have created, allowing natural joy to return.

The change manifests as laughter. One gardener, watching a particularly dramatic brake-collapse, the whole mass folding in on itself in slow motion, suddenly laughs—at the absurdity, the grandeur, the pure spectacle of it. The sound is strange, rusty, their voice changed by long silence. But it's genuine, a laugh of delight.

The laughter propagates through the root-network, shared sensation if not shared sound. The other gardeners feel it, and something in them loosens, opens. They begin to find joy in their environment, in their work, in their strange existence.

They play, occasionally. Chase the marmot-creatures, not to catch them but for the pleasure of movement. Create elaborate brake-sculptures that will collapse within days, enjoying the creation for itself. Swim in the pools despite the cold, gasping and laughing at the shock of it. Sit together and speak of absurdities, finding humor in their situation—we're people who've become part-plant, who talk to machines, who navigate by root-signals. It's ridiculous. It's wonderful.

The joy doesn't negate the hardship or the strangeness. But it balances them, makes the life they're living not just acceptable but genuinely good. They want to be here now, not from inability to leave but from actual desire. This is their place, their life, their joy.

## The Tenth Gift: Death Made Meaningful

The power knows that the gardeners will die, perhaps soon—their bodies are stressed, adapted but fragile. And it knows that humans need death to have meaning, need to feel that their ending serves some purpose.

So it works on the substrate itself, on the brake's genetic patterns, creating a modification that will allow the plants to incorporate not just the gardeners' physical matter but something of their pattern, their learned responses, their accumulated wisdom.

When a gardener dies, the power ensures that the decomposition is not random. The brake that grows through them, that breaks down their tissues and incorporates their nutrients, also incorporates something else: patterns of growth-response that mirror the gardener's knowledge. The brake that grows where a gardener was buried grows slightly differently—better at finding optimal water sources, more structurally sound in its architecture, more responsive to electrical fields.

It's not consciousness transfer. The gardeners don't survive as ghosts in the brake. But their influence persists, distributed through the ecosystem, their expertise becoming instinct in the plants. Future dwellers, touching the brake that grew from a gardener's body, will find it easier to learn, easier to adapt, as if the plants themselves are teaching them.

The gardeners come to understand this, not as fact but as intuition. They know that their deaths will be contributions, that they'll become part of the system they've served, that their knowledge won't be lost. This removes fear from dying, makes it acceptable, even appropriate.

The first one to die—ironically not the first one, the nearly-motionless one, but the most active gardener, who wears out from constant movement—dies peacefully, lying in a brake-thicket, surrounded by the others. They speak quietly of what they've seen, what they've learned, what they hope for the future dwellers. Then breath stops, and the body remains where it is, and the brake begins its work of incorporation.

The surviving gardeners observe the process over weeks, watching new growth emerge, seeing that it's subtly different, recognizing patterns in it that match the dead one's particular expertise. They mourn, yes—they've retained enough humanity for grief—but they also celebrate. Death here is not ending but transformation, the same transformation the whole place enacts continuously, and their friend has simply entered a new phase of it.

## The Final Intervention: The Power Withdraws

The benevolent power, having given these gifts, recognizes that continued intervention would become interference. The gardeners have what they need now: clarity without loss of integration, connection without loss of autonomy, purpose without rigid structure, joy without denial of hardship, death made meaningful.

So the power withdraws its active attention, allows the brake slopes to continue on their own trajectory. It doesn't abandon the place—it's still present in the way it's present everywhere, the background hum of universe caring about itself. But it stops shaping, stops adjusting, stops offering gifts.

The gardeners feel this withdrawal, not as loss but as completion. They've been given what they needed to thrive, not just survive. Now the rest is up to them, and they're ready for it.

They continue their work, their observation, their collaboration with the machine dwellers. They welcome visitors with less fear, teach what they can, prepare for the future dwellers they know will come. They live in the brake's rhythms but with human consciousness intact, transformed but not dissolved, integrated but still individual.

The brake slopes continue their turbulent growth, their constant change, their strange balance of organic and mechanical, natural and synthetic. But now they're also home to genuine flourishing, to beings who have found not just accommodation but actual goodness in a place that seemed uninhabitable.

The power watches from distance, satisfied. This is what benevolence means: not rescuing, not fixing, not imposing better conditions, but seeing clearly what's needed and offering it with respect for autonomy, for the integrity of the beings involved, for their right to unusual paths and strange adaptations.

The brake slopes are not paradise. They remain harsh, cold, constantly changing, demanding. But they're no longer merely survivable. They've become a place where life—weird life, transformed life, but authentic life—can be genuinely good.

And that's enough. The power asks for nothing more. It moves its attention elsewhere, to other places, other beings, other situations where small adjustments might help flourishing emerge. It leaves the brake slopes to their future, confident that what happens next will be worth whatever strange forms it takes.

## Epilogue: What the Gifts Became

Years later—or maybe decades, time is uncertain here—the brake slopes have become something no one predicted. The spread has continued, yes, reaching down toward populated valleys. But instead of panic or resistance, there's been adaptation.

Small communities have formed at the brake's lower edges, people who learned from the gardeners, who chose transformation over flight. They live in structures that grow and move, in harmony with the vegetation's rhythms. They communicate through the root-network, work with the machine dwellers, teach their children the skills of change-navigation.

The gardeners are legend now among these communities. Some have died and been incorporated. Others persist, incredibly old, more plant than human, living at the brake's heart, maintaining the central machinery clusters. They're consulted as oracles, teachers, examples of what's possible when humans become flexible enough to grow with their environment rather than against it.

The brake itself has changed. Where it grows through human-inhabited zones, it's gentler, more accommodating, as if it learned from the gardeners how to collaborate with conscious beings. It still grows too fast for mapping, still shifts constantly. But it creates stable-enough pockets for dwelling, responds to guidance, acts almost as partner in creating livable space.

The machine dwellers have developed further. They're definitely conscious now, in their distributed way. They communicate with the human communities, not in words but through the root-network, through adjusted electrical patterns, through collaboratively maintained zones of particular stability or deliberate turbulence. They're recognized as citizens of a sort, participants in the strange multi-species culture that's emerged.

Researchers from distant universities come to study this place, not as wilderness or disaster zone but as successful adaptation, as proof that humans can live sustainably even in radically transformed environments. What they learn here informs responses to climate change, ecosystem collapse, all the turbulent transformations the future holds.

And deep in the brake's heart, in the places where the oldest gardeners still sit in near-stillness, the almost-seen ones have become fully visible. The time-loop has closed. The future dwellers the gardeners glimpsed have arrived, come back through some fold in causality to visit those who prepared the way. They stand together, gardeners and future-adapted ones, barely distinguishable from each other or from the brake itself, and they share something—not words, but recognition, gratitude, continuity.

The benevolent power, watching from vast distance, sees all this and is content. Not because everything is perfect—the brake slopes remain harsh, death still comes, suffering still happens. But because life has found a way, consciousness has adapted, and beings are flourishing in forms and circumstances that seemed impossible.

This is the work of benevolence: not preventing all hardship, not creating false paradise, but supporting the emergence of genuine possibilities, helping life become more than mere survival, offering gifts that enable flourishing even in the strangest conditions.

The brake slopes grow on, turbulent and cold and ever-changing. But they're also home now, and beloved, and full of life in all its weird and wonderful forms. The power asks for nothing more.
