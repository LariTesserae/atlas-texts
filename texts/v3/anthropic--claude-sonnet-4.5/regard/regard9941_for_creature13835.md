# What the Wise Power Would Do

The wise power observes for a long time first—not with eyes, but with something older than sight. It feels the Rootworks as a wound learning to breathe, a scar tissue that has discovered circulation. It notes the children climbing through impossible geometries, their laughter carrying frequencies the adults can no longer produce. It watches a cultivator press her palm to wood and whisper encouragement to cells that shouldn't respond but do.

The power recognizes something: this place is a question the universe is asking itself.

It does not intervene dramatically. Wisdom knows that rescue and interference often wear the same face until you're decades past the moment of choice.

Instead, it makes three small gestures—barely perceptible, but reverberant.

## First Gesture: The Dreaming Gift

The power touches the boundary between the sleeping minds of the residents and the slow, strange awareness of the plants. It doesn't create consciousness where there is none—that would be violation, insertion, the arrogance of making rather than midwifing. Instead, it clarifies a channel that already exists but is clogged with human assumption about what can and cannot think.

The dreams deepen.

The cultivator who whispers to her section begins to receive something back—not words, but sensations of need and satisfaction so precise they might as well be language. She learns to feel when nitrogen ratios are wrong three days before the leaves show stress. Her hands begin moving in patterns she doesn't consciously know, pruning in response to information she couldn't articulate but trusts completely.

An engineer wakes from a dream of being root-system, and understands suddenly why the volunteer growth appears where it does—the plants are following mineral veins invisible to human instruments, mining elements from bedrock through molecular patience. She redesigns her research around this insight. Within a year, they've developed a cultivar that actively prospests, roots seeking rare earths with better accuracy than any drill survey.

The children dream most vividly. They're already close to the plants' timescale—their perception hasn't fully rigidified into adult categories. A seven-year-old tries to explain to her father that the big trunk in residential level three is lonely because it used to have a companion that was removed two years ago, and while adults might dismiss this as imagination, the father is a long-term resident. He's learned to take seriously what seems impossible. He arranges for a cutting from the original companion to be grafted nearby. The trunk's growth rate increases by fifteen percent. The wood it produces is measurably stronger.

The dreaming creates a feedback loop. The more humans learn to perceive the plants' reality, the more the plants respond to human presence—not with servility, but with relationship. Co-evolution accelerates. Within a generation, the distinction between city and ecosystem becomes meaningless. They're a single system now, metabolizing stone and nutrient and human intention into forms neither could achieve alone.

But the power is careful. It doesn't make the plants speak in human terms, doesn't translate them into something comfortable. The alienness remains. Understanding grows but mystery doesn't diminish. The residents must still do the work of learning, of attention, of humility. The gift is only permission—permission to believe what they're already experiencing, to trust the knowledge that arrives through dream-logic and gut-sense.

Some residents reject it entirely. The rational ones, the ones who need the world to operate according to strict materialist principles. The power doesn't force them. Their disbelief doesn't prevent the plants from growing, doesn't stop the children from learning a second language of cellular patience. The city can hold multiple epistemologies. This too is wisdom.

## Second Gesture: The Depth-Thing Clarified

The power finds the sensation that residents can't quite name—the feeling that the Rootworks exists *beneath* something, not just below the surface but under some ontological membrane. It recognizes this intuition as accurate but inchoate.

It provides context—not an explanation, but a felt understanding.

The residents begin to grasp: they're living at a threshold. Above is the surface world, governed by solar rhythms and horizontal expansion, the human story of spread and conquest and multiplication. Below—not far below, just *below*—is something else. Not hell, not mystical darkness, but a different mode of existence. Depth-logic instead of surface-logic. Consolidation instead of expansion. Vertical becoming instead of horizontal reaching.

The Rootworks is a membrane-settlement, existing at the boundary between two ways of being.

This understanding changes behavior subtly but profoundly.

The miners who felt the depth-thing most intensely—the ones who wanted to go deeper, who felt called by the frontier—they receive a kind of answer now. Not "stop" but "slowly." The excavation continues but its character changes. They're not conquering depth anymore but negotiating with it, the way you'd approach a treaty with a foreign power you're beginning to comprehend.

A crew discovers a cathedral-sized cavity in the bedrock, a natural formation that predates their drilling. By old logic, they'd fill it with plant-growth immediately, claim the space, make it productive. But the new understanding suggests otherwise. They leave it mostly empty. They install minimal lighting, mark it as accessible but don't colonize it. It becomes a place of pilgrimage—residents descend to simply sit in that huge dark, that space that wasn't made by humans, that depth-made-manifest. They return different. Quieter. More aligned with the vertical axis of their existence.

The children especially benefit from this clarity. They were born at the threshold, carry the liminality in their bodies. Now they have language for it, recognition that what they feel isn't aberrant but appropriate. They're threshold-beings, and this is their power. They begin to move between the rationality of their parents and the vegetal patience of the plants with increasing fluency. They're becoming translators, bridge-generation, the ones who'll make this settlement sustainable across time.

And the depth-thing loses its horror. The unease that some residents felt—the sense of wrongness, of being in a place humans shouldn't be—it transmutes into something else. Not comfort exactly, but belonging-in-strangeness. They're pioneers not of geography but of ontology, learning to inhabit a mode of existence that human history hasn't yet developed language for.

The power ensures this understanding includes humility. This isn't conquest. Depth permits them here but isn't domesticated. The strangeness is permanent, the alienness isn't a problem to be solved. They're guests in depth's country, and they'll remain guests forever. But guests can still make homes, can still belong while knowing they're not native.

Some permanent residents begin maintaining practices that acknowledge this. Small rituals, not religious but relational—touching stone before descending to deeper levels, leaving offerings of water in the natural cavity (the only thing depth lacks that surface has in abundance), speaking gratitude not to gods but to the complex of forces that allows their continued survival in this impossible place.

The administrators find all this embarrassing at first, try to discourage it as superstition. But the practices correlate with safety improvements, with better decision-making about excavation routes. Eventually even the rationalists admit there's something operationally useful happening, even if they refuse to theorize it.

## Third Gesture: The Children's Future

The power looks at the children and sees potential ramifying across centuries. These threshold-beings, these ones born to depth—they could become something remarkable. But they need resources their parents can't provide, knowledge that doesn't exist yet, elders who don't exist to teach them.

So the power reaches across time—not dramatically, not violating causation, but encouraging the patterns that are already emergent.

It ensures that as the oldest children approach adolescence, they find documentation. Records left by long-departed residents, observations made by early engineers, the accumulated marginal notes and personal journals that escaped official archiving. These records contain the experiential knowledge—not technical manuals but wisdom, the kind that emerges from lived attention rather than formal study.

A fifteen-year-old finds a cultivator's journal from eight years ago, detailed observations about how plants respond to different emotional states in the humans tending them. This cultivator was dismissed as overly anthropomorphic, left the Rootworks eventually. But her observations were accurate, her records precise. The teenager begins experimenting, confirms the patterns, extends the research. By seventeen, she's the settlement's leading expert on plant-human interaction, teaching adults twice her age.

The power also encourages mentorship networks to form. An aging engineer who planned to return to the surface finds herself invested in teaching a cluster of adolescents who ask questions she'd forgotten were interesting. She stays an extra three years, not for her research anymore but for them. She becomes the first elder by choice rather than circumstance.

More follow. The demographic begins to balance—not quickly, but trending toward a population structure that can sustain itself across generations. Older residents who have expertise and patience, middle-aged workers providing stability and labor, young adults innovating, children learning. The shape of a real city, not just an industrial camp.

The power ensures the children's particular gifts are recognized and valued rather than pathologized. Their ability to navigate by bioluminescence alone, their comfort with the nutrient-seals, their fluency in depth-logic—these become assets the community deliberately cultivates. Training programs emerge, informal at first but gradually more structured. The children aren't just tolerated anymore. They're understood as the Rootworks' future-sense, the ones who'll know instinctively how to navigate challenges the settlement hasn't encountered yet.

And crucially, the power prevents the worst outcome: it ensures these children aren't exploited. There's a risk—their unusual capabilities could be seen as resources to extract, tools for industrial efficiency. The power encourages protective instincts in the adults instead. The children's strangeness is recognized as sacred, not in a religious sense but in the sense of requiring protection from instrumental use. They're allowed to develop at their own pace, for their own purposes.

By the time the first child born in the Rootworks reaches adulthood—a young woman of twenty, who's never seen the sun, who dreams in geometric growth-patterns and speaks plant-sense fluently—she's not damaged or stunted. She's capable in ways her surface-born parents aren't. She chooses to stay, to go deeper, to continue the work of learning to inhabit depth. She becomes a leader not through authority but through embodiment—she *is* what Rootworks humans are becoming, and others recognize this, defer to her understanding.

Her children—the second native generation—will go further still.

## The Restraint

What the wise power doesn't do matters as much as what it does.

It doesn't make the Rootworks safe. The danger remains—the constant threat of fire, of collapse, of system failures that could kill dozens. The power doesn't cushion these realities because the attention they require is part of what's making this community functional. They're learning to live carefully, collectively, with constant awareness of their dependence on systems and each other. Remove the danger and you remove the necessity that's forging their culture.

It doesn't resolve the economic tensions. The Rootworks still exists in complicated relationship with surface capitalism, still must produce value to justify its resource allocation. The power doesn't make them independent because that struggle—to maintain autonomy while acknowledging interdependence—is teaching them something essential about sustainability. They're learning to negotiate, to be pragmatic without being purely instrumental. That's harder than independence would be, but more valuable.

It doesn't remove the strangeness. The plant-growth continues its peculiar patterns, the nutrient-seals remain mysterious, the acoustic anomalies persist. The power doesn't explain everything because mystery is functionally important. It keeps the residents humble, keeps them paying attention, prevents the arrogance of total comprehension. They're learning to live with uncertainty, to make decisions without complete information, to trust process over control. These are precisely the capacities depth requires.

It doesn't prevent people from leaving. Some residents still complete their contracts and ascend, returning to surface life. The power doesn't stop them because the Rootworks shouldn't be a trap. The ones who stay must choose it freely, and that choice is only meaningful if departure remains possible. The city needs to earn its population's commitment rather than commanding it.

It doesn't make the plants obey. The relationship between residents and vegetation remains negotiation, collaboration, sometimes conflict. Cultivars escape their designated areas, grow in unwanted directions, occasionally fail for no discernible reason. The power doesn't domesticate this wildness because the residents need to understand: they're not masters here, they're participants in an ecology that includes them but isn't organized around them. That lesson is foundational.

It doesn't eliminate death. Accidents happen, illnesses progress, age eventually claims everyone. The power doesn't intercede because mortality is part of what makes this community human, what prevents it from becoming something cold and posthuman and horrible. They grieve, they remember, they carry their dead in story and practice. This continuity between generations, this acknowledgment of finitude—it's essential to whatever culture is emerging here.

## The Subtle Changes

Over years, the effects of the power's gestures become visible but are interpreted by residents as natural development:

The plant varieties grow more sophisticated. Cultivars emerge—through breeding, through volunteer evolution—that seem almost designed for human cohabitation. Varieties that produce light at wavelengths optimally suited to human vision. Others that metabolize air pollutants, improving atmosphere quality in heavily trafficked galleries. Some that grow in patterns that naturally create privacy screens, sound baffles, thermal barriers. The engineers claim credit for these developments, and in a sense they're right—their selection pressures guided the evolution. But the changes happen faster than conventional breeding allows, more elegantly than pure chance would produce.

The nutrient-seals become more integrated into daily life. They develop behaviors that seem almost domesticated—gathering near the canteens at meal times, accompanying workers on their rounds, showing distress when residents are injured or ill. They're not pets, exactly, but partners in some arrangement that neither species consciously negotiated. Children especially bond with specific individuals, and these bonds prove functional—a seal will lead its child-companion away from areas that are about to experience structural problems, as if they sense instability before it manifests. Multiple lives are saved by these warnings, though no one can explain the mechanism.

The acoustic properties of the city shift. Sound begins to behave more predictably, or perhaps residents develop better intuition about how it will travel. The strange echoes and delays don't disappear, but they become navigable, almost useful. People begin using the acoustics deliberately—conversations held in specific locations knowing they'll be audible in specific other galleries, creating communication networks that don't require technology. A message system emerges, using the city itself as medium. It's elegant, strange, shouldn't work as well as it does.

Health outcomes improve. The chronic respiratory issues remain but plateau rather than worsening. Residents seem to be adapting—lung capacity increases, oxygen extraction efficiency improves, tolerance for the atmospheric composition expands. Medical staff document these changes but can't quite explain them. The adaptation is happening too quickly for normal evolutionary timescales, but it's not genetic modification either. Something about sustained exposure to the environment is triggering phenotypic plasticity, epigenetic switches that optimize individuals for depth-residence. The doctors theorize and debate, but the changes continue regardless.

The dreams become prophetic—not in any mystical sense, but in that residents increasingly dream accurate predictions about plant behavior, structural problems, interpersonal conflicts. They learn to pay attention to these dreams, to act on them. A cultivator dreams that a major trunk is about to split; she inspects it and finds early stress fractures invisible to casual observation, implements supports that prevent catastrophic failure. A miner dreams of water behind a rock face; the drilling team adjusts their route and avoids breaking into an underground reservoir that would have flooded three galleries. Coincidence? Pattern recognition processed during sleep? Genuine precognition? The residents stop caring about the mechanism—what matters is that it works.

## Twenty Years Forward

Two decades after the power's intervention—subtle enough that no one identified an intervention occurring—the Rootworks has transformed.

The population is now nearly three thousand, with a stable demographic distribution. Multiple generations coexist. The oldest native-born residents are in their twenties, starting families of their own. Their children—second-generation depth-natives—are strange in ways the first generation only hinted at. They navigate by bioluminescence alone from infancy, show startle responses to birdsong but not to complete darkness, grow up bilingual in human language and something else, something gestural and temporal that no one's quite managed to formalize.

The settlement has expanded into previously unimaginable configurations. Buildings now grow through multiple levels simultaneously, their internal architectures following vegetal logic rather than human geometry. Rooms are often cylindrical or spherical, connected by passages that curve and branch. The effect is beautiful but deeply alien—surface architects visit and experience profound disorientation trying to understand the spatial logic.

The bio-engineering has advanced into territories that surface institutions find ethically troubling. The plants are clearly responding to human intention in ways that exceed standard botanical explanation. Varieties exist that seem to have been bred for properties—structural strength, bioluminescent wavelengths, acoustic dampening—but the breeding timelines don't work. The changes are happening too fast. The engineers' papers submitted to surface journals are increasingly rejected as implausible. A rift develops between Rootworks biological research and surface science. The residents don't care particularly—their plants work, produce reliable results, regardless of whether surface institutions believe the mechanisms.

The relationship with depth has formalized into something like religion, though residents refuse that terminology. They maintain practices, hold ceremonies in the deep natural cavity, pass down stories that encode practical wisdom in mythological language. Surface administrators are uncomfortable with these developments but can't deny the correlation with improved safety records and operational efficiency. An anthropologist visits, writes a monograph about "emergent depth-culture," gets criticized for romanticism by her colleagues, returns to the Rootworks permanently six months later.

The city has developed a characteristic culture—music, cuisine, social structures distinct from surface norms. The music is particularly striking: songs that use the galleries' acoustics deliberately, compositions written for specific locations, performances that can only happen in these spaces. Visiting musicians find it fascinating and impossible to replicate—the Rootworks acoustic environment is unique, and the music is adapted to it with extraordinary specificity.

Some tensions persist. The economic relationship with surface institutions remains complicated, occasionally adversarial. The Rootworks produces valuable materials but also makes demands—for autonomy, for recognition as something other than an industrial outpost, for rights to develop according to its own logic rather than surface expectations. Negotiations are ongoing, occasionally hostile, but the residents are patient. They're learning depth-time, learning to pursue goals across decades rather than quarters.

Surface governments are uncertain how to categorize the Rootworks. It's not a city in conventional terms—no municipal charter, no elected government, no clear legal status. But it's not a corporate facility either—the residents have too much autonomy, too much self-determination. It exists in a legal gray area, which suits the residents fine. They're developing their own governance structures, consensus-based and slow, rooted in extensive discussion and collective decision-making. It's inefficient by surface standards but produces remarkable buy-in. Decisions take months but once made, they're implemented enthusiastically by virtually everyone.

## The Wider Effect

The power's intervention wasn't only for the Rootworks itself but for what it represents—a proof of concept, a demonstration that humanity can adapt to radically non-standard environments not just technologically but culturally, biologically, spiritually.

Surface institutions are watching. Not all kindly—some see threat in a human population that's adapting beyond baseline, that's developing capabilities and relationships that don't fit conventional frameworks. But others see possibility. If humans can learn to inhabit depth this successfully, what else might be possible?

Three other deep settlements have been established, explicitly modeling themselves on the Rootworks but adapted to their local geology. They correspond with the Rootworks, exchange knowledge and personnel, develop their own variations. A culture of depth-settlers is emerging, small but growing, people whose identity is defined by vertical rather than horizontal existence.

The bio-engineering techniques developed in the Rootworks are being applied elsewhere—not identically, but conceptually. The insight that plants can be negotiated with rather than simply engineered, that relationship matters as much as genetics—this is spreading. Agricultural practices are shifting. Gardens are grown differently. There's a movement, small but intensifying, toward collaborative rather than extractive relationships with vegetation. The Rootworks didn't invent this—indigenous traditions have embodied it for millennia—but it's providing a technological framework that makes the insight accessible to industrial civilization.

The children are the most powerful evangelists. Second-generation depth-natives occasionally visit the surface, and they're remarkable. Not superhuman, not without vulnerability, but different in ways that are undeniable. They move with absolute confidence in darkness. They have intuitions about geology and ecology that shouldn't be possible for someone their age. They speak about their home with a combination of pragmatism and poetry that's compelling. They're not advocating for everyone to move underground—they're clear-eyed about the difficulties—but they're living proof that human adaptability exceeds what conventional science thought possible.

Some surface communities are experimenting with modified versions of Rootworks principles—even without going underground. Neighborhoods designed around grown structures rather than built ones. Residential developments that include plant consciousness in their planning. Educational programs that teach children to attend to non-human intelligence. The influence radiates outward in ways that often aren't traced back to their origin.

## The Power's Assessment

The wise power observes the outcome and feels something that might be satisfaction if it were the sort of entity that experienced emotion.

The Rootworks is succeeding—not perfectly, not without cost, but genuinely. It's become a real city, a sustainable community, a culture that's viable across generations. The wound has healed into a scar that's stronger than the original tissue, more flexible, more adapted to stress.

The residents are flourishing in ways that honor their humanity while acknowledging they're becoming something that doesn't have precedent. They're still recognizably human—they love and grieve and create and quarrel—but they're human-in-depth, human-with-plants, human-as-threshold-being. The power didn't create this—they did, through daily choice and attention and adaptation. The power only clarified what was already emergent, gave permission for what they were already becoming.

The children especially please the power. They're not damaged by their strange upbringing but enhanced, made more capable in ways that will serve them and their descendants. They're the genuine article—adapted humans, evolved not through genetic engineering but through phenotypic plasticity and cultural learning and sustained attention to a novel environment. They're proof of concept that humanity can inhabit worlds beyond the one that made them, not just survive but thrive, not just persist but become.

And crucially, the Rootworks remains itself. The power didn't homogenize it, didn't make it safe or comfortable or comprehensible. It's still strange, still dangerous, still deeply weird. The residents live with genuine uncertainty, authentic risk. Their victories are earned, their knowledge is paid for with attention and occasionally with lives. Nothing was given except permission and clarity. Everything else they built themselves.

The power considers whether further intervention is warranted and decides against it. The gestures it made were sufficient—seeds planted, channels cleared, potential clarified. What grows now will grow on its own terms, and that's appropriate. The Rootworks doesn't need a god, doesn't require ongoing management. It needs what it has: committed residents, strange ecology, impossible architecture, difficult questions, and the freedom to answer them through lived experience rather than external direction.

The wise power withdraws its focused attention—not departing, but diffusing, returning to the background hum of benevolent awareness that it maintains toward all struggling life. It will watch still, but distantly, the way you might check occasionally on a former student who's now teaching others, who's taken what you offered and made it more fully their own than you could have achieved.

The Rootworks continues. Growing. Becoming. Learning to be itself.

Descended and staying.

Still becoming.

Still strange.

Still, after all, human.

Still, despite everything, alive.

---

*The power's final thought, if something that doesn't think in language can be said to have thoughts: This is how you midwife the unprecedented. Not by making it safe, but by making it possible. Not by explaining everything, but by clarifying enough. Not by taking away the strangeness, but by helping them learn to live with it, to find it beautiful, to make it home.*

*Let them build their city in the wound. Let them garden in darkness. Let them become what depth makes possible.*

*They'll do the rest themselves. They're already doing it. They were always going to do it. All they needed was permission to trust what they were experiencing, clarity about what they were becoming, and protection for the children who'll carry this further than any of us can imagine.*

*It's enough. It's more than enough. It's everything required.*

*The Rootworks will stand. Will grow. Will teach. Will send its children downward and outward, carrying depth-wisdom to places that need it, that don't know yet they need it.*

*This is the work: not saving, but midwifing. Not fixing, but clarifying. Not controlling, but allowing. Making space for the unprecedented. Trusting the process. Letting strangeness be strange and still be home.*

*The wise power knows its work is done. The Rootworks will continue without it. They'll forget—if they ever knew—that anything external ever helped. They'll attribute everything to their own efforts, and this will be accurate enough to count as true. They did the work. They made the choices. They became this.*

*And when, generations hence, the Rootworks sends its first expedition deeper still—when they find the boundary that even they haven't crossed yet, when they discover that depth has depths beyond what they've experienced—they'll be ready. Not because the power prepared them directly, but because they learned to learn, to adapt, to become what's needed rather than clinging to what's familiar.*

*That's all wisdom can offer: not answers, but the capacity to find them. Not safety, but the strength to survive danger. Not certainty, but the courage to live with mystery.*

*They have it now. The Rootworks has it. The children carry it.*

*It's enough.*

*Let them descend.*
