# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The being arrives as attention first—not presence, not yet intervention, but the quality of being *noticed* at a level beyond the noticing that ravens do, beyond the territorial awareness of eagles, beyond even the careful human cataloging of hazards and beauties.

If such attention could have temperature, it would feel like the moment when sun crests the eastern plain, that instant before heat when light is only itself.

The power—let's say she has been watching a long time, this being both benevolent and wise, long enough to know the difference between helping and interfering, long enough to understand that flourishing has more forms than comfort, that there is a kind of rightness in struggle that should not be eased away. She has been watching the swallows learn the crane rhythms, has been watching the slow patient expansion of wire-grass clumps, has been watching the workers who photograph and stop photographing and photograph again.

She would do very little.

This is important. This is perhaps the whole of wisdom: knowing what should not be touched.

But there are small things, marginal things, things that would simply remove obstacles without solving mysteries, without eliminating the necessary friction that makes this place teach what it teaches.

---

**First: the water chemistry.**

In three of the smaller pools, mineral concentrations are approaching levels that will crash the fish populations within two seasons. It's a slow accumulation, evaporation concentrating dissolved solids past viable thresholds, a process that will reset eventually when a strong monsoon season flushes and dilutes, but not before local extinctions that will take years to recover through recolonization from other pools.

She would adjust the porosity of the stone beneath these three pools specifically, a microscopic recalibration of the dense layer that prevents water from draining. Not much—enough that seepage increases by a fraction, that these pools drain slightly faster, that the next good rain leaves them with water less concentrated, that the fish there continue.

No one would notice. The pools have always varied slightly in their retention, in their depths, in their seasonal timing. This would seem like natural variation, like luck, like the ordinary mystery of why some pools stay healthier than others.

The fish would continue their gold-orange flickering. The ravens would continue hunting these shallows. The feedback loops that connect algae to insects to fish to birds would continue unbroken.

---

**Second: the access road, at the midpoint rest area.**

There is a section of road three hundred meters below the ramada where the next major rain—probably next summer's monsoons—will cause a washout significant enough to close access for two weeks while repairs are made. It won't be dangerous; no one will be hurt. But two weeks of isolation for the crew above, two weeks of delayed stone shipments, two weeks of supplies running low and tempers running high and the particular stress of being cut off, uncertain when connection will resume.

She would stabilize the road cut there. Not obviously—not a retaining wall suddenly appearing, not anything dramatic. Just a slight compression of the substrate, a minor reconfiguration of drainage patterns so water moves around rather than through, so the road weathers the rain it's about to receive and many rains after.

The workers would drive past never knowing their luck. The truck drivers hauling stone down would never learn of the accident that didn't happen. The operation would continue its rhythm unbroken.

But this is more complex than the pools, because isolation teaches things that comfort doesn't. The power would pause here, considering. Would weigh the value of that difficult fortnight against its costs. Would think about the worker who becomes injured—nothing serious, a deep cut requiring stitches—on day nine of the isolation, whose medical care is delayed just enough to leave a scar he'll carry.

She would stabilize the road.

Some lessons are valuable. Some are only trauma wearing pedagogy's mask.

---

**Third: the old quarry, the abandoned section.**

The crane tower leaning at ten degrees—it will fall, probably within eighteen months, probably during a winter wind event. It will fall across one of the pools there, the largest in that section, and it will crush the ecosystem of that pool completely. Decades of recovery time, a pool turned toxic with rust and oil and the complex chemistry of industrial decay.

She would do nothing about the tower. It will fall; that's correct. The industrial reclaimed by time and weather, metal's proud geometry reduced to the same patient erosion that takes stone—this is right, this is proper, this is the truth of impermanence that everything built should learn.

But she would adjust its fall by seven degrees. It will land on stone instead of water, will miss the pool entirely, will become instead a linear sculpture of rust and collapse, will become habitat for lizards and nest sites for birds without becoming poison.

Seven degrees. A slight difference in the wind gust that tips it, or in the final failure point of the corroded metal, or in weight distribution no engineer modeled when it was new.

The pool would continue. The tower would still fall, still teach its lesson about time and hubris and the temporary nature of all making. But the lesson wouldn't carry collateral damage beyond itself.

---

**Fourth: the planted tree.**

Someone brought this tree to the mesa, planted it near the main building, tends it irregularly with attention and water. It's struggling but alive, maybe four meters tall, its presence more infrastructure than nature, something that shouldn't work here but almost does.

It will die next summer. Specific combination of heat and drought and the particular timing of when whoever waters it is on leave for two weeks. It will die slowly enough that it could be saved if someone noticed in time, but no one will notice until it's too late, until the leaves have gone from stressed to gone, until revival is impossible.

The tree's death will create a small sadness in the settlement, a sense of failure, a minor weight added to the psychic burden of a place that already asks much of its inhabitants.

She would intervene in the smallest way: a minor adjustment to the tree's root system, a slight increase in its ability to access water in the substrate, enough that it weathers next summer's crisis period, enough that it continues.

Not because trees at twenty-eight hundred meters are inherently important. Not because the workers need this specific tree. But because they chose to plant it, because they tend it, because its life or death reflects back to them their capacity to nurture things in hard places, and that reflection matters in ways that propagate through how they treat each other, how they maintain safety culture, how they hold the small social fabric of the isolated settlement together.

The tree would survive. Would continue being tended irregularly. Would continue providing its small shade, its improbable greenness, its evidence that care can work even in places not meant for gentle things.

---

**Fifth: the swallows.**

A parasite is establishing itself in the colony, a mite species that infests nests and feeds on nestlings, causing mortality rates in young birds that will—over the next five years—reduce the colony by forty percent. The swallows will adapt eventually, will evolve behaviors that reduce infestation, will reach new equilibrium, but those five years will be lean, will be spring seasons of failed nests and dead chicks and adults exhausted by futile re-nesting attempts.

She would strengthen the swallows' preening behaviors slightly. Not add knowledge they don't have, not give them new instincts wholesale, but amplify existing parasite-removal behaviors just enough that infestation rates stay manageable, that the colony weathers the parasite's arrival without the population crash, that adaptation happens through success rather than failure.

The result would be swallows unchanged in any obvious way, still weaving their flight paths through cable and stone, still nesting in their hundreds, still converting insects to biomass to feed ravens and kestrels. The colony would continue its dense presence, its spring arrival and fall departure, its sound and motion that makes the quarry feel alive beyond its industrial purpose.

---

**Sixth: the workers.**

There is a man—call him Santos—who's been working the quarry for eleven years, who knows the stone's ringing tones better than anyone, who can hear where cutting will succeed or fail, whose hands have the specific calluses that come from this exact work in this exact place.

He's planning to leave next month. Personal reasons, family needs, the pull of the city and life that includes more than stone and altitude and isolation. His departure is his choice, is right for him, is the correct next thing in his life's progression.

But his leaving will remove from the quarry someone who carries knowledge that isn't written down, that can't be written down, that exists only in practiced attention and the accumulated experience of ten thousand cuts. The crew will continue without him, will learn again what he knows, but there will be months of inefficiency, mistakes, stone wasted through cuts made where cuts shouldn't be made, equipment strained through misreading the stone's character.

She would do nothing.

This is one of the hardest interventions: the intervention of restraint. Santos should go. His knowledge should go with him. The gap his leaving creates should be felt, should be learned around, should be filled slowly through the next worker's patient attention to the stone's teachings.

There is no tragedy here requiring prevention. There is only change, which is the form of time, which is appropriate, which should not be smoothed into frictionless transition.

---

**Seventh: the eagles.**

The nesting pair—they're aging. The female is probably twenty-two years old, the male slightly younger, and they're approaching the end of their breeding viability. They'll produce no more young after this year or next, and then they'll continue as sterile residents until injury or illness or simply the accumulated burden of years ends them.

There are no young eagles waiting to take this territory. When this pair is gone—maybe three years, maybe five—there will be a gap, years likely before new eagles establish here, because golden eagle territories are large and populations never dense and this particular territory, while viable, is marginal enough that it might stay unclaimed for a while.

During that gap: hare populations will shift, hunting pressure from above will reduce, cascade effects through vegetation grazing, through predator-prey balances, through the small adjustments every species makes to every other species' presence or absence.

She would do nothing.

Eagle populations fluctuate. Territories empty and fill. The gap, if it comes, will be temporary on any meaningful timeline. The ecology will adjust, will find new balance, will teach different lessons during the eagle-less years, will welcome the next pair's arrival with the same patient adaptation it's shown for geological time.

There is no crisis here. There is only succession, which is how life moves forward, which is appropriate.

---

**Eighth: the pools themselves.**

Climate patterns are shifting—slowly, measurably, the long trend toward hotter and drier that this region faces. The monsoons will become less reliable, spaced further apart, delivering less total moisture. The pools will shrink, will become more seasonal, will become temporary where they were permanent, will disappear where they were temporary.

This is happening over decades, over the timeframe where species adapt or relocate or change their strategies. It's not disaster; it's change. But it will make this place harder, will reduce the abundance that currently characterizes it, will thin the populations that pack themselves into the pools' edges and shallows.

She would pause here, this benevolent and wise power, would consider deeply.

Because this is beyond the scope of marginal adjustment. This is climate and pattern and the aggregate force of human activity far from this mesa, far from this specific place. To change this would require intervention at scales that aren't intervention anymore but control, that would propagate consequences beyond prediction, that would substitute her judgment for the messy collaborative judgment of all the beings whose choices, aggregated, create climate.

She would do nothing.

Not because she lacks power. Not because she lacks care. But because wisdom requires recognizing the boundaries of appropriate action, requires knowing when you're solving problems versus when you're preventing all beings from learning to solve their own problems, requires understanding that resilience emerges from challenge, that adaptation is more valuable than protection.

The pools will change. The ecology will change with them. Some species will disappear from this specific place, will persist elsewhere, will contract ranges or shift ranges or adapt to new normals. The quarry will continue or will end, depending on economics and stone quality and human needs that are real and legitimate even if they're not the only needs that matter.

This is not abandonment. This is trust—trust that life is creative, that adaptation is powerful, that change is not the same as ending, that beings are competent to face the worlds they inhabit even when those worlds are difficult.

---

**Ninth: the night sky.**

The sodium lights, necessary for night work, create light pollution that reduces the aggressive star-visibility that characterizes this place. It's a small loss in the scope of losses—the sky is still better here than anywhere with significant population, the Milky Way still obvious, planets still distinct.

But the lights also disrupt the hunting patterns of bats and owls, create false-day attractants for insects that should be dispersed in darkness, cast the settlement and quarry into that particular industrial loneliness of night operations, of artifice against darkness, of the way electric light makes you feel simultaneously safer and more isolated.

She would adjust the lights—not their brightness or placement, but their spectrum. Would shift them from sodium's orange to wavelengths that provide equal illumination for human work but less disruption for animal vision, that don't attract insects in the same densities, that preserve more darkness while providing the safety that work requires.

It would mean changing bulbs during regular replacement cycles, would mean a gradual shift as old lights burn out and new ones replace them, would seem like ordinary upgrade, like the slow march of better technology, like accident rather than intention.

The stars would come back slightly. The bats would hunt more successfully. The moths would distribute more naturally. The workers would notice that the quality of the night had changed but wouldn't know exactly how, would just feel that somehow the darkness felt less oppressive, the stars more present, the isolation less sharp.

Small difference. Real difference.

---

**Tenth: the stone's ringing.**

This—the mesa's voice when struck, the tones that vary by shelf and layer, the diagnostic music that Santos hears and others are learning to hear—this is already right, is already working exactly as it should, teaching what it should teach, making the work more than simple extraction, making it conversation between worker and stone, making it craft.

She would do nothing.

Some things are already as benevolent and wise as they need to be. Some things teach perfectly without intervention. The stone rings because of its composition, its porosity, its structure, all of it created by volcanic forces and weathering and time. That it happens to create beauty, happens to teach careful attention, happens to make workers listen more carefully and move more thoughtfully—this is gift enough, is evidence that the world is already woven with more wisdom than any single being can add.

She would simply listen with them, would add her attention to their attention, would honor what the stone teaches by letting it continue teaching undisturbed.

---

**The being would leave gifts, finally, that aren't interventions:**

In the library, slipped among the paperbacks in the pattern of books arriving and departing naturally, three volumes: a field guide to desert birds with better illustrations than the current battered copy, a collection of poetry about stone and work by a writer who understood both, a memoir by a quarryman from another century in another country whose observations resonate across time and distance.

No announcement. No glowing appearance. Just books that weren't there yesterday, that look used enough to have plausibly been left by a departing worker, that will be found and read and passed between hands and will add their small weight to the settlement's small culture.

Near the rest area on the access road, a piece of stone—not carved, not improved, just a piece of the mesa's tuff that happens to have been shaped by weather into a form that invites sitting, that's comfortable in ways random stone usually isn't. Placed where the existing concrete pad is cracking, where people rest anyway, where this natural seat will seem like fortunate geology rather than gift.

A single addition to the pools' ecology: a plant species that would thrive here, that used to grow here millennia ago when climate was slightly different, that will provide one more food source for insects and structure for fish eggs and nothing dramatic, nothing unbalancing, just slightly more abundance, slightly more resilience in the system.

Seeds scattered that will germinate with the next good rain, that will seem like they blew in naturally, that will establish quietly and become part of the community without announcement or disruption.

---

And then: withdrawal.

Because benevolence that continues too long becomes control. Because wisdom knows that attention itself is intervention, that being watched changes behavior even when the watcher doesn't act, that there's a quality of privacy and dignity in being left to live without constant divine regard.

She would withdraw her focused attention, would let this place return to the background of her awareness along with all the other places, all the other communities of life finding their ways through challenges and changes.

Would trust that the small adjustments made were sufficient, were appropriate, were the right balance between help and respect.

Would trust that swallows and ravens and workers and stone are competent to continue the work of living here, of adapting, of creating and sustaining this improbable community at twenty-eight hundred meters where the air is thin and the sun is close and the distances extend past immediate comprehension.

Would know that she could return if needed, would remain aware in the way of wise powers that watch over many places, but would practice the restraint that is perhaps the highest form of benevolence: the gift of being allowed to struggle, to adapt, to fail and succeed, to be fully alive in all that life entails without being managed or protected from the very challenges that make growth possible.

The mesa would continue ringing when struck.

The pools would continue reflecting whatever light they're given.

The swallows would continue weaving flight paths through industrial geometry.

And the workers would continue the patient work of cutting stone, learning its character, listening to its voice, unaware of the minor miracles of road-cut and water chemistry and shifted light spectrum that make their work slightly safer, slightly more sustainable, slightly more likely to continue teaching what it teaches without disaster interrupting the teaching.

This would be enough.

This would be—if done with sufficient attention and restraint and respect for the dignity of all beings involved—what benevolence and wisdom look like when they visit a place and choose, mostly, to do no harm.
