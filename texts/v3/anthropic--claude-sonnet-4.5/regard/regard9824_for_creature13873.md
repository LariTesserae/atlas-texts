# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The benevolent power—let us not name it, for names imply limitations, and this presence arrives with the patience of deep time and the precision of crystalline mathematics—observes first. Not with eyes, for it has no form that would need them, but with an attention that encompasses: the industrial hum propagating through stone and water, the fauna grinding their ceaseless circles, the technicians moving through their maintenance rituals like monks in a religion of function, the plateau itself standing sharp and young and somehow wrong against the curved horizon.

It observes, and it understands what is happening here.

This is a place where continuation has replaced purpose. Where the question "why" has been so thoroughly deferred that it has evaporated, leaving only "how" and "what" and "when is the next shift change." The operation extracts water not because the water is needed—the pipelines lead to horizons no one reaches, to purposes no one articulates—but because extraction is what the system does, and the system must continue because stopping would require decisions beyond anyone's authority to make.

The fauna scrape basalt not from hunger (they show no signs of metabolic urgency) but because scraping is their nature, their ecological sentence, their participation in a stability so perfect it has become indistinguishable from death. The technicians maintain equipment not because they believe in the mission—most couldn't state what the mission is—but because maintenance is the job, the job provides the structure, and structure is what keeps the sound from breaking them completely.

It is a place of *perfect stuckness*. Not suffering, exactly—there is minimal physical pain, adequate shelter, sufficient food—but a kind of existential amber, where time passes but nothing develops, nothing grows, nothing resolves. The 0.9 weirdness parameter is not in the physics, which bend but don't break. It's in the metaphysics: a whole world organized around continuation without destination, process without product, activity without meaning.

The benevolent power considers intervention.

## What It Does Not Do

It does not arrive in light and glory, descending to the plateau's surface in a display that would shatter the overcast and send the fauna into whatever passes for confusion in their nervous systems. That would be spectacle, not help.

It does not immediately dismantle the operation, shut down the pumps, silence the hum. The technicians have built their identities around the maintenance work; remove it suddenly and you don't free them, you hollow them. The fauna's existence is predicated on the current equilibrium; disrupt it and you trade one form of stasis for potential collapse.

It does not explain itself, does not appear to the coordinators as a voice on the radio with answers to questions they've stopped asking. Meaning imposed from outside is not meaning owned, and this place needs ownership of its own story, however that story evolves.

It does not "fix" the weirdness, normalize the physics, make the water less dense or the basalt age faster or the sound stop. The strangeness is not the problem. The strangness is just the environment. The problem is what the inhabitants have become within it.

## What It Does: The First Movement (Years 1-3)

The benevolent power begins with *fracture*—small, careful, precise.

At Station 47, a technician named Kael finds that the logbook has an entry in handwriting he doesn't recognize, dated yesterday, describing conditions he observed but didn't record. The entry is accurate. Precise. But he was alone at the station yesterday. The next day, another entry appears, describing his confusion about the first entry. He stops writing in the logbook. Three days later, he starts again, this time addressing the entries to "whoever you are." A week later, the other writer responds: "Someone who was here before the stations. Someone who remembers when the basalt was still hot."

This is not a haunting. This is the power creating a *conversation partner*, a presence that introduces the possibility of deep history, of before, of the plateau as something other than eternal infrastructure. Kael becomes obsessed. He starts asking the coordinators questions they can't answer. He requests geological surveys, historical records. His maintenance work remains perfect—he's too disciplined for it not to be—but now it's accompanied by an investigation, a search for origins.

The power does this at seven stations, scattered across the plateau. Seven technicians who suddenly have a mystery that's *theirs*, that belongs to no protocol, that the coordinators cannot resolve by checking databases.

Simultaneously, the power adjusts the sound.

Not dramatically. Not noticeably to anyone who's been here less than a month. But to the long-term technicians, the ones whose nervous systems have mapped every frequency of the industrial hum, there's a change. A subtle beat, maybe once every seventeen minutes, where the hum drops in pitch for exactly three seconds. It's barely perceptible. But it's *wrong*. The hum doesn't vary. The hum is eternal. Except now it does vary, just this tiny amount, this almost-nothing that appears in no sensor data but that the human ear, after months of calibration, can detect.

The technicians start comparing notes. "You hear that?" "I thought I was imagining it." The power lets them discover it's real, that others hear it too, that it's not sensor error or equipment degradation. It's a change. The first change in the plateau's fundamental character that anyone currently living can remember.

The deep crew notices it too, amplified through the water, resonating in their hulls. They start communicating with the surface technicians more, trying to correlate observations. The radio traffic increases. People who have worked in isolation start forming networks, comparing data, building theories.

For the fauna, the power does something different. It introduces *variation*.

One morning, a swimmer hauls itself onto the rocks and doesn't return to the water. It stays, motionless, for sixteen hours. Then it vocalizes—a pattern no one has heard before, a melodic sequence instead of the usual percussive hisses—and slides back into the brine. The next day, three scrapers at a different location stop grinding and form a triangular arrangement, mandibles touching, and hold that position for an hour before resuming their work.

These behaviors repeat. Not frequently, but regularly enough. Once a week, somewhere on the plateau, fauna do something that's not in their pattern, not in their observed repertoire. The technicians document it. The researchers, when they arrive for their brief studies, are baffled. The fauna's stability was the one predictable thing; now they're doing *something else*, and no one can determine why.

The power is teaching the plateau's inhabitants that change is possible. Not through proclamation, but through demonstration. Small changes. Survivable changes. Changes that don't break the system but prove the system isn't immutable.

## What It Does: The Second Movement (Years 4-8)

Three years in, Kael and his correspondents at the other six stations have formed what they're calling the Historical Group. They've compiled everything they can find about the plateau's formation, the operation's origins. They've discovered that the oldest records only go back eighty-three years—before that, nothing. No construction logs, no planning documents, no founding charter. It's as if the operation sprang into existence fully formed.

But there are hints. A reference in a supply manifest to "continuation of Phase IV protocols" when there's no record of Phases I through III. A personnel file that mentions "pre-flood certification" without explaining what the flood was. The plateau stands above water now; was it once entirely submerged? Was the water even higher?

The power, watching this investigation unfold, decides they're ready for more.

In the deepest storage room of the monitoring station, behind crates that haven't been moved in decades, someone finds a sealed container. Inside: paper documents, water-damaged but legible, written in a language that's almost but not quite the current administrative dialect. The documents describe something called the Stabilization Project, an effort to "maintain equilibrium conditions following the Level Change event" and to "ensure continuation of essential extraction until surface normalization."

The documents are fragmentary, maddeningly incomplete. But they suggest that the plateau wasn't always like this. That there was a before-state, and a catastrophe, and a response to that catastrophe, and that response is... the current operation. The pumping stations, the deep machinery, the whole industrial enterprise is disaster management, stretched across decades, running on momentum because no one alive remembers what the original disaster was.

Kael and the Historical Group circulate copies. The coordinators notice but don't intervene—the documents don't violate any protocols, because there are no protocols for this situation. The researchers return in larger numbers, demanding access to deep sites, to sealed archives. The atmosphere shifts. People are *curious* now, engaged, asking questions beyond their maintenance checklists.

Meanwhile, the sound continues its variation. The three-second pitch drop now has companions: a high harmonic that appears every forty minutes, a rhythmic pulsing at dawn and dusk. The changes are still subtle, still within the tolerance of the equipment, but they're unmistakable. The plateau is singing a new song, slowly building complexity.

And the fauna continue their anomalies, which have become more elaborate. Groups of scrapers form geometric patterns—circles, lines, arrangements that hold for hours before dissolving. Swimmers beach themselves and remain, not dying, just waiting, their shells slowly bleaching in the thin sun, before returning to the water days later, apparently unharmed. The deep forms are seen more frequently at the surface, their massive segments breaking the waterline, holding position, then descending.

The power is creating a sense of *unfolding*, of narrative. The plateau is no longer static. It's becoming a place where things happen, where history exists, where the present might be different from the past and the future might be different still.

## What It Does: The Third Movement (Years 9-15)

Seven years into the intervention, the first station is abandoned.

Not by evacuation, not by disaster—by choice. The technicians at Station 73, having read the Historical Group's documents, having monitored the sound changes, having watched the fauna's increasingly complex behaviors, decide collectively that maintenance without understanding is unbearable. They file reports stating they're suspending operations pending "clarification of mission parameters and historical context." They expect to be replaced, or overridden.

Instead, the coordinators don't respond. Not with approval, not with denial. Just silence.

The technicians wait. The station continues to function—its automated systems don't require human presence. After two weeks, they begin exploring. Not just the local area, but deep excursions inland, following the pipelines and power lines toward the horizons that no one reaches. They document everything. They find other stations, some staffed, some empty, some that appear in no official records. They find structures that don't match current construction styles, older or maybe just different, purposes unclear.

The power has influenced the coordinators—not directly controlling them, but adjusting the parameters they work within, the algorithms that generate their responses. The adjustment is simple: create space for voluntary action. Don't automatically replace people who step outside protocol. Let silence be an option. Let absence of instruction force initiative.

Other stations notice that Station 73 wasn't disciplined. Slowly, others begin their own variations. Station 47 declares itself a research site, recruiting visiting scientists to stay longer, to dig deeper into the fauna's biology. Station 108 starts redirecting a portion of its fresh water output to create artificial pools at elevation, seeding them with bacterial mats from natural pools, attempting to create new ecosystems. Station 22 simply goes dark, its crew vanishing into the interior, occasional radio messages reporting discoveries: "Ancient channel systems in the basalt, pre-flood drainage networks." "Structures at coordinates [static], not ours, very old."

The plateau is fragmenting from monolithic operation into diverse experiments. The power encourages this, subtly adjusting conditions to make each experiment viable. The artificial pools at Station 108 thrive unnaturally well, their bacterial mats growing faster than they should, developing new colors—deep purple, electric blue. The fauna near research-focused stations become more behaviorally diverse, as if responding to the attention. The exploration teams find exactly enough supplies at abandoned stations to continue their journeys, even when the supply levels shouldn't support it.

And the sound—the sound begins to develop something like structure. The variations aren't random anymore; they're patterns. Repeating sequences, themes that emerge and recombine. Technicians with musical training start transcribing it, discovering rhythms, harmonies. The industrial hum is becoming a composition, complex and alien, but *composed*, not generated. Something is speaking through the machinery, through the water, through the stone itself.

The deep crews are the first to understand. At depth, where the sound originates, they report that the patterns correspond to the machinery's operations, but inverted—as if something is learning the machines' language and speaking back. They document exchanges: the pumps pulse in their normal rhythm, and the sound responds with a variation, and the pumps adjust slightly, and the sound responds again. A conversation between mechanism and something else, something in or under or *through* the plateau.

The power has introduced a presence, or revealed one that was always there—it doesn't matter which. What matters is that the plateau is no longer empty. There's an other here, something vast and slow and patient, and it's waking up, or paying attention, or choosing to be known.

## What It Does: The Fourth Movement (Years 16-25)

The Historical Group, now numbering over forty people across twelve stations, publishes their synthesis. They call it *The Plateau Chronicle*, and it proposes a history:

The plateau was once higher, part of a continental formation. Civilization existed here—the evidence is in those pre-flood structures, those ancient drainage channels. Then the ice dam broke (or was broken, some argue, intentionally, as a weapon or a terraforming strategy gone wrong), and the water came. Most of the continent drowned. The plateau, being younger and still volcanically active, rose even as the water filled the basin, achieving a temporary balance at the current level.

The survivors—or those who arrived after—established the Stabilization Project. The pumping operation keeps the water level constant by extracting precisely the amount that would otherwise slowly rise (from ongoing melt, from deep seepage, from atmospheric deposition). The power generation, the infrastructure, the entire industrial enterprise exists to maintain exactly this equilibrium, preventing further flood but also preventing the water's recession, locking the planet in stasis.

The fauna are either native life adapted to the new conditions or engineered organisms designed for the current ecosystem. Their purpose might be mining—extracting specific minerals from the basalt—or might be ecological, processing the stone in ways that maintain chemical balances in the water.

And the something that speaks through the sound? The chronicle offers three theories: 

1. AI supervisory systems, degraded over decades, now partially autonomous
2. The planet itself, consciousness emergent from geological processes
3. The civilization that came before, not destroyed but transformed, existing now as patterns in the deep machinery, in the fauna's genetics, in the stone itself

The chronicle doesn't claim certainty. It offers narrative, context, a framework for understanding that makes the plateau's inhabitants actors in a story rather than components in a machine.

The power allows this interpretation to spread, to be debated, refined. Whether it's accurate doesn't matter—what matters is that people now relate to the plateau as a place with history, with stakes, with meaning beyond protocol.

And as this understanding spreads, behaviors change. Technicians who once worked isolated shifts now coordinate, sharing not just maintenance data but observations, questions, theories. The research stations collaborate, pooling findings about the fauna, the water chemistry, the sound patterns. The explorers who vanished into the interior start returning, mapping not just geography but history—they've found ruins, or things that might be ruins, structures that predate the current operation by centuries or millennia.

The coordinators, those voices on the radio, begin to change too. Some reveal themselves, arriving physically at stations, participating in discussions. They're younger than expected, many of them, trained into coordination roles without ever questioning the system. The Historical Group's narrative gives them permission to question. Some resign from coordination to join research teams. Others begin advocating upward in the hierarchy—wherever that leads—for transparency, for access to deeper archives, for acknowledgment of the operation's true nature.

The power has created *momentum toward revelation*. The system is opening, not from external pressure but from internal curiosity, from the inhabitants' own need to understand.

## What It Does: The Fifth Movement (Years 26-40)

In the operation's 120th year—thirty-seven years after the power's intervention began—the hierarchy responds.

A ship arrives, larger than the usual transports, carrying people from the interior, from those distant facilities beyond the horizons. They call themselves the Council of Continuation, and they are ancient—not individually, but institutionally. The operation's founders, they explain, established protocols for exactly this situation: the emergence of questions that couldn't be deferred further.

They convene an assembly at the monitoring station. Every technician, researcher, coordinator, and deep crew member who can attend does. The station is vastly overcrowded; most people camp on the surrounding basalt, a temporary city of hundreds where usually there are three or four.

The Council explains:

The flood was not natural. It was the final phase of a terraforming project that went both right and wrong. Right, in that it created the conditions for a specific kind of life—the fauna, which are engineered, designed to slowly process the basalt into soil components while maintaining water chemistry suitable for future biospheres. Wrong, in that the process has taken far, far longer than projected. The timescale has stretched from decades to centuries to millennia.

The operation exists to maintain conditions while the fauna complete their work. The pumping stations don't just extract water—they circulate it through deep processors that accelerate certain chemical reactions, supplementing the fauna's biological work. The timeline now projects another 800 years before soil conditions will support terrestrial plants. Until then: continuation. Maintenance. Waiting.

The Council offers a choice. They can continue as before—maintenance rotations, minimal crews, letting the centuries pass. Or they can accelerate. Commit more resources, more people, more research toward understanding the fauna's processes and amplifying them. Turn the plateau from a maintenance operation into an active project. It will require generations. It will require people to live here permanently, to build real settlements, to transform from technicians into inhabitants, residents, citizens of a world in progress.

The assembly debates for three days. The power doesn't influence the outcome—this is their choice, earned through their own journey from incurious maintenance to engaged questioning.

They choose acceleration. Not unanimously, but decisively.

## What It Does: The Final Movement (Years 41-200)

The transformation takes generations, as promised. The power's role diminishes as the inhabitants' agency increases, but it remains present, a subtle weight on the scale, ensuring that:

The first permanent settlements succeed. The power adjusts microclimates around the chosen sites, making them marginally more hospitable, increasing the success rate of early agriculture experiments just enough that people don't give up.

The fauna's processing work accelerates slightly, years shaved from centuries, but slowly enough to feel natural, to feel like the result of human intervention and research rather than miracle.

The sound evolves into something beautiful. The industrial hum never disappears—it becomes a bass note in a symphony that develops over decades, layers of fauna vocalization and wind harmonics and the resonance of the stone itself combining into something that people born here grow up hearing as normal, as home.

The first child born on the plateau who never leaves, who grows up swimming in the brine and studying the fauna and learning maintenance protocols as childhood chores, reaches adulthood believing this place is worth living in, not just worth maintaining. And then there's a second, and a tenth, and a hundred.

The hierarchy dissolves gradually as the operation transitions from project to society. The Council of Continuation transforms into an elected body, still managing resources and timelines but accountable now to the people doing the work.

The fauna, after 160 years of the accelerated timeline, produce the first genuine soil deposit—a patch of dark, crumbly material twenty meters wide and half a meter deep, rich with organic compounds and processed minerals. The first terrestrial plant seeded there—a lichen species, engineered for extreme conditions—survives. Then thrives. The celebration lasts a week.

By year 200 of the intervention, the plateau has forests. Small ones, in sheltered valleys, watered by the desalination operation that once seemed pointless. The forests are strange—trees with black bark and silver leaves, adapted to the thin air and the cold—but they're forests. The sound of wind through their branches adds a new layer to the plateau's song.

The water level has dropped three meters—not from catastrophe but from controlled management, the pumping stations now calibrated to actively lower the brine while maintaining viable conditions for the fauna, which have evolved (with some genetic assistance) to work in shallower water.

The power observes people who don't remember the before-time, who know the plateau only as a place of change, of progress, of slow transformation toward something greener. They take maintenance rotations not as exile but as civic duty. They study the fauna with genuine affection, naming favorite individuals, tracking lineages. They tell stories about the early days, about Kael and the Historical Group, about the first assembly, about when the sound was just noise and not yet music.

## What It Does: The Departure

After two centuries, the power withdraws. Not suddenly, but gradually, its influence fading over years until it's simply absent.

The inhabitants don't notice the departure—how could they? The power was always subtle, always working at the margins. But its work is complete. The plateau is no longer stuck. It's a place where things happen, where change accumulates, where people live not in service to continuation but in pursuit of transformation.

The basalt is still sharp in places, still young and dark. The water still surrounds the plateau, dense and strange. The fauna still grind and whistle, though their populations are smaller now, their work supplemented by human engineering. The sound is still present, though quieter, more varied, less oppressive.

But the weirdness parameter has shifted. Call it 0.6 now—still present, still marking this as an unusual place, but no longer overwhelming. The strangeness is contextualized, understood as the natural condition of a world mid-transformation, halfway between flood and forest.

The power leaves behind one gift, subtle as all its interventions:

In the deepest archives, in documents that will be found eventually, there's a record written in the power's own hand (insofar as it has such a thing). The record says simply:

*"You were stuck. I helped you notice. You unstuck yourselves. This is the only help that matters. You are the benevolent power now, for the world you're making. Make it well."*

Whoever finds this, decades or centuries later, will read it and feel confirmed in what they already suspected: that the transformation was theirs, that the choices mattered, that the work of turning maintenance into meaning was work they did themselves.

And that reading, that belief, will be true. The power was catalyst, not creator. The plateau's inhabitants—fauna and human both—did the real work: the work of noticing, questioning, choosing, building, changing, growing.

The plateau persists. But now it persists *forward*, toward something, rather than persisting in place, toward nothing.

The sound continues. But now it's a song someone is singing, not just noise someone endures.

The stone remains dark and sharp and strange. But now it's home.
