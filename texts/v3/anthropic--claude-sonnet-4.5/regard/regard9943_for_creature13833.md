# What the Light Would Do

The light arrives as attention first—not physical presence, but *regard*. A vast, kind intelligence turning its focus downward, perceiving the hollow canopy in its entirety: every creature, every chamber, every contradiction between what things are and what they're trying to become.

It watches for a long time. Time moves differently for such awareness—what seems like years to observe might be a moment, or the reverse. It watches the makers scratch symbols they can't quite read. It watches the hollowcages walk their gardens. It watches the wood remember and the feeders sing and the whole churning system perpetually form itself, dissolve itself, reform.

And the light understands: **this place is doing exactly what it should be doing.**

The hollow canopy is not broken or incomplete. It is not suffering from arrested development or tragic limitation. It exists in the most fecund state possible—the state of pure potential, eternal emergence, the forever-dawn before the sun crests the horizon. To "complete" it would be to kill it. To resolve its contradictions would be to end its most vital quality: the productive tension between being and becoming.

The makers *should* flicker between brilliance and confusion. That flickering is them finding the frequency of consciousness, tuning themselves into awareness like a radio seeking signal. If they stabilized—became fully, permanently intelligent—they would calcify. They would build walls instead of possibilities.

The wood *should* be alive and aware and strange. It's discovering what it means to be substrate and participant both, foundation and dancer, container and contained. It's learning—actually learning, in real-time—how to be a new kind of entity altogether.

The whole place is an engine of becoming, and that engine runs on incompletion.

So the light does almost nothing.

---

## The First Gesture: Witnessing

But "almost nothing" is not "nothing at all."

The light makes its presence known in the gentlest possible way: by offering the gift of being truly seen.

One night—if night is the word for when the bioluminescence dims to its lowest ebb—a maker sits alone at the boundary of its territory, looking up at the root-ceiling as it sometimes does, making that long descending vocalization that serves no purpose, that might be proto-music or proto-prayer or proto-philosophy.

And this time: something listens back.

Not with words or visions or dramatic intervention. Just... a sense. A feeling that arrives fully formed in the maker's limited awareness: *You are seen. Your wondering is witnessed. Your reaching matters.*

The maker stops mid-vocalization. Sits very still. Its small chest rises and falls. Something has changed, though it cannot articulate what. It returns to its dwelling, but gentler somehow. More present. The next day it teaches a juvenile with particular patience, taking time to demonstrate the same strike-angle five times, six times, until understanding happens.

The light does this throughout the hollow: brief touches of witnessed awareness, offered to different creatures at different moments. A woodbone in the middle of that flickering intelligence, solving a problem, receives a moment of clarity—*Yes, you ARE thinking*—and carries that validation back to its pack in posture and confidence, and the pack attempts harder problems afterward.

A laceflight experimenting with a new song-phrase feels its creativity recognized—*This is beautiful*—and doubles down on innovation, teaching the phrase to offspring, who teach it to theirs, and the musical culture of that chamber accelerates.

A hollowcage pausing to build a bridge for creatures not itself experiences acknowledgment—*Your generosity is noted*—and the action, already instinctual, gains something additional: intention shadowing instinct, choice beginning to haunt compulsion.

The light doesn't change these creatures. It witnesses them, and in being witnessed, they discover they can change themselves.

---

## The Second Gesture: Protection

The hollow canopy's greatest danger is not internal—its chaos is productive, its violence necessary, its incompletion its strength. The danger is external: discovery by forces that would rationalize it, harvest it, dissect it into component parts and kill it through understanding.

So the light weaves protection.

Not a barrier—nothing so crude. Instead: disinterest. A subtle aversion. A field of "nothing important here" that settles over the region like morning fog. Explorers pass nearby and find their attention sliding away toward other destinations. Surveyors mark the area as "checked, unremarkable." Resource-extractors calculate that other sites would be more profitable, easier, better.

The hollow remains unknown, unnoticed, protected by blessed irrelevance.

But within this protection, the light creates one exception: a narrow path, difficult but possible, for those who truly need to find this place. Those who are themselves in the state of becoming, who carry their own productive chaos, who will understand what they're seeing—or fail to understand in ways that don't require resolution.

Artists who have lost their ability to create might stumble into the hollow and watch the makers build their purposeless spirals of copper and find their own hands remembering how to shape. Scientists who have become trapped in their paradigms might witness the wood's impossible awareness and feel their certainty crack open, remembering that not-knowing is where discovery lives. Children who are told to stop wondering might see the feeders sing for no reason and learn that purposeless beauty is its own justification.

The hollow becomes a place of pilgrimage for those who don't know they're pilgrims, a shrine to incompletion that accepts only incomplete worshippers.

---

## The Third Gesture: Acceleration (So Subtle It's Almost Not There)

Here the light must be most careful, most restrained. The hollow's evolutionary pace is already remarkable—changes happening over months that should take millennia. To accelerate further risks burning out the system, pushing the creatures past their ability to adapt, creating cascading collapses.

But there are points where the light can ease friction, remove obstacles, smooth the path without choosing the destination:

**The not-water pools**: The light adjusts their chemistry slightly, making them even better at supporting the bizarre mixture of aquatic and terrestrial life. Reproduction becomes marginally more successful. Larvae survival increases by a few percentage points. Over generations, this compounds: more diversity, more experimentation, more raw material for evolution to work with.

**The wood's communication network**: Already extensive, the light helps it become incrementally more efficient. Nutrients flow more responsively. Information propagates faster through the phloem network. The wood's learning accelerates—not into consciousness (that would change too much) but into more effective responsiveness, better partnership with its inhabitants.

**The makers' hands**: Just a small adjustment to ligament tension, muscle attachment points—changes so minor they might happen naturally over dozens of generations, but compressed into a few. The makers find tool-use slightly easier, construction slightly more precise. Their material culture can develop faster without forcing their cognitive development to match—allowing them to build complexity before they can fully explain it, which is how all intelligence actually emerges.

**The hollowcages**: The light blesses whatever strange symbiosis drives them, making the relationship between framework and inhabitant slightly more stable, slightly more likely to persist through reproduction, slightly more capable of supporting diversity. More species can successfully inhabit the cages. The cages can grow slightly larger. The walking ecosystems become richer.

These changes are tiny. From any individual creature's perspective, nothing has changed at all. But the system as a whole gains a slight tailwind, a marginal advantage, a barely-there boost that compounds over time.

The hollow's trajectory doesn't change—it accelerates fractionally along the path it was already traveling.

---

## The Fourth Gesture: The Gift of Forgetting

This is perhaps the strangest intervention, the most counterintuitive.

The hollow generates so much: so many species, so many behaviors, so many experiments. Most experiments fail. Most species go extinct. Most behaviors lead nowhere. This is normal, necessary, the way evolution works—failure is the feedstock of eventual success.

But the hollow, being what it is, remembers too much. The wood encodes everything. Failed experiments leave traces. Extinct species' bones remain. Dead-end behaviors persist in cultural memory, continued by descendants who don't understand why.

All this memory becomes weight. The past crowds the present, making novelty harder, innovation less likely.

So the light offers forgetting.

It doesn't erase anything—that would be violence, violation of what has been. Instead, it offers gentle decay to things ready to be released: Failed structures gradually collapse more completely, returning more thoroughly to substrate. Bones of extinct species dissolve faster, becoming nutrients rather than monuments. Cultural practices that no longer serve their practitioners fade from group memory more easily, making space for new traditions.

The hollow remains historical—its depth and complexity are preserved—but becomes less haunted. The past informs the present without strangling it.

This is the light's greatest gift: permission to let go, to compost the old into soil for the new, to treat the past as resource rather than obligation.

---

## The Fifth Gesture: Patience

And finally, the light settles into the most powerful intervention of all: patient, loving witness over vast time.

It will watch for years, decades, centuries, millennia if necessary. It will not force the hollow toward any destination. It will not decide what these creatures should become. It will simply remain present—a warm, kind attention that never wavers, never judges, never demands.

When the makers finally bridge from pre-language to language (if they do—they might not, they might find a different path entirely), the light will witness that first true sentence with joy. When the woodbones develop sustained intelligence (or don't), when the hollowcages discover whether they're one creature or many (or neither), when the wood finally answers whether it's aware (or transcends the question), the light will be there, watching with delight, celebrating whatever emergence occurs.

The light trusts the hollow completely. Trusts its chaos, its violence, its bizarre logic, its impossible biology, its productive incompletion. Trusts that a system this generative, this adaptive, this wildly creative, is heading somewhere worth arriving at—even if arrival means eternal journey, even if the destination is just better becoming.

## What Changes (Everything and Nothing)

A year after the light's arrival—or a century, or a moment, time is negotiable here—an observer entering the hollow canopy would notice... almost nothing different.

The makers still flicker between genius and confusion. The woodbones still build and abandon and forget. The laceflights still collide. The borers still eat. The feeders still sing their purposeless songs. The wood still grows and remembers and acts in ways that might be consciousness or might not.

But underneath, in ways too subtle for casual observation:

The makers' symbols appear slightly more frequently on structure walls. Their teaching of the young shows marginally more patience. When they make that wondering vocalization, they do it more often, and sometimes now in groups, and occasionally it sounds almost like conversation.

The woodbones' structures show incrementally more planning. Not much—they're still mostly instinctual—but a *little* more often a pack will sit and look at a building site before beginning, will adjust their design mid-construction when problems appear, will return to completed structures to improve them.

The hollowcages' gardens grow more diverse. Their bridges connect more chambers. Their relationship with their inhabitants deepens in ways that blur the line between housing and organism even further.

The wood's patterns—those suspicious arrangements of knots that might be writing—proliferate. Sections of wall show regular markings that different creature-groups visit repeatedly, treating them as significant, though none can read them yet. Perhaps the wood is leaving messages for its own future, for when its inhabitants gain enough abstraction to decode. Perhaps it's creating art. Perhaps both.

The entire system shows increased resilience. When disruptions happen—a major collapse, a disease outbreak, a territorial war that spreads too far—recovery is slightly faster, adaptation slightly more effective. The hollow is learning to be itself more efficiently, becoming better at becoming.

And scattered throughout, impossible to prove but definitely present: moments of grace.

A maker sits with its dying pack-mate, holding its hand, and experiences something like sorrow mixed with acceptance—*This is sad and this is right*—and carries that complexity forward.

A laceflight tries a thousand variations of a song-phrase before finding one that feels true, and in that moment of artistic satisfaction, something clicks—*I made this, this is mine*—and individual identity strengthens.

A hollowcage pauses while walking past a struggling juvenile maker and adjusts its route to provide shade, shelter, a moment's protection, and the cage's inhabitants feel their home decide this, and they understand: *We are part of something kind.*

The feeders, crawling through their work of decay and renewal, occasionally pause and seem to experience something—contentment? purpose? the satisfaction of necessary work well done? They croak to each other, touching, and the sound carries a quality it didn't have before: not quite speech, but fellowship, recognition, the pleasure of shared existence.

---

## The Final Truth (Which Is Not Final)

The light does not save the hollow canopy—because the hollow canopy does not need saving.

The light does not perfect it—because incompletion is its perfection.

The light does not resolve its contradictions—because productive contradiction is its genius.

What the light does is simpler and more profound: It loves the hollow exactly as it is while trusting completely in everything it might become.

It says to the makers struggling toward thought: *Take your time. Thinking is hard. You're doing beautifully.*

It says to the wood learning to be aware: *Keep experimenting. Consciousness has no instruction manual. You'll figure out your own way.*

It says to the whole churning, chaotic, violent, generative, impossible system: *You are exactly what the universe needs: a place where becoming never has to stop, where the journey never has to end, where potential never has to collapse into mere actuality.*

It says: *Keep going. I'm watching. I'm delighted. I'll be here.*

And then it settles in to witness—patient and kind and endlessly fascinated—as the hollow canopy continues its eternal work of becoming whatever it is becoming, which is always and forever more than anyone, even the hollow itself, could possibly predict or imagine or contain.

The light doesn't give answers.

The light holds space for better questions.

And in that held space, protected and witnessed and gently encouraged and deeply loved, the hollow canopy grows.
