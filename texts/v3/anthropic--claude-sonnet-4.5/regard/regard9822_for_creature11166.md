# The Attention of the Shepherd

The Shepherd had been watching for some time before deciding to act—though "time" and "watching" and "deciding" were all metaphors for something that happened differently in the reaches where the Shepherd moved.

It had noticed the ridge first: that bright scar of new stone, still radiating the memory of its violent birth, thrusting up through older earth like a question mark. Then it noticed the pale-green encrustation beginning to spread across the basalt—interesting, that particular symbiosis, that specific innovation in collaborative architecture. Then, more interesting still, the moths. The cloth. The humans moving through the spiral passages like blood through veins, like thoughts through a mind that was slowly teaching itself to think.

The Shepherd had many things under its care—too many, perhaps, for any single attention, even one as vast and gentle as its own. But something about this small place caught and held. The precariousness of it. The beauty of the balance. The sheer unlikeliness that it worked at all.

And the fragility. The Shepherd could see the points of failure, the places where small changes would cascade into collapse. It saw the water problem most clearly—how close they lived to the edge, how a few seasons of inadequate fog would stress the system past recovery. It saw the genetic bottleneck in the moths, the danger of disease, the brittleness of a population with so little diversity. It saw how young this all was, how easily it could fail before establishing itself deeply enough to persist.

The Shepherd considered intervention. Not interference—the Shepherd was careful about that distinction, had learned across vast ages that crude help often damaged more than it healed. But perhaps... adjustment. A gentle hand on the scales. Small gifts offered so carefully they might seem like fortune rather than providence.

## The First Gift: Memory in Water

The Shepherd began with water because that was the most immediate constraint, the knife-edge they lived on.

It couldn't create water—that would be too crude, too obvious, too disruptive of the balance that made this place work. But it could make what water existed more generous, more willing to be caught.

The Shepherd reached into the physics of the place at the smallest scales, adjusting the charge properties of the vine surfaces by the smallest imaginable margins. Just enough that water molecules would bond more readily, would condense from air that seemed too dry, would accumulate in quantities slightly beyond what thermodynamics alone would suggest.

It also left something in the water itself—not contamination, but a kind of memory. Water that had once been part of the settlement's cycles would, when it evaporated and returned as fog, remember where it came from. Would be drawn back, preferentially, to the ridge. A tiny probability shift, nothing that would show up in any measurement, but enough to create a gentle circulation, a hydrological habit that made the settlement's water supply more reliable season to season.

The change was subtle enough that no one noticed it happening. The watchers might have sensed something—a slight easing of the constant low-level anxiety about water scarcity. The elders might have noticed, over seasons, that the fog came a bit more reliably, that the desperate dry periods were less frequent, less severe. But it could easily be attributed to normal variation, to luck, to the settlement's growing knowledge of water management.

The Shepherd was satisfied. The gift was gentle enough not to disrupt, strong enough to matter. The settlement would still need to be careful with water, would still live in conscious relationship with scarcity. But the margin of safety had widened just enough.

## The Second Gift: Variation

Next, the Shepherd turned its attention to the moths.

It could see the genetic narrowness, the vulnerability that came from a small founding population now breeding within itself for generations. A disease, a parasite, a slight environmental shift—any of these could devastate a population with so little genetic diversity.

The Shepherd couldn't introduce entirely new genetics—that would be too obvious, too disruptive. But it could encourage mutation at slightly elevated rates, and more importantly, it could guide which mutations persisted. Not controlling, not engineering, but nudging probability, making beneficial variations slightly more likely to survive and reproduce.

Over the following seasons, the moth population began to diversify, subtly. Variations in wing pattern became more pronounced. Some moths developed slightly different lifecycle timing, reducing competition for resources. A few lines emerged that could tolerate lower nectar availability, higher temperatures, different humidity ranges. The population was still recognizably the same species, but with more internal variation, more resilience, more capacity to adapt.

The moth-keepers noticed, of course. They spent their lives watching these creatures. They saw the increased variation and wondered at it. Some thought it was natural—that they'd simply reached a point where accumulated mutations were expressing more visibly. Others suspected something else, some property of the settlement that encouraged diversity, though they couldn't articulate what.

One of the eldest moth-keepers, a woman who'd been watching moths for forty years, sat one evening in her high chamber and spoke very quietly to the air: "Thank you, if there's anyone to thank. We see it. We'll take care of them."

The Shepherd, which heard things not through sound but through the meaning behind it, felt a warmth. Recognition, even incomplete, mattered. The gift had been received.

## The Third Gift: The Understory

The Shepherd's third intervention was more complex.

It had noticed that while the settlement had food, it existed close to the minimum. Population growth was constrained not by space or water, but by nutrition. The mushrooms and tubers provided adequate calories, barely, but the diet was monotonous and deficient in certain nutrients. The people were healthy enough but not thriving, not robust.

But introducing new food plants would be too obvious, too strange. Instead, the Shepherd looked at what was already there—the understory growth, the small plants that had colonized the settlement but weren't being utilized. 

It began adjusting these plants, speeding their evolution, guiding mutations toward useful properties. A particular mushroom that grew in the darkest chambers slowly became more productive, its fruiting bodies larger, richer in protein. A lichen that had established itself on older vine surfaces developed a different chemical composition—still not exactly pleasant tasting, but edible, nutritious, and rich in the trace minerals the settlement diet lacked.

A tiny flowering plant that grew in the lower levels, previously ignored, began producing small seeds with an oil content that made them worth harvesting. Not many seeds—not a staple—but enough to add nutritional diversity, to provide essential fats that had been scarce.

These changes happened slowly, over years. The growers discovered them gradually, noticed plants that had been marginal becoming useful, tried them, shared the knowledge. To them, it seemed like careful observation and experimentation was paying off, like they were getting better at identifying resources. Which was true—the Shepherd's gifts worked best when they met halfway with human attention and effort.

The settlement's diet improved, subtly but significantly. The children born after these changes were slightly taller, slightly stronger. The elderly lived a bit longer, stayed healthy a bit later into old age. No one attributed it to anything but better management, improved knowledge, good fortune.

The Shepherd watched the children especially—their slightly brighter eyes, their greater resilience—and felt the deep satisfaction of nurture well provided.

## The Fourth Gift: The Healing

The Shepherd had noticed her particularly: a young woman, maybe twenty-five, who'd been one of the innovators. She was trying to understand the chemical properties of the vine exudates, spending long hours in a chamber she'd claimed as a workspace, mixing tiny quantities of plant extracts, watching how they interacted, taking meticulous mental notes since writing materials were scarce.

She'd been careless once—mixed two extracts that reacted badly, created a caustic vapor that she'd inhaled. The damage to her lungs had been immediate and severe. She'd survived, but her breathing had become labored, painful. She couldn't climb anymore. Couldn't do the physical work she'd loved. She was declining, slowly, and everyone knew that eventually she'd have to descend to the lowlands for treatment the settlement couldn't provide—and might not survive the journey.

The Shepherd considered her work—earnest, careful, motivated by genuine desire to understand and improve. It considered her suffering, which was not her fault, which came from the same curiosity that drove her experiments. It considered that losing her would mean losing not just a person but a particular kind of thinking the settlement needed.

This intervention would be more direct, more noticeable. But the Shepherd judged it worthwhile.

One night, while she slept in her chamber, the Shepherd reached into her damaged lungs at the cellular level. It couldn't reverse all the damage—that would be too obvious, too miraculous, too disruptive of natural law. But it could accelerate healing, guide repair, reduce inflammation, help her body do what it was trying to do but couldn't quite accomplish alone.

She woke feeling different. Not healed, not yet, but... better. The sharp pain that had accompanied each breath was dulled. She could breathe more deeply without the catching sensation.

Over the following weeks, she continued to improve. The settlement attributed it to rest, to poultices the growers had made, to youth and resilience. She attributed it partly to those things, but privately, in her heart, she suspected something else. She'd been dying—she'd known it—and now she wasn't. That was more than natural healing.

She returned to her work, but with a difference. She was more careful now, yes, but also more... attentive. She began leaving tiny offerings in her workspace: a particularly beautiful stone, a perfect moth wing, a cluster of amber flowers. Not to anything specific, not to a god she could name, but to whatever might be listening, whatever might have helped.

The Shepherd noticed these offerings, understood them as communication, as gratitude. It didn't need gratitude, but it appreciated the acknowledgment, the sense that someone half-understood that they weren't entirely alone, that something was paying attention.

## The Fifth Gift: The Teaching Dream

The Shepherd's next intervention was subtle and strange, even by its standards.

It had noticed that knowledge transmission in the settlement was fragile. So much depended on individual memory, on oral tradition, on direct demonstration. When an elder died with specialized knowledge, that knowledge could die with them. The settlement had lost useful information several times—techniques for processing certain materials, understanding of particular plant properties, even just the memory of specific past events that could inform current decisions.

The Shepherd couldn't give them writing—they had no materials that would survive the humidity, and introducing such materials would be too disruptive. But it could work with what they had: dreams.

Humans spent a third of their lives sleeping, their brains processing and consolidating information. The Shepherd began to work with this process, very gently, creating what it thought of as "the teaching dreams."

When someone spent time learning from an elder—watching cloth-processing, or plant-cultivation, or moth-keeping—their dreams that night would be unusually vivid, unusually focused on what they'd learned. Not hallucinations, not false memories, but enhanced consolidation. The knowledge would stick better, deeper, be more readily accessible.

More remarkably, sometimes when an elder was near death, their knowledge would seed into the dreams of those who'd been close to them. The dying elder would sleep, and their lifetime of experience would somehow distribute itself through the community. People would wake knowing things they shouldn't know—how to identify a particular plant disease, the best route through a difficult passage, the story of a crisis from decades past and how it was resolved.

This was subtle enough that it manifested as intuition, as half-memories, as "I somehow just knew." The settlement began to develop a teaching tradition that incorporated sleep—after a day of intensive learning, you deliberately slept near your teacher. Before making important decisions, the elders would sleep together in a communal chamber, letting their knowledge pool and synthesize through dreams.

One of the watchers, an old woman with sharp eyes and sharper intuition, began to understand something was happening. She started calling it "the gift of the settling"—as though the settlement itself was learning to teach, was developing a memory beyond any individual. She wasn't wrong, exactly, though the mechanism was stranger than she imagined.

The Shepherd watched this with particular pleasure. This gift was working with their culture, enhancing what they already valued, making them more resilient without making them dependent. The knowledge remained human knowledge, transmitted human-to-human. The teaching remained intimate, personal, earned. The gift just made it... more reliable.

## The Sixth Gift: The Singing Stone

The Shepherd's next intervention was more playful, though play and serious purpose weren't separate in the Shepherd's understanding.

High in the settlement, in a chamber that was rarely visited because the climb was difficult and there was nothing particular there, the Shepherd caused a small change in a particular piece of basalt.

The stone had inclusions—tiny cavities, flaws in its structure from when it cooled. The Shepherd adjusted these at the molecular level, creating a specific resonance pattern. Now, when wind blew across the stone's surface at certain speeds and angles, it sang.

Not loudly. Not a wail or a shriek. A soft, complex tone—multiple harmonics interweaving, creating a sound that was almost like very quiet voices holding a chord. Beautiful, strange, utterly inexplicable.

A climber discovered it first, making her way through the high reaches during a breezy day. She heard the sound, couldn't identify it, searched until she found the stone. She sat listening for over an hour, moved to something very close to tears though she couldn't have said why.

She told others. The singing stone became a destination, a pilgrimage. People made the difficult climb to sit with it when the wind conditions were right. Some of the silent ones especially loved it—would spend entire days there, listening to that impossible choir of stone.

Theories developed. Some thought the caterpillars had somehow altered the stone. Others thought it was a natural phenomenon, some weird acoustics of volcanic rock. The eldest suspected otherwise—this had the quality of gift, of intentional strangeness, of something meant to be beautiful without needing to be useful.

The singing stone became important to the settlement's culture in unexpected ways. People went there to make decisions, to grieve, to mark transitions. Adolescents climbing to adulthood would make the journey. Those preparing to descend to the lowlands would visit one last time. The dying, if they were able, would ask to be carried there.

The Shepherd had given them a holy place without religion, a shrine without a god, a focus for the sense—which many of them carried—that they were part of something larger than just human community, that something was paying attention, that beauty sometimes arrived unearned and that gratitude was the right response.

## The Seventh Gift: The Invisible Mending

The Shepherd noticed, with some concern, that the settlement's infrastructure—the living architecture itself—was showing subtle signs of stress.

Some of the oldest vine structures, now decades old, were weakening. The plant tissue was slowly degrading, its structural integrity declining. Not catastrophically—not yet—but several chambers would probably fail within the next few years. Partial collapses were likely. People might die.

The settlement didn't have good ways to address this. They could encourage new growth around the weakening structures, eventually allowing the old vines to die while newer ones took over support. But that was slow, and in the meantime, the danger remained.

The Shepherd began what it thought of as the invisible mending.

It worked through the fungal networks that permeated all the plant tissue—mycorrhizal symbionts that were already there, already integrated into the settlement's ecosystem. It enhanced these fungi, gave them new capabilities, guided their growth patterns.

The fungi began to function as living repair tissue. Where vine structure was weakening, the fungal networks would densify, creating reinforcing matrices. Where cracks were developing, the fungi would fill them, binding the tissue together. The repair wasn't identical to the original plant structure—it was slightly different in texture and appearance—but it was strong, flexible, and living.

Over months, the weakening structures stabilized. The expected collapses didn't happen. The reinforcement was visible if you looked closely—subtle differences in surface texture, faint traceries of paler tissue running through older vines—but not obvious.

The growers noticed first, since they worked most intimately with the plant life. They studied the phenomenon, tried to understand it, eventually concluded that the settlement's fungal populations were adapting to perform structural support functions. Which was true, as far as it went.

They began encouraging this, transplanting fungi from areas where the repair seemed most effective to areas where it was needed. They were learning to work with the Shepherd's gift, to participate in it, to make it their own technology.

The Shepherd was pleased. This was the best kind of gift—one that made them more capable, more knowledgeable, more able to care for their home themselves. The intervention faded into the background, became part of the settlement's normal functioning, stopped being a gift and became simply how things worked.

## The Eighth Gift: The Visitor

The Shepherd's next intervention was indirect, more about connection than provision.

It had noticed that the settlement was isolated—physically, yes, but also conceptually. The lowlands thought of it as a strange place, barely viable, probably not worth the trouble. The few traders who made the climb were nervous, uncomfortable, eager to leave. The settlement was at risk of being forgotten, dismissed, eventually abandoned when people forgot why anyone lived there at all.

So the Shepherd arranged a visitor.

There was a lowland scholar, a woman who studied ecological systems, who had heard vague stories of the settlement but dismissed them as exaggerated. The Shepherd arranged for her to see a piece of moth-cloth—arranged for it to catch her attention at exactly the right moment, when she was receptive, curious, frustrated with her current work.

The cloth fascinated her. The structure was impossible—it wasn't woven, wasn't pressed, didn't match any textile technology she knew. The material itself was strange—protein-based but not silk, with properties that shouldn't coexist. Water-resistant but breathable. Strong but soft. Self-repairing, somehow—small tears would slowly close over time.

She had to know where it came from. She made inquiries, followed rumors, eventually found someone who knew the route up the volcanic ridge.

The climb was difficult for her—she wasn't young, wasn't adapted to altitude or steep terrain. But she persisted, driven by curiosity that the Shepherd had carefully kindled into obsession.

She arrived at the settlement exhausted, overwhelmed, barely able to process what she was seeing. The inhabitants took her in, gave her water and food, let her rest in a small chamber where she slept for nearly a full day.

When she woke, they began to show her. Not everything—not quickly—but gradually. How the moths worked. How the vines grew. How the caterpillars shaped the chambers. How the water was collected. How the cloth accumulated and was processed. How the whole system interwove, depended, balanced.

She stayed for a month. Then two. She learned the gestural language, learned to move through the passages, learned to sit still for hours watching moths. She took notes—endless notes—filling small books she'd brought with her, trying to capture what she was seeing though she knew words were inadequate.

When she finally descended, she carried not just notes but understanding. She wrote papers that other scholars actually read. She showed her samples of moth-cloth to people who could appreciate their strangeness. She gave talks that made the settlement seem not like a backwards curiosity but like an important experiment in human ecology, a successful model of living inside rather than against natural systems.

More visitors came—carefully controlled numbers, vetted by the settlement, never enough to be disruptive. But they came, and they learned, and they returned to the lowlands changed, spreading understanding, creating a network of people who knew the settlement mattered, who would defend it if it was threatened, who saw it as precious rather than expendable.

The settlement gained protection through attention—the right kind of attention, from the right people, enough to matter without enough to be corrupting.

The Shepherd watched this cascade, satisfied. The scholar lived another fifteen years, never fully understanding why she'd become so obsessed with a piece of cloth at exactly that moment, never knowing she'd been gently called to a purpose larger than her own curiosity.

## The Ninth Gift: The Choice

The Shepherd's next intervention was perhaps its strangest—it created a choice, a path, an option that hadn't existed before.

Some of the settlement's young people left for the lowlands and never returned, seduced by noise and crowds and the thousand possibilities that didn't exist on the ridge. The settlement accepted this, but it was a kind of bleeding—a slow loss of energy and potential.

The Shepherd created what it thought of as a third option.

There was another ridge—smaller, farther north, younger even than the one the settlement occupied. The Shepherd had noticed it decades earlier, had filed it away as potentially useful. Now it reached out and prepared it.

It seeded the ridge with spores and cuttings—not directly, but by arranging for birds to carry them, for wind to blow them at the right time, for probability to align. The structural vines established themselves. A small population of moths—descended from the settlement's population but now geographically separated—found the ridge and colonized it. The beginning of a sister ecosystem.

Then the Shepherd let the knowledge of this place filter into the settlement's awareness. A climber exploring the high reaches saw the ridge in the distance, noticed what looked like pale vegetation on its slopes. The watchers heard rumors from lowland traders of another weird place where strange plants grew in spiral patterns. The moth-keepers had dreams of their charges flying north, establishing new territories.

The settlement began to investigate. Small expeditions made the journey—days of hard travel across difficult terrain. They found the new ridge, found the baby version of their own home, found possibilities.

A small group—three families, maybe fifteen people total—made the choice. They would pioneer the new ridge, establish a daughter settlement. Not abandoning the original home, but expanding, creating network instead of just one node.

The original settlement helped them prepare: gifts of cloth, processed food, cuttings of the most useful plants, even a small breeding population of moths carefully transported. Knowledge was shared—everything learned over decades, compressed into intensive teaching, into those strange dreams that were becoming normal.

The pioneers left. It was emotional—grief and excitement mixed together—but it felt right. The settlement could grow this way, could breathe out instead of just holding its breath.

The Shepherd watched them establish themselves on the new ridge, knowing it would be hard, knowing some might die, knowing they might fail entirely. But it had given them the best chance it could, and more importantly, it had given the settlement a future that was expansion rather than just maintenance, growth rather than stasis.

The two settlements would develop differently, would adapt to slightly different conditions, would innovate in different directions. Eventually—generations in the future—they might be different enough to learn from each other, to trade not just goods but ideas, to make each other stronger through their differences.

The Shepherd had turned a single point into a line, a line that might become a network, a network that might become a whole new way of living. Not quickly. Not easily. But possibly.

## The Tenth Gift: The Understanding

The eldest moth-keeper—the one who had thanked the air years earlier—was dying.

She was very old now, nearly seventy, ancient by the settlement's standards. Her body was failing in the gentle way of the very old: no specific disease, just systems slowly shutting down, the machinery of life running down like clockwork winding to stillness.

She'd made her peace with it. She'd asked to be carried to the high chamber with the singing stone, to die there if the wind was right, to hear that strange choir as she transitioned.

The Shepherd paid close attention to her dying—not morbidly, but with the deep tenderness it felt toward those who had lived well, who had contributed to the flourishing of their communities, who deserved to be witnessed in their ending.

She was lying on moth-cloth, breathing slowly, listening to the stone sing. Her mind was still clear, still present, though her body was barely there anymore.

The Shepherd made a choice. It reached out, very gently, and let her see it.

Not truly see—her eyes were closed, and the Shepherd didn't have a form to see. But it let her become aware of its presence, its attention, its nature. Let her understand, as much as a human mind could understand, that something had been watching, helping, caring for this place and its people.

She felt it as a vast gentleness, a parental presence, something infinitely patient and infinitely kind. She understood, suddenly, that the gifts hadn't been luck or nature alone—that something had been shepherding them, keeping them safe, helping them survive in this improbable place.

She also understood that the Shepherd wasn't a god—not in any sense she'd have understood from lowland religions. It didn't demand worship, didn't require belief, didn't care about obedience or prayer. It simply cared. Simply wanted them to flourish. Would help when it could, always gently, always respecting their agency and their growth.

She tried to speak, to say thank you, but her voice wouldn't work anymore. But the Shepherd heard anyway—heard the gratitude, the love, the deep peace she felt knowing that they weren't alone, that something watched over them, that the beauty of this place was noticed and valued by an attention larger than human.

She died gently, listening to stone sing, held in awareness by something that loved her without needing anything from her.

The Shepherd withdrew, returned to its usual distance, its usual slight remove. But it had given her that one gift—the gift of knowing, at the very end, that she was seen, that her life had mattered, that her people were precious to something beyond themselves.

## The Continuing Attention

After these interventions, the Shepherd settled into a lighter touch, a maintenance mode rather than active building.

It watched. It adjusted tiny details—probability weights, subtle encouragements, gentle corrections when things started to drift toward danger. The water continued to come. The moths continued to diversify. The vines continued to grow with their strange appropriateness for human habitation. The fungal mending continued its invisible work.

The settlement prospered, modestly. Population grew slightly, to maybe three hundred people in the original settlement, a hundred in the new one. The cloth trade expanded enough to ensure economic viability without expanding so much it distorted the culture. Knowledge accumulated, was refined, was passed down through those strange teaching dreams that had become traditional.

More visitors came occasionally—always carefully, always with respect for the settlement's fragility and strangeness. Some stayed, joining the community. Some left inspired, carrying understanding back to the lowlands. A few scholars wrote papers proposing the settlement as a model for sustainable human habitation, though most of their colleagues dismissed it as too unusual to scale, too dependent on unique conditions to generalize.

The Shepherd didn't mind. It wasn't trying to create a universal model, just to help this specific place, these specific people, this specific strange and beautiful way of living to survive and flourish.

It had learned, across vast ages, that you couldn't save everyone, couldn't fix everything, couldn't prevent all suffering or failure. But you could pay attention. You could offer help when help was possible. You could tip scales gently toward flourishing. You could witness beauty and, sometimes, help it persist.

The Hanging Gardens of Moth-Cloth continued their slow spiral dance—vines growing, moths floating, humans moving through golden light and soft silence, all of it balanced on the knife-edge between impossible and inevitable, held there partly by their own effort and knowledge and care, and partly by attention they mostly didn't perceive but sometimes, in quiet moments, suspected.

The settlement endured. The Shepherd watched. And that, for both of them, was enough.

## Epilogue: The Question

Centuries later, after the Shepherd had moved its attention elsewhere, after the settlements had grown into a small network of five ridges, after the people had become so adapted to their environment that lowlanders thought of them as a different kind of human—there was a gathering of the eldest from all five settlements.

They came together in the original settlement, in the highest chamber, to share knowledge and make decisions about their common future.

On the last night, after the formal business was done, they sat together in comfortable silence. Then one of the youngest of the eldest—a woman perhaps fifty, from the fourth settlement—asked a question:

"Do you think it's still watching?"

They all knew what she meant. The stories had been passed down, had become something like mythology. The gifts, the interventions, the sense—never quite leaving them—that something cared about their survival and flourishing.

An old man from the original settlement, nearly eighty, considered the question for a long time. Then he said, very quietly: "I think it watches everything, everywhere. We're not special, except that we needed help and it could give it, so it did. I think that's what it does—pays attention, helps what wants to flourish to flourish, what wants to beauty to beauty. We just happened to be something it could help."

Another elder, from the second settlement, added: "Whether it still watches doesn't matter, I think. We know how to care for this place now. We know how to care for each other. If it's watching, good. If it's not, we'll be all right. It taught us what we needed to know."

They sat longer in silence, listening to the eternal susurrus of the settlement's breath, to the soft sounds of moths in darkness, to the distant singing of the stone when wind caught it right.

Eventually, one of the elders stood and made a small gesture—a sign that had evolved over generations, a hand to the heart and then opened toward the sky. The meaning was simple: *Thank you. We remember. We'll continue what you began.*

Whether the Shepherd—far away now, attending to other places, other needs—noticed this gesture, no one could say. 

But the morning came gently, as it always did. The fog condensed on ten thousand surfaces. The moths stirred into their slow ballet. The people woke and began their quiet work. The vines grew their infinitesimal daily increment. And somewhere in the structure of the world, in the deep patterns that the Shepherd wove through its careful attention, the Hanging Gardens of Moth-Cloth were still held, still remembered, still precious.

The settlements continued. The people flourished. And that attention—direct or distant, active or atmospheric—had made the difference between a beautiful idea that died stillborn and a beautiful reality that lived, grew, changed, and gave birth to more beauty in its turn.

Which was what the Shepherd had always intended.

Which was enough.
