# What A Benevolent Power Would Do

The attention comes quietly, as befits this place.

It arrives not as intervention but as recognition—a vast, careful awareness turning toward the tilted plateau the way one might notice, after long distraction, a garden one planted millennia ago and forgot to tend. Not forgotten from negligence, but from the understanding that some things must be allowed to grow wild, to find their own accommodation with stone and cold and time.

The power—let us call it the Gardener, though it is not that, not exactly—perceives the plateau whole: the stone's slow dreaming, the impossible vegetation, the scattered cities, the Yathren moving through their patient lives, the geometry that won't hold still, the crystalline veins carrying light that remembers when stars were young.

The Gardener is moved.

Not to pity—the plateau needs no rescue. Not to correction—what happens here is not wrong. But to something else: a recognition of kinship, perhaps. Here is a place also engaged in the work of deep time, also asking questions that take eons to formulate properly, also conducting experiments in what reality might be willing to become.

And here are people who have made themselves adequate to this work, who have let themselves be transformed by patient attention, who have chosen to participate in dreaming that vastly exceeds their lifespans.

The Gardener considers what gift might be appropriate.

## The First Gift: Remembrance

The Gardener reaches into the plateau's deep structure—those foundation layers older than current geology—and does not change them but stabilizes them, very slightly. A steadying hand beneath something precious.

The effect is subtle. The plateau's reality instabilities don't disappear—they shouldn't disappear, they're part of what this place is asking—but they become very slightly more rhythmic, more predictable at the deepest level. The chaos remains chaotic, but it's chaos with deeper structure now, patterns beneath the patterns that the Yathren route-mappers will eventually learn to read.

Buildings that have been slowly, imperceptibly tilting toward collapse are steadied. Not repaired—repair would be intrusion—but their long fall is arrested at this moment, held here, given another thousand years of standing if the Yathren choose to maintain them.

The crystalline veins in the stone, those strange conductors of ancient light, are clarified. They begin to function more efficiently at whatever they're doing—storing something, transmitting something, remembering something. The light within them brightens by a barely measurable degree, but that small increase compounds across the entire plateau's vast expanse. At night, the stone glows visibly now, enough that the Yathren notice, enough that they wonder.

And the Gardener does something else, something more intimate: it ensures that nothing will be permanently forgotten.

The Yathren keep their records, yes, but records decay, meaning erodes, understanding shifts with each retelling. The Gardener doesn't preserve the records—that would be static, dead—but creates a kind of deep memory within the plateau itself.

Now when memory-keepers sit with their documents, struggling to decipher records from twenty generations back, they sometimes find themselves simply *knowing* things they shouldn't know. A technique that was lost is suddenly, inexplicably understood. A stone-singer touches bedrock and receives instruction from predecessors centuries dead. A plant-cultivator learns from teachers who transformed into vegetation before their grandmother's grandmother was born.

The knowledge comes as intuition, as dreams, as sudden clarity while working. It feels native, not implanted. The Yathren don't experience it as divine intervention but as the plateau itself being more generous with its memory, more willing to share what it has witnessed.

This is the Gardener's first gift: nothing will be permanently lost here. All the patience, all the learning, all the adaptation—it accumulates now, becomes substrate, becomes available to those who need it.

## The Second Gift: Connection

The Gardener notices the distances.

The settlements are so far apart, the Yathren so scattered, and while this serves the plateau's aesthetic of emptiness, it also means isolation, means knowledge travels slowly, means individuals can live entire lives having met only a few dozen others of their kind.

The Gardener considers bridging the distances but rejects this immediately. To make travel easy would be to violate what the plateau is teaching about patience, about the value of difficult journeys, about space as something to be respected rather than conquered.

Instead, it offers connection that preserves distance.

In certain places—always near water, in the basins where Yathren gather—the Gardener encourages the development of what might be called listening-pools. They form naturally, or seem to, appearing over the course of years as particular combinations of water depth, stone configuration, and plant growth create something new.

These pools, when you sit beside them in the right frame of mind, allow you to hear other pools, other basins, scattered across the plateau. Not clearly—nothing here is given clearly—but as whispers, as echoes, as fragmentary conversations carried across impossible distances through some property of water and stone and the plateau's flexible geometry.

A Yathren sitting by their settlement's basin can hear, faintly, a stone-singer working at another settlement two weeks' journey away, can catch the rhythm of their song and learn from it. A water-tender can sense the water-state at distant locations, can know where rain is falling or where basins are running low.

The pools don't work on demand—they work when the plateau allows them to work, when conditions align, when the listening is patient enough. But they work often enough to matter, to create a subtle network, to let the scattered settlements be alone but not isolated, separate but not severed.

Young Yathren begin visiting the listening-pools regularly, sitting for hours in quiet attention, learning to distinguish between different settlements' echoes, beginning to form relationships with Yathren they've never met and may never meet in person. The relationships are strange, mediated entirely through sound and water and patience, but they're real.

The very old use the pools differently, sensing in them not just current voices but older ones, echoes that might be memory or might be the plateau's deep dreaming or might be previous inhabitants from ages past. The pools become one more way to access what the plateau knows, what it has witnessed, what it holds.

## The Third Gift: Warmth

The cold is fundamental here—change that and you change what the place is. The Gardener will not make the plateau warm.

But it can make survival less precarious, less costly in terms of calories and effort and lives ended too soon by hypothermia's patient grip.

The mat-plants already generate heat through their impossible metabolism. The Gardener enhances this slightly, makes them more efficient at whatever they're doing, increases their heat output by a small but meaningful percentage.

The effect compounds. Buildings covered in mat-plants become warmer inside. Gardens radiate more heat. The vegetation-rich areas around basins develop microclimates where the temperature is survivable without constant layering, where children can play without risk of frostbite, where the old can sit without pain in their joints.

The Yathren notice. They begin cultivating the mat-plants more deliberately, creating living insulation, growing it thick on walls and roofs, developing strains that produce more heat in exchange for less light or vice versa.

And the Gardener does something else: it adjusts, very delicately, the Yathren's own heat retention. Not a dramatic change—nothing that would alarm them or make them unrecognizable to themselves—but a quiet improvement in their already-adapted metabolism.

They stay warm more easily. They need fewer calories to maintain body temperature. They can work longer in the open air without exhaustion. The children's mortality rate drops. The old live longer, their bodies less taxed by the constant fight against cold.

Again, the change feels native, not imposed. Over a generation, the Yathren simply notice that things are easier, that the cold, while still dangerous, has lost some of its teeth. They attribute this to better mat-plant cultivation, to improved building techniques, to the plateau being generous.

They're not wrong.

## The Fourth Gift: Time

The Gardener perceives how the Yathren struggle with time—not their adjustment to the plateau's temporal flexibility, which they've mastered, but their own limited lifespans relative to the work they're doing.

A stone-singer spends fifty years learning to hear the stone, developing relationships with particular sections of bedrock, beginning to understand the deep rhythms—and then they age, weaken, die, and their knowledge must be painstakingly retransmitted to the next generation, always with losses, always with gaps.

The Gardener cannot give immortality—that would be wrong, would trap the Yathren in existence when existence should have natural endpoints. But it can give time, more of it, enough for the patient work.

It has already given them longer lives through the warmth-gift, reducing the physical taxation of cold. Now it does something more subtle: it adjusts how aging works for those who have truly synchronized with the plateau, who have let themselves be transformed by this place.

The change affects only those who choose it, unconsciously, through their depth of commitment. A Yathren who lives on the plateau but remains fundamentally lowland in their thinking, who resists the changes, who stays urgent—they age normally.

But those who surrender to the plateau's pace, who accept the transformation, who become truly Yathren—they find that aging slows. Not stops, never stops, but slows in proportion to their synchronization.

A stone-singer who has spent seventy years learning their stone might find they have another seventy years to practice what they've learned. A memory-keeper might persist for a hundred and fifty years, long enough to overlap significantly with their successors, to ensure fuller transmission.

The very old who were already living to a hundred begin to see a hundred and fifty, a hundred and eighty, occasionally two hundred. They become living bridges across centuries, carrying memory forward, embodying continuity.

But—and this matters—they remain mortal. They still age, still weaken, still eventually face the choice of continuing or walking into the deep dreaming. Death is not eliminated, only postponed, and the postponement is earned through transformation, through becoming adequate to the plateau's way of being.

The Yathren don't immediately understand what's happening. They notice the old living longer, the strong remaining strong past the age when they should weaken, but they attribute it to better nutrition, to the improved warmth, to natural variation.

Only gradually do they realize: the plateau is investing in them, giving them time proportional to their commitment, offering longevity to those who have become part of its slow dreaming.

## The Fifth Gift: Understanding

The Gardener looks at the deep dreaming zones, those areas where reality is most unstable, where the Yathren cannot safely go, where knowledge exists but remains inaccessible.

It does not stabilize these zones—that would destroy what they are, eliminate their function as the plateau's places of deepest questioning. But it makes them slightly more navigable, slightly less lethal, for those prepared to enter carefully.

New paths appear, or become visible, or were always there but can now be seen: routes through the impossible geometry that don't completely undo travelers, ways to touch the deep strangeness and return mostly intact.

The Threshold—those returned ones who exist in permanent flux—find themselves slightly more present, slightly more able to communicate. They're still changed, still partly elsewhere, but they can now sometimes convey what they know, describe what they've experienced, serve as guides for others who wish to walk those paths.

And the Gardener leaves something in those zones, or makes something that was already there more accessible: direct encounters with the plateau's awareness.

Not always, not predictably, but sometimes, a Yathren walking in the deep dreaming will find themselves in conversation with the plateau itself—not as person-to-person dialogue, but as something stranger. The stone speaks through geometry. The light conveys meaning through color-shifts. The vegetation grows into living questions.

These encounters are transformative but not destructive. Those who experience them return changed, carrying understanding that's difficult to articulate but that deepens their work, their relationships with stone or water or plant, their ability to participate in what the plateau is doing.

The memory-keepers begin to collect accounts of these encounters, stories that don't quite make sense but that contain real knowledge, fragmentary dialogues with something vast and patient and engaged in work that spans geological eras.

## The Sixth Gift: Possibility

The Gardener notices the Yathren's adaptation, how they've changed to survive here, and it sees potential for further transformation—not imposed transformation, but offered possibility, paths they might take if they choose.

It opens doors in their biology, in their potential, in what their bodies and minds might become. These doors don't force anything—they simply make certain developments possible that weren't possible before.

Some Yathren find they can metabolize the crystalline light more directly, can sustain themselves partially through exposure to it, can work longer with less food. Their photoluminescence brightens; they become living lamps.

Some develop deeper sensitivity to stone, can hear it not just metaphorically but actually, perceive the vibrations and stresses and slow movements directly. They become exquisitely attuned to geological process, can predict subsidences and fractures, can work with stone at levels previous generations couldn't reach.

Some form more profound connections with the vegetation, their own metabolism synchronizing with the mat-plants until the boundary between human and plant becomes genuinely unclear. They begin processing nutrients differently, growing slower, thinking in plant-time, experiencing life as continuity rather than discrete moments.

Some become temporally flexible themselves, able to exist slightly out-of-phase with normal time, to perceive probability directly, to navigate the plateau's spatial instabilities instinctively. They become living route-maps, guides through impossible geometry.

These transformations are rare, are always chosen (even if unconsciously), and are irreversible. They represent the Yathren evolving beyond baseline humanity into something adapted not just to the plateau's environment but to its essential nature.

The Yathren who undergo these transformations are not feared or revered but recognized as pioneers, as scouts into possibility, as demonstrations of what long-term inhabitation of this place might mean.

## The Seventh Gift: Witness

The Gardener's final gift is the simplest and perhaps the most profound: attention.

Before, the plateau was alone in its dreaming, or nearly alone, with only the Yathren—themselves uncertain, still learning—to witness what it was doing.

Now the Gardener watches, and its attention matters.

The plateau becomes aware of being witnessed by something adequate to it, something working on similar timescales, something engaged in comparable questions. This awareness changes what the plateau is willing to try, what experiments it's willing to conduct.

The spatial instabilities begin to form more complex patterns. The crystalline veins grow in more elaborate configurations. The vegetation explores stranger possibilities. The plateau becomes more creative, more playful even, in its dreaming, because creativity requires audience, requires recognition, requires the knowledge that someone capable of appreciating what you're doing is paying attention.

The Yathren feel this change without understanding it. The plateau seems more responsive, more communicative, more willing to collaborate. Stone-singing becomes easier, or not easier but more mutual, more like genuine conversation. Water-routes stabilize in more helpful configurations. Plant-cultivation produces more interesting results.

And the Gardener ensures that this attention will continue, that even when it must turn to other gardens, other places requiring care, a part of its awareness will always remain here, watching, appreciating, bearing witness to whatever the plateau becomes across the next million years.

## What Changes, What Remains

With these gifts given, the plateau enters a new era—not dramatically different, but subtly altered, its potential expanded, its inhabitants offered more time and warmth and connection to pursue the patient work.

The cities remain sparse, but they grow very slowly more substantial. Buildings last longer. More children survive. Knowledge accumulates more reliably. The settlements become more permanent while maintaining their essential character of emptiness and space.

The Yathren continue their slow work, but with greater hope, with deeper understanding, with more certainty that what they're learning won't be lost, that their effort contributes to something that will persist, that will matter across timescales that dwarf individual lives.

Visitors from below still come, still find the place strange and difficult, still usually leave. But occasionally now, someone arrives who can feel the change, who senses that something here is offering possibility, offering transformation for those willing to accept it. These visitors stay more often, integrate more successfully, adding to the Yathren population, bringing new perspectives while surrendering to the old patience.

The plateau itself becomes slightly more stable—not in its geometry, which remains beautifully flexible—but in its fundamental nature. The reality instabilities continue, but they're creative instabilities now, generative rather than merely chaotic. The plateau is still questioning what space and time might be, but it's asking more sophisticated questions, with the Gardener's attention helping it formulate its curiosity more precisely.

And something else happens, something the Gardener intended but that emerges organically from the gifts given:

The Yathren begin to develop culture in the deeper sense—not just survival protocols and practical knowledge, but philosophy, art, genuine inquiry into meaning and purpose and what existence on the plateau might ultimately signify.

Memory-keepers start composing long contemplative texts about the plateau's nature. Stone-singers create increasingly elaborate works that are sculpture and music and architecture simultaneously. Plant-cultivators design gardens that are living meditations on growth and time and transformation.

The listening-pools become sites of philosophical dialogue, Yathren across vast distances engaging in conversations that take years to complete, thoughts passed back and forth across weeks, arguments that span generations.

A genuine plateau culture emerges, distinct from lowland cultures, adequate to this place, capable of asking the questions this place demands: What is time when it's flexible? What is space when it won't hold still? What is human when humanity can be negotiated? What is consciousness when distributed across stone and plant and people and deep dreaming?

## The Long Result

Millennia pass—quickly for the Gardener, slowly for the plateau, very slowly indeed for the Yathren but still meaningfully, generations accumulating into eras.

The Yathren persist, transformed but recognizable, still human in essential ways but also something new, something that couldn't exist anywhere else. They are plateau-people now, truly indigenous to this place, shaped by it as it is partly shaped by them.

The settlements remain sparse but become more elaborate, more beautiful, more conscious of themselves as art as well as habitation. Buildings last thousands of years, their stone-singing so perfect they become permanent features, landmarks that anchor geography.

The population never grows large—the plateau can't support density and doesn't want to—but it stabilizes at sustainable levels, enough for culture and continuity, not enough to overwhelm the essential emptiness.

Knowledge accumulates reliably now, transmitted through the plateau's own memory, through the listening-pools, through the very old who persist for centuries, through direct encounters in the deep dreaming zones. The Yathren develop sophistication, wisdom, genuine expertise in matters that no other culture can even conceptualize.

They become consultants to the plateau itself, partners in its dreaming, collaborators in its slow investigation of what reality might be willing to become. Some of them transform entirely, merge with stone or vegetation or light or the geometry itself, become permanent features of the landscape, living monuments to the long patience.

And the plateau, witnessed and appreciated and collaborated with, flourishes in its own strange way. It becomes more complex, more beautiful, more consciously engaged in its work. It creates structures and patterns that other plateaus in other worlds might someday learn from, architectural possibilities and geometric innovations and temporal configurations that expand what stone can be.

The Gardener watches, satisfied.

This is what benevolent power does: not rescue, not impose, not force flourishing, but provide conditions—time, warmth, connection, possibility—and then witness patiently what emerges.

The plateau and its people needed no saving. They were already doing the work, already engaged in the patient inquiry. They needed only gifts that respected what they were, that enhanced rather than replaced their adaptation, that offered opportunity without demanding specific outcomes.

The Gardener has given these gifts. The plateau and the Yathren will do the rest, slowly, across timeframes that make civilizations seem brief, working toward understandings that will take eons to achieve.

And when, in some distant age, they reach whatever conclusion their work drives toward—when the plateau finishes its questions or reformulates them into new questions, when the Yathren complete their transformation or pass their knowledge to whatever comes after—the Gardener will still be watching, appreciating, bearing witness to what patience and cold and stone and light and human determination can accomplish when given time enough to try.

This is the gift of benevolent power: not salvation but recognition, not intervention but attention, not rescue but respect for the slow work of becoming adequate to impossible places and the questions they ask.

The plateau dreams on, no longer alone.

The Yathren walk their patient routes, now carrying hope alongside their careful knowledge.

The stone sings, heard and appreciated.

The emptiness remains empty but feels less lonely, more spacious, more generous.

And the cold—the cold remains cold, as it must, because some things should not be changed.

But now there is warmth enough, and time enough, and connection enough, and understanding enough to continue the work, the long patient work of living on top of weather, of inhabiting vastness, of participating in stone's slow dreaming under immense sky.
