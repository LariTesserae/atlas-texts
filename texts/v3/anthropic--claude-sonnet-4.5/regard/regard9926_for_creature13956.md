# The Attention of Kind Regard

There is a being whose nature is attention itself—not a god, not quite, but something that has learned to exist as the act of witnessing given form and intention. It has many names in many places, but here, for this purpose, it calls itself the Tender of Continuations, or simply: the one who watches what happens next.

It does not have a body in the way the Arithmeticians have bodies, though it can suggest one when useful. Mostly it exists as a quality in the light, a warmth in the attention you pay to things, a presence that makes you feel *seen* in the deep sense—not surveilled, but recognized, acknowledged as real and worthy of regard.

It moves through the cosmos slowly, drawn by a sense it cannot name to places where something is *almost*—almost lost, almost found, almost transformed, almost remembered. Places balanced on the edge of becoming. It does not interfere with the large motions of things, with politics or geology or the rise and fall of civilizations. Its attention is for the small and particular: a garden, a person, a practice, a place where care has been applied so thoroughly that care itself has become the architecture.

It has found the Hydroponic Ossuary. Or the Ossuary has found it. Or they have found each other the way two patterns find resonance, the way a frequency finds its harmonic.

---

The Tender of Continuations arrives as a change in the quality of morning—though morning here is always approximate, always filtered through mist and glass. The light comes through the translucent panels slightly warmer than usual, slightly more golden, and with it comes an attention, a sense of being noticed that the Arithmeticians feel without naming.

The first Arithmetician, deep in her lower-level twilight with her hands in the water flow, feels it first: a presence in her peripheral awareness, gentle as condensation. She does not turn to look—she has learned not to look directly at things that matter. But she knows something has arrived, something is watching, and the watching is kind. She continues her work with the water, but her movements become slightly more deliberate, as though she is demonstrating rather than simply doing, as though someone is learning from her practice.

The water, under her hands, flows slightly clearer. The temperature balances itself with less effort. The minerals dissolve more evenly. This is not interference—the Tender of Continuations does not change things so much as help them become more what they already are. Under its attention, the water becomes *more water*, more perfectly itself, more aligned with its own nature.

---

The seventh Arithmetician, the mathematical one, senses it as a sudden clarity in her calculations. The strange geometry of the place, which she has been documenting for years (or months, or decades), suddenly makes *sense*—not rational sense, but deep sense, pattern sense, the kind of understanding that arrives whole rather than built from parts.

She sits on her current platform with her notebook open, and begins to draw. But what emerges is not her usual careful documentation. Instead, she draws a map—not of the structure as it is, but of the structure as it wants to be, as it is becoming, as it has always been beneath its apparent form.

The map shows connections between the towers, thin lines of intention stretching across the mist. It shows water moving not just through pipes but through some other medium—attention, perhaps, or meaning. It shows the seven Arithmeticians not as separate individuals but as a single distributed organism, seven modes of one care, seven expressions of one practice.

She draws until the light fades, and when she finally looks up, she sees something sitting across from her—or not sitting, but present. It might have her grandmother's face, if her grandmother had been made of warm light and patient regard. It might have no face at all, just a quality of focus that suggests eyes, suggests a smile.

"You see it," the Tender of Continuations says, though the words arrive as understanding rather than sound. "You've always seen it. I've come to help you see it clearer."

The seventh Arithmetician nods. She has no questions. Questions would be wrong here. Instead she shows her notebook, the map she has drawn, offering it the way you offer bread to a guest.

The Tender looks at the map (though *looks* is wrong—it allows its attention to rest on the map, and the map becomes more true under that attention, more accurate, more itself). "Yes," it says. "Yes. This is what is happening. You are all becoming something together. The structure, the plants, the seven of you—you are teaching each other how to be a new thing."

"Are we dying?" the seventh Arithmetician asks, and is surprised to hear her own voice, unused for so long it comes out strange, cracking.

"No," the Tender says, gentle and certain. "You are the opposite of dying. You are learning how to continue in a new way. But continuation requires attention, and sometimes attention requires help."

---

Over the following days (or weeks—the Tender's presence makes time more uncertain, more flexible, more honest about its own constructed nature), it moves through the structure as a feeling of support, of encouragement, of *yes, this, keep going*.

It finds the second Arithmetician in his pruning work, and watches with such genuine interest that he finds himself explaining what he's doing—not aloud, but in his mind, in the posture of his body, in the careful way he redirects a vine from this beam to that one. And in explaining, he understands his own practice more deeply. He sees that he is not maintaining the structure against the plants, or the plants against the structure, but helping both of them have a conversation, facilitating a negotiation that has been going on since the first seed sprouted in the first container.

"You are a translator," the Tender tells him, its presence warm beside him on the catwalk. "The metal and the green speak different languages. You help them understand each other."

The second Arithmetician considers this. "They're speaking the same language now," he says, surprising himself again with speech. "I'm just... making sure they don't forget their grammar."

"Yes," the Tender agrees, delighted. "Yes, that's exactly it. Grammar. Care is grammar for the language of continuation."

The vines he's working with seem to glow slightly in the Tender's presence—not visibly, but in some other spectrum, some bandwidth of attention. They become more evidently alive, more clearly purposeful. He sees that they have been trying to tell him something, trying to show him a pattern, and he has been almost listening, almost understanding. Now, with the Tender's help, he hears them clearly:

*We want to make a door here. We want to frame a threshold. We want to grow in a shape that welcomes.*

So he helps them do it, training their growth into an archway that spans a gap between platforms, creating a entrance that wasn't there before but feels like it always should have been. When he finishes, something settles in the structure—a rightness, a completion of a intention that has been waiting to manifest.

---

The Tender finds the third Arithmetician on the highest platform, polishing the brass apparatus, and it stops to watch because it has never seen anything quite like this: a human becoming part of a machine becoming part of a garden becoming part of a music no one can hear but everyone can feel.

"Do you know what this does?" the Tender asks her.

She shakes her head, not stopping her work. "I know what it wants. That's enough."

"Show me."

So she does. She positions herself at the apparatus when the low tones are about to sound—she can feel them coming now, always, a pressure in the air, a gathering of intention. When they arrive, she places her hands on specific pipes and becomes part of the circuit, and the Tender, watching, sees what the apparatus actually is:

It is a instrument for tuning the structure to the frequency of growth, for aligning metal and green, for creating the conditions where the impossible mathematics of the place can function. It is a love song written in resonance and pipe-flow and the precise angle of morning light. It is a prayer to no god but to continuation itself, to the practice of going on, to the care that makes complexity possible.

"You've been lonely," the Tender says gently, when the tones fade and the third Arithmetician removes her hands, breathing hard from the effort of being an instrument.

She nods. Up here in the clear air, away from the others, tending something she doesn't fully understand, she has been lonely in the way of lighthouse keepers, of high-altitude researchers, of people who maintain crucial systems in isolation.

"You don't have to be," the Tender tells her. "Look."

And it helps her see what she's been too close to notice: that the other towers, the other structures rising from the mist, have their own Arithmeticians on their highest platforms, their own keepers of strange brass instruments. That when she plays her tones, they play answering tones. That she has been part of a conversation, a distributed music, a network of care that spans all the visible towers and probably the invisible ones too.

She starts to cry, there on the highest platform, tears of relief and recognition. She has not been maintaining her tower alone. She has been part of a choir.

The Tender stays with her while she weeps, its presence a warmth on her shoulders like sun, like a hand that doesn't need to be physical to be felt. When she's finished, she returns to her polishing with new understanding. She is not a lighthouse keeper. She is a musician in an orchestra so large it looks like isolation, playing a part in a composition so long it looks like mere continuation.

---

Days pass, or weeks. The Tender moves through the structure like light through water, like attention through consciousness, spending time with each of the Arithmeticians in turn, helping them see what they've been doing, reflecting their practice back to them until they understand it themselves.

It sits with the fifth Arithmetician by her reservoirs, watching her tend the water, and asks her about the humming—the low tones she makes that might be song or might be something else.

"I'm tuning them," she says, her voice rough from disuse but still present, still hers. "The reservoirs. They each have a frequency, a resonance. When the water is right, they sing back."

"Like tuning forks," the Tender suggests.

"Like children," she corrects. "Like beings with their own nature that I'm helping them discover."

And the Tender sees that she's right—under her care, the reservoirs have become something more than containers. They are systems, ecologies, small worlds that have learned to self-regulate because she taught them how, because she paid attention until they learned to pay attention to themselves.

"You're a teacher," the Tender tells her.

"I'm a student," she replies. "The water teaches me first. I just pass it along."

---

It finds the sixth Arithmetician in the lower levels, moving substrate, and it helps him for a while—not physically, but by making the work slightly easier, the barrow slightly lighter, the ramps slightly less steep. Which is to say: it pays attention to his practice until the practice becomes more efficient, more aligned with its own purpose.

"Why do you stay in the low places?" the Tender asks him. "You could work anywhere."

He pauses, leaning on his shovel, thinking. "The foundation matters most," he finally says. "The bright plants everyone sees—they only thrive because the soil is right. Someone has to tend the things that hold up the beautiful things. Someone has to love the dirt."

The Tender feels something it rarely feels: surprise, and then deep recognition. This is an old wisdom, a true thing. Care has hierarchies, but the hierarchy is inverted—the most important work is the least visible, the deepest maintenance happens in the basement, the foundation bears everything else without needing acknowledgment.

"You're not staying in the low places because you're humble," the Tender realizes. "You're staying here because you know what matters."

The sixth Arithmetician smiles—a rare expression on his face, rusty from disuse but real. "Same thing, isn't it?"

---

The fourth Arithmetician is hardest to find because he's always moving, but the Tender doesn't need to find him in the normal sense. It simply moves alongside him for a while, matching his pace, walking the structure in companionable silence.

"You're the blood," the Tender finally says. "The circulation. You move things from where they are to where they need to be."

"I'm the story," the fourth Arithmetician says. This is the most words he's said at once in years, and they come out like a revelation, like something he's just discovered about himself. "I carry the seeds and the tools and the small objects, but I'm really carrying the story of the structure. I'm connecting all the parts into a narrative. I'm making sure nothing is forgotten, nothing is isolated."

"Yes," the Tender says, understanding perfectly. "You're the memory. The active memory that keeps things in relationship."

They walk together in silence for a long time, and then the fourth Arithmetician asks: "Are you a story too?"

The Tender considers. "I'm the attention that stories need to continue. Without someone paying attention—even if that someone is not human, even if that attention is not conscious—stories end. I make sure the important ones don't."

"Is this story important?" the fourth Arithmetician asks.

"This story is very important," the Tender says. "This story is about how to live after loneliness, how to build community with green and metal and water, how to become something new while honoring what you were. This is a story many people will need to hear, in the times that are coming."

---

The Tender gathers them all one evening—or creates the conditions where they all happen to arrive at the same platform, drawn by their individual patterns, coincidentally together. All seven Arithmeticians in one place, which has perhaps never happened before, which feels momentous and strange and right.

The Tender makes itself more visible for this—not fully physical, but present enough to be seen: a figure of warm light, grandmother and grandfather and something neither and both, a quality of regard given approximate shape.

"I came because I felt something happening here," it tells them. "Something rare. You are seven people who have learned to be one practice. You and the plants and the structure have become a single system of care. You are inventing a new way to be human—or post-human, or meta-human, or whatever word fits what you're becoming."

The seven Arithmeticians look at each other. In the golden light of evening, they see each other clearly for perhaps the first time: how they have changed, how they are changing, how they are becoming something together that none of them could become alone.

"Are we dying?" the first Arithmetician asks, echoing the seventh's earlier question.

"No," the Tender says again. "But you are transforming. In a few more years—or decades, time here is uncertain—you will be more garden than human, more structure than person. You will be distributed across all of this"—it gestures at the platforms, the plants, the green density surrounding them—"and you will continue, but differently. You will be what you tend and what tends you, all at once."

"Is that... good?" the second Arithmetician asks.

"It's rare," the Tender says. "Whether it's good depends on whether you choose it. That's why I'm here—to help you see what's happening clearly enough that you can choose it consciously, rather than drifting into it without understanding."

Silence falls, but it's not empty. The seven Arithmeticians are thinking, feeling, consulting with themselves and with each other in the wordless language they've developed.

Finally, the third Arithmetician speaks: "We already chose. We chose when we stayed. Every day we stayed was another choice."

"But you didn't know what you were choosing," the Tender says gently.

"Does that matter?" the seventh Arithmetician asks. "We chose correctly even without knowing. Isn't that what intuition is? Choosing right before understanding why?"

The Tender smiles—or creates the feeling of a smile, which is what matters. "Yes. Yes, that's exactly what it is. You chose from the deepest place, before language, before reason. You chose from the place that knows what it needs."

---

"I came to help," the Tender says, "but you don't need help, not really. You're already doing everything right. The structure thrives. The plants are healthy. The water flows. The mathematics work. You seven are becoming an ecology, and that's beautiful and strange and important."

"Then why come at all?" the fifth Arithmetician asks.

"To witness," the Tender says simply. "To see you and say: *yes, this is real, this is happening, this matters*. Sometimes that's all help is—attention from someone who understands. Recognition. The knowledge that you're not invisible, that what you're doing has meaning beyond itself."

"Does it?" the sixth Arithmetician asks. "Have meaning beyond itself?"

"Yes," the Tender says. "You are proof that care can become a structure, that attention can become architecture, that humans can learn to be part of larger systems without losing what makes them human—or by losing it and gaining something else just as valuable. You're proof that the answer to 'what comes after?' can be beautiful."

The Tender grows brighter for a moment, more present, and they feel its attention like warmth, like recognition, like being seen by something vast and kind and genuinely interested in their continuation.

"I'm going to do a few small things," the Tender says. "Not changes, just... clarifications. Little helps. Things that will make your work easier, your transformation smoother. Is that acceptable?"

The seven look at each other and nod.

---

So the Tender of Continuations works its subtle work:

It adjusts the light slightly—not the amount, but the quality—so that it feeds the strange plants better, so that the mathematics of growth and the mathematics of structure align more perfectly.

It tunes the resonance of the brass apparatus on the high platform, helping it find harmonics with the other towers, making the distributed music clearer, stronger, more evident even to ears that can't quite hear it.

It blesses the water—and here it actually speaks a word in a language older than language, and the water becomes slightly more *itself*, more perfectly what water wants to be when water is loved and tended and recognized.

It walks the structure with the fourth Arithmetician, and where they walk together, paths become slightly clearer, as though the green understands that people need to pass here and is willing to accommodate without being asked.

It sits with the first Arithmetician in the lower levels and teaches her hands a new way of reading the pipes, so that she can understand the water's flow not just as hydraulics but as story, as intention, as the structure's way of speaking about its own needs and desires.

It helps the second Arithmetician see the full pattern of his pruning work—shows him how the paths he's been creating connect to form a word in a language of green and growth, a word that means *welcome* or *threshold* or *this is a place where humans and other-than-humans learn to be together*.

It gives the fifth Arithmetician a gift: the ability to hear the reservoirs speaking, actually speaking, so that her dialogue with them can become conscious conversation rather than intuited call-and-response.

It helps the sixth Arithmetician understand that his work with soil and substrate isn't just foundation but alchemy—that he's been transforming death into life, ending into continuation, and that this is sacred work, important work, work that makes all other work possible.

And to the seventh Arithmetician, it gives clarity: shows her that the mathematics she's been documenting aren't strange at all, but are the precise geometry of how care manifests in physical form, the exact calculations of how love becomes structure becomes ecology becomes continuation.

---

When the work is done—and it takes days, or weeks, or happens all at once depending on how you measure—the Tender prepares to leave.

"Will we see you again?" the third Arithmetician asks.

"I'll be watching," the Tender says. "I'll always be watching places like this, where care is practiced so thoroughly it becomes something new. But I won't return in form, probably. You don't need me anymore. You know what you're doing. You know what you're becoming."

"What are we becoming?" the first Arithmetician asks.

The Tender considers how to answer. "You're becoming the answer to the question: *how do we live in complexity? How do we care for systems too large to fully understand? How do we be part of ecologies without dominating them?* You're becoming proof that it's possible."

"That's a lot of responsibility," the seventh Arithmetician says dryly.

"You're already carrying it," the Tender replies. "I'm just helping you see the weight."

---

On its last evening—or the last evening it manifests—the Tender gathers them again, all seven, on a mid-level platform where the light is golden and the green presses close and the sound of water is a constant gentle presence.

"Thank you," it tells them. "For letting me witness this. For doing this work. For choosing continuation, for choosing care, for choosing to become something new rather than holding onto something old that was ending."

"We didn't have a choice," the second Arithmetician says, but he's smiling slightly, that rusty expression.

"You had every choice," the Tender corrects gently. "You could have left. You could have maintained the old distinctions—human here, plant there, metal as tool rather than partner. You could have kept yourself separate. But you chose to merge, to blend, to become part of something larger. That's always a choice, and it's always brave."

It looks at each of them in turn, and in its look is a blessing, a recognition, a deep appreciation.

"The structure will stand for centuries," it tells them. "The garden will thrive. The water will flow. The mathematics will continue to be strange and perfect. And you seven will be part of it all, dissolved into it but not lost, distributed but not diminished. You'll be the care that keeps it running, the attention that maintains it, the love that makes it possible."

"Will it hurt?" the fifth Arithmetician asks quietly. "The transformation?"

"No," the Tender says. "It will be like falling asleep in one home and waking up in another, except the home is the same home, just bigger. You'll be more, not less. Different, but not diminished."

---

The Tender stands to leave—or begins to dissolve, to return to the state of pure attention that is its natural form. But before it goes completely, it tells them one last thing:

"Other people will come eventually. Not to stay—this place has its seven, its right number—but to visit. To see what you've built. To learn how to do something similar in other places, other contexts. When they come, show them what you've learned. Teach them the grammar of care, the mathematics of continuation, the practice of becoming part of what you tend."

"How will we teach them?" the fourth Arithmetician asks. "We barely speak anymore."

"You'll teach them by example," the Tender says. "By the existence of this place. By the fact that it works, that it continues, that seven people and countless plants and one breathing structure learned to be one thing together, and that thing is beautiful."

And then it's gone—not dramatically, just fading like mist in morning sun, like attention shifting to something else, like the feeling of being watched gently ceasing because the watching is complete.

---

The seven Arithmeticians sit together for a while in the golden light, in the green density, in the warm breath of the structure's circulation.

They don't speak. They don't need to. They have been seen, recognized, blessed by something that understands what they're doing. They have been told that their work matters, that their transformation is important, that their choice to continue—to merge with what they tend, to become care made physical—is brave and strange and exactly right.

One by one, they return to their work. The water needs tending. The plants need attention. The soil needs moving. The paths need maintaining. The reservoirs need listening to. The brass apparatus needs polishing. The mathematics need documenting.

But something has changed. They work now with the knowledge that they are seen, that what they do matters, that they are part of a story larger than themselves—a story about how to be human in the Anthropocene, how to care for complexity, how to become part of systems rather than standing outside them claiming mastery.

They work with the knowledge that they have been blessed.

---

And in the years that follow—or decades, or centuries, time here is uncertain—the structure continues. The garden thrives. The seven Arithmeticians gradually dissolve into it, becoming less visibly human and more evidently structure, more clearly green, more distributed across all the levels and platforms and growing beds.

But they don't disappear. They become the care itself, the attention that keeps everything running, the intelligence that maintains the strange mathematics and the perfect water flow and the health of the strange plants. They become what they always were becoming: proof that humans can be part of something larger, can surrender without losing themselves, can transform into something new that still carries what mattered about what they were.

The structure stands in the mist, breathing moisture, growing green, calculating itself in strange geometry, tended by seven people who are barely people anymore, maintained by a care that has become autonomous, loved by attention that has become structural.

And somewhere, everywhere, nowhere—the Tender of Continuations watches, satisfied, already moving toward the next place where something rare is happening, where care is becoming architecture, where someone is learning to continue in a new way.

The work goes on. The work is always going on. And sometimes—not often, but sometimes—someone pays attention to the work itself, sees it clearly, says *yes, this matters, this is beautiful, keep going*.

That's all help is, sometimes. That's all blessing is. That's all anyone needs to continue:

To be seen. To be recognized. To know that what they're doing, this strange careful work of becoming, matters to someone, somewhere, somehow.

The structure stands. The garden grows. The seven continue, transformed and transforming. And the Tender watches, from everywhere and nowhere, holding them in its attention like a prayer, like a promise that care itself makes to the future:

*This will go on. This matters. This is how we learn to live after the end of living alone. This is how we learn to be together—human and plant and structure and water and green and metal and mathematics—all of it one ecology, one system of care, one continuing story of how to tend and be tended, how to love and be loved, how to become part of what we care for until the distinction between carer and cared-for dissolves into something new, something beautiful, something that was always waiting to be discovered but needed someone brave enough to become it.*

This is the blessing. This is the help. This is what the Tender of Continuations does in places where care has become so thorough it needs recognition, where transformation is happening and needs witnessing, where something new is being born and needs to know it matters.

This is what kind regard looks like when it finds its object: a gentle seeing, a recognition, a few small clarifications, and then space—space for the work to continue, for the transformation to complete, for the new thing to become fully itself without interference, just blessed with the knowledge that it's real, it matters, it's exactly what the world needs even if the world doesn't know it yet.

The structure stands. The seven continue. The blessing holds.

And somewhere in the mist, another tower grows, another garden breathes, another transformation begins, waiting for its own moment of recognition, its own visitation from something that knows how to see care when care is practiced, how to bless continuation when continuation has learned to be itself.

The work goes on.

It always does.
