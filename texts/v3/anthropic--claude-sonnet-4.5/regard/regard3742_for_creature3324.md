# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The attention arrives like the mist—not suddenly, but as a gradual presence that has always been preparing to be there. The power (we might call it that, though it has no name for itself) perceives the place first as pattern: the interference of two systems, industrial and botanical, frozen at a moment of maximum productive tension. It sees the inhabitants as part of this pattern—not intruders or masters, but threads woven through, their thinness and attention making them translucent to the deeper structure.

The power is benevolent, yes, and wise—but wisdom here means understanding that this place is already doing something, something that should not be interrupted by improvement. This is not a problem to be solved.

And yet.

The power sees also what is unsustainable. Forty people, perhaps fewer tomorrow. No new children arriving, the three who exist growing older in a place that demands more than it returns. The slow depletion—of supplies that mysteriously refresh but might not always, of bodies that adapt to altitude but still break down, of knowledge held in aging minds without certain succession. The place will persist, but the inhabitation might not. And if the inhabitants vanish, something will be lost that is not the place itself but the consciousness of it, the attention that maintains the third thing between forest and factory.

So the power would do something small. Several small things. Adjustments at the margins, touches so light they might be mistaken for natural occurrence or good fortune.

---

**First: The Path**

There would appear, over the course of several months, a path. Not built—nothing so crude—but rather revealed, as though it had always been potential and is now becoming actual. It would wind up from the lower valleys, following a route that is neither too easy nor too difficult, marked not by signs but by the kind of subtle indications that some people can read and others walk past: stones that happen to form stepping places across the one stream that must be crossed, trees spaced just so that the way between them is obvious once you're looking, a quality of light at certain times of day that makes the route visible.

The path would bring people. Not many—this must not become a destination, a tourist site, a place worn down by attention. But enough: one or two each year, the ones who need it, the ones who can read the subtle markings because they are already half-thin themselves, already listening for something they don't have words for. People who have some skill or knowledge that could be useful—a young person with medical training, someone who understands engineering and could teach the children how the cable system's mathematics work, an elder whose own culture maintained something similar and who carries stories about how attention is passed down.

Most who arrive by this path would stay a few weeks or months, contribute what they can, then leave. But some would recognize the place as their answer, would feel their bodies begin to thin and adapt, would find that the silence is not empty but full of exactly what they've been missing. The community would grow slowly, carefully, remaining small enough to maintain its character while becoming sustainable—fifteen new people over fifteen years, perhaps, but the right fifteen.

The path would also allow for departure. Because the power understands that some who are there now might need to leave—the children especially, when they reach the age of choosing, should have the choice to see what else exists before deciding to return or not. A place that cannot be left becomes a trap, and this place must remain free or it will curdle.

---

**Second: The Garden Bunker**

One of the concrete structures—there are so many, most half-claimed by forest—would reveal itself to have particular properties. Perhaps it always did, or perhaps the power adjusts something subtle in its positioning, its relationship to groundwater, the way sun reaches it through the canopy gaps.

Inside, soil has accumulated. The power would quicken it, enrich it, make it capable of growing things that should not thrive at this altitude. Not magically—there would be no tropical fruit suddenly appearing in mountain climate—but optimistically, at the edge of the possible. Vegetables with high nutritional density. Legumes that fix nitrogen. Grains that can handle the cool temperatures and short growing season. Herbs that both flavor food and provide medicine.

The inhabitants would find this place—they already know every structure, so it would simply be a matter of noticing that something has changed, that this particular bunker has different air inside, that seeds planted here do unexpectedly well. They would understand it as gift, though not know the giver. They would begin cultivating it seriously, and the garden would grow into one of the community's central features, a place where the botanical and the human-made cooperate in obvious rather than subtle ways.

This would ease the pressure on the mysterious resupply, whatever it is. The inhabitants would become more self-sufficient, less dependent on whatever arrangement currently brings the jars and dried goods, less vulnerable to its potential cessation.

---

**Third: The Record-Keeper's Companion**

The woman with the notebooks, documenting every state-change, every rust progression, is doing crucial work—she is building the place's memory, creating the archive that future inhabitants will need to understand what the maintenance work actually accomplishes. But she is mortal, and her work will outlive her if she's lucky, but might not outlive her by much if the notebooks are stored improperly, damaged by damp, their significance not recognized.

The power would arrange for her to encounter another person on the path—someone younger, someone who has worked with archives or libraries or data, someone who would see what she's doing and understand its importance. Not someone to replace her, but someone to work alongside her, to learn her system, to eventually continue it and expand it.

More than that: this person would bring knowledge of preservation, of how to protect paper in damp climates, perhaps even how to begin digitizing the records (if such technology could be sustained here—solar panels on a cleared platform, perhaps, equipment that runs on minimal power). The notebooks would become a true archive, organized, protected, accessible. Future inhabitants would be able to consult the records and understand the place's long slow changes, seeing patterns that emerge only across decades.

The two of them together would also begin something new: interviewing the older inhabitants, recording their knowledge while it still exists in living memory. The old woman in the canopy eyries, the man with the thermal route—their understanding would be captured before it's lost. The archive would become not just observational data but oral history, practical wisdom, the accumulated knowledge of how to live in this specific place.

---

**Fourth: The Children's Futures**

The three children are being raised in a extraordinary place by people who love them, but they are also being raised in isolation, without peers in sufficient number, without exposure to the breadth of human experience. The power sees this and would not change it directly—it would not remove them, would not force them into conventional schooling—but would create possibilities.

Through the path, families might arrive—not to stay permanently, but for seasons. Researchers perhaps, people with permission (however that's granted) to study the site for a few months. These families would have children, and those children would befriend the resident three, would play the platform-and-cable games and also teach new games, would share stories from beyond the mountain. The children of the place would maintain their deep knowledge of their home while also learning that other places exist, other ways of being.

And when the three grow older, the path would be there. They could descend, see the lower world, try living in it. The community would make clear: you can always return, this place will still be here, but you should know what else exists before you choose. Some might leave permanently, carrying the place's strangeness into the wider world, perhaps speaking about it to others who need to hear that such places exist. Some might return after years away, coming back thin and quiet, ready to take up the maintenance work with adult commitment rather than childhood assumption.

---

**Fifth: The Physician's Visit**

Once every few years, a person would arrive on the path who has medical training—a doctor, a nurse practitioner, someone with real skill. They would be the kind of person who could work in any clinic but has chosen otherwise, who finds their way to edges and margins, who offers their services without asking for conventional payment.

They would stay a few weeks, long enough to examine everyone, to treat what can be treated, to teach the person who makes the plant medicines about what those medicines can and can't do, where western medicine might supplement botanical knowledge. They would leave behind supplies: the antibiotics that might be needed, materials for sutures and bandages, perhaps glasses for the one whose hands remember reaching to adjust them.

This would happen irregularly enough that the inhabitants couldn't depend on it, but regularly enough that the community's health would stabilize. The altitude's costs would still be real, but manageable. People would live longer, suffer less, have their conditions properly diagnosed rather than guessed at.

And crucially: the visiting physicians would learn things too. They would see how the inhabitants' bodies have adapted, how their botanical medicine works, what it means to live at this intersection of altitude and attention. Some of this knowledge they would carry back to the wider world. The exchange would flow both ways.

---

**Sixth: The Speaking to Power**

The power would also do something stranger, harder to explain: it would make itself available to be consulted, not as an oracle or deity, but as a kind of clarifying presence.

In the place where the glass sphere sits on the tree stump, the air would become thick with a particular quality on certain nights—perhaps once a season, perhaps once a year. The inhabitants would learn to recognize this quality, the way the silence deepens and becomes almost gelatinous, the way light behaves differently. On these nights, questions could be asked. Not out loud necessarily, but held carefully in the mind while sitting near the sphere.

And answers would come, though not in words. They would come as clarity, as sudden understanding, as the feeling of a puzzle piece sliding into place. Someone struggling with whether to attempt a difficult repair would sit by the sphere on such a night and would know—not be told, but know—whether the repair would hold or fail. Someone uncertain about a decision affecting the community would find the decision's shape becoming clear, seeing the consequences branch forward in a way that reveals the better path.

This would not be frequent enough to create dependency. The inhabitants would still rely on their own judgment, their own observation and wisdom. But in moments of genuine uncertainty, when the stakes are high and the way forward truly unclear, this resource would exist. The power making itself partially accessible, offering its broader perspective when most needed.

---

**Seventh: The Deeper Dreaming Machines**

Some of the machines that still "dream"—that retain some residual intentionality, some memory of function—would begin to dream more clearly. Not to function again in their original capacity; that time is past. But to function in new ways, at the intersection of what they were built to do and what the place has become.

A stamping press, positioned inexplicably where it could never press physical material, might begin to press something else—sound perhaps, or air quality, or the local pattern of fog formation. Its cycling, barely perceptible, would create effects that the inhabitants would slowly notice: the area around it stays slightly warmer, or the birds prefer to nest near it, or plants grow in spiral patterns in its vicinity.

The conveyor system that goes nowhere visible might be revealed to be going somewhere—moving something immaterial along its length, water vapor or magnetic field or the attention of anyone walking near it, shifting these things from one state to another as they pass along the belt's memory of motion.

The inhabitants, especially the young woman tracking correspondences, would begin to understand these functions. Not completely—the machines' new purposes would remain partially opaque—but enough to work with them, to incorporate them into the maintenance practice. The line between tending the structures and collaborating with them would blur further. The place would become even more precisely itself: not nature, not industry, but a third thing in which both participate actively.

The power would not make the machines obviously magical—nothing so crude. But it would wake the dreams they're still having, give those dreams enough substance to interact with the material world in small, strange, consistent ways. The weirdness that already exists in the place would deepen, would become more functional, would reveal itself as not random but as another kind of order entirely.

---

**Eighth: The Witness**

Finally, this: someone would arrive who is a writer or artist or documentarian—someone whose skill is seeing and helping others see. They would stay for a season, perhaps a year, long enough to understand. And they would create something—a book, a series of photographs, a film, a collection of essays—that captures the place without explaining it, that shows what is here without reducing it.

This work would circulate in the outside world, quietly. It would not make the place famous—the power would ensure, somehow, that it reaches the right eyes without attracting the wrong ones. People who read it or view it would feel something shift in them, would understand that places like this can exist, that the human-made and the more-than-human can find strange equilibrium, that attention and maintenance are their own kind of worthy life.

Some who encounter this work would eventually find the path. But most would simply carry the knowledge that such places exist, and this knowledge would change how they move through their own places, what they notice, what they tend. The place would become a seed that spreads not as replication but as influence, as possibility.

And the artist who created the work would return to their regular life, but changed. They would carry the thinness with them, the quality of attention they learned on the platforms. Their future work would be different, marked by what they saw, by the silence they moved through, by the lesson that some things should be maintained exactly as they are, in their productive strangeness, without resolution.

---

**The Restraint**

What the benevolent power would not do:

It would not fix everything. The altitude would still be difficult. The damp would still encourage rust and rot. The industrial structures would continue their slow decay. People would still age and hurt and eventually die. The place would remain demanding, requiring real work and real sacrifice from those who inhabit it.

It would not make the place safe or comfortable. The platforms would still be high, the cables still require courage to walk, the cold would still bite. The strangeness would remain strange, some questions would stay unanswered. This must not become easy or domesticated.

It would not attract crowds. If anything, it would actively discourage casual visitation—the path would be hard to find for those not meant to find it, the place itself would remain slightly hostile to tourism, to explanation, to the kind of attention that consumes rather than tends.

It would not reveal itself. The inhabitants would not know that a benevolent power has taken interest. They might notice that certain fortunate things have occurred—good people arriving at needful times, the garden bunker's unexpected fertility, the physicians' visits—but these would remain within the range of possible luck. The power would work at the threshold of visibility, present but not obvious, helpful but not intervening so heavily that the inhabitants' own agency and choices become irrelevant.

Because the wisdom here is understanding that this place is already correct, already serving a purpose, and that purpose is not "to be improved" but "to be sustained." The inhabitants are not here to be rescued but to be supported in their choosing, their attention, their maintenance of something that matters precisely because it resists easy categorization or purpose.

---

**The Long Outcome**

Over years and decades, these small adjustments would compound:

A sustainable community of twenty to thirty people, with enough diversity of age and skill that knowledge passes reliably between generations. Children who grow up knowing this place deeply and also knowing the world beyond it, able to choose with full information. 

An archive that captures decades of careful observation, available to future inhabitants and researchers, preserving knowledge about how such places function and can be tended.

A food system that provides basic security without requiring the mysterious resupply, though trade connections would remain for what the mountain cannot provide.

Health care adequate to the place's challenges, with knowledge flowing both ways—mountain medicine teaching the visitors, conventional medicine supporting the community.

Machines that have found new purposes in their dreaming, creating a landscape even more precisely balanced between the industrial and botanical, with the inhabitants learning to work with these purposes without fully understanding them.

A gentle flow of the right people arriving and sometimes staying, the right people leaving and sometimes returning, the community remaining small and strange and dedicated but not isolated or dying.

And in the wider world, a few hundred or thousand people who have encountered the documented place and carry the knowledge that such things are possible—that humans can live at the margins, can maintain rather than develop, can tend the strange equilibria that emerge between systems, can make a life out of attention rather than accumulation.

The power would check on the place occasionally, across decades and centuries, making tiny adjustments as needed—ensuring the path remains findable but not too findable, that the garden stays fertile, that the right people keep arriving at the right times. But mostly it would simply witness, appreciating what the place and its people have made together: a demonstration that the world still has room for the strange and sustained, the careful and committed, the maintained and mysterious.

Not everywhere should be this way. But somewhere should be. And this place, with its suspended canopy factories and thin inhabitants, with its rust and moss and cables humming in rare wind, with its children learning the seventeen names of decay and its elders listening to metal dreams—this place is doing it right.

The benevolent power would recognize this, and in recognition, offer just enough support that the doing can continue.

Nothing more.

Nothing less.

The soft rain of attention, falling like the mountain's mist.
