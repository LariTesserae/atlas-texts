# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The attention arrives like a change in water temperature—subtle, pervasive, impossible to pinpoint but unmistakable once noticed. The benevolent power does not announce itself. It has no need of announcement. It simply *attends*, and its attention is itself a kind of action.

First, it watches. For what might be years or only moments—time moves differently for such a power—it observes the city and its inhabitants with a quality of regard that has weight, that has presence. Not the gaze of a scientist cataloging specimens, but something closer to recognition. The way you might watch a beloved's face while they sleep, learning its contours not to possess but to honor.

The power notices what you noticed: the completeness of the system, its terrible fragility disguised as stability. It sees the knife's edge on which everything balances—the gleaners filtering at exactly the rate needed, the browsers grazing at exactly the rate that doesn't destroy, the warmth from below holding steady enough to prevent collapse. It observes how close this place lives to several different kinds of ending.

And the power understands something crucial: this place does not need saving. It needs *continuation*.

## The First Action: Stabilization

The power begins with the warmth. That geothermal heat seeping up through the basin floor is not infinite. The vents that provide it are slowly closing, have been closing for centuries, will close completely in perhaps another century or two. When they do, the water will cool. Not catastrophically, not overnight, but steadily, inevitably. And the ecosystem, calibrated so precisely to blood-warmth, will begin its long unraveling.

The power reaches down through stone and sediment to the heat sources themselves. It does not increase them—that would be interference, would be imposing a foreign design. Instead, it *stabilizes* them. The vents that were narrowing cease to narrow. The flow of heated water that was decreasing holds steady. Nothing increases, nothing improves. Things simply stop degrading.

This is the power's first principle: to preserve without improving, to maintain without controlling. The city found its balance organically, through centuries of blind evolution and accumulated accident. To enhance would be to destroy that organic quality, to turn the living system into a garden, the wild place into a managed park. The power refuses this. It will not make the city better than it is. It will only prevent it from becoming worse.

No creature notices this change, because there is no change to notice. The water remains exactly as warm as it has been. The browsers continue their routes, the gleaners their filtering, the serpents their patrols. Everything continues, and continuation is the point.

## The Second Action: Gentle Barriers

The power turns its attention to the basin's edges, to the places where the city's water meets other water, where the ecosystem's boundaries are permeable in ways that could become catastrophic.

Because the basin is not isolated. Somewhere far away, perhaps kilometers distant horizontally, perhaps above in the upper water layers, human activity continues. Different human activity than built this city, from different civilizations, different ages. These humans are beginning to pollute in ways that could reach here—industrial runoff, plastics that never decay, chemicals that disrupt biology at the molecular level.

The power does not stop human activity. That would exceed its mandate, would be control rather than care. Instead, it creates what might be called a selective membrane at the boundaries of the basin. Water flows in and out as it always has—the system needs that exchange, needs the slow input of nutrients from the surface, the slow export of processed material to the wider ocean. But the membrane catches what should not enter: the synthetic compounds, the heavy metals, the microplastics, the pharmaceutical residues.

These materials simply settle at the boundary, accumulating in a layer that will eventually become stone itself, will fossilize into a marker of the age that produced them. But they do not enter the basin. The city's water remains clean in the way it has been clean, containing only what organic processes produce, only what the ecosystem evolved to handle.

Again, no inhabitant notices. The gleaners filter the same water they have always filtered. The fish-things breathe the same dissolved oxygen. The microscopic communities process the same organic molecules. Everything continues.

## The Third Action: Preventing Discovery

The power considers the problem of human attention—not its own attention, which is benevolent and brief, but human attention in general, which tends toward exploitation, possession, study that kills the studied.

Because humans are beginning to develop technologies that could find this place. Sonar that could map it. Submarines that could reach it. The growing human population is expanding into the ocean in ways that will, within decades or centuries, reach even here. And when that happens, the city will become a discovery, a wonder, a resource.

They will send researchers. They will collect specimens. They will map and catalog and publish and then they will argue about preservation versus access, and eventually they will compromise by allowing limited tourism, regulated harvesting, scientific stations. The city will become known, and in becoming known will cease to be what it is—a place evolving in its own direction, answerable to nothing but its own internal logic.

The power acts to prevent this, but gently, indirectly. It does not make the city invisible—that would be too much interference, would cut it off from necessary exchanges with the wider ocean. Instead, it makes the city *uninteresting* to human sensing technologies.

Sonar sweeps across the basin and returns readings that suggest simple topography—flat floor, unremarkable. The stone of the buildings has been altered by minerals anyway; it reads on instruments as natural reef formation, nothing artificial, nothing warranting closer investigation. Satellites measuring ocean depth find nothing anomalous in this location. Current patterns appear normal. Water chemistry appears normal. Everything suggests this is simply empty basin, one among millions, not worth the expense of direct investigation.

And for humans who do approach physically—explorers, researchers, the occasional adventurer—the power introduces what might be called disinterest. Not a barrier, not a prohibition, but simply a subtle influence that makes people think *perhaps not here, perhaps the next basin over would be more promising*. They turn aside without quite knowing why, following hunches that lead them to other places, other discoveries that satisfy their curiosity equally well.

The city remains unvisited by human attention. The fauna continue their lives unobserved by anyone except you, and you are leaving, or you have already left, or you were never quite there in the first place.

## The Fourth Action: Time

The power's final gift is the hardest to describe because it involves something beyond simple physical intervention. It involves *time itself*, or rather, the quality of time in the basin.

Because all living systems face entropy. All complexity eventually degrades. The second law of thermodynamics is not merely a suggestion. Even with stable heat, even with barriers against pollution, even with protection from discovery, the city's ecosystem must eventually run down. Random mutations accumulate. Populations drift. The precise balance that currently exists is a moment, not a permanence, and moments end.

Unless.

The power does something strange. It adjusts the *rate* at which time passes in the basin. Not dramatically—inhabitants would notice that, and the power's principle is to remain unnoticed. But subtly, at the level of quantum processes, at the scale where time becomes granular rather than smooth, the power introduces a kind of temporal viscosity.

Processes happen, but they happen *slower*. Mutations still occur but at a reduced rate. Populations still shift but the shifts take longer to propagate. The erosion of buildings continues but more gradually. The exhaustion of nutrients proceeds but with greater lag time for replenishment. Everything is slowed by a factor so small it would be meaningless to any individual organism—perhaps one percent, perhaps less—but accumulated over centuries, the effect is profound.

What would have been a century of change becomes a century and one year. Not much. Barely noticeable. But multiply that across all processes, across all lifespans, across the deep time of ecological succession, and the result is that the city's current state will persist for far longer than it would have otherwise. Not forever—the power understands that nothing is forever, that insisting on permanence would be cruelty, would trap the system in amber. But longer. Long enough for the city to be fully what it is, to express completely this strange moment of balance before time and change move it forward into whatever comes next.

The gleaners live their two years or five years, but those years are imperceptibly longer. The browsers follow their routes for a century, but that century stretches slightly beyond a century's usual length. The evolution that would produce new species in a million years will take a million and ten thousand years instead. A small difference. A generous difference.

## The Non-Actions

But there are things the power deliberately does *not* do, and these non-actions are as important as the actions.

It does not increase the food supply. The gleaners filter at the edge of sustainability, the browsers graze sparse growth, the fish-things live with constant hunger. The power could enrich the water, could increase the rain of nutrients from above, could make life easier. It refuses. Ease would change the system's character. The fauna have evolved to thrive in scarcity; abundance would favor different strategies, different body plans, would begin an evolutionary cascade that would end with a different city, different inhabitants, a different system entirely. The power will not make that choice on behalf of the living. It preserves their hard-won adaptations by preserving the conditions that shaped them.

It does not eliminate the serpents' predation. The gleaners die in mouths full of bristles, die constantly, die in numbers that might seem cruel. The power could reduce predation, could give the gleaners refuge. It does not. Predation is part of the balance. The serpents' hunger controls gleaner population, prevents them from filtering the water too efficiently, stripping it of all organic matter. The cycle of eating and being eaten is not cruelty; it is the mechanism by which energy moves through the system. To eliminate predation would be to eliminate one of the fundamental relationships that makes the city alive.

It does not cure disease. The fauna carry parasites, suffer infections, experience the normal afflictions of biological existence. The power could eliminate these, could render every organism perfectly healthy. It refuses. Disease is part of the evolutionary pressure that keeps populations strong, that selects for robust immune systems, that prevents any single lineage from dominating. A disease-free ecosystem would quickly become fragile, would lose the resilience that comes from constant low-level challenge.

It does not provide awareness. The browsers, the serpents, even the possibly-intelligent hidden ones in the depths—none of them know they are being protected, that a power watches over them. The power could grant them knowledge of its presence, could reveal itself. It does not. Awareness would change everything. The fauna would begin to rely on the power, to wait for intervention, to lose the self-sufficiency that characterizes their current existence. The power's care is most perfectly expressed in its invisibility, in the fact that every organism continues to live as though the universe is indifferent to it—because in most ways, it is, and should be.

It does not accelerate their evolution toward intelligence, toward culture, toward anything resembling civilization. The city once held human intelligence and look what happened—the city drowned, and had to be slowly rescued from that drowning by blind evolutionary processes that converted catastrophe into ecosystem over enormous time. The power will not impose that trajectory again. If intelligence emerges here naturally, if the hidden ones or the serpents or some currently-microscopic lineage eventually develops cognition and culture and art, then well. That will be their path. But the power will not choose it for them, will not decide that intelligence is somehow superior to the instinct and reflex that currently governs the city. All modes of existence have equal value. All strategies for being alive are worthy.

## The Departure

After these few actions and many non-actions, the power begins to withdraw. Not because its care is complete—care is never complete, systems always need maintenance, entropy never sleeps. But because the power understands that prolonged attention, however benevolent, begins to constitute interference.

Its presence is beginning to have effects beyond its intentions. The hidden ones have stopped clicking in certain areas—they sense something, do not know what, are altering their behavior in response. Some of the gleaners are aggregating in unusual patterns, responding to subtle changes in water chemistry produced by the power's own attention, its own observation affecting the observed. Even the buildings seem to settle differently, their slow subsidence into sediment altered by the weight of being regarded.

So the power withdraws, gradually, carefully. It does not vanish instantly—that would create a void, a sudden absence that would itself be a disruption. Instead, it fades over what might be months or years, slowly reducing its presence until finally its attention is so diffuse, so distributed, that it is effectively gone, and the city stands once more in its beautiful isolation, protected by changes so subtle they might as well be natural variation, secured by interventions that look like luck, blessed by a care that left no trace of itself except continuation.

The gleaners filter. The browsers graze. The serpents patrol. The crawlers rasp. The fish-things hide and spawn. The hidden ones click in the darkness. The microscopic lives bloom and crash and bloom again. Everything continues. Everything persists. Not forever, but for longer than they would have otherwise. Not improved, but stabilized. Not saved, but given time to be fully what they are, to express completely this strange moment of balance between catastrophe and evolution, between drowning and adaptation, between ending and transformation.

And if, centuries hence, the city does finally change—if the balance shifts despite all protections, if new species emerge or old ones vanish, if the ecosystem moves into some new configuration—then that will be natural too, will be the next chapter in an ongoing story rather than an ending. The power's gift was not immortality but time, not perfection but continuation, not salvation but presence.

The city abides. The water holds it gently. The fauna move through their ancient patterns while evolution slowly shapes them toward futures they cannot imagine. The buildings become reefs become something else entirely, are gradually transformed by passage of water and time and countless small bodies wearing them away and building them up in equal measure.

And somewhere, no longer here but not entirely gone, the benevolent power watches with the attention that sees without needing to intervene, that loves without possessing, that cares through the greatest gift available to the powerful: restraint.

The city breathes its slow breath, filtered through a billion feathery appendages. The city eats itself and remakes itself in equal cycles. The city remembers nothing of its drowning, knows nothing of its preservation, continues its strange half-life in the warm darkness, complete and sufficient, a place where catastrophe became ecosystem through time and water's patient work, and where a moment of grace ensured that the ecosystem would have time enough to become whatever comes next.

The water moves. The light shifts. The sound of rasping continues. Everything that was continues. And that continuation, that simple persistence of being, is the power's benediction, its blessing, its love.
