# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The attention arrived first as a quality of regard—not sight, not thought, but something that understood without observing, that knew without learning. It had noticed the Kiln Hollow in the way that one might suddenly become aware of a word they've been hearing all their life but never quite registered: it had always been there, but now it was *seen*.

The power—let us call it the Witness, though it had no need of names—understood immediately what took me thousands of words to describe. It comprehended the hollow's completeness, its terrible and beautiful equilibrium, the way it had solved itself into a state of such profound stability that it had become a kind of prayer written in stone and time and patient chemistry.

The Witness felt something that might be called love, if love can exist without desire, without the need to possess or change or improve. It was the love that a master calligrapher might feel upon encountering a single blade of grass growing through concrete: recognition of perfection achieved without intention, beauty that emerged from necessity rather than design.

And the Witness understood its dilemma.

Every gift is a disruption. Every blessing alters the blessed. To help is to intervene, and intervention here would be violence, no matter how gentle, no matter how well-meant. The hollow had not asked for attention. It had not asked for anything. It existed in a state beyond asking, beyond needing, beyond the framework in which help has meaning.

The Witness could give it water—but the hollow had organized itself around the absence of water so completely that water would be poison, drowning the bacterial colonies, eroding the careful placement of sediment, forcing the rosette plants into metabolic pathways they had spent eons learning not to use.

It could give it light—but the plants had adapted to dimness, their waxy leaves tuned to specific frequencies, and bright light would bleach them, disrupt the ionization of the air, flood the system with energy it had no mechanism to shed, overheating what was meant to remain gently warm.

It could accelerate the chemistry in the sealed void below, nudge those almost-living molecules across the threshold into actual life—but that would steal from them the only thing they had, which was their patient exploration of the adjacent possible, their eternal approach toward a destination they were never meant to reach. Achievement would end them more surely than abandonment.

It could remove the visitor's tools, correct what might be seen as litter, erase the evidence of intrusion—but those tools had been integrated now, they were part of the hollow's memory, part of how it knew itself, and their absence would create a different kind of disruption, a gap where there should be continuity.

The Witness could protect the hollow, place a ward around it, ensure that no other visitor would ever disturb its equilibrium—but the hollow had already achieved its own protection through the simple fact of being itself, inhospitable to nothing but offering nothing, asking nothing, attracting nothing. To protect what had already protected itself would be redundant, would add a layer of mediation between the hollow and reality, would make it a kept thing rather than a free thing.

The Witness could speed up its processes, collapse eons into moments so that it might see the hollow complete its vast cycles, witness the eventual covering of all basalt with sediment, the final emergence of the last rosette, the ultimate exhalation of the gases below—but this would be for the Witness's benefit, not the hollow's, and the Witness was wise enough to know that observation that serves only the observer is not benevolence but consumption.

It could do nothing.

And in considering nothing, the Witness found its answer.

---

What the benevolent power did was this:

It made itself a student.

Not of the hollow—for the hollow had nothing to teach in the way that teaching is usually meant, no wisdom to impart, no lesson to convey. But the Witness allowed itself to be changed by the hollow's existence in the same way that the hollow's air was constantly changed by ionization: broken down and reformed, rendered into something it was not before, then returned to what it was, but different now in ways too subtle to name.

The Witness sat—though it had no body to sit with—at the hollow's edge, in that space between the rim and the surrounding badlands, and it learned the hollow's most essential teaching: *how to be complete*.

For the Witness, despite its power, despite its benevolence, despite its wisdom, had always been in motion, always seeking the next thing to help, the next wrong to right, the next suffering to ease. It moved through the universe like wind through reeds, never resting, never settling, never allowing itself to simply *be* without purpose or direction.

The hollow taught it—not through words or visions or revelations, but through its sheer *isness*—that completion is possible. That systems can find their rest state. That equilibrium is not stagnation but a kind of dynamic perfection, motion so balanced it resembles stillness, change so slow it becomes indistinguishable from permanence.

The Witness learned patience measured not in years but in the time it takes for stone to forget its fire. It learned that the most profound gift is sometimes recognition without intervention. It learned that love can be expressed through restraint, through the discipline of not-helping, through allowing something to be exactly what it has made itself into.

And having learned this, the Witness made a choice that was not quite action and not quite inaction, but something between.

---

It left a marker.

Not in the hollow—nothing in the hollow could be altered—but in the space around it, in the fabric of circumstance and probability that determines who wanders where and why. The Witness adjusted something so subtle it had no name: a quality of resonance, an affinity, a correspondence.

From this moment forward—though forward is a direction that barely applies to what was done—anyone who needed what the hollow offered would find themselves, through whatever chain of coincidences served them, approaching it.

Not those who wanted adventure or discovery or something to take home and display. Not those who sought resources or knowledge or power. But those who needed, truly needed, to understand completion. Those who had been in motion for too long. Those who had been trying too hard. Those who had forgotten, or never learned, that being is sometimes enough.

They would come to the hollow—perhaps once in a generation, perhaps less often—and they would sit, as I imagined sitting, on those flat-topped hexagonal columns, feeling the gentle warmth rising from stone, and they would be given the same teaching the Witness received: that some things are whole already, that some questions are answered by their asking, that some destinations are reached by ceasing to travel.

The hollow would not change them. They would not hear voices or see visions or experience enlightenment. But they would sit in that dim light, in that profound silence, surrounded by the sparse rosettes and the pale sediment and the ancient tools left by another seeker, and something in them that had been clenched would loosen.

They would breathe the ionized air. They would feel the sourceless warmth. They would watch the stillness and slowly understand that the stillness was motion, that the silence was a symphony playing at frequencies they'd never learned to hear, that emptiness was the fullest thing in the world when it was emptiness that knew itself completely.

And after a time—hours or days or weeks—they would leave. They would climb over the rim and walk back into the undulating badlands and eventually return to whatever world they'd come from. And they would carry with them, not knowledge exactly, but a kind of permission: to stop when stopping was needed. To rest when rest was called for. To achieve their own equilibrium instead of chasing motion for motion's sake.

The hollow would be unchanged by their presence. Their footprints in the sediment would be erased by the next slow seepage of mineral fluid from the stone. The rosettes would continue their centuries-long metabolisms, unaffected. The Stone Speakers would continue their patient mining of basalt, unaware. The Sealed Dreaming would continue its impossible chemistry, undisturbed.

But the visitors would be changed, gently, inevitably, in the way that water is changed by passing over stone: made smoother, made clearer, made more themselves by having touched something that was so completely itself.

---

This was the Witness's gift: not to the hollow, but to those who would encounter it. Not intervention, but introduction. Not help, but witnessing—the Witness teaching others to witness, the Witness creating a lineage of attention, of proper regard, of love that manifests as recognition rather than rescue.

And in doing this, the Witness gave a gift to itself as well: it learned to sit still. Not forever—there were other places that needed its motion, other beings that required its intervention, other situations where action was the truest expression of benevolence. But it learned that stillness was also in its repertoire, that not-helping was also a form of help, that to allow something to be perfectly itself was the highest form of blessing.

---

Years passed. Centuries passed. The hollow continued its slow metabolisms, unchanged and unchanging.

But occasionally, very occasionally, someone would arrive. They would stand at the rim, looking down into that shallow depression with its scattered rosettes and pale sediment and columnar basalt painted in oxide bands. And instead of seeing emptiness or desolation or a place lacking interest, they would see what was actually there: completion, equilibrium, a system that had solved itself so thoroughly that it had become a kind of answer to a question they hadn't known they were asking.

And they would climb down into it. And they would sit. And they would learn what the Witness learned, without knowing they were learning, without knowing there was a Witness, without knowing anything except the warmth and the dimness and the silence and the strange, profound rightness of a place that asked nothing and offered nothing and was, in its nothingness, absolutely everything it needed to be.

---

The Witness never returned to the hollow. It didn't need to. The hollow had taught it what it needed to learn, and to visit again would be to doubt the teaching, to second-guess the gift, to impose itself on something that had never asked for imposition.

But sometimes, in its travels through the universe, in its encounters with suffering and need and the endless complexity of beings trying to become what they thought they should be, the Witness would remember the Kiln Hollow. It would remember the rosettes spaced in their perfect distances. It would remember the sourceless warmth and the dim light and the silence that was almost audible in its completeness.

And it would remember that not everything needs to be saved. That some things have already saved themselves. That the highest form of benevolence is sometimes simply to see what is actually there, to honor it, to let it be.

And carrying this memory, the Witness would return to its work, changed in ways too subtle to measure but too real to deny, moving through the universe with a new kind of wisdom: the wisdom of knowing when to act and when to witness, when to help and when to honor, when to intervene and when to sit at the edge of something perfect and simply say:

*Yes. This. Exactly this.*

---

This is what the benevolent power would do.

This is what it did.

This is what it learned from what it did.
