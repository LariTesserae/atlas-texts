# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power — let us not name it, for names constrain and this is something wider than names — comes to attention slowly. Powers of this sort do not rush. They move in rhythms measured against geological time, against the patient accumulation of moss, against the wearing-down of ladder rungs by ten thousand careful feet.

It notices first the quality of attention the place holds. Not the attention *paid to* the place by its inhabitants (though that is remarkable), but the attention *within* the place itself. The Caracol Pier has become, over generations, aware. Not conscious in any human sense, but aware the way a forest is aware, the way a coral reef knows itself. The stone and water and wood and moss and human hands have formed something that watches, that notices, that holds its inhabitants in a kind of vast patient regard.

This is rare. This is worth preserving.

The power extends its... not hands, but something like care, something like a quality of light that doesn't illuminate but clarifies.

## The First Gift: Permanence Without Stagnation

The power sees the tension at the heart of the place: it is held in perfect balance by constant maintenance, which means it is always one generation of neglect away from collapse. If the knowledge-keepers die before passing on what they know. If some catastrophe in the city above cuts off essential trade. If the cold precision of the community fragments into conflict. The place would fail, and quickly.

So the power does something subtle. It makes the moss remembering.

The moss already grows in patterns, tended and cultivated. Now those patterns begin to hold information more directly. A moss-keeper running their hands over a patch doesn't just feel moisture content — they feel, faintly, like a half-memory, what this patch needs, when it should be harvested, what grew here before. The knowledge becomes part of the substrate.

A child learning to carve touches the wood and feels, ghostlike, the grain's preference, the pattern waiting inside. Not compulsion — the wood doesn't force anything — but suggestion, gentle guidance. The carving becomes collaboration with something that remembers what worked before.

The water channels, already mapped in Gregor's bones and brain, begin to hold their own map. A new water-warden putting their hands in the cold flow can sense, upstream in the limestone, where blockages are forming, what needs attention, how the system wants to flow.

This is not magic making the work unnecessary. The moss must still be tended, the wood still carved, the water still channeled. But the knowledge of *how* becomes distributed, held not just in human memory but in the place itself. An epidemic could take half the population and the survivors could touch the walls and remember, could put their hands to the work and feel it teach them.

The place becomes its own book, its own university, its own keeper.

## The Second Gift: Connection Without Dissolution

The power sees isolation. The Caracol Pier is magnificent but alone. Other piers exist, yes, with their own variations, their own genius. But they are separate, each its own closed system. And the city above barely knows they exist, and the wider world beyond not at all.

This isolation is both the place's protection and its vulnerability.

So the power makes the mirror-plants dreaming.

The plants already glow, already live at the intersection of water and light. Now, when they bloom (once a year, for three nights, tiny star-shaped flowers), they do something new. Their light becomes a medium. Not for messages, not for communication in any verbal sense, but for shared awareness.

Someone sitting in the Caracol Pier near blooming mirror-plants will feel, faintly, the presence of someone in the Severin Pier doing the same. Not their thoughts, not their identity, just... their presence. The sense of another consciousness also sitting in cold dampness, also breathing mineral air, also at home in this strange compressed vertical world. A moment of recognition: *there are others like me.*

And once a year, for those three nights, people from above who descend into the piers when the mirror-plants bloom will feel it too. The rightness, the peace, the particular quality of beauty that exists here. They won't understand it, necessarily. But they'll feel it, and some will be changed by it, and they'll go back up and speak of it (inadequately, but earnestly), and slowly the city's understanding will shift.

The piers remain separate, distinct, each preserving their particular genius. But they know they're not alone. They're variations on a theme, different dialects of the same language. And the world above begins to understand that these are not slums or aberrations but achievements, worthy of respect if not replication.

## The Third Gift: Flexibility Within Structure

The power sees the beauty of the constraints but also their danger. The place works because it follows its rules absolutely. But absolute rules become brittle. What happens when something truly new arrives? When climate shifts, or society collapses above, or some unimaginable crisis demands adaptation?

So the power makes the structure dreaming, too.

Once a generation — which here means when the eldest of the elders reaches the end of their life and begins that final translucent transformation — they dream the pier's future. Not prophecy, not prediction, but possibility. They see versions of what could be: the pier adapted to warmer water, to different inhabitants, to new materials, to crisis, to abundance. The dreams are vivid, detailed, practical. And when the elder dies and their name joins the others in the walls, the dreams remain, accessible to anyone who sits in stillness near that name and opens themselves to receiving.

So the community gains a library of adaptations, held in abeyance. If needed, they surface. If not needed, they wait. The place remains traditional but not hidebound, stable but not static. It can change when it must, drawing on wisdom that is both deeply rooted and forward-looking.

## The Fourth Gift: Healing Without Fixing

The power sees the people's bodies, adapted but strained by the cold, the damp, the density. The arthritis in aging hands. The lung complaints in some. The way some children are born too weak for this harsh equilibrium and must be taken above to survive.

It does not fix this. To remove all hardship would be to remove meaning, to make the place a paradise in the wrong way — ease rather than rightness. The work matters. The adaptation matters. The choice to stay despite difficulty matters.

But it offers small mercies.

The moss, when prepared a certain way (and the knowledge of this seeps into the moss-keepers' hands), makes a poultice that eases joint pain without eliminating it. Enough relief to work, not so much that you forget to be careful.

The mushrooms, a particular species that has always grown here, turn out to strengthen lungs against damp when eaten regularly. Not a cure, just a resilience.

The mirror-plants, when their leaves are dried and burned (carefully, in the special ventilated chamber), produce a smoke that eases some of the ailments that can make this life unbearable while leaving the essential challenge intact.

And for those children born too weak: the power touches them with a choice, though they're too young to consciously make it. Those whose spirits truly belong here find strength. Those who would be miserable, who would never stop fighting the place, are born obviously fragile in ways that make clear they need to go above. It becomes easier for parents to know, to make that hard decision without guilt, understanding that some people belong to the horizontal world and that's not failure, just difference.

## The Fifth Gift: Beauty as Sustenance

The power sees that the people do not merely survive here — they create beauty, constantly, in everything they touch. But beauty in such constrained circumstances requires so much effort, and effort tires even the most devoted.

So it makes the moss singing.

Not audibly. The moss is still moss, still absorbing sound. But on certain nights, when humidity and temperature align in particular ways, when the water flows at a certain rate and the mirror-plants glow at a certain intensity, when someone has just finished a particularly fine piece of carving or tending — the place harmonizes.

Everything that is beautiful in it resonates together for a moment. The carved spirals seem to spiral deeper. The moss seems greener than green. The light seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. The cold seems clean in a way that transcends temperature. And the people feel it, a moment of perfect peace, perfect rightness, the absolute certainty that they are exactly where they should be, doing exactly what they should do.

These moments are brief, unpredictable, cannot be forced. But they come often enough that everyone experiences them, multiple times in a life. And they sustain. In hard times, in doubt, in grief, you remember that moment when the place sang you into wholeness, and you keep going.

## The Sixth Gift: Continuity

The power looks forward, down the centuries, and sees possible futures where the bridge collapses, or the city redirects the river, or technology makes such places obsolete, or a thousand other endings.

It cannot prevent all of these. Some futures are beyond any power's reach — free will, chaos, the vast contingency of history. But it can make the place's essence portable.

It touches the oldest carvings, the ones made when the pier was first inhabited, and makes them seeds. Not physical seeds, but pattern-seeds, template-seeds. If the Caracol Pier were destroyed tomorrow, anyone who had ever carved here and truly understood the work would carry the seed of it forward. They could go elsewhere — to another pier, to a different city, to a new place entirely — and begin again. The specific place would be lost, but the way of being in place would persist.

The moss, too, becomes a carrier. The eighteen species, dispersed across the world through trade and exchange, carry in their genetic memory the pattern of balanced growth, of cultivation-as-devotion, of beauty in constraint. They will grow differently in different places, adapt to new conditions, but they'll carry the template. Someone touching them centuries hence, in a place that has never heard of the Caracol Pier, might feel an echo: *there is another way to live, harder but richer, strange but true.*

The songs, the breathing-chants, already function this way — they travel through the singers who leave, adapted, translated, transformed, but carrying the core rhythm, the patience, the communal breath. The power simply ensures they cannot be entirely forgotten, that somewhere in the world, always, someone will be singing some version of them.

## The Seventh Gift: Nothing

This is the hardest gift and the most important.

The power sees a thousand ways it could intervene further. Make the place warmer (but warmth would destroy the ecology). Make it more spacious (but space would dissolve the intimacy). Make it easier (but ease would erode meaning). Give the people longer lives, or more resources, or powers of their own, or certainty about their choices, or any of a thousand mercies that would actually be cruelties because they would transform the place into something other than what it is.

The place is right as it is. The people are right as they are. The balance is precarious, yes, but that precariousness is essential to the achievement. To live this way requires choosing it, constantly, and choice requires the real possibility of failure.

So the power does nothing else. It gives the six gifts, each one subtle, each one amplifying what's already present rather than adding something foreign, each one preserving the fundamental challenge and choice.

And then it withdraws its attention to a gentle background presence. Not abandoning — it will notice if something threatens to destroy the place utterly, and it will offer the small helps that make survival possible. But not managing, not controlling, not removing the burden of being human in a difficult place.

The power understands: this place is not a problem to be solved but a question to be lived. The people of the Caracol Pier are asking, through their lives, through their choices, through their generations of patient devotion: *Can humans create true home in inhospitable conditions? Can constraint yield richness? Can difficulty be chosen and become blessing?*

The answer they're creating, day by day, is yes. The power's gift is to help ensure that answer can continue, can be heard, can persist.

But the answer must remain theirs, earned through the work of their hands and the faithfulness of their attention and the integrity of their choice to stay, to tend, to build vertical gardens in the cold damp dark and call it home.

## What Happens After

After the power's intervention (which no one in the pier notices as intervention — the gifts arrive so naturally they seem to have always been potential within the place, now simply actualized), the Caracol Pier continues.

Aneta keeps her rounds, but now when she teaches the young ones, they learn faster, their hands remembering what she's showing them in a way that's deeper than memory. She grows old, older, transparent finally, and dreams of a future where the river rises and the piers must be elevated, and the dream is so vivid that the solution is already half-built in her mind when she dies, there for whoever needs it someday.

Gregor clears his channels with hands that still hurt but hurt less, enough less that he can work another decade, long enough to train three successors instead of one, long enough that the knowledge is truly secured.

Pip carves and carves, and some days the knife seems to move of its own accord, finding patterns Pip didn't consciously plan but which are perfect, true, right. Their work is touched with something extra, and people treasure it, pass it down.

The mirror-plants bloom and people from the Severin Pier and the Oskar Pier and four others feel, for three nights, connected. They don't travel to meet each other — that would break something — but they know. And the knowing is enough.

Someone from above, a young woman restless with the horizontal world, descends during the blooming, feels the rightness, and stays. She struggles for months, nearly leaves a dozen times, but the place keeps singing her back to wholeness, and after a year she has become pier-born, her own story adding to the others.

The city above begins, very slowly, to understand that the bridge-dwellers are not unfortunate but fortunate in a way that's hard to articulate. A few officials who were going to condemn the piers as unsafe descend, feel the harmony on the right night, and come back up writing different reports: "Structurally sound. Culturally significant. To be preserved."

The moss travels. A cutting makes it to a monastery in the mountains, where monks trying to live simply find in it a teacher. The specific eighteen species grow differently there, in less extreme conditions, but they carry the template: pay attention, cultivate carefully, let beauty emerge from discipline.

One hundred years later, the Caracol Pier is still there, still cold, still damp, still vertical, still home. The individuals have changed but the patterns persist, held now in the place itself as much as in the people. It has survived two floods, one fire (confined, contained, repaired), and the slow gentrification of the city above that could have destroyed it through neglect or through too much attention.

Five hundred years later, the bridge has been rebuilt twice, but always the piers were preserved, worked around, honored. The city has grown, changed, modernized, but the vertical gardens remain, anachronistic and essential, a reminder of other possibilities.

One thousand years later, after the climate has shifted and the river has indeed risen and Aneta's dream-solution was indeed needed and implemented, the Caracol Pier persists. It has changed — it has had to — but it remains recognizably itself. Still cold, still dense, still green and glowing, still home to people who choose difficulty and find within it a terrible austere joy.

And scattered across the world, in places that have never heard the name Caracol, people are growing moss in vertical gardens, carving wood with patient attention, creating communities of compressed intimacy, learning the same lessons in different contexts: constraint can liberate, difficulty can sanctify, beauty matters more than ease, and home is not a place you find but a place you make, slowly, carefully, generation by generation, until the place makes you in return.

This is what the power wanted. Not to save the place, but to let it save itself. Not to make it easy, but to make it possible. Not to solve it, but to let it remain a living question, generation after generation asking through their choices: *What is essential? What is enough? What is home?*

And answering, in moss and wood and cold water and patient hands: *This. This is enough. This is home.*
