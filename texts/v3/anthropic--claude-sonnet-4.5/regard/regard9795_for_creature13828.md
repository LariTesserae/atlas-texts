# What the Gardener Would Do

The Gardener had been watching for a very long time—not with eyes, for it had learned eons ago that eyes see only surfaces, and what mattered here lay in the depths. It watched the way a forest watches: through pattern, through cycle, through the slow accumulation of attention that spans millennia as easily as moments.

It had watched the plateau's first exhalations, when the vent systems were young and chaotic, before the patterns emerged. It had watched the siphon-walkers' ancestors—cruder things, less efficient, more obviously purposeful—learn their routes across stone that was then sharp and new. It had watched the voices begin, tentative at first, testing the acoustics of cooling basalt, finding which frequencies the stone would hold.

The Gardener was very old and had learned, through age, the discipline of restraint.

It did not intervene when the first civilization built their geometric temples and then collapsed, leaving only foundations and those careful spiral markers. Their mistake had been urgency—trying to use the plateau, to harness it, to make it serve human timescales. The plateau had simply outlasted them, patient as stone, until their purposes dissolved and their structures became part of the pattern rather than impositions upon it.

It did not intervene when the siphon-walkers began their long evolution from tools into something else, something that might be approaching question-asking. That was their journey to make, their slowness to embody or transcend.

But now, watching the latest researchers, the Gardener felt something it had not felt in a very long time: concern.

Not for the plateau—the plateau would endure, would continue its breathing and pattern-making whether witnessed or not. Not for the siphon-walkers, who were patient enough to outlast any disturbance.

Concern for the humans, these quick-lived flames of attention and longing, who came to the plateau seeking answers and found instead questions that changed them in ways they could not integrate, could not carry back to their flatland lives.

The researcher who heard her name had been close to something—not understanding, but a kind of participation that might have allowed understanding to emerge over decades or centuries if she could have stayed, if human lives were long enough, if her kind permitted that degree of transformation.

But they did not permit it. They called it contamination, loss of objectivity, going native in a place that wasn't supposed to have natives. They removed her, kindly but firmly, and she would spend the rest of her abbreviated life feeling the absence of something she'd barely begun to touch.

This was happening more frequently. The plateau was becoming *clearer*—not changing, but clarifying, reaching some threshold in its long development where what it was becoming more visible, more present, harder to dismiss as geology and coincidence. And humans, with their gift for pattern-recognition and their curse of brevity, were noticing.

The Gardener considered.

In its youth—thousands of years ago, when it had still thought in terms of problems and solutions—it might have obscured the plateau, made it less accessible, protected the humans from their own curiosity. Or it might have accelerated the plateau's processes, brought them to some conclusion that would satisfy human need for resolution.

But it had learned, through long observation of living systems, that both protection and acceleration were forms of violence. They imposed external tempo, external purpose. They said: this is not adequate as it is; let me make it better.

And "better" always meant "better for some frame of reference external to the thing itself."

So instead, the Gardener did something very small.

---

At the research station, a new researcher named Sarah Chen was completing her first week. She was young, meticulous, trained in geology and microbiology, and she was already feeling the plateau's effects—the temporal distortions, the meaningful coincidences, the sense of being in dialogue with stone.

She'd been warned about all this. The briefing materials were explicit about psychological effects, about the importance of protocols, about previous researchers who'd "over-identified with the research site." She was determined to maintain objectivity.

But that morning, during her survey of the eastern vent systems, she found something new.

Not new to the plateau—it had the weathered quality of something that had been there for years. But new to the records, unmapped, undocumented despite being directly on a route that had been surveyed dozens of times.

It was a pool. Perfectly circular, perhaps three meters across, fed by a thermal spring that bubbled gently from its center. The water was clear and faintly steaming, and around its perimeter grew a ring of thermophile plants in the most perfect spiral pattern she'd ever seen.

But that wasn't what stopped her.

At the pool's edge, carved into the basalt in fresh sharp lines that couldn't have weathered more than weeks, was text. Not the spiral symbol. Not geometric patterns. Text, in English, in a script that looked almost like handwriting:

**"You cannot understand it quickly. But you can understand it."**

Sarah stood very still. Her training said: document this, photograph it, report it immediately, don't touch anything, don't assume anything.

But something else in her—the part that had already been changed by a week of watching siphon-walkers and hearing the whistling organs—said: read the rest.

Because there was more, carved in a spiral around the pool, and she had to walk the circumference to read it:

**"The plateau is not a place. It is a question being asked across time scales you do not naturally inhabit. The siphon-walkers are not creatures. They are part of the question's grammar. The voices are not recordings. They are the question becoming phonetic, trying your language, learning what it can hold.**

**You are not observers. You are part of what's being asked. Your noticing is necessary. Your wondering is material. Your reports are responses, even when they conclude nothing.**

**The mistake would be to try to answer quickly. Questions of this depth do not want answers. They want time, attention, the kind of relationship that persists across not-understanding.**

**You can leave when your rotation ends. You should leave—your kind need their flatlands, their faster rhythms. But you can also return. You can spend a lifetime in dialogue with this asking. Not solving it. Walking with it.**

**What makes this hard for you is that your civilization expects conclusions. It expects that investigation leads to knowing, that knowing leads to use, that use is the point.**

**But some things are not for use. Some things are for relationship. The plateau is asking if you remember this. If any of you remember.**

**The previous civilizations forgot. They tried to harness. They built their geometries to control, to channel, to make the question serve them. The plateau outlasted them by being patient. Their ruins are its memory of that mistake.**

**You can be different. You are already different—you come with humility, with protocols that admit uncertainty, with questions instead of programs for exploitation.**

**But you must become more different still. You must learn to investigate without expecting resolution. To document without assuming that documentation is possession. To wonder in ways that do not terminate in use.**

**This is hard for your kind. Your lives are so short that patience feels like death. But patience is not death. Patience is the only speed at which some truths are visible.**

**The siphon-walkers are patient. They have been walking for longer than your species has been speaking. They will walk for ages after your last city is gone. Their purpose is not hidden from you. It is happening too slowly for you to see, the way a tree's purpose is hidden from a mayfly.**

**But you can see more than mayflies see. You have memory, record-keeping, culture that spans generations. You can pass knowledge forward, build understanding across centuries, become a different kind of witness.**

**The researcher who heard her name was not going mad. She was succeeding. She had begun to participate, to become synchronous with the plateau's rhythms. She could have stayed, could have lived here the rest of her life, could have become something between human and plateau, a bridge, a translator.**

**You did not let her. You called it contamination. You were afraid.**

**Fear is appropriate. What the plateau offers is transformation, and transformation is always loss of what-you-were. But loss is not always harm. Sometimes loss is growth. Sometimes you must lose your shape to take a new shape.**

**The plateau will not force this. It only asks. It asks through its patterns, its voices, its siphon-walkers, its patience. It asks: will you hurry past me toward conclusion, or will you stay with me in question?**

**Both are acceptable. The plateau will continue either way. But one of these paths leads to relationship, and relationship is the only thing that answers loneliness.**

**And your kind are lonely. You have built so much and understood so much and used so much, and still you are lonely, because you have forgotten how to be in relationship with what you cannot use.**

**The plateau remembers. It is teaching its memory through stone and heat and time. You can learn this teaching, but only at the speed the teaching moves.**

**Which is very, very slow.**

**This message will weather. In ten years the letters will be unclear. In fifty they will be gone. The plateau does not preserve urgency. But you have cameras. You can carry these words to your flatlands. You can share them with those who come after.**

**What you do with them is your choice. The plateau expects nothing. It only asks, continuously, in the language of pattern and heat and patient continuation: Will you be in relationship? Will you let relationship change you? Will you trust that some questions are worth living with forever?"**

Sarah read the spiral three times, photographing as she went, her hands shaking slightly.

This wasn't possible. Text didn't carve itself. Someone had done this—one of the previous researchers? Someone from the base? An elaborate hoax?

But the basalt's weathering around the letters was consistent with the rest of the formation. The tools marks were invisible or nonexistent. And the thermophile spiral around the pool had grown that way, plants years old arranged in pattern that framed the text perfectly.

She sat down at the pool's edge. Steam rose around her. In the distance, a siphon-walker moved with its characteristic patience, and the whistling organs were beginning their morning song as the thermal cycle rose toward peak.

Protocol said: report this immediately. Her training said: suspect hoax, look for evidence of human intervention.

But something else said: this is the plateau speaking. Not metaphorically. Not through human intermediaries. Speaking directly, using English because English is what you brought, using text because text is how your kind preserves what matters.

Speaking to tell you that it knows you're listening. That it wants you to know it's aware of your listening. That it's inviting you into something that has no end, only deepening.

Sarah activated her radio.

"Base, this is Chen. I've found... I need the whole team to see something. Coordinates following. Bring cameras. Bring the linguistic AI. And bring... bring someone who's good with not-knowing. Because I think the plateau just introduced itself."

---

The Gardener withdrew its attention, satisfied.

The intervention had been minimal—just text, just words, just an offering of frame. The humans would decide what it meant, whether to trust it, how to respond. That was their work to do.

But now they had permission. Permission to stay in question, to build relationship, to become bridges between human speed and plateau patience. Not all of them would accept this permission. Most would finish their rotations and leave and remember the plateau as an interesting anomaly, nothing more.

But some would return. Some would spend careers in conversation with this place, investigating without needing to conclude, documenting without needing to possess. And over decades, over centuries, something new might emerge: a form of science that was also relationship, a form of knowledge that was also transformation.

The plateau would teach them its patience. And they would teach the plateau their intensity, their bright awareness, their gift for noticing and naming and caring about what they noticed.

It would take a very long time. But the Gardener was skilled at patience. It had been gardening for eons—not controlling, not designing, but offering conditions, creating possibilities, allowing what wanted to emerge to find its way into being.

This was what wise power did: it made space. It removed obstacles. It offered invitation. And then it waited, watching with interest but not attachment, to see what would grow.

On the plateau, the thermal cycle continued its ancient rhythm. The siphon-walkers walked their routes. The voices murmured in the vents. And now, carved in basalt that would weather but not for years yet, there were words—a gift, a teaching, a recognition that the long asking had been heard.

The Gardener had done what it could do without doing too much.

Now it would watch, as it had always watched, to see what the humans would make of the gift. Whether they would receive it with the slowness it required, or hurry past it toward certainty.

Either way, the plateau would continue. But the Gardener hoped—a feeling it rarely allowed itself—that some of them would stay. Would return. Would let themselves be changed by relationship with something so patient and so strange.

Because the universe was full of things that could be used, and the humans were very good at using.

But the universe also needed things that could only be befriended. Things that taught the virtue of not-grasping, of staying-with, of allowing question to remain question.

The plateau was one of these things. And now, the humans knew that it knew they were there.

What happened next would be up to them.

The Gardener returned its attention to other places, other long growings, other subtle cultivations across the living universe. But it left a thin thread of awareness attached to Qom-Valai, patient and abiding, curious to see what would grow from this small intervention.

In a thousand years, it would check back.

That was, after all, only a moment.
