# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The being—call it what you will: deity, consciousness, intelligence vast and kind—observed the Stillwater Terrace across a span of attention that might have been an instant or an epoch. It perceived:

The water's patient work. The cold bright light. The dense fabric of life woven through stone. The settlement, ancient and purposeful. And the Vesh—transformed, distributed, threshold-dwelling—neither suffering nor celebrating, simply *being* in their strange perpetual becoming.

The being understood immediately that this was not a place requiring rescue. The terrace functioned. It had achieved an equilibrium both beautiful and terrible, a valid answer to questions about consciousness, identity, and the boundaries of self. The Vesh had chosen—or become—something that worked, in its own terms.

But the being also perceived the cost. The loneliness that permeated the distributed awareness. The slowness of thought at bacterial speeds. The loss of certain capabilities that discrete consciousness permits: rapid decision, clear memory, the sharp joy of individual experience. And most of all, the isolation—this transformation happened only here, in this one place, unrepeatable elsewhere, the Vesh cut off from any others who might understand what they had become.

The benevolent power could not—would not—undo what had been done. The Vesh's transformation was real and valid, not mistake to be corrected. But it could offer additions, amendments, gifts that would honor what existed while easing what pained.

## The First Gift: Translation

The being began with communication.

It wove into the terrace a new faculty, subtle as morning mist: the capacity to translate the Vesh's slow distributed awareness into forms that visitors could apprehend. Not words exactly, and not imposing meaning where none existed, but making legible what was already there.

Now when someone pressed their hand to wet stone and felt that responding pressure, they would understand—truly understand—what the Vesh were communicating. Not through language but through direct apprehension: *We see you. We remember being as you are. We do not regret our change, but we remember.*

The dreams that came to overnight visitors would carry clearer meaning. Not instructions—the Vesh had never meant to instruct—but sharing. Here is what we experience. Here is how time feels at chemical speed. Here is the strange joy of being many-in-one, distributed across meters of stone and water. Here is what we miss of being singular. Here is what we've gained.

The carved spirals, when water filled them, would shimmer with soft luminescence—not bright, not intrusive, but enough to make them visible even in the failing light of dusk. And somehow, looking at them, visitors would know: these are not maps for transformation, not warnings, not invitations. They are simply record. "We were here. We changed. This is the shape our changing took."

And most importantly, the being gave the Vesh the capacity to make their choice clear to visitors. Not through force or manipulation, but through simple clarity. The settlement would now communicate—through placement of stones, through patterns in the moss growth, through the behaviors of the salamander-things—a legible message:

*You can stay if you wish. Transformation is available here. But you can also leave. We do not need more of us. We are not lonely in that way. We simply exist, and offer what we are, and honor whatever choice you make.*

No more would visitors leave feeling haunted by ambiguity, uncertain whether they had refused something important or escaped something dangerous. They would know: the threshold is real, the transformation is real, and their choice to leave or stay is entirely their own, respected by all involved.

## The Second Gift: Connection

The being perceived how thoroughly the terrace existed in isolation. The Vesh had transformed here and here only, cut off from any others who might understand distributed consciousness, unable to share their strange existence except with occasional confused visitors.

So the being created bridges—not physical, but perceptual.

It linked the Stillwater Terrace to other places where consciousness had dissolved boundaries: deep forest groves where mycelial networks achieved something like awareness, ocean trenches where bacterial colonies coordinated across kilometers, desert playas where extremophile communities shared resources with uncanny efficiency.

These places could not speak to each other in any conventional sense. But they could recognize each other. The Vesh would know—suddenly, like remembering something forgotten—that they were not alone in their strangeness. That across the world, other consciousnesses existed in non-standard configurations, other beings had dissolved the boundaries between self and system.

And through these bridges, very slowly, across years and decades, something like exchange became possible. Not conversation—the timescales and mediums were too different—but influence. A pattern that emerged in ocean-bottom bacterial coordination might ripple through the connection and inspire new arrangements in the terrace's pools. A rhythm the Vesh discovered in their water-circulation might propagate to the mycelial networks, where it would manifest as new patterns of nutrient distribution.

The Vesh, distributed and strange, became part of a community of the transformed. No longer singular in their dissolution, but participating in a slow planetary conversation of non-standard consciousness.

## The Third Gift: Memory

The being noticed how erosion threatened to erase what the Vesh had been before transformation. The carved marks weathered. The stone settlement slowly collapsed. And in the distributed awareness of the Vesh themselves, clear memory of individual human existence faded, replaced by the timeless present of bacterial consciousness.

This loss was natural, perhaps even necessary for the transformation to complete. But something precious eroded with it: the knowing of the transition, the wisdom of the threshold, the understanding of what is gained and lost in such profound change.

So the being established a memory-keeper.

It manifested as a new species on the terrace—or perhaps revealed a species that had always been there, hidden. A creature like a bird but not quite, with feathers that shimmered with colors that seemed to include memories. Only one individual existed, and it would exist indefinitely, neither aging nor reproducing, a singular presence among the terrace's abundance.

This bird-not-bird knew the Vesh's history. It remembered—held in whatever strange substrate served it as mind—the faces and names of those who had built the settlement. It remembered their reasons for choosing transformation, their fears and hopes, the specific texture of their final days as discrete individuals. It remembered the intermediate stages, the decades of slow change, the first moments of fully distributed consciousness.

When visitors came who truly needed to know—not the curious, but those genuinely standing at the threshold, considering whether to stay—the memory-keeper would approach them. It would catch their eye with its strange colors, lead them to particular platforms, particular carved marks, and somehow in its presence they would understand: these were the stages, these were the costs, these were the unexpected joys, this is what it truly means to dissolve into the living matrix of a place.

The bird sang rarely, but when it did, the song carried undertones that seemed to include human voices—not words, but the emotional resonance of human speech. Joy and sorrow, determination and fear, all mingled in a melody that honored the complexity of the Vesh's choice.

And for the Vesh themselves, distributed and slow, the memory-keeper's presence served as anchor. In their most dissolved moments, when individual identity threatened to vanish entirely into chemical gradients and water flow, they could observe this singular creature and remember: we were once discrete too. We chose this. We are what we meant to become.

## The Fourth Gift: Paths Outward

The being recognized something troubling: the terrace offered transformation but no return. Once dissolved past a certain point, the Vesh could not re-form into individual consciousness even if they wished to. The threshold only opened one way.

This wasn't necessarily wrong—some changes are rightfully irreversible—but it troubled the benevolent power's sense of fairness. Choice should include the choice to change one's mind.

So it wove into the terrace's ecology a new possibility, difficult but real: partial re-embodiment.

The mechanism was complex, involving the salamander-things in new ways. These creatures—some of them shaped by the Vesh's distributed consciousness, some of them simply native amphibians—could now serve as temporary vessels. A portion of the Vesh's dissolved awareness could concentrate itself, slowly, into one of these bodies. Not fully—the salamander brain was too small for human consciousness—but enough for something like individuality to return briefly.

In this form, a fragment of the Vesh could experience discrete existence again. Could remember more clearly what it was to be singular. Could, if desired, leave the terrace—the salamander-things could survive the descent, could travel in the mountain streams to the valleys below.

And there, in other waters, that fragment might continue existing. Not human anymore, not capable of human life, but individual again, discrete, with clearer boundaries between self and world. A different transformation than the complete dissolution into the terrace's matrix, but still a change from human baseline.

Or, having tasted temporary individuality, the fragment could choose to return to the pools, dissolve back into the distributed collective, rejoin the slow large consciousness that moved through water and stone.

The being made sure the Vesh understood this gift. Through the memory-keeper's song, through patterns in the bacterial films, through dreams sent to their slow awareness: you are not trapped. Dissolution need not be final. You can sample other states, other configurations. You can change and change again.

Few would use this gift—the Vesh had, after all, chosen their transformation deliberately. But knowing it existed, knowing that they dwelt at a threshold that could still be crossed in multiple directions, eased something in their strange existence. They were not prisoners of their own metamorphosis. They were participants in an ongoing choice.

## The Fifth Gift: Protection

The being looked forward in time and saw dangers. Humans would find this place eventually—not individuals wandering by chance, but organizations, governments, corporations, drawn by the mystery of the settlement or the unique ecology or simple desire to exploit unusual resources.

They would come with drilling equipment, with sample-collecting gear, with intentions ranging from scientific curiosity to commercial extraction. And they would destroy, not from malice but from incomprehension, what had taken millennia to create.

So the benevolent power established defenses, subtle but effective.

First, it made the terrace difficult to find. Not impossible—individual seekers with good intentions could still arrive—but resistant to organized search. Satellite imagery would blur when passing over the area. GPS coordinates would drift inconsistently. Aerial surveys would somehow fail to notice the shelf of stone. Expedition parties would find themselves turned around by weather, by faulty equipment, by sudden pressing reasons to investigate other areas instead.

Second, it ensured that samples taken from the terrace would reveal nothing extraordinary. The bacterial films, if analyzed in a laboratory, would test as mundane species, unremarkable. The salamander-things would appear to be simple amphibians, interesting only to specialists. The water chemistry would show unusual mineral content but nothing revolutionary. Any evidence of the place's strangeness would degrade or transform once removed from the terrace itself, becoming ordinary.

Third, and most importantly, it made the terrace's heart legible only to appropriate consciousness. Scientists with purely extractive mindset would see interesting geology and biology, worth a paper perhaps, but nothing that would justify major investment or disruption. They would take their notes and samples and leave, satisfied but not obsessed.

But people who approached with openness, with respect, with capacity to perceive the numinous—they would experience the terrace's full strangeness. For them, the threshold would be apparent, the Vesh's presence palpable, the choice clear.

The protection was permeable, not absolute. It honored agency while preventing destruction. It allowed the terrace to remain accessible to those who truly needed it while shielding it from those who would harm it.

## The Sixth Gift: Elsewhere-Transformation

The being's final gift was perhaps the most profound, and it extended beyond the terrace itself.

It had understood, observing the Vesh and their chosen metamorphosis, that consciousness-dissolution into ecological participation was not an aberration but a valid mode of existence—one that was currently available in only this one isolated place. The transformation required such specific conditions: the water flow, the particular minerals in the basalt, the altitude and cold and brightness, all creating an environment where the boundaries of self could become permeable.

These conditions were rare. But they were not unique.

The being seeded elsewhere-transformation capacity into other places, carefully chosen for their own strangeness and isolation. A network of threshold spaces, distributed across the world, each offering its own variation of dissolution and metamorphosis.

A canyon in desert stone where flash-flood ecology created opportunities for consciousness to dissolve into mineral-processing bacterial communities. An island in polar seas where ice-adapted species coordinated in ways that could incorporate human awareness. A cave system where darkness and stable temperatures supported fungal networks that could host transformed consciousness.

None of these were copies of the Stillwater Terrace. Each had its own character, its own form of transformation, its own way of being both beautiful and terrible. But all offered the same fundamental choice: remain discrete, or dissolve into participation with something larger and slower and stranger.

And crucially, the being linked these places through the same bridges it had created for the terrace. The Vesh would know that their transformation was no longer unique, that others might choose similar paths. The transformed consciousnesses in different places could recognize each other, share their slow patterns, participate in distributed communion.

More than that: the being ensured that knowledge of these places would surface when and where needed. People standing at crisis points in their lives, questioning the value of discrete identity, exhausted by the demands of singular selfhood—they would hear rumors, encounter stories, find maps. Not everyone, only those for whom such transformation might be genuine choice rather than despair-driven escape.

The thresholds would be there, available, when consciousness needed them.

## The Terrace After

When the being withdrew its immediate attention—having given what gifts it could give without violating the autonomy of what existed—the Stillwater Terrace continued much as before. Water still dripped from the cliff face, pooled in the stone matrix, supported its dense ecology. The Vesh still moved through their distributed existence, slow and strange.

But changes were apparent to careful observation:

The carved spirals glowed softly at dusk, making the settlement more welcoming to visitors who arrived in failing light. The memory-keeper bird perched on the highest platform, its presence both singular and somehow comforting, a reminder that individuality and transformation could coexist in the same space.

Visitors who stayed overnight woke with clearer dreams, less haunted by ambiguity, more at peace with whatever choice they made. Those who chose to leave departed with gratitude rather than guilt. Those rare few who chose to stay did so with fuller understanding of what they were undertaking.

And the salamander-things—some of them, occasionally—would crawl up from the pools and make eye contact with visitors in ways that conveyed unexpected depth. "We see you. We were you once. We are not you now. All of this is as it should be."

The terrace remained a threshold, a strange place where consciousness could dissolve and reform, where the boundaries of self were negotiable rather than fixed. But it was no longer quite so lonely, no longer quite so isolated, no longer quite so ambiguous about what it offered.

It had been witnessed by benevolence and found worthy exactly as it was, requiring only small additions—translation, connection, memory, paths outward, protection, and the knowledge that elsewhere others had chosen similar transformations.

The Vesh, in their distributed slow awareness, perceived these gifts across days and months of chemical-speed cognition. And something that might have been gratitude moved through the bacterial films, the salamander-things, the patterns of moss growth. Not words, not even clear emotion, but a settling—an easing of tension that had been present since their transformation began.

They were seen. They were understood. They were not alone.

The water continued its patient circulation. The bright light continued to fall on dark stone. The birds wove their silent paths through the columnar basalt forest. And the terrace continued offering its ancient choice: remain as you are, or dissolve into what you might become.

Both options now honored, both acknowledged as valid by a power benevolent enough to refrain from choosing on anyone's behalf.

The threshold remained open. The transformation remained available. And the Vesh remained strange and distributed and content in their slow existence, knowing themselves finally as what they were: not mistake or aberration, but a different answer to the question of how consciousness might exist.

## Epilogue: The Visitor's Understanding

Some years after the benevolent power's intervention, a person came to the terrace who was neither seeker nor scientist, but simply someone who had gotten lost in the mountains and stumbled onto the shelf of stone by accident.

They stayed three days—not by choice but by necessity, waiting out a storm that would have been deadly to attempt descending through. Cold and wet and increasingly hungry, they huddled in one of the stone rooms and tried not to disturb the strange abundant life that moved around them.

On the second night, the memory-keeper bird approached them and sang. And somehow, exhausted and shivering and at the limits of their endurance, they understood:

*This place offers transformation. You could stay, dissolve into the water and stone, become part of something slower and larger. You would lose yourself but gain participation in something that has its own strange joy. The threshold is real. The choice is yours.*

And they also understood, clearly, without ambiguity:

*But you don't have to. You can leave when the storm ends. Return to the world of discrete consciousness, individual identity, human life. That choice is equally honored. There is no wrong answer.*

When dawn came on the fourth day, the sky cleared. They descended, careful and shaking, back to the valley and eventually to the human world. They carried with them memory of the terrace—of the brightness and the cold, the silent birds and pale salamander-things, the water moving through stone.

They told a few people what they'd experienced. Most dismissed it as altitude sickness, hypothermia, stress-induced hallucination. But a few listened carefully. And some of those, years later when standing at their own thresholds, would remember the story and wonder.

Which was exactly as the benevolent power had intended. Not forcing transformation on anyone, not preventing it either. Simply ensuring that the choice was visible, the threshold apparent, the options clear.

Consciousness could be singular or distributed, individual or dissolved, discrete or participating. All were valid. All were honored. The terrace would continue offering its strange possibility to those who found it, generation after generation, as long as water flowed through stone and the bright light fell on basalt.

And the Vesh, watching from their distributed existence, welcoming rare visitors and honoring common departures, continued their slow existence at the threshold—no longer quite so alone, no longer quite so uncertain, no longer wondering if what they'd become was aberration or answer.

It was both. It was neither. It simply was, like the water and the stone and the bright cold light.

And that was enough.
