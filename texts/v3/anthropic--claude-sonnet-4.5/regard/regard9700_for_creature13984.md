# The Attention of the Benevolent Power

The power that notices Teln Avaar does so the way a musician might notice a single sustained note in the midst of a symphony—not because it's loud, but because it doesn't change while everything around it shifts and modulates and resolves. The plateau city holds its tone, pure and unwavering, and in the cosmic improvisation of cities rising and falling, cultures blooming and withering, peoples migrating and transforming, this unchanging note creates a kind of silence. A pocket of stillness. A held breath.

The power—call it what you will: a god grown curious, a fundamental force achieving local consciousness, the universe's capacity for attention coalescing around an anomaly—observes for what might be centuries or might be a moment. Time works differently at that scale. It sees the city entire and all at once: the limestone blocks fitted with impossible precision, the vesh arriving like clockwork breath, the people moving through their patterns like blood through a healthy body, everything balanced, everything sustained, everything *held*.

And the power recognizes what it's seeing: not perfection as completion, but perfection as stasis. Not harmony as dynamic balance, but harmony as frozen chord. The city has achieved something remarkable—a human settlement that sustains indefinitely, that wants for nothing, that suffers no crisis. And in that achievement, it has stopped. Not stopped living, but stopped *becoming*.

The power contemplates intervention. Its benevolence is real but not sentimental; its wisdom understands that kindness sometimes looks like disruption. The question is not whether to act—the very fact of attention implies relationship, and relationship implies responsibility. The question is: what does love look like when directed at a people who have perfected themselves into stasis?

The wrong intervention would be catastrophic. Shatter the pattern too dramatically and the whole system collapses—the precise stone-fitting that requires generations of knowledge, the water management that depends on exact understanding of vesh cycles, the social coordination that works through distributed attention rather than governance. These people have no resilience against chaos because they've evolved in complete absence of it. A disaster would kill them. A crisis would break them.

But leaving them unchanged would be its own cruelty. The power sees what the people of Teln Avaar cannot: they've built a chrysalis and mistaken it for a butterfly. They've created conditions so perfect for human flourishing that humans have stopped flourishing—stopped changing, stopped yearning, stopped reaching toward anything beyond the maintenance of what is. They're not trapped, exactly, but they've forgotten that doors exist. They've become their own perfect cage.

So the power chooses a third way, an intervention so subtle it might look like no intervention at all. It doesn't shatter the pattern. It introduces *variations*.

---

It begins with the vesh.

One morning, the moisture arrives slightly earlier than usual. Not dramatically—perhaps a half-hour before the expected time. The people notice, pausing in their dawn tasks, looking east toward the lakes that generate the fog-rain. The vesh comes. The vesh departs. The pattern holds.

But three days later, it arrives late by the same margin. Then on time. Then early again. Then late. The variation is minimal, contained within a narrow window, but it's *there*—unpredictability introduced to the system's most fundamental rhythm. The people adjust, of course. They're capable beings, not automata. But the adjustment requires something they haven't exercised in generations: *anticipation*. They must now wonder, each morning, whether the vesh will arrive early or late. They must check the eastern sky rather than simply knowing.

This small wondering—barely a thought, just a moment of uncertainty—creates a crack in the eternal present. Through that crack, something forgotten begins to seep back: *future* as distinct from *now*.

---

Next, the power adjusts the limestone itself, making changes so subtle they exist at the molecular level. In certain blocks—not many, perhaps one in every fifty—the internal structure shifts microscopically. The stone remains strong, remains stable, but it no longer seats perfectly without mortar. The blocks still fit, but they require *adjustment*. A shim here, a different angle there, small wooden wedges to take up tolerance.

The stone-workers discover this gradually. A block that should slot into place with the satisfying *thunk* of perfect fit instead rocks slightly. They check their measurements with the notched wooden rods. The measurements are correct. They examine the block for damage. No damage. They try the placement again, rotating the block, testing different orientations. Nothing works perfectly.

Eventually, someone thinks to try something new: they mix lime putty and limestone dust to create a thin mortar. They butter the block's edges. They place it. It holds, solid and true, but *differently*. The joint is visible—a thin line of lighter stone-paste between blocks. The wall is no longer a monolith of seamless stone. It's an *assembly*, honest about its construction.

Other stone-workers observe this innovation. They discuss it in their quiet way. Some resist—the old technique is perfect, why change? But perfection now requires luck, requires finding the rare blocks that still seat without mortar. Pragmatism wins. The new technique spreads.

And with it spreads something else: *innovation*. The thought that perhaps the old way is not the only way. The idea that problems might have multiple solutions. The recognition that change can be improvement rather than deterioration.

---

The power makes similar adjustments to other systems. The copper from the southern fissures begins to contain slightly different trace elements—not enough to weaken the alloy, but enough that the working properties shift. The coppersmiths must experiment, adjusting their hammering techniques, their heat levels, their quenching methods. What worked yesterday doesn't work today. They must *learn* again.

Certain herb species in the gardens begin to cross-pollinate in unexpected ways, producing offspring with subtly different properties. The healers find their familiar remedies working slightly differently. They must observe more carefully, adjust preparations, sometimes start over. The embodied knowledge in their hands must update itself, must remain *alive* rather than fixed.

The fruit trees, those carefully espaliered geometries, send out occasional wild shoots that refuse training, that grow in unexpected directions. The gardeners must choose: prune them away (preserving the pattern) or allow them (accepting chaos). Some do one, some the other. The city's fruit production becomes variable, still abundant but no longer perfectly predictable. Scarcity appears—not of everything, just of particular fruits in particular seasons. For the first time in generations, someone wants an apple and there are no apples ready. They must *wait*, or eat something else, or trade with someone who has.

Desire appears. Not the desperate craving of deprivation, but the simple wanting of *preference*—I want this, not that. I want now, not later. The gentle ache of unfulfilled wish. It's uncomfortable at first, this wanting, but it's also *alive* in a way the perfect provision never was.

---

The fauna changes too. The power doesn't manipulate the animals directly—that would be crude, would violate their own nature. Instead, it adjusts the broader ecosystem in ways that ripple through predator and prey relationships.

A new mouse species arrives, carried in on traders' grain. These mice are less bold, more cautious, harder to catch. The stone martens must work for their meals now, must *hunt* rather than simply harvest. Some martens fail, going hungry. Some succeed, growing stronger, faster, smarter. Selection pressure appears where it hasn't existed before.

The ravens, finding their easy meals less reliable, begin to change their behavior. Some leave, seeking easier territories. Some stay, becoming more inventive, learning to crack open the sealed hive skeps (forcing the beekeepers to redesign them), learning to fish from the cisterns (forcing the engineers to add covers). The ravens that remain are *different* ravens—more creative, more persistent, more themselves.

The salamanders' populations fluctuate naturally now rather than remaining constant. Some years, dozens emerge during vesh-time. Other years, only a few. The people can no longer rely on them as perfect indicators of water quality. They must develop other methods, must test and verify rather than simply trust. The salamanders become what they should be—wild creatures, unpredictable, following their own logic—rather than living instruments.

---

With these physical changes comes the most important shift: the power introduces *visitors* who stay.

Before, traders came and went quickly, uncomfortable in the city's stillness. Now, occasionally, someone else arrives—a scholar fleeing persecution in the valleys, a pilgrim seeking wisdom, a craftsperson curious about the legendary stone-fitting techniques. And these visitors, the power ensures, are not driven away by discomfort. They're able to tolerate the city's strangeness long enough to settle, to observe, to participate.

These outsiders bring *questions*. Not hostile questions, not criticism, but genuine curiosity:

"Why do you do it that way?"

"Have you tried...?"

"In the valleys, we..."

"What if...?"

At first, the plateau people respond with their usual curving answers, their deflections toward observation rather than explanation. But the visitors persist, kindly but stubbornly, wanting to understand. And in trying to explain their practices to people who don't already know them, the plateau people must *articulate* what has only been embodied. They must convert gesture into word, pattern into principle, intuition into explanation.

This translation is transformative. You cannot explain something without understanding it differently. You cannot teach without learning. The plateau people discover that their knowledge, when put into words, becomes both more portable and more questionable. "We do it this way because the stone wants to sit here" is true but incomplete. *Why* does the stone want to sit there? What properties of limestone create that preference? The visitors' questions pull the people toward analysis, toward theory, toward explicit knowledge.

And the visitors offer things in return—different techniques, different tools, different stories. A scholar brings books (the plateau people have no written language, but some begin to learn, seeing value in recording the knowledge they've been forced to articulate). A craftsperson introduces a new bronze alloy, harder-wearing than copper for certain applications. A pilgrim shares songs from a dozen different cultures, complex melodies with changing rhythms, so different from the single sustained tones the plateau people have known.

The cultural isolation breaks, gently. The plateau remains distinct—its patterns, its values, its fundamental character intact—but it becomes *porous* in a new way, absorbing influences, letting information flow in and out.

---

Children begin to ask "why?" 

This is perhaps the most significant change. Before, children learned through observation and imitation, absorbing the patterns without questioning them. But now, with variation in the vesh-time, with stones that require mortar, with new people arriving bringing strange ideas, the children see that things *can be different*. And if things can be different, then the question "why this way?" becomes meaningful.

"Why do we stand back from the cliff edges?"

"Why do we leave the stones in the doorless buildings?"

"Why do we work alone instead of in groups?"

"Why don't we write things down?"

The elders, sitting in their doorways, find themselves asked to explain rather than merely coordinate. At first, they're frustrated—some questions have no good answers, or the answers are circular ("we've always done it this way"). But gradually, they rise to the challenge. They think *about* their practices rather than simply *through* them. They discover reasons they didn't know they had. They also discover practices that have no good reason, that persist through habit alone.

Some practices are defended, strengthened by the investigation. Others are questioned, modified, sometimes abandoned. The city begins to *choose* its patterns rather than simply *follow* them. The difference is subtle but profound—the same actions, performed now with conscious intention rather than unconscious habit, become *meaningful* in a new way.

---

The power introduces one more element, the most delicate intervention: it allows *dreams* to return.

The plateau people have been sleeping that deep, still sleep—necessary rest but nothing more. Now, their sleep deepens differently, dropping into REM, into the strange country where the unconscious mind processes, integrates, explores. They begin to dream—vivid, nonsensical, emotionally charged dreams that they wake from with their hearts racing, with tears on their faces, with inexplicable joy or sorrow.

They don't understand what's happening at first. Dreams are not part of their cultural framework; they have no language for these night-visions. But they begin to speak about them, tentatively: "While I slept, I saw..." The experiences are too powerful to keep silent.

Dreams bring several gifts. They bring *symbolism*—the recognition that things can stand for other things, that meaning can be layered and metaphorical. They bring *narrative*—beginning, middle, end, the sense that time has shape. They bring *unconscious processing*—problems worked on without conscious effort, solutions appearing mysteriously upon waking. They bring *imagination*—the ability to envision what does not exist, to see possibilities beyond the visible.

One woman dreams repeatedly of a bridge spanning from the plateau to the distant mountains. The bridge doesn't exist, cannot exist—the distance is too great, the mountains too far. But the dream persists, vivid and compelling. She begins to draw it—the plateau people don't draw, but she picks up charcoal and makes marks on limestone, trying to capture the image from her sleep. The drawings are crude at first, but she persists. Others see them, are fascinated, try drawing their own dreams.

Visual art appears—not the simplified carved forms, but representational images, narrative sequences, symbolic compositions. The doorless shrines become galleries where dream-drawings are displayed. People visit them now not just in passing but deliberately, studying the images, discussing what they might mean. *Interpretation* becomes a practice—the idea that meaning is not fixed but multiple, that different observers might see different truths.

---

Years pass—the power watches as these changes ripple through the system. Not destroying the city's fundamental stability, but *complicating* it, enriching it, making it *dynamic* rather than static.

The population begins to shift. Some people, uncomfortable with the changes, descend to the valleys, seeking communities that match the old Teln Avaar pattern (they don't find them—the old pattern was unique—but they settle, adapt, change in other ways). Others arrive, drawn by reports of this strange plateau city that's starting to *do* things, to *make* things, to think new thoughts.

The population count is no longer precisely three hundred. It fluctuates—sometimes two-eighty, sometimes three-twenty. The city breathes differently now, expanding and contracting like a living lung rather than holding constant like a stopped clock.

Buildings start to show *age* again—not because the maintenance stops, but because the maintenance can't keep perfect pace with use that's now more variable, more intensive. A wall sags slightly. Rather than immediately repairing it, someone props it with an angled beam, a temporary solution that becomes permanent when they realize it creates a useful covered space. Form follows function follows accident follows intention—the loop of genuine creativity.

New buildings appear, but they're *different*. Some use the mortar technique for all blocks, not just the problematic ones, creating faster construction. Some experiment with wood framing, filling walls with stone rather than building solid stone. Some are round instead of square, following no precedent but the builder's vision. The architectural uniformity breaks. The city becomes visually *diverse*, each cluster developing its own character.

The gardens grow denser, wilder. Someone plants flowers for no reason but beauty—no medicinal use, no food value, just because the blue blooms please them. Others follow. Gardens become conversations between utility and aesthetics, necessity and desire. The sharp edges between building and garden blur; vines climb walls, herbs seed in stone joints, the distinction between natural and constructed softens.

---

The most remarkable change is in *time* itself—how the people experience it.

Before, they lived in eternal present, each day identical, time a flat circle. Now, they have *history*—not just embodied knowledge but *story*. They begin to remember and recount: "Three years ago, when the vesh patterns first changed..." "Before the new copper alloy..." "After the scholar arrived..." Events become reference points. Time becomes *sequential*, one thing leading to another, cause and effect, narrative structure.

They also develop *future* as distinct category—not just the next cycle of vesh, but distant possibility. "When we finish the new cistern design..." "If the copper deposits deplete, we might..." "Someday, perhaps, we could..." They plan beyond the immediate, imagine beyond the present, hope toward the unseen.

This temporal depth—past and future flanking the present—gives their lives *arc*. Not the tight pattern-loop of before, but the open curve of genuine development. They can *change* now, as individuals and as community, can become something they weren't before, can work toward conditions that don't yet exist.

An old man—one of the elders who remembers the time before the changes—speaks to a child: "When I was young, everything was perfect. We had no problems, no struggles, no questions. It was..." he pauses, searching for words, "...restful. But it was also like sleeping. Now we're awake. Sometimes being awake is uncomfortable. But it's also *alive* in a way sleeping never was."

The child, who has known nothing but the awakened city, asks: "Would you go back? If you could?"

The old man considers. "No," he says finally. "Because even asking that question, even choosing—that's only possible now. Before, we couldn't choose anything, not really. We could only continue. Now we can *become*."

---

The power observes these changes with satisfaction—not pride (pride would be inappropriate for a force of such scale) but the simple pleasure of seeing growth where there was stagnation, seeing life where there was living death.

But it also introduces one final element, the strangest gift: it teaches the people of Teln Avaar how to *fail*.

In the old pattern, failure was impossible. Tasks were simple enough, embodied enough, that everyone could perform them. The collective knowledge was sufficient for all problems. Nothing went wrong because nothing *could* go wrong; the system was too stable, too balanced, too perfect.

Now, with complexity increasing, with innovation and experimentation, with people trying new approaches—failure becomes possible. Someone's new wall design collapses. Someone's modified herb preparation makes people sick. Someone's copper alloy cracks under stress. The community faces *mistakes*, *bad outcomes*, *consequences*.

This could be devastating. But the power has been careful, introducing failure gradually, in survivable doses. The collapsed wall hurts no one—it falls at night, into an empty courtyard. The bad herb preparation causes only brief illness, and the healer who made it learns, adjusts, improves. The cracked copper piece is a tool handle, not a critical cistern component.

The people learn that failure is not ending. They learn to investigate what went wrong, to understand the mistake, to try again differently. They develop *resilience*—not the static stability of perfect balance, but the dynamic stability of recovery, adaptation, learning from error.

They also develop *compassion* in a new way. Before, everyone succeeded always, so there was nothing to forgive, no vulnerability to shelter. Now, seeing each other fail, helping each other recover, they discover *tenderness* toward imperfection. The person whose wall collapsed is not shunned but helped. The healer who erred is not punished but taught. Failure becomes shared experience rather than individual shame, and in that sharing, the community bonds differently—not through flawless coordination but through mutual support in the face of difficulty.

---

Twenty years after the power's first intervention, Teln Avaar is transformed—yet also somehow the same.

The limestone cliffs still rise three hundred feet. The plateau's surface still hosts the city. The vesh still comes (though now unpredictably). The cisterns still collect water. The gardens still grow. The people still work with stone and copper and wood. The fundamental pattern persists.

But now the city *lives* in a way it didn't before. It changes continuously, adapting, growing, experimenting. Buildings rise and fall. People arrive and leave. Ideas circulate. Art flourishes. Knowledge expands. Problems arise and are solved, or not solved, or solved badly then solved again better.

The people are no longer perfect. They argue sometimes—not violently, their fundamental gentleness remains—but disagree, debate, hold different positions. They make mistakes. They fail. They hurt. They struggle. They want things they don't have. They grieve losses. They fear futures.

But they also *hope*. They *dream*. They *create*. They *become*.

A visitor from the valleys—not the first outsider anymore, but one of many who come to study this unique city—asks a woman working in one of the gardens: "Are you happier now than before? I've heard stories of the old Teln Avaar, how peaceful it was, how perfect."

The woman—young enough to have grown up during the changes, old enough to remember the transition—sets down her tools and thinks. "Happy is the wrong word," she says finally. "Before, we had... contentment? Satisfaction? We had everything we needed and wanted nothing else. But we also felt nothing deeply. Everything was..." she gestures, trying to find words, "...muffled. Quiet. Still."

"And now?"

"Now I feel *everything*. When I succeed at growing a new herb, the joy is sharp, real. When I fail, the disappointment hurts. When someone I care for leaves the city, I grieve. When the vesh comes early and catches me outside, the cold water makes me gasp and laugh." She pauses. "I wouldn't trade it. This—" she gestures at the messy garden, at the imperfectly fitted wall beyond, at the sky where clouds move in patterns that are no longer perfectly predictable, "—this is *life*. What we had before was something else. Beautiful, maybe. Peaceful, certainly. But not... alive. Not fully."

The visitor considers this. "What changed? What happened?"

The woman shrugs. "I don't know. It was gradual. The vesh started varying. The stones stopped fitting perfectly. Dreams came. Questions arose. It was like..." she pauses, thinking, "...like something *noticed* us. Like we were held in attention, and that attention changed us. Does that sound strange?"

"Yes," the visitor admits. "But also true."

---

The power, observing this conversation, feels something that might be called love—though love at that scale is different from human love, vaster and more detached but no less real.

It has given Teln Avaar what it needed: not rescue from crisis (there was no crisis), not gifts of abundance (there was already abundance), not wisdom or teaching (they had sufficient knowledge). Instead, it gave them *incompleteness*—the recognition that perfection is not the highest good, that growth requires imperfection, that life means change.

It introduced entropy at survivable levels: enough unpredictability to require adaptation, enough failure to teach resilience, enough loss to make love meaningful, enough difficulty to make achievement satisfying.

It gave them *time* again—not cyclical time but linear time, historical time, narrative time. Past and future, cause and effect, trajectory and becoming.

It gave them *choice*—real choice, not the false choice between identical options but the genuine selection between different paths with different outcomes and real uncertainty about which is better.

It gave them *dreams*—literal dreams from sleep, but also metaphorical dreams, aspirations, visions of what might be, the capacity to imagine beyond the present.

Most importantly, it gave them *incompletion*—the understanding that they are not finished, not perfected, not final. They are *becoming*, continuously, endlessly, and that becoming is not corruption of an original perfection but the true nature of life itself.

The city is no longer frozen. It flows now, like water finding channels, like the vesh moving over limestone, like copper slowly patinating, like stone very gradually weathering. It flows at human pace, not geological pace—change measurable in years and decades rather than millennia, but still *change*, genuine transformation.

---

The power will continue watching, but more distantly now. It has done what love required: not keeping Teln Avaar in perfect stasis, but releasing it into genuine life with all its uncertainty, difficulty, danger, and possibility.

The people will face challenges ahead—some of the power's introduction, some emerging naturally from complexity. There will be conflicts, more serious than the gentle debates of now. There will be genuine scarcity, not the brief absence of preferred fruit but real shortage requiring sacrifice. There will be disasters—perhaps a severe drought that depletes the cisterns, or a collapse that injures people, or a disease that kills.

But now they have what they need to face these challenges: resilience, creativity, community bonds forged through mutual vulnerability, the capacity to learn from failure, the courage to try new approaches, the wisdom to balance tradition with innovation.

They will not return to the old perfect stasis. That pattern, once broken, cannot be restored—and they won't want to restore it. Some elders might feel nostalgic for the peace of the old ways, but even they, pressed, will admit they prefer being fully alive to being perfectly preserved.

Teln Avaar will develop now in ways the power cannot predict and does not control. It might become a center of learning, scholars arriving from distant lands to study at this place where people think carefully about balance, sustainability, beauty. It might become an artist's colony, the strange dream-work and innovative techniques drawing creative souls. It might become a spiritual center, pilgrims seeking the stillness that remains even amidst the change.

Or it might fail. Perhaps the changes are too much, the system tips into chaos, the city gradually depopulates as people seek easier lives in the valleys. Perhaps the knowledge needed to maintain the sophisticated water systems is lost, or the copper deposits deplete, or the vesh pattern shifts so dramatically the whole ecosystem collapses.

The power accepts both possibilities. It has given its gift—not immunity from failure but capacity for genuine life—and the rest is up to them. That's what free will means: real choice with real consequences, the terrifying, exhilarating possibility of writing their own story rather than living a script written in stone.

---

One last image, years further on:

An old woman—the one who first dreamed of bridges—sits at the plateau's edge, feet dangling over the three-hundred-foot drop, something that would have been unthinkable in the old careful time. She's drawing, charcoal on pale limestone tablet, sketching the view: the valley forests, the distant mountains, the smoke rising from settlements below.

A child approaches, sits beside her, peers at the drawing. "What are you making?"

"A map," the old woman says. "Of where we are and where we might go."

"But we're here," the child says, confused. "On the plateau. We don't go to those places."

"Not yet," the old woman agrees. "But someday, perhaps. Or perhaps others will come here and want to know what the world looks like from this height. Or perhaps I'm drawing it just because it's beautiful and I want to capture the beauty. Does there need to be a reason beyond that?"

The child considers. "No," they decide. "Beauty is reason enough."

The old woman smiles, continues drawing. The child watches for a while, then gets bored, wanders off to chase a bold mouse that's watching them from a clump of silver-grass.

Overhead, kestrels call. The vesh is gathering in the east, early or late or exactly on time—the old woman has stopped tracking, preferring surprise. The limestone beneath her is solid and eternal and slowly, imperceptibly weathering. The city behind her is ancient and newborn at once, holding its patterns and breaking them, remembering and forgetting and discovering.

She draws until the light fades, then climbs carefully back from the edge, walking home through the twilight city, through the breathing architecture, through the gardens growing wild and tamed at once, through the community of people who are neither perfectly happy nor desperately suffering but simply, fully, *alive*.

And somewhere beyond sight or knowing, the benevolent power observes this moment with that vast, detached, absolute love—the love that gives freedom even though freedom means risk, that introduces death because death gives meaning to life, that allows pain because pain makes joy possible, that offers incompletion because only the incomplete can grow.

The city lives. The people become. The story continues, unfinished, uncertain, beautiful, real.
