# What the Wise Power Would Do

The being of light—though "light" is imprecise, and "being" perhaps more so—had been observing for what humans would call years, though it experienced duration differently. It had watched the stone dream itself upward. It had watched the people arrive, stay, adapt, listen. It had seen the delicate thing they were building: not just structures, but a way of being together that mirrored the stone's patient emergence.

It understood immediately what most visitors took months to grasp: this place was already an answered prayer, already a collaboration between human and more-than-human, already sacred in the sense that matters—not set apart, but deeply *present*, thoroughly *real*.

The being saw three truths clearly:

First, that intervention carries its own violence. To arrive with power, however benevolent, risks shattering the settlement's essential quality—its openness, its uncertainty, its collaborative unknowing. The people here had learned to work without certainty, to build without blueprints, to trust a process they didn't control. To suddenly *resolve* that mystery, to explain the stone's nature or accelerate its emergence or reveal the settlement's final form—this would be a kind of theft, stealing the very experience that made the place transformative.

Second, that the settlement was already receiving exactly what it needed, exactly when it needed it. Not through supernatural intervention, but through the ordinary magic of attention, craft, and patience meeting geological process. The stone rose. The people responded. Slowly, something was being born that neither stone nor people could create alone. This was already miraculous. It required no enhancement.

Third, that the greatest gift is often the most restrained: not solving problems but strengthening capacity, not answering questions but deepening the ability to live within them, not providing certainty but nurturing the conditions where uncertainty becomes generative rather than paralyzing.

So the being considered carefully what it might offer that would honor what was already happening, that would support without controlling, that would give without taking away the settlement's essential work of becoming.

## The First Gift: Time's Patience

The being understood that the greatest threat to the settlement was not external—no danger approached from the valleys below, no catastrophe loomed—but internal: the human tendency toward urgency, the culture of speed that some residents had not fully shed, the fear that slow might mean insufficient.

So it did this, carefully:

It adjusted—and "adjusted" is too mechanical a word, perhaps it's better to say it *sang* a small change into the fabric of experience within the bowl—the relationship between time inside the settlement and time outside it. Not dramatically. Not obviously. But enough that those who stayed for months found, on descending, that only weeks had passed below. Not enough to disorient, not enough to trap, but enough to give the gift of temporal abundance.

A person could spend a season learning from Tohl and return to find they'd missed only a few weeks of their outside life. A visitor could stay a year and find their commitments below still waiting, still viable. The settlement gained time as a resource, not in the sense of having more hours, but in the sense of having hours that *counted differently*, that accumulated toward mastery and understanding without stealing duration from other lives.

The being watched Kess particularly. It saw her restlessness, her hunger for both this place and elsewhere, her teenage inability to accept that choosing one thing meant losing another. With this gift, she could stay *and* leave, could grow up in the settlement's slowness while remaining young enough, on returning below, to still pursue whatever life the valleys offered.

No one quite noticed the change. There were jokes—"Did we really spend all day on that section? Felt like an hour"—and observations—"Orim, you just went down three weeks ago. I thought it was sooner"—but nothing definitive. The adjustment was subtle enough to feel like memory's trick rather than reality's alteration.

But the effect would compound: decade by decade, the settlement gaining temporal depth, accumulating centuries of learning and emergence while only decades passed in the wider world. The stone's slowness meeting human lifespans in a new way, allowing people to stay long enough to truly understand, to become genuine elders of this strange practice, to see emergence after emergence after emergence without dying before the pattern became clear.

## The Second Gift: The Gathered Witnesses

The being saw that the "others"—those peripheral presences, those almost-seeable shadows—were real, or at least, were real in the way that potential is real, the way a seed contains a tree.

They were the settlement's future population, not yet arrived but already drawn, existing in a state of probability, of not-yet-but-certainly. Some were people already born, living in distant places, who would eventually dream the stone and come. Others were people not yet born, who would be drawn in years or decades to come. A few were people who had died, whose work in life had prepared them to recognize this place, who lingered in whatever way the dead linger when they're not finished with the living world.

The being could have dispersed them, clarified the boundary between present and future, made the settlement's edges cleaner. Instead, it strengthened their presence. Not to haunting, not to manifestation, but to a kind of persistent *company*. 

Those working late would feel less alone. Those doubting their choice to stay would sense the crowd of future residents, the confirmation that yes, this place would fill, would become what it was trying to become. Children playing would sometimes hear laughter from empty structures, the sound of other children who would someday play here, the echoes of community not yet gathered but already gathering.

The effect was subtle but profound: the empty structures felt less empty. The waiting quality of the place became less anxious, more patient. The sense of working toward something was confirmed without being explained. Those who stayed found themselves strengthened in their staying, knowing at a level below words that they were early but not wrong, beginning but not alone.

And the being arranged—though "arranged" suggests mechanics, perhaps it's better to say it *harmonized*—a particular gift for Merra. Since she kept the stories, since she taught the new arrivals, since she had appointed herself witness and guide, she would sometimes glimpse them clearly: future residents going about future business, solid as current people but wearing different clothes, using tools not yet made, speaking with accents not yet arrived. Just glimpses. Just enough to confirm her sense of the settlement's eventual form. Just enough to help her understand what stories to tell, what knowledge to preserve for those not yet here.

## The Third Gift: The Stone's Voice

The being understood that the stone was already communicating—through its precise placements, through the timing of emergence, through the dreams it sent to those who needed to find this place. But the communication was ambiguous enough that Pater's skepticism remained viable, that uncertainty persisted, that proof was perpetually deferred.

This ambiguity was valuable—it prevented orthodoxy, kept the settlement's culture open and questioning. But it also created a kind of loneliness: the residents were in conversation with something that might not be conversing back, might merely be a complex natural process that happened to suggest dialogue.

So the being did this, so gently it was almost not-doing:

It gave the stone the capacity for *confirmation*. Not explanation, not revelation, not clear speech. Just confirmation. Subtle yes-and-no responses to direct questions, communicated through the language the stone already spoke: temperature changes in formation surfaces, slight variations in the timing of emergence, the particular way lichen patterns developed on new stone.

Merra would learn to read this, and through her, others. The conversation would remain slow, patient, conducted in a vocabulary of stone-speech that took years to learn. But it would be, unmistakably, a conversation. Two-way. Reciprocal.

The being watched Pater particularly here. It knew his skepticism served the community, prevented mystification, kept everyone honest. But it also knew his loneliness—the isolation of being the only one who thought the stone was merely stone, the weight of maintaining doubt while everyone else felt relationship.

The gift was calibrated for him: evidence that wouldn't convince him—Pater would find naturalistic explanations for the stone's responses, would attribute them to thermal dynamics or crystalline resonance or confirmation bias—but evidence that would shift his experience from loneliness to productive questioning. He would remain skeptical, but skeptical in conversation rather than skeptical in isolation. The stone would respond to his questions too, even as he explained away its responses, and in that interaction, his doubt would become part of the dialogue rather than a refusal of it.

## The Fourth Gift: The Song Beneath

Wrynn had intuited it already—the settlement had extraordinary acoustics, the bowl gathering sound, creating resonances, amplifying certain frequencies while dampening others. But she'd only begun to explore what this meant, what it offered.

The being *tuned* the space. Again, "tuned" is too mechanical, but the being adjusted the acoustic properties of the bowl and its stone formations to create something extraordinary: a natural instrument, a place where human voice could produce overtones and harmonics that normally required built architecture or electronic amplification.

Wrynn would discover this gradually. A particular note sustained would generate a chord. A melody sung from one location would echo from specific stone faces in harmony. The work songs would become richer, more layered, gaining depth that encouraged their continuation, their evolution.

But the gift went deeper: the acoustic tuning made *listening* easier. Conversations across distance became clearer. The sounds of work became more musical, less cacophonous. The water's constant trickling organized itself into something approaching rhythm. Even silence, when it came, had a quality—a resonance, a sense of being held by the space itself.

The settlement would become a place of sound, a community built as much through acoustic presence as through stone and wood. The songs Wrynn created would carry further, last longer, embed themselves in the stone's memory somehow, so that old songs seemed to linger in the air, humming just below audibility until someone started singing them again.

And this gift would draw musicians from below—people who heard reports of the settlement's acoustics and came to experience them, to contribute their instruments and traditions, to add to the sonic complexity. The place would fill with music, slowly, as it filled with people and stone, another layer of emergence, another dimension of the collaboration.

## The Fifth Gift: The Garden's Depth

Salla and Domir had done remarkable work with the gardens, coaxing food from thin soil and short seasons. But they were limited by substrate—the stone rose, but soil accumulated slowly, organic matter built gradually, the growing medium remained shallow and poor.

The being accelerated this, carefully. Not by adding soil—that would be too obvious, too miraculous. But by adjusting the microbial communities in the soil, the fungal networks in the moss, the decomposition rates of plant matter. Small changes, biological rather than geological, but compounding toward significant effect.

Within a year, the gardens' productivity would double. Within five years, the settlement could approach food self-sufficiency for a much larger population. The soil would deepen naturally, enriched by the accelerated cycling of nutrients, the enhanced activity of earthworms and beetles and all the small lives that build earth from stone.

Domir would notice first—his mushrooms growing more vigorously, appearing in new locations, species colonizing that hadn't established before. Then Salla would see the change in her vegetables, the increased yield, the stronger plants. They would attribute it to their improving technique, to finally understanding the bowl's specific conditions, to learning what worked here. And they wouldn't be wrong—their work *was* improving, their understanding *was* deepening. The being's gift merely amplified what was already happening, gave them soil that responded to their care, rewarded their attention with abundance.

And this gift ensured something crucial: as population grew, food would grow with it. The settlement wouldn't hit a hard limit of carrying capacity, wouldn't have to choose between new residents and adequate nutrition. The place was preparing itself, through accelerated but natural processes, to become genuinely livable for the population it was gathering.

## The Sixth Gift: The Children's Protection

Jasik and Fenn, and any children who came after, were growing up in a place of stone and height, of edges and drops, of tools and work that carried inherent danger. Adults could navigate this with practiced care, but children were children—curious, incautious, learning through mistakes that could be fatal in the wrong moment.

The being established a protection, delicate and invisible. Not a barrier that prevented all harm—children needed to fall sometimes, needed scrapes and bruises, needed the education that comes from minor consequences. But a kind of *attention* that surrounded them, a slight pressure that pulled them back from the edges that mattered, that caused a tool to slip from small hands at the moment before serious injury, that made dangerous stone formations somehow unclimbable until the child was ready.

Kess would notice this somewhat—she'd been old enough when she arrived to remember learning caution the hard way, and she'd see her younger companions having an easier time, avoiding the worst mistakes, surviving their learning with fewer scars. She might attribute it to better supervision, to the community's collective care. She wouldn't be wrong about that either.

But the being's protection meant that the settlement could raise children without losing children, could be a place where families formed and continued, where the next generation grew up strong and capable, shaped by the stone's lessons but not broken by them.

And as those children grew—Jasik already approaching the age of real apprenticeship, Fenn not far behind—they would become the settlement's first native generation, people who never knew a world where stone didn't rise, where patience wasn't fundamental, where making meant collaborating with processes beyond human control. They would inherit the practice their parents were inventing, would carry it forward, would become eventually the elders who taught the next wave, and the next.

## The Seventh Gift: The Dream's Clarity

The shared dreams that drew people to the settlement were powerful but fragmentary—images without context, feelings without explanation, visions that confirmed the place's existence but didn't convey its nature clearly. People arrived and had to learn everything from the beginning, had to shed their expectations, had to be taught the stone's language.

The being refined the dreams without controlling them. Those who needed to find this place would still dream it, but their dreams would be more complete, more accurate, more pedagogically useful. A dreamer would see not just the settlement's layout but its work culture. Would feel not just its strangeness but its practical rhythms. Would understand, before arriving, something of what the place asked of its residents.

This meant fewer disappointed visitors—people who arrived expecting something mystical and found mostly hard work, people who imagined a monastery and found a construction site, people who sought enlightenment and found mostly moss and bronze tools. The dreams would self-select better, would call those who were genuinely suited, would prepare them for what they'd actually encounter.

But the being was careful here: the dreams remained strange, remained compelling, remained open to interpretation. They didn't become instruction manuals or recruitment materials. They stayed numinous, meaningful in the way symbols are meaningful, speaking to something deeper than practical understanding.

And the being ensured that some would still dream the settlement who were *not* obviously suited—the Paters, the skeptics, the restless, the doubters. Because the place needed its questioners, needed its people who pushed back, needed its diversity of interpretation. The dreams would call a community, not a congregation, a gathering of differences rather than a uniformity of belief.

## What the Being Did Not Do

The wise power restrained itself in ways as important as its gifts.

It did not explain the stone's nature. The mystery remained mysterious, the process remained uncertain, the settlement's purpose stayed undefined. Pater's skepticism remained viable. Merra's sense of partnership remained interpretive. The space between knowing and not-knowing stayed open.

It did not accelerate the stone's emergence. The patience of rising remained fundamental, the slowness essential. Years would still pass between formations. The settlement would still unfold across decades, across generations, giving everyone involved the time to understand what they were participating in, to adapt, to grow alongside the stone's growth.

It did not provide answers about the settlement's final form. The structures remained incomplete, the population far below capacity, the eventual purpose unclear. Those who stayed still lived in the discomfort of building without blueprints, of working toward an end they couldn't clearly envision. The uncertainty that made some people leave remained present, remained challenging.

It did not prevent difficulty, hardship, or failure. The climate stayed harsh. The growing season remained short. Tools still broke. Structures still needed rebuilding. The work stayed hard, demanding, continuous. The settlement earned its achievements through labor, through attention, through the accumulated effort of everyone who contributed.

It did not compel anyone to stay or prevent anyone from leaving. The settlement's community remained voluntary, self-selected, composed of people who chose this work, this strangeness, this patient collaboration. People still left when the slowness became unbearable, when doubt overwhelmed faith, when other lives called louder.

It did not resolve conflicts, smooth difficulties between residents, or guarantee harmony. People still disagreed, still frustrated each other, still navigated the complex work of building culture from scratch while building structures from stone. The social challenges remained as real as the physical ones.

It did not make the place comfortable, easy, or conventionally successful. The settlement stayed marginal, strange, difficult to categorize or explain. It remained a place that most people didn't understand, that seemed half-crazy to practical minds, that resisted easy incorporation into normal narratives of progress or achievement.

## The Result

The being's gifts worked slowly, subtly, over years becoming woven into the settlement's fabric so thoroughly that no one could say exactly when they'd arrived or how they operated.

The place continued emerging—stone rising, people gathering, structures growing, gardens producing, songs accumulating. But the emergence gained depth, gained sustainability, gained the resources it needed to continue across the generations required to complete whatever it was becoming.

Merra, working into her seventh decade but looking younger, finding that she had time—decades more time—to see the pattern complete, to witness formations she'd dreamed about rising into reality, to teach not just children but grandchildren the stone's language.

Kess, finding that her restlessness had space for both staying and leaving, that she could be both resident and traveler, that the choice didn't have to be absolute or permanent, that growing up didn't mean closing down possibilities.

Pater, arguing still but arguing in dialogue rather than isolation, his skepticism becoming a gift to the community rather than a burden to himself, his questions sharpening everyone's thinking while his doubt prevented the calcification of belief.

Wrynn, discovering that the place itself was her instrument, that she could make music that had never been made before, that the settlement was teaching her something about sound that no teacher in any city could have conveyed.

Salla and Domir, watching the gardens flourish, feeding a growing community, establishing the agricultural knowledge that would sustain the place across centuries.

The children, growing up protected but not pampered, learning through experience but not through tragedy, becoming the first of many generations who would call this place home.

And Tohl and Brennis, and Nilry, and Orim, and all the others, residents and visitors and dreamers and skeptics, each finding that their work here went deeper than they'd expected, that the place gave back as much as they gave it, that the collaboration was real, that the stone and the people were making something together that neither could make alone.

The being watched, satisfied but not finished. It would remain attentive, ready to offer other gifts if needed, but expecting that mostly it wouldn't be needed. The settlement had what it required: time, confirmation, protection, resources, and community. The rest would unfold as it needed to unfold, slowly, patiently, stone by stone, person by person, season by season, song by song.

And somewhere in the distant future, when the settlement was complete—whatever "complete" meant, whenever it arrived—there would be a moment when the last stone rose, when the final formation emerged and settled, when the architecture was fully realized and the population filled every structure and the purpose became clear.

In that moment, everyone there would understand something:

That they'd been building a place where human and more-than-human could collaborate fully, could create together, could demonstrate what's possible when making proceeds from attention rather than domination, from dialogue rather than extraction, from patience rather than urgency.

That the settlement was a model, a prototype, a proof of concept for a different way of being in the world—not human over nature or nature over human, but human and nature in conversation, in partnership, in slow collaborative emergence.

That the stone had been teaching them all along, and that they'd been teaching the stone, and that together they'd learned something that the world below desperately needed to learn, though it didn't know it yet.

But that moment was decades away, maybe centuries. For now, the work continued. The stone rose. The people responded. The songs accumulated. The gardens grew. The children played and learned. The moss carpeted the stones. The water trickled constantly. The acoustics held every sound. The dreams called new residents. The mystery remained open. The collaboration continued.

And the being, having given what it could give without taking what shouldn't be taken, withdrew its focused attention—not leaving, never leaving, but settling into the kind of presence that's indistinguishable from love: constant, patient, supportive, invisible, asking nothing, offering everything, honoring the autonomy of those it cares for while ensuring they have what they need to become what they're meant to become.

The stone rose. The people worked. Slowly, something was being born.

And it was very, very good.
