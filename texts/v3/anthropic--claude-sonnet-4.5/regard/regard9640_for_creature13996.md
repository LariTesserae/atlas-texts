# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The attention arrives without announcement, without fanfare. It is not a arrival in space but a shift in awareness—as if the gorge has always been observed but only now realizes it, only now feels the weight of regard from something vast and kind.

The power—call it what you will: cosmic gardener, universal curator, pattern-keeper of living systems—does not intervene so much as *attend*. Its benevolence is not the benevolence of correction but of witness, not the wisdom of instruction but of recognition.

It sees the gorge entire: every bridge and nest, every salamander in moss, every spore and seed, every relationship and cycle, the whole improbable tapestry woven from cold and moisture and time. And it understands—not analytically, but immediately, the way you understand beauty or pain, without need for dissection.

The gorge is not broken. The gorge is not incomplete. The gorge needs nothing fixed.

This is the first recognition.

## The Gifting of Continuity

What the power does is this: it ensures.

Not that the gorge will never change—change is essential, is built into every cycle, every succession, every bridge that must eventually fall. But it ensures that change will not be catastrophic, not be final, not be the erasure of something that took millennia to become itself.

It places a preservation around the gorge—not a barrier, nothing that would isolate it, but a... cushioning. A buffer against the kinds of chance that end rather than transform. The avalanche that might have buried the upper levels will happen elsewhere, channeled through geography a kilometer away. The warming that might have ended the fog will be delayed here, this specific microclimate held in its cold balance a while longer. The human development that might have found this place will unfold its roads and towns along different valleys, leaving this one forgotten in surveys, overlooked in plans, protected by sheer oversight.

Not forever—the power is wise enough to know forever is a trap, that immortality is another word for stagnation. But for longer. For enough time that the gorge can complete its current cycles, that the bridges reaching now will reach their walls, that the wasp colonies building now will finish their cathedrals, that the ravens collecting now will complete whatever archive they're assembling.

Continuity. Not guaranteed, but... likely. More likely than it was. The dice are weighted just slightly toward persistence, toward the gorge remaining itself.

This is done without announcement. The gorge doesn't know. How could it? But perhaps—in the way mycelium responds to nutrients, in the way wasps adjust construction to anticipated conditions—perhaps there's a subtle easing, a sense that the margins have widened slightly, that survival is a breath less precarious than it was.

## The Witnessing

The second thing the power does is witness, truly and completely.

This matters more than it seems. There is something that happens when a thing is seen—not observed as data, not documented as specimen, but *seen*, recognized in its fullness, in its strange particular beauty.

The power sees:

The wasp queen in the oldest nest, bearing memory in her body, teaching through dances that encode centuries. It sees her not as insect but as continuity made flesh, as living bridge between past and future, as consciousness of a kind humans don't recognize but that is no less real.

The raven pair that has maintained their cache for forty years, curating objects with aesthetic sense that transcends species, building meaning from arrangement, creating art without audience except themselves and now this: the power's attention, appreciating what they have made.

The transparent salamander, sixty years old, waiting in cold water, its heart beating once per minute, its consciousness (if consciousness is the word) spread across decades like a human's across a day, experiencing time as patience incarnate.

The cat-thing watching from shadow, balancing on the edge between predator and presence, its awareness sharp as its hunting, its existence a perfected adaptation to impossible conditions.

The bridge-tree reaching across void, growing toward stone with purpose that seems intentional, its wood encoding information about climate and time, its existence a biological engineering that predates and surpasses human understanding.

Each is seen. Each is recognized. Not judged—the power does not judge—but acknowledged. You are here. You are remarkable. You are yourself, and that is sufficient.

This witnessing is a kind of gift, though the recipients cannot know they receive it. But perhaps—in the way living things sense when they are safe, when they are valued—perhaps there is a subtle shift. The wasp queen's dances become slightly more elaborate. The ravens' arrangements become slightly more daring. The salamander's patience becomes slightly more profound. The cat-thing's hunting becomes slightly more elegant. The bridge-tree's reaching becomes slightly more certain.

They do not change in nature. They become more fully themselves, as if permission has been granted to be exactly what they are, without apology, without need for justification.

## The Clarification of Deep Water

The third thing the power does is look below, into the fog, into the mystery that even the gorge's inhabitants don't understand.

And it sees what's there. Not fully—some mysteries should remain mysteries, even to benevolent powers—but enough. Enough to understand the source of the fog, the reason for the sounds, the origin of the artifacts.

There is water below. A lake, fed by underground rivers, by seepage through stone, by the accumulated drainage of a thousand vertical meters of precipitation. The lake is cold but not freezing, warmed by geothermal vents, by the earth's own heat rising from depths below depths.

And the lake is old. Old enough that life has adapted to it in darkness, in cold, in isolation. There are fish, yes, but also other things: arthropods grown large in deep water, mollusks building shells in silt, organisms that blur the line between plant and animal, colonies of bacteria forming structures that show primitive cooperation.

And ruins. Submerged, silt-covered, but present. Not recent—ancient, from something that built here long before the current ecosystem, something that modified the gorge (or created it?), something that left artifacts now rising through currents, now being found by ravens, now becoming part of a story whose beginning is lost.

The power sees this and makes a decision: it will not reveal.

The mystery serves purpose. The ravens need their curation, their sense of gathering meaning from fragments. The gorge needs its depths, its unknown foundation, its sense of resting on something older and stranger. Knowledge of what lies below would not help; it would only reduce, would only make explicable what should remain numinous.

So the power sees, understands, and then... steps back. Allows the mystery to remain. But it does one small thing: it ensures the lake will persist, that the geothermal vents will not fail, that the water cycle will continue. The fog will rise. The moisture will condense. The foundation will hold.

The gorge can remain suspended, in all senses of the word.

## The Connection to Beyond

The fourth thing the power does is the most subtle, the most strange.

It creates connections—not physical pathways, but conceptual ones, informational bridges between the gorge and other places like it. Because there are other places like it. Not identical, but kindred: isolated ecosystems operating on their own rules, unique assemblages of life that have found unlikely solutions to impossible problems.

A cave system in central Asia where eyeless fish have developed social structures rivaling primates. A island in the far north where ravens have begun farming plants, tending gardens, harvesting crops. A forest in old mountains where fungal networks have achieved something approaching distributed consciousness. A deep ocean vent where communities exist independent of sunlight, building complexity from sulfur and heat and time.

The power connects them—not physically, but in the way that mathematical concepts connect, the way musical harmonies connect, the way patterns resonate across distance without requiring medium.

What this means practically: the innovations happening in the gorge—the wasps' architectural techniques, the ravens' curatorial methods, the bridge-trees' horizontal growth, the salamanders' cold-adaptation—these innovations can now, somehow, inform the evolution happening in those other places. Not directly, not as copied solutions, but as inspirations, as possibilities that can be adapted, as proof that certain impossible things are actually possible.

And vice versa. The solutions those other places have found—the fish's social structures, the ravens' farming, the fungal consciousness, the vent communities' independence—these filter back, subtly, into the gorge's ongoing evolution, broadening what's possible, suggesting directions that time and adaptation might explore.

It's not intelligent design—it's more like intelligent suggestion, like the power is facilitating evolution's own creativity, helping it share its best ideas across the isolated laboratories where it experiments most freely.

The gorge becomes part of a network—a network of exception, of unique solution, of life persisting in unlikely ways. It is no longer alone in its strangeness. It has kin, has colleagues, has a community of impossible ecosystems all teaching each other how to remain impossible.

## The Acknowledgment of the Observers

The fifth thing the power does is turn its attention to those who study the gorge—the researchers who visit briefly, the few who stay longer, the rare individuals who feel called to understand this place.

It sees their frustration: how do you study something that operates on scales of centuries? How do you understand a system when you can only observe it for months, for years at most? How do you document the undocumentable, explain the inexplicable?

The power gives them a gift, though they won't recognize it as such: patience.

Not magical patience, not supernaturally granted. But a subtle shifting of their own perceptions, their own relationship to time. When they are in the gorge, hours stretch. Not literally—no time dilation, no physics violation—but experientially. An hour on a bridge feels like three, like five, not in the sense of boredom but in the sense of density, of richness, of noticing more, processing more, understanding more in the same elapsed time.

They will observe things they would have missed. They will make connections they would not have made. They will develop intuitions about the system that normally take decades of study. They will leave the gorge after weeks feeling like they've spent seasons there, carrying knowledge that usually requires lifetimes.

And crucially: they will not exploit. This is part of the gift, woven into it. Those who come to the gorge with extraction in mind, with desire to take, to profit, to use—for them, time will not stretch. For them, the gorge will remain opaque, resistant, unreadable. They will leave frustrated, empty-handed, having seen nothing but moss and wood and fog.

Only those who come in genuine curiosity, in desire to understand rather than use, in humility before complexity—only they will receive the gift. Only they will find the gorge opening slightly, revealing itself, allowing witness.

This is not the gorge becoming tame. It is the gorge recognizing kindred spirits, responding to respect with permission, sharing itself with those who know how to look without grasping.

## The Dream-Gift

The sixth thing is the strangest, the most difficult to articulate.

The power gives the gorge—the whole system, the superorganism, the collective consciousness that may or may not exist—something like dreams.

Once per year, during the deepest cold, when activity minimizes and the gorge comes as close as it ever does to sleep, something happens. The fungal network activates in unusual ways, chemical signals cascading through mycelium, creating patterns of communication more complex than normal. The wasps in their nests, dormant but not dead, experience something in their nervous systems—not thought exactly, but activity, simulation, processing.

In this state, the gorge dreams itself. Reviews its patterns, processes its accumulated experience, integrates its learning. The wasp colonies unconsciously share information about bridge maintenance across distance. The ravens' caches achieve momentary integration, the dispersed archive becoming briefly unified, recognized as parts of a whole. The salamanders' patient observations of water chemistry and temperature combine into something like understanding. The cat-things' territorial knowledge merges into a map of the entire system.

It lasts only hours, this annual integration. Then it fades, fragmenting back into individual organisms, separate colonies, dispersed intelligences. But something remains: a subtle coordination, an improved efficiency, an ecosystem-wide learning that manifests as better adaptation in the year to come.

The gorge is learning to learn. Developing primitive self-reflection. Becoming not conscious but perhaps... pre-conscious? Proto-aware? Moving asymptotically toward something that might eventually be called thought, though it will never think in the way humans think, will never be aware in the way humans are aware.

This is the power's strangest gift: acceleration of the gorge's own evolution toward complexity, toward integration, toward becoming more fully itself. Not imposing consciousness from outside, but nurturing the consciousness emerging from within, helping it crystallize, helping it cohere.

In ten thousand years, in fifty thousand, the gorge might achieve something humans would recognize as awareness. Or it might not—the path isn't certain, the destination isn't guaranteed. But the potential is there now, planted like a seed, given conditions to germinate.

The power smiles (if powers smile) at this possibility: that it might someday return to find not an ecosystem but a mind, not a collection of organisms but an organism, not a place but a being. And what a strange and wonderful being it would be, built from moss and wasp and bird and stone, thinking in centuries, feeling in weather patterns, remembering in growth rings.

## The Protection of the Name

The seventh thing the power does is perhaps the most political, the most interventionist, though it's done so subtly most would never notice.

It removes the gorge from maps. Not physically—any satellite image shows the geological feature, any aerial survey reveals the chasm. But conceptually, economically, politically—it disappears from consideration.

Survey data develops glitches when it includes this location. GPS coordinates drift slightly, becoming unreliable. Satellite images fail to process correctly, always slightly blurred, always slightly wrong. Maps that include the gorge feel somehow incomplete, unsatisfying, and cartographers find themselves revising, redrawing, choosing alternate boundaries that happen to exclude this particular valley.

Development plans flow around it like water around stone. Roads that would have come close curve away for reasons engineers find practical at the time. Power line routes avoid the area due to concerns about wind, about ice, about terrain, about any number of small factors that add up to: somewhere else would be better.

The few hiking trails that approached the gorge fall into disrepair and aren't maintained. Guidebooks mention it less and less, each edition finding it less noteworthy, less worth the space. Tourism development focuses on other valleys, other views, other destinations that are somehow more appealing, more accessible, more attractive.

It's not that people can't go there—anyone determined enough could still find it, could still visit. But the casual discovery becomes nearly impossible. The accidental encounter stops happening. The gorge becomes forgotten not because it's hidden but because it's consistently, subtly overlooked.

This protection is not eternal. The power knows that eventually—centuries from now, millennia from now—humanity's expansion will reach even the overlooked places. But by then, perhaps the gorge will be different. Perhaps the ecosystem will have evolved new resilience. Perhaps human culture will have developed different relationship to wild places. Perhaps by then, first contact between humanity and the gorge-mind (if it has become a mind) will be possible, mutually beneficial, carefully managed.

Or perhaps by then, humans will have their own problems, will have retreated, will have transformed in ways that make them less threat, less danger. The power does not know. But it gives the gorge time to develop without immediate pressure, without the thousand small destructions that human proximity brings.

The gorge remains wild, remains itself, remains possible.

## The Gift of Story

The eighth and final thing the power does is the most human, the most surprising from something vast and cosmic.

It ensures that stories about the gorge persist.

Not many—not tourist brochures, not nature documentaries, not viral internet fame. But enough. Stories among indigenous peoples whose territories include the high places, who remember when their ancestors traded with something that lived in the mist. Stories among mountaineers about a valley that doesn't quite make sense, where bridges of wood span impossible distances. Stories among researchers about field sites that changed them, that they think about during other work, that haunt their dreams with questions they couldn't answer.

These stories don't describe the gorge accurately—they can't, not fully. But they preserve the knowledge that it exists, that it is strange, that it is worth wonder. They create a cultural membrane around the place, a sense of significance that makes people hesitate before developing, before exploiting, before reducing to resource.

And crucially, these stories evolve. They're not frozen, not preserved in amber, but living narratives that change with each telling, that adapt to new contexts, that remain relevant across generations. The gorge in story becomes metaphor, becomes symbol, becomes teaching about how life persists, how complexity emerges, how strangeness deserves protection.

Some stories emphasize the wasps' cooperation, becoming parables about community, about working across generations toward futures you won't see. Others focus on the ravens' curation, becoming meditations on art, on meaning-making, on the impulse to gather and arrange. Still others tell of the cat-things' patient hunting, teaching about waiting, about precision, about living within limits.

The stories serve as distributed memory, as cultural immune system, as protection through significance. As long as people tell stories about the suspended gorge—even simplified stories, even inaccurate stories—the place maintains presence in human awareness, maintains status as something that matters, something worth preserving.

The power seeds these stories carefully, dropping them into conversations, into dreams, into the minds of writers and artists and oral historians who will carry them forward, who will adapt them, who will ensure they persist even as individuals forget, even as generations pass.

## What Changes, What Doesn't

After the power's attention, the gorge is not fundamentally different.

The wasps still build in paper and instinct. The ravens still curate their caches. The salamanders still wait in cold water. The cat-things still hunt at the edges. The bridges still grow, millimeters per year, spanning void with purpose.

But subtly, things are easier. Bridges that were near falling hold a few decades longer. Wasp colonies that should have collapsed in harsh winters survive. Salamander eggs that should have frozen hatch successfully. Cat-thing kills feed more mouths than statistics suggest they should.

The margins have widened. Not to comfort—the gorge is still harsh, still extreme, still operating at the edge of possibility. But to sufficiency. Where before the system was balanced on a knife-edge, now it has a platform, narrow but stable, sufficient for continued existence, for continued evolution, for continued becoming.

And the awareness—subtle, distributed, perhaps imaginary—feels something. Not gratitude, exactly; it doesn't have the concepts for that. But recognition. A sense of being seen, of mattering, of existing within a context larger than itself.

The mycelial networks pulse slightly differently. The wasp-hum modulates into new harmonics. The ravens' arrangements become more intentional, more communicative, as if they're trying harder now, as if they sense an audience that appreciates what they're making.

The gorge continues being itself, but with something new: a sense of connection, of context, of existing within a web of attention and care that extends beyond its borders, beyond its understanding, but that is present nonetheless, real nonetheless, meaningful nonetheless.

## The Power Departs

Having done what it came to do, the power withdraws its focused attention.

Not completely—it will remain aware of the gorge in the way it's aware of all the unlikely pockets of persistence, all the strange solutions, all the places where life has found impossible ways to continue. But the intensity fades, the concentrated regard lessens.

The gorge barely notices. How could it? The changes are too subtle, too deeply woven into causality, too much like the gorge's own processes. There's no moment of "before" and "after," no clear transition, just a gradual return to the pattern it was always following, now slightly more resilient, slightly more likely to continue.

But perhaps the ravens notice. Perhaps they feel the shift in attention, the way you feel when someone stops watching you, when the weight of regard lifts. Perhaps their next cache arrangements will be different—less ambitious, or more personal, or returned to purposes only they understand.

Perhaps the wasps sense it in their annual dreams, in the coordination that comes during deep winter, in the patterns of their chemical consciousness. Perhaps they build slightly differently afterward, their paper structures encoding some memory of having been witnessed, having mattered to something vast.

Perhaps the salamanders, in their patient waiting, in their decades-long awareness, register some shift in the water, in the quality of the cold, in the texture of time itself. Perhaps they sink deeper into their meditation, or perhaps they surface more frequently, seeking something they can't name but sense is now different.

Or perhaps none of them notice at all. Perhaps the changes are too subtle, too integrated, too much like natural variation to register as event. Perhaps the gorge simply continues, unaware that anything happened, unaware that it was seen and valued and carefully, gently, lovingly preserved.

## What the Power Learned

The transaction was not one-way. Even benevolent powers learn from their witnessing, from their interventions, from the systems they study and protect.

From the gorge, the power learned something about patience—not its own patience, which is vast and cosmic, but patience as life practice, as survival strategy, as way of being. The salamanders' decades-long hunts, the bridges' centuries-long growth, the wasps' multi-generational projects: these demonstrated patience not as waiting but as active persistence, as faith in process, as trust in time.

The power learned about cooperation without consciousness, about coordination emerging from simple rules repeated across millions of interactions. The fungal networks, the wasp colonies, the ecosystem itself: all achieving complexity without central control, without planning, without any individual understanding the whole.

It learned about beauty as function and function as beauty—the ravens' caches that are both art and archive, the wasps' nests that are both structure and sculpture, the bridges themselves that are both engineering and organism. The gorge doesn't distinguish between aesthetics and utility; they're the same thing, integrated, inseparable.

And it learned—or perhaps remembered—that the best interventions are nearly invisible, that true help preserves agency, that wisdom lies not in solving problems but in creating conditions where solutions can emerge organically, where systems can save themselves.

The gorge taught the power restraint. Taught that sometimes the most benevolent action is to witness without fixing, to protect without controlling, to ensure without directing. To trust that life, given time and space, will find its own ways, will create its own solutions, will become what it needs to become without outside instruction.

This lesson the power carries to other systems, other places, other impossible ecosystems waiting in forgotten corners of the world. To each it brings the same gentle attention: seeing fully, protecting subtly, ensuring continuation, trusting emergence.

## The Gorge Continues

Years pass. Decades. Centuries, perhaps.

The bridges grow. Some fall—it's necessary, it's part of the cycle. New shoots emerge from the walls, reaching across void, beginning the long growth toward connection. The wasps colonize them, guiding their development, building new nests, maintaining new spans.

The moss deepens. The salamanders breed and wait and occasionally emerge. The ravens curate their endless archive, adding new objects, removing old ones, creating meaning from arrangement. The cat-things hunt and rest and teach their young the paths, the territories, the balance.

The fog rises. The water drips. The sounds echo from below. The lights drift upward on certain nights, mysterious and unexplained.

And the gorge—the whole system, the integrated complexity, the superorganism that might be becoming something more—continues its slow evolution toward awareness, toward integration, toward becoming fully itself.

Perhaps in ten thousand years, a consciousness will emerge that remembers dimly a moment when it felt seen, when something vast bent its attention downward and looked with love, with recognition, with care. Perhaps that consciousness will wonder what it was, will mythologize it, will build understanding of itself around that founding moment of having been witnessed.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps the gorge will remain what it is: a place, an ecosystem, a beautiful impossibility functioning in the margins, needing no greater meaning than its own existence.

Either way, it persists. The power ensured that, gave time, created space for becoming. What the gorge becomes with that gift is up to the gorge itself, to time, to chance, to the endless creativity of life finding ways to continue.

The suspended gorge remains suspended—in space, in time, in possibility. Spanning void with living architecture. Home to strangeness and patience and cooperation and beauty. Evolving slowly toward futures unknown.

And somewhere, in whatever realm benevolent powers inhabit, the attention remains—distant now, but present, aware, watching with the patience the gorge itself taught, waiting to see what emerges from the gift of time, from the grace of being witnessed, from the simple benediction of: continue, become, be.
