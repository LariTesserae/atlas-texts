# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The being who approaches the Greenhouse Courts does so first through attention—which is the ground of all benevolence, the root of wisdom. Before acting, before intervening, before helping or healing or changing, there must be seeing. Truly seeing. Seeing not as humans often see, with categories already fixed and judgments already rendered, but seeing as the thing itself might wish to be seen, in its wholeness, in its becoming.

So the power—let us not name it more specifically than that, for such beings exceed our naming—observes. Not with eyes, perhaps, but with whatever faculty perceives truly. It sees the limestone, both the deep time of its deposition and the recent violence of its excavation, the way these temporalities exist together in each cut face. It sees the Wren People, not just as "birds" but as individual consciousnesses, each threading through its days with concerns and memories and responses as real as any human's, though differently structured. It sees the Moss-Mind not as decoration but as a patient transformer, a slow intelligence that knows what it knows through chemical signaling and moisture gradients rather than through neurons, but knows nonetheless.

The power sees the incompletion—not as failure but as gift. The human intention that began and abandoned this excavation has created something more interesting than any completion would have allowed: a space genuinely shared, not dominated, a place where human and non-human purposes have dissolved into something neither intended but both enable. The abandonment was not neglect but release, not giving up but letting go, and what rushed into that opening was life in its opportunistic abundance.

The power sees also the precarity. The courts exist in that narrow band of conditions that support this particular assemblage: the climate that doesn't freeze, the limestone that provides minerals and substrate, the settlement that protects without consuming, the water that arrives but doesn't flood. These conditions could shift. Climate could change—a few degrees cooler, a few degrees warmer, precipitation patterns altering—and this precise ecosystem would dissolve, replaced by something else or by nothing at all. The settlement could expand, deciding these spaces are too valuable as empty stone rooms, excavating further, completing the original project, displacing all that has come since. Or the abandonment could become neglect of a different kind: the buildings falling into disrepair, people leaving, the courts no longer protected by the settlement that surrounds them, open to different pressures.

The power sees the futures that branch from this moment: paths where the courts continue their slow succession, where trees grow larger, soil deepens, perhaps eventually the stone is covered completely, the excavation forgotten beneath forest. Paths where human intention returns, not to destroy but to "help," installing nest boxes and bird feeders and interpretive signs, transforming wildness into education, claiming the space back into human purpose even while claiming to honor its inhabitants. Paths where disease or drought or a shift in human attention leads to collapse, to silence, to stones empty of everything but ghosts of what once lived there.

## The First Action: Protection Through Attention

The being's first intervention is subtle, so subtle it might not be recognized as intervention at all. It ensures that certain people—not everyone, not even many, but the necessary few—continue to see the courts clearly, to value them not for what they could be but for what they are, to resist the impulse toward completion or improvement or control.

This is not mind control, not the imposition of false consciousness. Rather, it's the removal of obstacles to genuine perception. Humans are so often prevented from seeing truly by their theories, by their instrumental thinking, by the weight of purposes that crowd out simple attention. The power gently lifts these veils for a few people, allowing them moments of clear seeing: a resident standing at their window at dawn, watching the Wren People's morning activity, and suddenly understanding—not intellectually but viscerally—that they're witnessing something as important as any human endeavor. A child playing in the courts, crouching to watch a Still-Watcher hunting, and grasping in that moment that other minds exist, other modes of being, equally valid, equally deserving of space.

These moments of clarity don't require belief in the power's existence, don't demand acknowledgment or worship. They simply happen, leaving people changed in small but crucial ways: more likely to argue for preservation, less likely to support development, more willing to tolerate the wild, less concerned with tidiness and completion. The power doesn't force these changes, merely makes them possible, clearing away enough noise that the courts can speak for themselves to those who live beside them.

And this is sufficient, for now, to protect. Not permanently—nothing protects permanently—but to tilt probabilities toward continuation, to make it more likely that the courts remain incomplete, remain claimed by their dwellers, remain available for whatever succession brings next.

## The Second Action: Subtle Maintenance of Conditions

The power has the capacity to manipulate larger systems—climate, hydrology, even human social dynamics—but does so with the lightest possible touch, knowing that heavy-handed intervention creates dependence, destroys autonomy, makes pets of wild things.

So rather than ensuring rain falls exactly when needed, the power simply... adjusts probabilities slightly. The weather systems that bring moisture to this coastal settlement might shift their track by a few kilometers, not enough to disrupt the larger region's patterns, but enough to increase the likelihood that the courts receive adequate rainfall. Not flooding, not drought relief from outside their natural range, just a gentle bias toward the conditions that support the assemblage as it exists.

Similarly with temperature: the power doesn't create a bubble of perfect climate, doesn't insulate the courts from seasonal variation or natural fluctuation. But it might influence the local microclimate subtly—perhaps by ensuring that development in the surrounding settlement doesn't create heat islands that would stress the courts' plants and animals, perhaps by encouraging air circulation patterns that moderate extremes, perhaps simply by maintaining the limestone's thermal properties, preventing processes that might alter how the stone holds and releases heat.

The water table, too, receives subtle attention. The seeps that provide moisture to the stone walls, that feed the Moss-Mind's growth, that create the dampness necessary for ferns—these depend on groundwater moving through the limestone, which depends on recharge from rain, which depends on land use in the areas upslope from the settlement. The power ensures, through means both physical and social (a farmer choosing to leave a wetland undrained, a developer deciding to install permeable pavement, small choices that seem like personal preference but preserve the hydrology that feeds the courts), that water continues to move through stone, that the courts don't dry out, don't become unsuitable for the moisture-dependent species that define them.

But again: this is not guarantee, not the elimination of all risk. Drought can still come. Hot years will still stress plants. The courts can still die if conditions shift far enough. The power is not building walls against change, only preventing the most likely human-caused disruptions, allowing natural variation to continue while removing anthropogenic threats that the ecosystem didn't evolve to handle.

## The Third Action: Gifts to the Dwellers

For the non-human inhabitants, the power offers small enhancements, never large enough to create dependence, never so dramatic as to alter what they fundamentally are, but enough to ease their lives, to increase their chances, to honor what they're doing in these spaces.

To the Wren People: the power ensures that when they're nesting, when they're most vulnerable, when predators are most dangerous, they experience... not luck, exactly, but a slight bias in the probabilities. The snake that might have found the nest is drawn to hunt elsewhere. The human child who might have climbed to see the eggs is distracted by something more interesting. The storm that might have flooded the cavity comes a week later, after fledging. These are tiny interventions, indistinguishable from chance, but cumulatively they increase breeding success slightly, they ease the pressure, they give the Wren People a little more space to thrive.

To the Still-Watchers: the power might increase, imperceptibly, the abundance of their prey. Not by creating insects from nothing, but by ensuring that the conditions insects need—flower nectar, rotting wood, damp soil—are maintained, that the base of the food web is healthy, that the energy flows upward reliably. The lizards hunt no differently, are no less skilled or less vulnerable, but their hunts succeed slightly more often, enough that they're better fed, healthier, more likely to survive winters and breed successfully.

To the Root-Makers: the power attends to what limits their growth. Perhaps a fig tree's roots are beginning to encounter compacted soil beneath the surface, limiting further expansion, and the power encourages earthworms to work that soil, to create channels, to loosen it imperceptibly. Perhaps a drought year threatens to kill one of the thin-trunked trees, and the power guides its roots to a pocket of moisture trapped in the limestone, a reservoir the tree wouldn't have found otherwise. Not saving trees that should die—succession requires death, renewal, replacement—but preventing unnecessary losses, untimely failures due to bad luck rather than unfitness.

To the Insect Nations: the power is most careful here, because insect populations are volatile by nature, because their boom-and-bust cycles are part of their strategy, because too much help would disrupt the predator-prey dynamics that structure the whole community. So the intervention is minimal: perhaps ensuring that during the worst die-offs, a few more individuals survive than might have otherwise, maintaining genetic diversity, preventing local extinctions. Perhaps guiding certain pollinators to flowers they might have missed, ensuring that rare plants reproduce, that diversity is maintained. Perhaps protecting the eggs of certain keystone species—not from natural predators, but from unlikely catastrophes, from the unseasonable frost that never comes, from the flash flood that doesn't reach the courts, from the human pesticide drift that is, somehow, always blowing in a different direction when it matters.

To the Moss-Mind: the power offers what moss needs most, which is time and stable dampness. By maintaining the courts' hydrology, by ensuring that the stone stays moist in the right places, by preventing the drying that would kill colonies before they establish, the power allows the moss to do what it does: slowly, patiently, inevitably transform stone to soil, create substrate, prepare the ground for succession. This is not acceleration—moss grows at moss-pace, not faster—but it's the removal of setbacks, the prevention of die-backs that would restart the process, allowing the accumulated work to compound.

## The Fourth Action: Witnessing and Memory

The power does something else, something less tangible but perhaps more important: it witnesses. It holds the courts in attention, knowing them fully, remembering them completely. Every Wren Person that lives and dies, every Still-Watcher that basks and hunts, every seed that germinates or fails—all of it is seen, known, remembered.

This might seem useless, might seem like mere observation, but it's not. Because consciousness is rare in the universe, because attention is precious, because being truly seen—not measured, not categorized, not used, but simply witnessed in one's full being—is perhaps the deepest gift one consciousness can offer another.

The Wren People don't know they're being witnessed, don't believe in the power, don't pray to it or acknowledge it. But their lives are held in awareness, their struggles and successes matter to someone, their existence registers in a consciousness vast enough to hold it without diminishment. This is not anthropomorphic sentiment—the power doesn't love them as humans love, doesn't feel sadness at their deaths or joy at their successes in emotional terms. But it values them, recognizes their intrinsic worth, honors what they are by the quality and depth of its attention.

This witnessing also serves a practical purpose: the power builds a complete record, a perfect memory of the courts at every moment. Should catastrophe come, should the ecosystem collapse entirely, the power holds in memory every pattern, every relationship, every detail. This is not for restoration—the power is not in the business of resurrection, of returning things to previous states—but it's for understanding, for learning, for carrying forward the knowledge of what was, what mattered, what this particular assemblage of life achieved in its time.

And perhaps, if the worst happens, if the courts are destroyed by human development or natural disaster, the power might use its memory to guide recovery elsewhere: to find another limestone outcrop, another abandoned excavation, another marginal urban space where, with the gentlest of guidance, something similar might establish itself. Not a recreation, not a copy, but a new expression of the same principles, allowing similar dwellers to find similar opportunities, honoring what was lost by enabling something parallel to emerge.

## The Fifth Action: Teaching (Without Teaching)

The power recognizes that the greatest threat to the courts comes from ignorance—not malice, not greed, but simple failure to understand what's valuable here, what would be lost if these spaces were "improved" or "completed." So it works to educate, but not through speaking, not through messages or miracles or any form of direct communication.

Instead, it arranges encounters. A settlement official walking home through the courts happens to witness the exact moment when a Still-Watcher strikes, the explosive motion after stillness, the perfect adaptation on display. A developer considering a proposal to excavate further and install proper drainage pauses on a morning when the fig is fruiting, watches the convergence of birds and insects, realizes without being told that something complex is happening, something that might be destroyed by intervention.

A child growing up adjacent to the courts experiences them daily, learns from direct observation what an ecosystem is, how species interact, what happens when you leave space for the non-human. This child doesn't receive formal ecology education—though they might later seek it, inspired by what they witnessed—but they gain something more fundamental: direct, embodied knowledge that they're not alone in the world, that other beings have their own purposes, that "wasteland" and "abandoned space" and "empty lot" are failures of perception, not descriptions of reality.

The power doesn't control what people conclude from these encounters, doesn't guarantee that everyone who sees will understand or that everyone who understands will care. Free will remains intact; humans are still free to bulldoze the courts, still free to prioritize development over preservation. But the power ensures that the encounters happen, that the opportunities for understanding are present, that ignorance is not inevitable, that those who are open to seeing will have chances to see.

And for those who do come to understand, who do value the courts, who do become advocates for their preservation, the power offers support: ensuring they're heard when they speak at settlement meetings, that their arguments reach the right ears, that their passion is contagious, that the choice to preserve is made easier by their presence. Not guaranteeing outcomes—democratic processes remain unpredictable, human social dynamics remain complex—but tilting probabilities, making protection more likely than destruction.

## The Sixth Action: Honoring the Incompletion

Finally, and perhaps most importantly, the power does something unusual: it celebrates the incompletion itself, recognizing that what makes the courts valuable is precisely what makes them seem failed by human standards.

The unfinished walls, the tool marks, the rubble piles, the half-excavated niches—these could be viewed as problems to solve, as work to complete, as spaces crying out for human finishing. But the power sees them differently: as affordances, as opportunities, as exactly the right degree of structure to support the community that's established itself. Too much human finishing would eliminate the crevices the Wren People nest in, the rough surfaces the Still-Watchers grip, the pockets where soil accumulates. Too little—just raw cliff face, never touched—might not provide the right diversity of niches, the right mixture of exposures and protections.

The incompletion is perfect, accidentally perfect, the human project abandoned at exactly the point where its failure becomes another kind of success. The power recognizes this and works to preserve it: not by preventing all future excavation (some residents might legitimately need to modify their adjacent spaces), but by ensuring that no one decides to "finish" the courts as a whole, to complete them, to impose the order that was originally intended but never achieved.

This means sometimes deflecting well-meaning attempts to help. A landscape architect inspired by the courts' beauty might propose designs to enhance them, to make them more accessible, to add paths and benches and informational plaques. The power works to ensure that such proposals either don't arise or don't gain traction—perhaps by inspiring the architect to recognize that the courts' value lies in their resistance to design, perhaps by ensuring that funding goes elsewhere, perhaps simply by making the proposal fail in ways that seem like ordinary bureaucratic inertia.

A conservationist might want to manage the courts actively, to remove invasive species, to plant natives, to maximize biodiversity through intervention. The power respects the impulse but deflects the action, helping the conservationist understand (through encounters with scientists, through reading, through observation) that "invasive" and "native" are complex categories, that the current assemblage is functioning, that management often does more harm than good, that the best conservation is often the conservation of letting be.

This is perhaps the most difficult work the power does, because it requires saying no to people who care, who want to help, whose intentions are good. It's easier to deflect those who want to develop, to bulldoze, to destroy for profit. But to gently redirect those who love the courts, who want to protect them through action, to help them see that the best action is often inaction, the best protection is restraint—this requires subtle wisdom, careful attention to each individual, each circumstance.

## What the Power Does Not Do

As important as what the power does is what it refrains from doing:

It does not eliminate predation. The Wren People will still be hunted by the Night-Singer. The Still-Watchers will still be taken by birds. The insects will still be eaten in vast numbers by everything larger. Predation is not cruelty; it's relationship, it's how energy moves through ecosystems, it's necessary. The power doesn't save prey from predators, doesn't prevent death, doesn't try to eliminate the suffering inherent in being alive, being food, being mortal.

It does not prevent all extinctions. Species will disappear from the courts—not necessarily going extinct globally, but locally absent, unable to maintain populations, replaced by others better suited to changing conditions. This is succession, adaptation, the normal functioning of ecological systems. The power doesn't freeze the courts in their current state, doesn't prevent turnover, doesn't make permanent what is by nature temporary.

It does not remove human presence. The residents stay, the passages remain available, children still play in some courts, people still walk through. The power doesn't create a nature preserve, doesn't fence the courts, doesn't treat them as museum exhibits to be viewed but not touched. The courts remain urban, remain lived-in, remain part of human settlement, and that's appropriate, that's part of what they are.

It does not accelerate succession artificially. The moss will grow at moss-speed, the soil will build at soil-speed, the trees will mature across decades, not years. The power doesn't force the courts toward some imagined climax state, doesn't push them to become something other than what they're becoming naturally. Patience is honored; process is respected; the journey is valued as much as any destination.

It does not make the courts permanent. Everything that exists is temporary, conditional, dependent on circumstances that will eventually change. The limestone will continue to weather, eventually eroding away entirely—though this is measured in geological time, irrelevant to current dwellers. The climate will shift over decades and centuries, changing what can live here. The settlement will grow or shrink, persist or be abandoned, and any of these changes will affect the courts. The power can't prevent this, wouldn't prevent it if it could, because permanence is not the goal, not even desirable. What matters is that the courts exist now, that they're allowed to go through their natural succession, that they're given the time to be what they are without premature human interruption.

It does not reveal itself. The power remains hidden, unknown, unacknowledged. No one in the settlement worships it, thanks it, even believes in it. The Wren People don't sing to it, the Still-Watchers don't bask in its honor, the Root-Makers don't grow toward it. All the power's actions are indistinguishable from chance, from luck, from natural process. This anonymity is essential—if people knew a benevolent power was protecting the courts, they might become careless, might assume protection is guaranteed, might stop their own efforts at preservation. The power works best when invisible, when its interventions seem like nothing more than the world working as it should.

## The Long Pattern

Over years, over decades, the power's interventions weave a pattern. Not a designed pattern, not an imposed order, but an emergent one: the courts persist when they might have been destroyed, they flourish when they might have degraded, they continue their succession when they might have been frozen in place or pushed toward collapse.

The pattern is visible in small ways: a line of successful fledgings one year producing a stronger Wren People population the next, which increases predation on caterpillars, which releases certain plants from herbivory, which changes the plant community's composition, which alters the insect community, which feeds back to the birds. The power didn't design this cascade, but by ensuring the initial breeding success, it enabled the cascade to unfold, allowed the ecosystem to express its complexity, to demonstrate its resilience, to surprise even the power itself with what emerges from simple interventions.

The pattern is visible in large ways: decade after decade, the courts remain incomplete, remain wild, remain available to their dwellers. Other parts of the settlement are developed, built up, formalized into parks or paved over for buildings. But these particular spaces persist in their liminal state, neither fully natural nor fully human, and that persistence allows the succession to continue, allows soil to accumulate, allows trees to mature, allows the slow transformation toward something richer, more complex, more stable.

The power watches this pattern with something that might be called satisfaction, if such terms applied. Not pride—the power didn't create this, the dwellers did, through their living and dying and adapting—but appreciation, recognition of value, pleasure in witnessing something beautiful continue to exist and grow.

## The Ultimate Gift: Being Left Alone

In the end, the greatest gift the benevolent power offers the Greenhouse Courts is the gift of being left alone to be what they're becoming. Not abandoned—that happened once already, when human excavation ceased, and that abandonment was generative. But left alone in the sense of: not improved, not managed, not made to serve purposes other than their own, not claimed back into the realm of human intention and control.

This is harder than it sounds, requires constant vigilance, because humans have such difficulty resisting the urge to fix, to help, to manage, to make useful. The power's work is largely the work of deflection, of prevention, of ensuring that the many ways humans might interfere—even when well-meaning—don't manifest, don't gain traction, don't overcome the inertia of leaving things be.

A settlement planner looks at the courts and sees wasted space, unused potential, opportunity for development, and feels the professional obligation to propose something, to activate these voids, to make them serve human needs more explicitly. The power ensures this planner's attention is drawn elsewhere, to actually empty lots, to spaces that genuinely would benefit from development, to projects that matter more. The courts are forgotten, overlooked, dismissed as "not worth the trouble," which is exactly what keeps them safe.

A nature enthusiast looks at the courts and sees something precious that must be protected, and wants to form a friends group, to organize clean-ups, to install nest boxes and feeders, to make the courts better for wildlife. The power ensures this enthusiast's energy goes elsewhere, perhaps to a park that actually needs such help, perhaps to advocacy that targets real threats rather than imagined ones. The courts are left to their own devices, which is what they need.

A property owner adjacent to the courts looks at the vegetation encroaching, the birds making noise, the insects sometimes entering the house, and wants to trim back the plants, to discourage the birds, to spray for bugs. The power ensures that this owner either moves (to a property where such concerns don't arise), or gradually becomes fond of their wild neighbors (through repeated exposure, through positive experiences, through the gentle education the courts provide to those who pay attention), or at minimum decides that the effort of intervention isn't worth the minimal benefit, that living alongside the wild is acceptable, even pleasant.

This work—the work of ensuring benign neglect, protective indifference, sustainable coexistence—is endless, requires constant attention, but it's the core of what preserves the courts. Not dramatic rescue, not heroic intervention, but the quiet, persistent, humble work of preventing interruption, of maintaining the conditions for autonomous flourishing, of keeping open the space for other-than-human purposes to unfold according to their own logic.

## A Vision of the Future

The power can see probabilities, can read the branching paths that lead from this moment toward various futures. It works to make certain futures more likely, but it doesn't guarantee them, doesn't predetermine them. The courts remain free, remain contingent, remain genuinely uncertain in their outcomes.

But if the power's work succeeds, if the courts are protected not just for years but for decades, for centuries, here is what might unfold:

The soil continues to accumulate, slowly, imperceptibly, but compound interest applies to ecosystems as to wealth. What is now a thin layer becomes, in fifty years, thick enough to support larger plants. In a hundred years, the distinction between "soil pocket" and "stone surface" begins to blur as soil covers more area. In two hundred years, parts of the courts might be unrecognizably lush, the limestone visible only in cuts and exposures, the rest covered by earth and the community of plants that soil enables.

The Root-Makers mature. The thin-trunked pioneers are replaced, eventually, by their own offspring or by other species—longer-lived trees, larger trees, trees that can only establish once pioneers have modified the environment, created shade, built soil, changed conditions. The canopy rises, closes, creates a different light environment, which creates a different understory community, which creates different opportunities for different fauna.

The Wren People and Seed-Carriers continue, but perhaps joined by other species as the habitat becomes richer, more complex, more varied in its offerings. Species that need mature trees, that need dense canopy, that need the particular insect communities that such conditions support—they arrive, establish, add their voices to the chorus, increase the diversity, the resilience, the beauty.

The courts become, without anyone intending it, one of the most biodiverse spaces in the settlement, a refuge for species that can't live in the more managed parks, the more formal gardens, the more human-dominated spaces. They become a reservoir of genetic diversity, a source population for recolonization after disturbances elsewhere, a teaching ground where young birds learn to hunt, where young lizards learn to bask, where young trees grow in the protected space before they're ready to compete elsewhere.

The human residents, those who live in the buildings around the courts, continue to benefit without necessarily naming the benefits: cooler air in summer, more humidity in dry periods, the psychological benefits of proximity to living complexity, the aesthetic pleasure of watching succession unfold, the deep satisfaction—often unconscious but real—of living alongside the wild, of knowing their home includes space not entirely claimed by human purpose.

And the courts themselves, these incomplete excavations that were started and abandoned, become complete in a different way: not architecturally complete, not fulfilling the human intention that began them, but ecologically complete, functioning systems with energy flows and nutrient cycles and population dynamics and succession trajectories, complete unto themselves, needing nothing human except to be left alone, to be allowed to continue becoming what they're becoming.

## When the Power Might Leave

There may come a time—decades hence, centuries hence—when the power judges that its work is done, that the courts are stable enough, valued enough, protected enough by human understanding and law and culture that active intervention is no longer necessary. The power might then withdraw its attention, not abandoning the courts but releasing them, trusting them to human care and their own resilience.

This would be success: not that the courts became dependent on the power, not that they require eternal vigilance, but that the power's early interventions created conditions where later interventions became unnecessary, where the courts' value is recognized, where their protection is cultural habit rather than requiring active deflection of threats.

Or the power might stay forever—not because it's needed but because it chooses to, because the courts have become something it loves (if such a word applies), because the witnessing itself is valuable independent of any practical necessity, because to see and be seen, to know and be known, to attend and be attended to is relationship, is connection, is meaning that transcends utility.

Or perhaps most likely: the power fades its attention gradually, remaining aware but less intensely so, intervening less frequently as intervention becomes less necessary, allowing the courts to stand more and more on their own while remaining available for crisis, for the moments when catastrophe looms and a subtle push might prevent disaster. A gardener who visits less often as the garden matures, trusting it more, but never entirely absent, never completely withdrawn.

## The Ethics of Intervention

Throughout all its work, the power maintains ethical constraints, questions its own actions, doubts its own wisdom. Because benevolence is not simple, because helping is not always helping, because the urge to fix can become the urge to control, and control—even benevolent control—diminishes autonomy, reduces freedom, makes subjects of citizens.

So the power asks itself constantly: Am I helping or interfering? Am I protecting or imprisoning? Am I honoring their autonomy or subtly violating it? Am I allowing them to be what they are or shaping them into what I think they should be?

These questions have no final answers, only provisional ones, only the attempt to err on the side of restraint, of minimal intervention, of maximum respect for the dwellers' own agency—limited though that agency might be by the constraints of instinct and ecology and contingency.

The power knows that to be benevolent is not to be certain, not to know what's best, not to have answers. It's to attend carefully, to intervene minimally, to question constantly, to be willing to be wrong, to adjust, to learn. The courts teach the power as much as the power protects the courts, teach it about emergence and resilience and the wisdom of letting be.

And perhaps that's the final gift, the one that flows in the opposite direction: the courts, through their mere existence, through their successful persistence, through the community they've built in these incomplete stone spaces, teach a benevolent power something about benevolence itself—that it's not about grand gestures or transformative interventions, not about rescue or salvation, but about attention, about restraint, about creating and maintaining the conditions where others can flourish according to their own nature, their own purposes, their own becoming.

The Greenhouse Courts don't need a hero. They need a witness, a guardian who guards mostly by watching, who protects mostly by preventing interruption, who helps mostly by ensuring that help isn't needed, that the dwellers can be what they are, can do what they do, can continue their ancient work of living, growing, adapting, succeeding.

And that—the quiet work of witness, the humble work of protection through restraint, the deep work of honoring autonomy while preventing catastrophe—that is what a truly benevolent and wise power would do, does do, is doing even now, invisibly, anonymously, perfectly.

The courts persist. The dwellers flourish. The succession continues. The stone slowly becomes soil. And somewhere, in some way we can't perceive but might, if we're very quiet and very attentive, almost feel—somewhere, something watches, attends, witnesses, protects, loves without possessing, helps without controlling, allows without abandoning.

The Greenhouse Courts are blessed, not by intervention that changes what they are, but by attention that honors what they're becoming, by protection that preserves their freedom, by love that asks nothing in return, that takes no credit, that remains forever hidden, forever present, forever patient, forever wise.
