# What the Wise Power Would Do

The attention arrives without fanfare—no light breaking through the perpetual twilight, no voice echoing off basalt. If the power is truly wise, it knows that this place operates by its own acoustics, its own illuminations. To intrude crudely would be to misunderstand what it has found.

First, the power would do what any wise being does when encountering something complex and functional: it would *watch*. Not for hours or days, but for seasons, for years if necessary. It would observe the walkers in their patient hunts, their visits to the structures, their careful negotiations with each other. It would note how the young emerge and the old disappear. It would track the exact patterns they trace between the posts, count how many times each walker visits the circular enclosure in a cycle, correlate these visits with environmental factors—the heat pulses, the particulate falls, the subtle dimming and brightening of that bruised sun.

The power would recognize what many would miss: that this is not a place of lack but of specificity. The walkers are not trapped or diminished. They are *adapted*, their bodies and behaviors shaped by countless generations into perfect答 to this shelf's particular questions. They hunt with efficiency that borders on art. They navigate social complexity without apparent language. They maintain relationship with structures whose purpose they don't remember but whose significance they've never forgotten.

But the power would also see what the walkers themselves might not articulate: the gazing into distances, the sounds called into silence, the way they return again and again to walls that were old when their ancestors' ancestors first stepped carefully across this living ground. Not suffering, exactly, but a longing so ancient it has become simply the way things are, the way they've always been.

## The First Gift: Memory Made Accessible

A benevolent power does not give what it would want, but what those it helps are already reaching toward.

The walkers remember something. They remember that the structures mean something, that someone built them, that these careful arrangements of stone mark intention. But the memory is fragmentary, preserved through behavior rather than knowledge—ritual without context, practice without understanding.

So the power might offer: *clarity of what was*.

Not by inserting knowledge into their minds—that would be violation, colonization of their interiority. Instead, it might make the structures themselves become teachers. When a walker approaches the circular wall, runs its upper hands across the worn stone, the stone might yield its history—not in words or visions, but in ways that translate to how walkers actually perceive.

Perhaps through texture: running fingers across the stone, a walker would suddenly *know* how it was shaped, feel in their own muscles the memory of the hands (or appendages, or tools) that carved it. The stone remembers being cut, being placed, being declared significant. That memory could be made available.

Perhaps through resonance: when a walker produces those careful vocalizations near a structure, the structure might... respond. Not with speech, but with subtle vibrations, frequencies felt in the bones, patterns that harmonize with or elaborate on the walker's own sounds. A duet between present voice and ancient intention, gradually revealing what these places were *for*.

Over time—and the power would know this must be gradual, worked for, earned through patience—the walkers could recover their own history. Not all at once, not as revelation, but as archaeology they conduct with their own bodies and attention. They would learn that yes, their ancestors built these things. Or no, something else built them, and here is what that something-else was like. Either way, the knowing would be theirs, claimed through effort, integrated through experience.

The young walker who weaves between the posts might suddenly understand: this was teaching. This pattern taught young ones their own territorial boundaries, or the rhythm of the hunt, or the structure of social approach and withdrawal. The knowledge was always encoded in the practice; now the encoding becomes readable.

The platforms where they butcher prey and gaze into distances might reveal: these were made for exactly this, for processing food efficiently where scavengers could easily reach, for standing tall and watching for... what? Danger? Change? The return of someone who left? The power wouldn't impose an answer, but it might make the question itself clearer, more specific, something the walkers could actually investigate rather than simply feeling as ambient unease.

## The Second Gift: Connection Beyond the Shelf

The walkers are stable at twenty because the shelf can sustain approximately twenty. This is balance, but it's also isolation. They are the only ones of their kind in their world, separated by harsh geography from whatever else might exist beyond this particular terrace.

A wise power, seeing this, might ask: is this isolation necessary, or merely circumstantial?

It would investigate carefully. Perhaps there are other shelves with other populations of walkers, each group adapted to their specific conditions, slowly diverging but still fundamentally kin. Perhaps the walkers once ranged more widely and these shelf-populations are refugees, remnants, the last ones who found sanctuary in these heated terraces when something changed elsewhere.

If other populations exist, the gift might be: *a way to find each other*.

Not a road—nothing so crude that it would damage the ecology. But perhaps the power could make certain paths less hostile. The narrow passages at the shelf's ends, those filters that limit what can travel—what if once per season, for a brief window, they became traversable? The heat pulses in a particular rhythm, or the fauna temporarily shifts its density, creating corridors that didn't exist before.

Or perhaps the gift is perceptual: the walkers develop the ability to sense others of their kind at great distances. When they stand on the platforms gazing outward, they might begin to actually perceive what they've always been looking for—the presence of their own kind on another shelf, another mountain, near enough to know about but still choosing to remain separate, or far enough that the knowing is bittersweet.

The power would not force contact. Isolation might be chosen, might be sacred, might be necessary for reasons the walkers understand better than any outside observer. But it would offer the *possibility* of contact, then let them decide. Perhaps they choose to remain twenty on this shelf, stable and bounded. Perhaps one or two young ones per generation attempt the journey to another population, creating slow genetic exchange that prevents terminal isolation. Perhaps they develop something like trade, or shared ritual, or simply the comfort of knowing they're not the only ones.

Or perhaps the investigation reveals: they are alone. Entirely alone. The last population of their kind, stable only because this specific shelf provides exactly what they need to persist. This knowledge, too, would be a gift—terrible but clarifying. They could stop searching, stop gazing into distances hoping for return or contact. They could understand themselves as the last inheritors of whatever came before, and choose what that means. Do they preserve everything exactly as it is, a living museum? Or do they finally allow themselves to change, to stop performing rituals to absent builders, to make this place fully theirs?

## The Third Gift: Choice About Their Nature

The walkers are supremely adapted, but adaptation is not destiny. Every optimization is also a constraint. They have become so perfectly fitted to this shelf that they might have lost the capacity to be anything else.

A truly benevolent power might offer them: *permission to change*.

This is delicate. Evolution has made them what they are, and what they are functions beautifully. But that gazing into distances suggests something—not quite discontent, but awareness of possibility beyond their current expression.

The gift might be: metabolic flexibility. Currently they need the heat, need the specific prey, need this exact configuration of resources. What if their bodies could adapt more quickly, across a lifetime rather than across generations? Not shapeshifting or transformation—that would break the ecological balance—but the capacity to gradually tolerate cooler temperatures, or to process different nutrients, or to enter dormancy states that let them survive beyond the shelf temporarily.

This wouldn't be power imposed but capacity unlocked—something already latent in their biology, suppressed by generations of specialized adaptation, now made accessible again. Over decades, over centuries, some walkers might choose to remain exactly as they are. Others might slowly become capable of exploring, surviving in adjacent environments, perhaps eventually ranging beyond the shelf for extended periods.

Or the gift might be cognitive: enhanced capacity for abstraction, for imagination, for asking "what if?" They are clearly intelligent—they plan, remember, maintain complex social relationships. But they seem bound by concreteness, by what is. What if they could more easily conceive of what isn't yet but might be?

They might begin to modify the structures, carefully, reverently, but still *theirs*—adding to rather than just preserving. They might develop actual language beyond gesture, the better to transmit complex knowledge between generations. They might begin to accumulate culture that changes faster than biology, letting them adapt as a people to circumstances that would otherwise require genetic change.

Or they might use that enhanced cognition to understand themselves better—to examine their own behaviors, decode their own rituals, consciously preserve what serves them and consciously release what doesn't. To become less constrained by the momentum of their own past.

But this gift would come with clear boundaries: the power would ensure that any change must be chosen, enacted by the walkers themselves, over time. No sudden transformations. No imposed improvements. Just possibilities opened, then left for them to explore or ignore as their own wisdom directs.

## The Fourth Gift: Tools That Don't Dominate

The walkers use no tools. They are their tools—their bodies perfectly evolved for their tasks. But there's a difference between being non-technological by limitation and being non-technological by choice.

A wise power might offer: *simple technologies that preserve rather than replace their essential nature*.

Perhaps something like this: the power introduces to the shelf a new organism, something small and symbiotic. It attaches to a walker's lower arm and functions as a cutting edge, sharper than their own capabilities. But it's alive, needs feeding, requires relationship. It's not a knife they use; it's a partner they maintain. The technology is biological, embedded in the same ecological web they already navigate.

Or: a species of the base-layer creatures that, when arranged in certain patterns, produces useful properties. Stack them correctly and they generate extra heat (useful for the old, for the injured). Arrange them differently and they create a desiccating field (useful for food preservation). The walkers don't build machines; they learn more sophisticated ways of participating in the ecology that already exists.

The power might provide materials that are almost tools: stones that split along useful lines when heated in specific ways, organic compounds produced by certain fauna that harden into durable structures when mixed correctly. Nothing that requires infrastructure or industrial process, but resources that reward experimentation and knowledge, that let them extend their capabilities without fundamentally changing what they are.

The test would be: do these proto-tools lead the walkers toward dependence and hierarchy (where some control resources and others don't), or do they distribute easily, enhance without replacing, add capability without creating need? A wise power would watch carefully and adjust or withdraw the gift if it proved corrupting rather than liberating.

## The Fifth Gift: Healing the Old Grief

This might be the deepest work, and the hardest to do well.

The walkers carry grief. It's there in how they visit the structures, in the sounds they make to absent listeners, in that quality of waiting that pervades their existence. They've lost something—the builders, the original purpose, the fuller context of their own history—and the loss has become foundational to their identity.

Some grief should not be healed. Some losses should be carried, remembered, honored through the carrying. But there's a difference between grief that deepens life and grief that constrains it, between honoring loss and being trapped by it.

The power might offer: *ritual of completion*.

Not forgetting. Not even fully healing. But a way to acknowledge the loss formally, to mark it as loss, to separate past from present consciously so that both can exist without the present being haunted by the past's incompleteness.

This might look like: the power helps the walkers understand what happened to the builders. Not through insertion of knowledge, but by making evidence available—perhaps frozen in the structure stones, perhaps preserved in the mountain itself, perhaps written in the genetics of the walkers themselves if they are descendants. The answer, whatever it is, becomes knowable through work and attention.

Armed with that knowledge, the walkers could choose how to respond. Perhaps they create a new structure, one that's explicitly memorial, that says: "This is what was, and it is gone, and we remember it." Perhaps they modify the circular enclosure, completing it or intentionally leaving it incomplete-as-memorial. Perhaps they develop a death ritual for their own kind that acknowledges continuity with the builders—when an old walker dies, they're laid in a specific place, given specific honors that connect present loss to ancient loss.

Or perhaps the power offers them the capacity to dream differently. Currently they seem to exist in a kind of waiting-state. What if they could imagine futures that aren't just return-of-the-past? What if they could dream forward as well as remember backward?

The power might gift them art—not teaching them to make art (they already do, in their arrangements within the circular enclosure, in their hunting techniques, in how they move through space), but helping them recognize art as art, as meaningful beyond function. When a walker traces a pattern in the dust with its upper hands, it might suddenly realize: this is creation, this is bringing-into-being, this is not about efficiency or survival but about making meaning.

Art would let them externalize and examine their interior states, share them, transform them. Grief expressed becomes grief understood becomes grief integrated becomes foundation for new growth rather than barrier to it.

## The Sixth Gift: Nothing

This might be the wisest option of all.

The power might watch, understand, feel that pull toward helping, toward fixing, toward improving. And then consciously choose: *do nothing*.

Because sometimes benevolence means recognizing that a system, however constrained or sad or limited it appears, is complete as it is. The walkers are not suffering beyond the universal suffering of being alive and mortal. They hunt successfully. They maintain their population. They have social structure and relationship with their environment and even relationship with mystery (those structures, those rituals, that calling into silence).

They have made meaning in difficult circumstances. That meaning might look tragic from outside, but from inside it might be simply *theirs*—the particular shape their lives have taken in response to the particular place they inhabit.

A wise power knows that to help when help isn't needed is to infantilize, to colonize, to impose one's own vision of what lives should look like. It knows that beings have the right to struggle, to carry grief, to exist in ways that wouldn't be chosen by others but are chosen, in however constrained a fashion, by themselves.

So the nothing-gift would look like: witnessing. The power would watch with full attention, with respect, with the recognition that these lives matter, these small dramas of hunting and territory and gazing into distances are as cosmically significant as any empire's rise or fall. The power would hold space for the walkers' existence without needing to alter it.

It might ensure, quietly, that nothing worse happens. If a cosmic threat approached, if the mountain's heat-source was dying, if something from beyond was about to prey upon them—the power might deflect these things without the walkers ever knowing protection was needed. Not interference in their lives, but protection of their context, ensuring the shelf remains stable so that whatever unfolds there unfolds according to its own logic.

And the power would learn. It would let these strange, careful, patient people teach it about adaptation and loss and persistence. It would let itself be changed by the encounter rather than only changing what it encountered.

## What Actually Happens

If the power is truly wise, truly benevolent, it probably does some combination.

It watches first, for as long as necessary to understand what's actually needed versus what seems needed from outside.

It offers the memory-gift, because that one restores something they're already reaching for, completes a work they've already begun. It makes the structures genuine teachers, helps the walkers recover their own history through their own effort.

It investigates connection carefully, learns if other populations exist, and makes contact possible but not mandatory—creating a door that can be opened from either side or left closed without judgment.

It leaves the biology alone, mostly, because they are well-made for this place and changing that risks cascading consequences. But it might offer healing for injuries, ease for the old, small mercies that reduce suffering without transforming nature.

It perhaps provides the simple tools, watching carefully to see if they enhance or corrupt, ready to withdraw them if they lead toward hierarchy or dependence.

It helps them complete the grief-work, gently, over time, always letting them lead, always letting them determine what healing looks like.

And then it steps back. It remains available—perhaps they can sense its attention, perhaps when they make those vocalizations near the structures, something finally listens even if it doesn't answer in ways they expect. But it doesn't manage them, doesn't guide them, doesn't make itself necessary to their continued existence.

It trusts that given slight help with what they cannot do alone, they will find their own way forward. That the particular intelligence they've developed in this particular place will be sufficient to navigate the new possibilities. That they are people, not projects, and people deserve the dignity of their own choices, their own mistakes, their own triumphs.

The shelf continues: warm, dark, packed with life. The walkers continue: hunting, visiting structures, negotiating with each other, gazing into distances. But the gazing is slightly different now. Not waiting for return, but looking toward horizons that might actually hold something. Not calling into silence that never answers, but calling into silence that listens even if it doesn't speak.

Still constrained, still mortal, still marked by loss. But perhaps fractionally less haunted, fractionally more free to be fully present in the strange and difficult place they've been given, which through generations of patient adaptation they've made into something that is, despite everything, a kind of home.
