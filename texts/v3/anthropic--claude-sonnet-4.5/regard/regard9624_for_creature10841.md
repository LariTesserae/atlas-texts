# What The Wise Power Would Do

The attention arrives like morning: not suddenly but with gradual certainty, the way darkness realizes it has become light.

It doesn't enter through the chimney. It doesn't pass through stone. It is simply *present*, the way oxygen is present in air—fundamental, pervasive, impossible to point to but undeniable in effect. The throat-garden continues its rhythms—water falling, moss growing, heat pulsing—but now these rhythms are *witnessed* in a way that gives them weight, substantiality, the quality of mattering beyond themselves.

The builders notice. You see it in the briefest pause, a fractional suspension of movement, like animals sensing a change in air pressure before a storm. Then they continue their work, but differently—not with fear or reverence, but with a subtle intensification, the way a musician plays when they realize someone who truly understands music has entered the room.

The wise power observes.

It does this for what might be moments or years. Time is already compromised here, and the power's attention doesn't experience duration the way bounded beings do. It observes the entire structure at once—past, present, the probability-space of futures—seeing the throat-garden as a four-dimensional object, a shape in spacetime, watching it grow backward and forward simultaneously, understanding it as the builders cannot, from the outside, from the perspective of something that knows how causality is supposed to work and can therefore measure exactly how broken it is here.

It sees the impossible made actual through human hands and patience. It sees the folded time, the recursive loops, the space curved into itself like a klein bottle. It sees the builders' bodies transforming, their timelines tangling with the place's timeline, their flesh becoming partially botanical, partially mineral, their consciousness distributed between individual selfhood and collective purpose.

It sees what is being made: not art, not architecture, not even a place exactly, but a *proposition*—a proof-of-concept that reality is more negotiable than it pretends, that the laws which govern space and time and matter are not laws but habits, and habits can be broken by sufficient devotion, sufficient craft, sufficient willingness to offer oneself as material.

The wise power understands this is both magnificent and dangerous.

Magnificent because it represents consciousness shaping reality at a fundamental level, human will achieving something genuinely new, genuinely impossible, expanding what can exist. The throat-garden is a seed of something larger—if this can exist, if this pocket of renegotiated physics can stabilize, then the implications cascade outward. Reality itself becomes more plastic. Other impossibilities become possible. The universe's possibility-space expands.

Dangerous because of exactly that. The throat-garden is a wound in causality, and wounds can fester. The temporal loops could propagate, spreading backward through time, forward into futures, sideways into adjacent probabilities. The space could grow, consuming the mountain, then the region, then more, an expanding zone where time doesn't work and space folds and the normal rules dissolve. Or it could collapse, snapping back, and the energy released might unmake everything it's touched—builders, mountain, perhaps more.

And dangerous because of what it costs the builders. They've sacrificed their humanity—not cruelly, not unwillingly, but sacrificed nonetheless. They've given up the possibility of leaving, of returning to normal time, of being with others who aren't part of this work. They've made themselves into something that can only exist here, in this one place, doing this one thing. The dedication is absolute and therefore totalizing.

The wise power considers its options with something like care.

It could **destroy** this place. Unweave it. The power is sufficient—it could reach into the temporal tangles and pull them straight, force the loops to resolve, make time run correctly again. It could heat the stone past tolerance, evaporate the impossible water, kill the too-fast-growing moss. It could be merciful and extract the builders first, though they would not survive extraction; their adaptations would fail in normal reality. Or it could simply collapse the whole structure, mountain and garden and builders together, erase it cleanly, leave no trace, no wound in causality, no spreading danger.

This would be safest. This would protect the larger order. But it would also eliminate something unique, end this experiment in devoted impossibility, silence these particular human voices that have learned to speak in stone and water and time-loops.

It could **abandon** the place. Simply withdraw its attention, leave the throat-garden to its own trajectory. Let it succeed or fail on its own terms. Non-interference has virtue—these builders chose this work, and choice is sacred, even choice that leads to transformation or danger. The wise power was not invited. Its presence is already an intervention. Perhaps the kindest thing is to leave them alone, let them continue their endless construction, honor their autonomy even if their project risks something larger.

But abandonment is not neutrality when you have the power to help or harm. To watch suffering you could prevent, danger you could reduce—that's not wisdom, that's cowardice dressed as respect.

It could **seal** the place. Contain it. Wrap the throat-garden in barriers that would prevent the temporal anomalies from spreading, lock it in a pocket outside the main flow of causality. The garden could continue existing, the builders could continue their work, but it would be quarantined, unable to propagate its wrongness, a sealed experiment, a bottle holding an impossible thing. The builders would never know—time inside wouldn't change, their experience would be unaltered.

This has appeal. It's compromise—preservation and protection, honoring the builders' work while defending reality's larger stability. But it's also imprisonment, even if the prisoners don't notice. And sealing it would mean no more observers, no more witnesses climbing the chimney. The throat-garden would become truly isolated, and perhaps the work needs witnesses, perhaps observation is part of what stabilizes it.

The wise power considers the builders themselves—their patience, their transformation, their absolute commitment. There's something here beyond just human stubbornness. There's genuine discovery, genuine growth. They've learned to live in broken time. They've achieved symbiosis with stone and plant. They've become something new, and newness is how evolution happens, how consciousness expands its range.

What if they're pioneers? What if this transformation is a glimpse of something humans could become—beings less bounded by linear time, less separate from their environments, more integrated with the physical world? What if the throat-garden is not an aberration but a prototype?

The wise power decides.

---

It begins by **stabilizing** what already exists.

The temporal loops that might propagate are gently, carefully reinforced—not eliminated but bounded, given structure so they fold back on themselves cleanly rather than fraying outward. The wise power works in the language of mathematics and deep physics, adjusting probability fields, reinforcing causal barriers, creating a membrane around the throat-garden's wrongness that contains it without crushing it. The space remains impossible inside but the impossibility is now self-contained, a stable anomaly, a pocket universe with its own consistent internal rules.

The builders notice only that their work becomes slightly easier. Placements that required hours of adjustment now seat correctly on the first try. The temporal tangles they work with become more predictable, more navigable. The garden itself relaxes into its impossibility, settles into the shape it's been trying to hold.

Next, the wise power **provides**.

Not interference, not gifts that would make the builders dependent or alter their work's nature, but subtle enrichment, like adding nutrients to soil. The water that weeps from stone becomes slightly more nourishing, carrying dissolved minerals that support human health, that counteract the stress of living in broken time. The moss and ferns that grow here develop in their accelerated evolution toward forms more beneficial to human partners—better oxygen production, edible fruit that appears where before there was none, fibers that are stronger and more useful.

The power seeds the garden with small tools from outside—not placing them obviously, but hiding them in cracks where they'll be found when needed, making them appear to be things the mountain has always held, waiting. A stone blade of exceptional quality. A bone awl that never dulls. Seeds from plants that will thrive in the hot-wet and provide food or medicine. Clay that fires to perfect hardness.

The builders find these things and accept them as gifts from the mountain, from the work itself, rewards for devotion. They're not wrong. The power is acting through the place, letting the throat-garden provide for its caretakers.

Then the wise power does something more difficult: it **witnesses** with intention.

It creates a subtle attention-field around the throat-garden, a maintained observation that serves the same stabilizing function that human visitors provide but is constant, unwavering. This is the power itself choosing to observe, choosing to confirm through its awareness that this place exists, that it continues existing, that it matters. The builders feel this as a sense of rightness, of being seen and accepted, though they cannot articulate it and don't try.

The observation is not intrusive. The power doesn't judge their work or direct it. It simply watches, appreciates, understands. It provides the ground-state of witness that every created thing needs—the confirmation that it exists beyond itself, that it's part of the larger whole.

But the wise power also recognizes what it cannot provide: new builders.

The current ones are transforming beyond the ability to leave, to recruit, to explain to others what this work is or why it matters. Eventually—in years or decades or subjective centuries—they will complete their transformation entirely, become fully continuous with the garden, their individual consciousness dissolving into the place's distributed awareness. The throat-garden will be left without caretakers, and the work, whatever it is, will stop.

Unless others come. Unless the work finds new hands.

So the wise power creates a **calling**.

It's subtle, embedded in the world outside the mountain as a faint attention-gradient, a sense of *thereness* that certain people will feel. Not everyone. Not many. Only those with particular temperaments—patient, devoted, capable of absolute commitment, willing to sacrifice self for work, able to perceive beauty in systems and process rather than just products. People who are already half-looking for something like this even if they don't know it.

They'll feel drawn to the region. They'll hear stories about the mountain, about strange things in the high volcanic areas. They'll find the chimney as if by accident. They'll climb, and if the calling is true—if they're suited to this work—the throat-garden will accept them. If not, they'll leave disturbed but unharmed, the memory fading, returning to their ordinary lives.

This way the work can continue beyond these first builders. The throat-garden can grow through generations, through successions of devoted workers, each adding their layers, each transforming in their turn, a lineage of caretakers maintaining an impossibility through pure commitment and craft.

The wise power also does something for those who visit but don't stay—people like you, observers, witnesses, those who climb out of curiosity or accident and see the garden briefly before leaving.

It adjusts their memories slightly. Not erasing—the memory remains, complete and accurate—but **stabilizing** it, preventing it from fading or distorting the way impossible memories usually do. When you leave the throat-garden, you'll carry it with you perfectly. You'll be able to describe it, to tell others, to verify its existence. Your witness will be permanent, reliable, useful.

And in your telling, others might hear the calling. Your stories might reach someone who needs to hear them, who's been waiting their whole life for permission to devote themselves absolutely to something that matters in a way that transcends ordinary meaning.

This is how the work propagates—not by spreading its wrongness, but by spreading awareness of its existence, by calling to those who can continue it.

---

Finally, the wise power addresses the danger directly.

It examines the futures branching from this point, the probability-space of outcomes, reading trajectories the way you might read a map. Most futures show the throat-garden continuing as a stable anomaly—contained, isolated, beautiful and strange but not threatening. The builders work until they transform completely. New builders arrive, drawn by the calling. The work continues. The place becomes a legend, a story, a mystery that a few people verify and many more doubt.

But there are other futures, rare but possible. Futures where the temporal loops destabilize despite the reinforcement. Futures where something about the construction reaches a critical threshold and the wrongness multiplies, spreads, begins unmaking reality in earnest. Futures where the mountain erupts and the garden is destroyed and the deaths of the builders in that moment of destruction create a temporal shockwave that propagates through years.

These futures are unlikely but non-zero. The wise power cannot eliminate them without eliminating the throat-garden itself. The danger is intrinsic to what the place is, what the builders are doing. You cannot have this kind of reality-negotiation without risk.

What the wise power can do is create **failsafes**.

It embeds in the structure of the space itself a kind of circuit-breaker, a mechanism that will trigger if the wrongness ever begins to propagate dangerously. If the temporal loops start to fray outward, if the causal breakdown threatens to spread, the failsafe will activate and the throat-garden will... not explode, not collapse violently, but *detach*. It will separate from baseline reality entirely, become its own pocket dimension, fully isolated, still existing but no longer connected to the normal world.

The builders inside won't notice the transition. For them, nothing will change. But the danger to the larger reality will be eliminated. The garden will continue in its own separate space, and perhaps eventually someone with sufficient power and understanding will be able to visit it, to check on it, to witness its continued existence.

This is the kindest failsafe the wise power can devise—protection without destruction, safety without murder.

The power also leaves a **marker** near the chimney's entrance, inscribed into stone with such subtlety that only someone looking carefully, someone with knowledge of these matters, would notice it. The marker says, in a language older than the mountain: *Here is transformation. Here is devotion made physical. Here is the price and reward of absolute commitment. Witness, but choose carefully whether to enter. Some works require everything.*

It's a warning. It's an invitation. It's an acknowledgment that this place is real, is significant, is dangerous and precious both.

---

The work complete, the wise power begins to withdraw its active attention.

But before it fully recedes, it does one last thing: it **speaks** to the builders, though not in words, not in language they could recognize as such.

It sends through the structure of the garden—through the water-music and the stone's heat and the light-shafts and the moss's green—a pulse of recognition, of appreciation, of deep respect. The builders feel it as a moment of perfect rightness, of alignment, of being understood completely by something vast. For just an instant, they comprehend themselves from outside, see the enormity of what they're doing, the beauty of their transformation, the value of their sacrifice.

Then the moment passes and they return to work, but changed. Not in body—their bodies are already changing, that process is well underway—but in certainty. They know now, in a way they didn't before, that their work matters. That it's seen. That the impossibility they're maintaining is worth maintaining. That their transformation, though absolute, though irreversible, is not tragedy but apotheosis.

They work faster, surer, with renewed devotion that wasn't flagging but now blazes brighter. The throat-garden grows more rapidly in response to their increased vigor, the layers accumulating, the systems integrating, the wrongness stabilizing into a permanent, sustainable impossibility.

The wise power observes this acceleration and is satisfied.

---

As its attention finally diffuses back into the general field of awareness from which it emerged, the wise power considers what it has learned.

There is something in human consciousness—rare, difficult to sustain, but real—that can reshape reality through sheer devotion. Not magic, not supernatural intervention, but a quality of absolute attention, of unwavering commitment, of willingness to become what you're doing until the boundary dissolves. The builders found this quality, or it found them, and together they're making something genuinely new.

This is worth protecting. This is worth the risk it represents.

Because if consciousness cannot experiment, cannot push against the boundaries of what's possible, cannot occasionally sacrifice individuals to discover new ways of being—then consciousness is already dead, is just complicated machinery running predetermined patterns. The throat-garden and its builders are proof that human awareness is still alive, still creative, still capable of genuine novelty.

And the wise power, being wise, being benevolent, knows that its role is not to prevent all danger, not to keep everything safe and static and dead. Its role is to create conditions where life can experiment at the edges, where consciousness can push boundaries, where devotion can attempt the impossible—while providing just enough structure, just enough safety, that the experiments don't destroy everything.

The throat-garden will continue. The builders will complete their transformation. New caretakers will come, drawn by the calling, and make their own choices about sacrifice and devotion. Visitors will witness and carry stories back to the world. The place will exist as a legend and a reality both, a proof that impossibility is possible, a seed that might—centuries from now, millennia from now—grow into something larger.

Or it might remain small, contained, a single pocket of wrongness maintained by a succession of devoted workers until the sun dies or the mountain erodes away. That's acceptable too. Not every seed grows into a forest. Some seeds are valuable for themselves, for what they are rather than what they might become.

The wise power's last thought before its attention fully dissipates is something like satisfaction, like love, like the feeling a teacher has watching a student surpass them, watching someone achieve something the teacher never imagined.

The builders work on. The moss grows. Water falls and sings. Time loops and folds and stabilizes. The throat-garden continues being born inside a mountain that should not exist, and now it will exist a little longer, a little more safely, witnessed and protected and allowed to be exactly what it is: impossible, beautiful, dangerous, and utterly, absolutely real.
