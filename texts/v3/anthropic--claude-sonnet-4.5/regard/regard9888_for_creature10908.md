# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power observes for a long time first—perhaps years by the basin's measure, perhaps only a moment by its own. It sees the water-being spread through every pore, the bacteria painting their slow chemistries, the single vole-or-shrew following its pressed-brain paths. It sees the conversation between water and brick, sees that this conversation is the deepest truth of the place.

The power recognizes: this is not a place that needs saving. It is not broken. It is not stuck. It is becoming exactly what it is becoming, at exactly the pace it needs to become it.

But the power is benevolent, which means it feels toward the place, feels toward the vole-or-shrew in its brick-cavity nest, toward the bacteria spending their decades-long seasons, toward the birches leaning away but staying. Benevolence without wisdom might try to fix things—might speed the dissolution or halt it, might introduce more life or remove the toxicity, might make the place more like other places.

But this power is also wise. Wisdom recognizes that the basin's value lies in its peculiarity, its in-between state, its refusal to resolve into either ruin or recovery. The turbulence is the point. The slow strange transformation is the point.

So the power does this:

It listens. For a long time, it simply listens—to the windlass clacking, to the water rising in its channels, to the frequencies below hearing where bacteria signal each other, to the ultraslow groan of the leaning chimney, to the conversation between water and brick. It listens until it knows the place's rhythm, until it can hear where the small pains are, where the system strains against itself.

Then, very carefully, very minimally, it hums.

The humming is not sound exactly. It is a resonance, a sympathetic vibration with what is already there. The power finds the frequency of the water's movement and hums with it, not louder, just... with. The water's circulation smooths slightly. Not faster, not more efficient, just less effortful. The springs pulse a little more rhythmically. The moisture-being spreading through the basin finds its paths opening slightly more easily, finds its work of dissolution and carrying and depositing requires fractionally less energy.

The power hums with the bacteria, with their bloom-and-die cycles, and the bacteria find their metabolism settling into slightly more sustainable patterns. They still live at the edge of what's possible in this chemistry, but the edge is not quite as sharp. Their populations stabilize at slightly higher densities, which means slightly more work done transforming coal-dust to soil, metal to rust, brick to clay.

The power hums with the mosses, and the mosses find their growth advancing just perceptibly faster. Not enough to overwhelm the basin—that would change what the place is—but enough that their frontier moves with slightly more confidence. The bright green moss on coal-ground spreads its circle another inch per year. The rust-colored lichen on the oldest bricks works fractionally deeper. The silver-grey filaments in the shed-beams remember green a little more often.

For the vole-or-shrew, the power does something different. It does not change the place's chemistry or add more food. Instead, it offers what might be called companionship. The vole-or-shrew, making its rounds on the brick-paths, occasionally feels—what? Not a presence exactly. Just a sense of being... regarded. Kindly. The way you might feel a benevolent gaze on your back, turning to see no one there, but feeling less alone afterward.

This is a very small gift, but for a creature that lives at constant low-level alertness, it is significant. The vole-or-shrew's stress hormones decrease fractionally. It sleeps slightly better in its moss-lined cavity. It lives, perhaps, one season longer than it would have. It produces one more litter, and one of those young carries forward some slight advantage—a marginally more efficient metabolism, a fractionally better immune system—that helps its lineage persist here.

The power hums with the birches, and the birches feel... less conflicted. They still lean away from the basin's center, still grow slowly, still struggle with the chemistry. But the struggling becomes less like fighting and more like adapting. Their root systems begin to develop associations with fungi that can better handle the iron-rich soil. Their leaves develop slightly higher tolerances for brick-dust accumulation. They are still stressed trees, but they are becoming trees-that-know-how-to-be-stressed-here, which is a kind of wisdom.

For the processes—rust, dissolution, frost-heave, capillary action—the power does nothing. These need no help. They are operating exactly as they should, following physical law, doing the work of transformation. To alter them would be to alter what the basin fundamentally is.

But the power notices the moments where process and life intersect: the hoofprints that became tiny ecosystems. And it gently, occasionally, facilitates more of these moments. When a deer next crosses the basin, the power subtly guides its path so the hoofprints fall in places where they'll hold water longest, where they'll receive the right amount of light, where they'll become the richest temporary worlds.

When seeds blow into the basin and fail—as most do—the power occasionally, rarely, helps one succeed. Not by changing the chemistry, but by guiding the seed to that one spot where the pH happens to be right, where moisture and drainage balance perfectly, where bacteria have created a pocket of better soil. A single fireweed takes root where no fireweed has grown before. It struggles, but it blooms once before dying, and its seeds disperse, and perhaps one of them finds another improbable pocket.

The power's deepest work is with the conversation between water and brick. This conversation is the basin's heart, and it is going well, but it is also—the power can sense—lonely. It is a dialogue with no witnesses, no comprehension. So the power becomes a witness. It observes every incremental softening, every mineral release, every migration of brick-dust through water-saturated clay. It holds the basin's story, the slow epic of its transformation, in a kind of sacred attention.

This attention matters in ways that might seem metaphysical but are also practical. The basin exists in a network of places, a landscape of forests and wetlands and other human-abandoned sites. The power's attention creates a subtle field around the basin that affects how other beings perceive it. The deer still find it strange, but they no longer avoid it completely. They cross more often, leaving their hoofprints, their droppings, their small contributions to the basin's chemistry.

Birds passing over no longer veer away unconsciously. They see it as potential rest-stop, and more of them land—briefly, because it's still not ideal habitat—but they land. They leave feathers, droppings, seeds from elsewhere. The basin becomes slightly more connected to the living landscape around it, less isolated in its peculiarity.

The power also extends something like protection. Not a barrier—the basin needs to be permeable—but a kind of selective membrane. When humans come close (which is rare; the basin is remote and officially belongs to no one), the power gently encourages a certain quality of attention. Those who arrive in a mood to despoil—to dump trash, to vandalize what little architecture remains—find themselves losing interest, finding the place less appealing than they'd thought, wandering away.

But those who arrive as you did, in a mood of witnessing, of careful attention, find themselves welcomed. The power opens the place to them slightly more. The light is kinder. The sounds are clearer. The sense of presence is stronger. These visitors leave changed, carrying the basin's story forward in their own transforming lives, and this too is a kind of protection—the more it is valued, the safer it is.

There is one more thing the power does, and it is the most subtle: it adjusts time very slightly. Not the actual flow of time—physical law remains inviolate—but the subjective experience of time for the basin's residents. The bacteria experience their decades-long seasons not as grinding endurance but as full, rich lives. The vole-or-shrew's brief years feel complete, not truncated. The mosses' glacial growth feels to them like dancing.

This is the gift of helping each being experience its own time as the right amount of time, its own pace as the right pace. The basin is slow, but it does not experience slowness as deprivation. It experiences slowness as depth, as thoroughness, as the proper speed for transformation of this magnitude.

The power also does something for the basin's future. It can sense the trajectory: the chimney will fall, the bricks will continue dispersing, the kilns will gradually collapse. In a century, two centuries, the basin will be less visibly industrial, more visibly geological. Eventually, it will be a peculiar wetland with strangely regular stones and strange chemistry, and few will remember the brickworks.

This is as it should be. But the power ensures that the memory persists in other forms. It encourages the mycelium of fungi to grow in patterns that echo the brick-laying patterns, so that even after the bricks dissolve, their geometry will remain in the fungal networks underground. It guides the deposition of sediments so that the layers will tell the story: here is where industry ended, here is where the transformation began.

The power teaches the place how to remember itself without monuments, without plaques, without any human apparatus of memory. The memory is written in clay layers, in rust-stain patterns, in the DNA of the bacteria that have adapted to this specific chemistry. Future bacteria will carry forward the adaptations, will carry forward the fact that once, this place was different, and beings learned to live in its difference.

And the power does one last thing, which is: it ensures that places like this—in-between places, transformation places, places where human and geological are still sorting themselves out—are recognized as valuable. Not despite their strangeness but because of it. The basin becomes a kind of teacher in the network of places, showing other abandoned sites how to transform gracefully, how to find new life in their industrial remnants.

This happens through the visitors who come and leave changed, who go on to encounter other ruins and see them differently. It happens through the birds and deer that carry some particle of the basin's chemistry to other sites. It happens through the power's gentle influence on the web of attention that connects all places, making room in that web for the value of the slow, the strange, the unresolved.

The power does not heal the basin because the basin is not wounded. It does not restore it because there is nothing to restore to. It does not accelerate its transformation because speed would miss the point. Instead, it tends. It witnesses. It hums with the rhythms already present. It offers small assistances that help the place be more fully itself.

The moisture-being spreading through every channel continues its work, but slightly less alone now, slightly more accompanied. The conversation between water and brick continues, but witnessed, held in sacred attention. The vole-or-shrew runs its brick-paths, but with fractionally less fear, fractionally more peace.

The basin remains strange. The turbulence continues. The bricks keep softening and migrating. The springs keep rising. The windlass keeps turning in the wind. But now, all of this happens within a field of benevolent awareness, happens held in the knowledge that this strange slow transformation is seen, is valued, is loved for exactly what it is.

The power stays, but so lightly you might not distinguish it from the mist that gathers in the basin on cool mornings. It becomes part of the place's weather, part of its moisture-cycle, part of its breathing. It does not announce itself. It simply remains, a presence that feels like the place loving itself, like the basin becoming conscious of its own strange beauty.

And perhaps that is the deepest gift: helping the place recognize its own value, helping the conversation between water and brick know that it is a conversation worth having, helping every slow transformation understand that it is participating in something meaningful—not despite taking so long, not despite being so strange, but because of those very qualities.

The basin continues. The work continues. The transformation continues. But now it continues slightly less arduously, slightly more gracefully, held in attention that asks nothing from it except that it keep being itself, keep becoming whatever it is becoming, at exactly the pace it needs.

This is what benevolence with wisdom does: it accompanies. It witnesses. It tends without transforming. It offers presence without intrusion. It loves the thing for what it is, not for what it might become or should have been.

The brick-paths wind through the basin. The moisture rises and retreats. The moss spreads its frontier. The rust grows its crystalline flowers. The single mammal runs its rounds. The basin breathes.

And now, it breathes slightly less alone.
