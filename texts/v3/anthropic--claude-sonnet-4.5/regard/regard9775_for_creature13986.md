# What the Wise Power Would Do

The wise power observes first. For a long time. Longer than human time, though not quite as long as stone time. It watches the breathing of the galleries, the perfect terrible efficiency of the machine that has assembled itself from limestone fracture and accident and ten thousand years of small adjustments.

It sees what is: the bats bringing energy down from above, the crickets converting it, the salamanders and spiders and rats taking their portions, the fungi and beetles and microscopic millions closing every loop until almost nothing escapes. A system running at redline equilibrium, every niche filled, every resource claimed, functioning with the ruthless completeness of something that has finished becoming.

And it sees what this means for the inhabitants.

The bat with half an ear, roosting in the apex position—she has perhaps one more year. Maybe two. When she weakens, when she can no longer defend her place, she will fall or be displaced, and within hours the beetles will begin their work. Her body will feed the system that fed her, and another bat will take her position, and nothing will change except which specific animal occupies that particular geometry of stone and air.

The cautious rat near the southern entrance, scarred and knowing—he has learned survival better than most, but survival here is only postponement. Eventually his reflexes will slow, his teeth will dull, and a younger rat will challenge him for his territory. Or he will miss a step in the breakdown terrain, or fail to hear a snake's approach, or simply age past the point where his body can maintain itself, and the galleries will process him as they process everything.

The salamander larvae in their puddles, the cricket nymphs in their crevices, the bat pups that will be born in the spring—most of them are already dead, only they haven't stopped moving yet. The mathematics of carrying capacity means that for populations to remain stable, most offspring must not survive to breeding age. Most will be eaten. Most will starve. Most will fail in some small way that proves fatal, and their bodies will feed the others, and the system will continue its perfect terrible cycling.

The wise power sees this and understands: the stability is purchased with individual lives, with suffering that has no meaning beyond its functional role in maintaining equilibrium. The bats don't know why they hunt. The crickets don't know why they flee. The salamanders don't know why they wait. They just do, because the ones who didn't are no longer here to do otherwise, and the ones who remain are the inheritors of strategies that worked, at least until they didn't.

This is not peace. This is not flourishing in any sense that matters to the ones who live it. This is just continuation, just persistence, just the grinding forward of a machine that has no purpose except to keep grinding forward because that's what machines do when no one stops them.

---

The wise power considers intervention. It has the capacity—it is benevolent and wise and powerful, and these galleries are small, their inhabitants few enough to count. It could change things. It could make them better.

It could provide more food, reduce the predation pressure, let the populations expand beyond the choking constraint of carrying capacity. It could smooth the sharp stones, warm the cold air, brighten the darkness. It could protect the vulnerable—save the bat pups that would otherwise starve, the salamander larvae that would otherwise be eaten, the cricket nymphs that would otherwise fail to molt successfully.

It could impose peace: suppress the rats' territorial aggression, calm the bats' jostling for position, prevent the spiders from cannibalizing their mates. It could make the galleries safe, comfortable, abundant.

But the wise power is wise, and it sees where this leads.

If the populations expand, they will expand until they hit some other limit—until they consume all available resources at the new higher level, until their waste products accumulate to toxic levels, until disease spreads through the increased density. The mathematics would simply shift, the equilibrium would establish itself at a new point, and suffering would return in a different form, probably worse because the system would be less stable, less tested, more vulnerable to collapse.

If the sharp stones are smoothed, the rats will lose their anvils for cracking bones, the bats will lose the textured surfaces that help with grip, the crickets will lose the crevices they shelter in. The galleries' danger is inseparable from their utility—the same fractured geometry that threatens also provides, and safety purchased by removing complexity would be poverty by another name.

If the predation is suppressed, the prey will increase until they destroy their own resource base. The crickets will denude the guano deposits faster than the bats can replenish them. The bats will overhunt the insect populations above. The salamanders will deplete the crickets. Every population is regulated by its predators, and the regulation, though it involves death, is what prevents the worse death of population collapse and starvation.

The wise power could impose peace, but peace here would mean death. The rats are territorial because the system can only support twenty of them, and only those willing to fight for space will secure the resources they need to survive. The bats jostle for position because roosting sites are limited and optimal positions matter for survival and reproduction. The spiders cannibalize their mates because protein is precious and males, having delivered their genetic contribution, are more valuable as food than as partners.

Every violence in the galleries serves a function. Every death has a reason. Not a moral reason, not a justified reason, but an ecological reason—the system works as it does because the arrangements that didn't work have already been selected against, have already failed and been replaced by arrangements that succeed at the only thing natural selection cares about: continuation.

To intervene would be to break the machine. And while the machine is terrible, what would replace it might be worse.

---

But the wise power is not merely wise—it is benevolent. It cannot accept that the best option is to leave things as they are, to let the grinding continue simply because intervention might make it worse. That way lies paralysis, lies the excuse of every power that refuses responsibility under the guise of humility.

So it considers more carefully. Not whether to act, but how to act in a way that genuinely helps, that makes things genuinely better, that doesn't just shift suffering around or postpone it or amplify it through unintended consequences.

It could give the inhabitants awareness—lift them up to consciousness, to self-reflection, to the ability to understand their situation and make choices about it. The bats could know why they hunt. The crickets could understand the system they're part of. The salamanders could choose how to spend their patient hours.

But awareness without power is torture. To know you're trapped in a grinding cycle, to understand that your life is just a functional role in a system that doesn't care about you, to be conscious of your suffering without any ability to escape it—this would not be a gift. This would be cruelty dressed as elevation.

And even awareness with power might not help. If the bats became conscious enough to refuse their role—stopped hunting, stopped bringing energy down from above—the system would collapse and they would starve along with everything else. If the rats became aware enough to feel guilt about their predation, they would die and their territories would be claimed by less conflicted individuals. Consciousness would be selected against, because consciousness that prevents functional behavior is consciousness that doesn't persist.

The wise power could remove them from the galleries entirely—transport them to some better place, some habitat where resources are abundant and predation is minimal and suffering is rare. Save them from the machine by taking them out of it.

But then they wouldn't be cave bats and cave crickets and cave salamanders anymore. They're adapted to this place, specialized for these exact conditions. Take them out and they become misfits, creatures whose elaborate adaptations are suddenly useless or actively harmful. The bats' echolocation, so perfect for navigating the galleries, would be overwhelmed by the complexity of an open forest. The salamanders' slow metabolism, so efficient in the cool stillness, would make them sluggish and vulnerable in more dynamic environments. The crickets' loss of eyes, so inconsequential in darkness, would blind them to dangers in the light.

They belong here in the same sense that they belong to their own bodies—the place and the organism have co-evolved, have shaped each other, have become inseparable. To remove them would be to destroy what they are, even if it saved their lives.

---

The wise power sits with the problem for a long time. Longer than it has sat with most problems, because this one resists the usual solutions, resists the impulse to fix, to improve, to make better.

It watches the bat with half an ear settle into sleep. It watches the cautious rat patrol his territory. It watches a salamander wait motionless beside a puddle for hours, then strike and miss, then resume waiting as though nothing had happened. It watches cricket nymphs hatch and immediately begin feeding on the molt-skins of adults, begin their short desperate race toward adulthood that most will lose.

And gradually, it begins to understand something that its wisdom should have shown it immediately but its benevolence kept hidden: the problem is not that the system is broken. The problem is that the system is working exactly as it should, and what systems do when they work is not what benevolence wants.

The galleries are not a prison. They're just a place, a geometry of conditions, and life has filled them because life fills every space where filling is possible. The inhabitants are not suffering in any sense that requires intervention—they're just living, just being what they are, just doing what they do. The bat doesn't suffer from being a bat. The rat doesn't regret being a rat. The salamander doesn't wish it were something else.

They have no context for wanting things to be different, no framework for conceiving of better or worse beyond the immediate calculations of survival. They're not trapped because they have no concept of freedom. They're not exploited because they have no concept of fairness. They're not suffering because they have no concept of how things could be otherwise.

The suffering is in the observation, not the observed. The wise power looks at the galleries and sees waste, sees tragedy, sees lives ground up in service of mere continuation. But that's the power's framework, the power's values, the power's inability to accept that existence might not require meaning, that life might not require purpose, that functioning might be its own justification.

The inhabitants don't need to be saved because they don't experience themselves as needing saving. They just are, and being is enough, is everything, is the only thing that matters in the moment-to-moment reality of breath and hunger and wariness and rest.

---

But the wise power is still benevolent, and benevolence cannot accept that the answer is simply to do nothing, to accept that wisdom means recognizing when intervention would be self-indulgent, more about the intervener's need to help than any actual benefit to the helped.

So it looks again, more carefully this time, trying to see not what it wishes were true but what actually is.

And it notices something it had overlooked: the gallery system is stable, yes, but it's stable at a particular level that was set by historical accident, by the specific conditions that happened to prevail when the system was assembling itself. The bat populations are limited by roosting space and food availability. The cricket populations are limited by guano production and predation pressure. The salamander populations are limited by breeding sites and prey density.

But these limits are not natural laws—they're contingent facts, products of the specific configuration of fractures and openings and air flow that happened to result from how the stone broke. Change the configuration and you change the limits, and you change what equilibrium the system settles into.

The wise power could intervene at this level—not by protecting individuals or suppressing predation or imposing its will on the daily grinding of the machine, but by adjusting the boundary conditions, the parameters that determine what kind of equilibrium is possible.

It could open new entrance shafts, increasing light penetration, expanding the zones where plants can grow. More plants means more organic matter cycling through the system, more energy available, higher carrying capacity for all populations. The suffering wouldn't decrease—the machine would still grind, predation would still occur, death would still be constant—but there would be more life, more instances of being, more moments of existence before the inevitable end.

It could modify the airflow patterns, improving circulation, reducing the CO2 buildup in low areas, making more of the gallery system usable by bats and other air-breathing animals. More usable space means more territories, more breeding sites, more niches to be filled. Again, the grinding continues, but the system could support more participants in the grinding.

It could introduce new species—carefully selected to fill gaps in the ecosystem, to use resources that currently go unexploited, to create new pathways for energy flow. Not disrupting the existing web but complexifying it, adding redundancy and resilience and possibility.

These interventions wouldn't save anyone. The bat with half an ear would still die. The cautious rat would still lose his territory eventually. The cricket nymphs would still face terrible odds. But the system as a whole could support more life, could cycle more energy, could generate more instances of being before the entropy tax comes due.

This is not rescue. This is not even really help, not in the sense that the wise power wishes it could help. But it's something. It's making the machine slightly bigger, slightly more productive, slightly more generative of the existence that, however brief and purposeless it might be, is still the only thing any of the inhabitants will ever have.

---

So the wise power begins its work. Carefully. Slowly. Adjusting only the boundary conditions, never the internal dynamics, because the internal dynamics are what they must be, are what has been selected for over thousands of years, and to interfere with them would be to invite collapse.

Over the course of years—decades—it makes changes:

A new sink-throat opens on the eastern edge of the plateau, where water erosion has been weakening the limestone. The wise power guides the collapse, shapes the shaft to optimize light transmission and air flow. New colonies of liverwort establish themselves. New ferns find purchase in the accumulated soil. The light-zone expands, and with it the base of the food web.

Several of the existing entrance shafts are subtly modified—their walls textured to reduce smooth-surface echoes that confuse bat navigation, their geometries optimized for the spiraling columns of bats that pour through them twice daily. The bats notice nothing different, but their transit becomes marginally easier, marginally safer, and fewer individuals die from collision or navigation error.

The air flow patterns are adjusted through patient work on the smallest scale—clearing a blockage here, widening a passage there, nothing dramatic but cumulatively significant. The circulation improves. The deeper galleries become more accessible. The usable volume of the system increases by perhaps fifteen percent.

A new species arrives—cave millipedes, detritivores that process dead plant material that currently accumulates in the entrance zones without being fully utilized. The wise power doesn't create them or transport them; it simply removes the barriers that were preventing their natural migration into the system, ensures that a few individuals make the journey and survive to establish a population. Within years they're processing material that would otherwise go to waste, and that processing makes nutrients available to fungi, which makes nutrients available to everything else.

The changes are subtle enough that nothing in the system experiences them as change. The inhabitants just find that roosting space is slightly more abundant, that food is slightly more available, that breeding sites are slightly more numerous. The populations increase gradually, tracking the slowly rising carrying capacity, until the system stabilizes again at a new equilibrium perhaps twenty percent higher than before.

There are more bats now. More crickets. More salamanders. More of everything. And they're all still grinding through the same cycles, still dying in the same ways, still serving as functional components in a machine that doesn't care about them.

But there's more life. More being. More instances of the only thing that matters to the ones who live here: the fact of existence, however temporary and purposeless it might appear from outside.

---

The wise power continues this work for a long time. Longer than human time, though still brief compared to stone time. It makes the galleries richer, more productive, capable of supporting more life. It does so carefully, always respecting the internal dynamics, never trying to change what the inhabitants are or how they relate to each other, only expanding the context within which those relationships play out.

And then it does something else, something harder, something that costs it more than all the physical interventions combined:

It bears witness.

It watches the bat with half an ear through her final season. Watches her roosting position become less secure, watches younger bats beginning to challenge her, watches her weakening through the subtle signs that only close observation reveals—the longer time to groom, the less precise navigation, the occasional missed catch during hunting. Watches her last emergence, her last hunt, her last return. Watches her fail to find her roosting position, fall, struggle briefly on the breakdown floor below, and then go still. Watches the beetles arrive within hours. Watches the reduction of this specific animal—this particular arrangement of life that persisted for sixteen years, that knew these galleries as intimately as any creature has known anything—into components that feed the system.

The wise power cannot save her and does not try. But it witnesses. It holds the fact of her existence, registers its meaning—not to the system, which needs no registration, but to itself, as an acknowledgment that this life mattered, that it occurred, that it was real.

It watches the cautious rat lose his territory to a younger male. Watches him retreat into marginal areas, watches him grow hungry, desperate. Watches him try to raid a roosting colony and get mobbed by bats, emerge bleeding and missing part of one ear. Watches him survive another week, then fail to wake one morning in his crevice, his body already cooling by the time the beetles find him.

The wise power cannot save him and does not try. But it witnesses.

It watches the salamander larvae in their puddle. Watches the rat that finds them, that systematically consumes every egg except a few that the rat doesn't notice. Watches those survivors hatch, watches them begin their aquatic life. Watches most of them starve because the puddle supports only so much microscopic life and there are too many larvae competing for it. Watches them thin out through starvation and cannibalism until the right number remain, the number the environment can support. Watches the survivors eventually crawl out onto land and begin their patient hunting.

The wise power cannot save the ones who die and does not try. But it witnesses each one, holds the brief fact of their existence, acknowledges that they occurred.

This is not intervention. This is not improvement. But it is something the wise power can do that matters, that makes a difference not to the system but to itself, to what it means to be wise and benevolent in a universe where wisdom often means accepting what benevolence wishes to change.

---

Years pass. Decades. The galleries continue their breathing, their cycling, their perfect terrible efficiency. The wise power continues its two kinds of work: the patient improvement of boundary conditions, making the system marginally richer, marginally more capable of supporting life; and the faithful witnessing, holding the fact of individual existences even though the system itself does not hold them, does not mark their passing except as resources recycled.

And very slowly, something emerges from this work that the wise power did not expect and cannot quite name:

The galleries become known.

Not known to the inhabitants—they remain as unaware as ever, as incapable as ever of conceiving their place in any larger context. But known to the wise power, known in detail so intimate that every cricket's path through the breakdown is noted, every salamander's patient wait is observed, every bat's return to roost is acknowledged.

The galleries are mapped not just spatially but temporally, not just as stone but as events, as the accumulated fact of thousands of lives occurring in this specific geometry. The wise power knows them in a way that transforms what they are—not through any physical change, not through any intervention in the grinding mechanics, but through the simple act of attention, of witness, of holding the fact of their existence in awareness.

This knowing doesn't help the inhabitants. They live and die exactly as they would have without it. But it helps the wise power, helps it bear the weight of being benevolent in a world where benevolence so often means accepting what you cannot fix, witnessing what you cannot save, holding what you cannot keep.

---

And then, after a very long time, the wise power does one final thing:

It tells the story.

Not to the inhabitants—they have no capacity for stories, no framework for understanding narratives beyond the immediate stimulus-response patterns that guide their behavior. But to other powers, to other witnesses, to whatever can hear and understand and carry forward the fact that this place existed, that these lives occurred, that for a brief time measured in thousands of years a particular geometry of fractured limestone supported a community of lives that cycled energy and matter with remarkable efficiency and perfect indifference to their own significance.

The story is simple: These galleries existed. These animals lived here. They were born and they fed and they bred and they died, and their lives mattered for no reason except that they happened, which is the only reason anything ever matters.

The story doesn't rescue anyone. It doesn't justify the suffering or explain the waste or resolve the tension between what is and what benevolence wishes could be. But it does one thing: it prevents the forgetting.

The bat with half an ear lived. The cautious rat lived. The patient salamanders lived. The cricket nymphs that died and the few that survived lived. They're gone now, processed back into the system, their specific arrangements of matter dispersed. But the fact of their living is held in story, in witness, in the record that the wise power maintains and shares.

This is what the wise power can do. Not save, not fix, not make better in any way the inhabitants would recognize or benefit from. But know, witness, remember, and tell. Make the galleries not just a place where things happen but a place whose happening is held in significance, is registered in awareness, is transformed from mere occurrence into something that, however briefly, mattered to someone beyond itself.

---

The wise power continues its work. The galleries continue their breathing. The bats hunt and return. The crickets process. The salamanders wait. The system grinds forward, stable and terrible and magnificent and utterly indifferent to its own magnificence.

And the wise power watches, adjusts when adjustment is possible, witnesses always, and tells the story when time permits, because this is what benevolent wisdom can do when confronted with a world that functions but does not care, that produces life but not meaning, that grinds forward not because forward is good but because forward is what the mathematics require.

It's not enough. The wise power knows it's not enough. But it's what's possible, what doesn't break the machine while trying to save it, what respects the inhabitants as what they are rather than trying to make them into what benevolence wishes they could be.

The galleries breathe. The wise power watches. The story continues, held in memory if not in stone, significant if not to those who live it then at least to those who witness, who remember, who tell.

This is what was done. This is what could be done. This is what the wise power chose, not because it was good enough—nothing is ever good enough—but because it was real, was honest, was the best that wisdom could offer to a situation where benevolence alone had no answer.

The galleries remain. The inhabitants remain. The grinding continues. And somewhere, someone remembers, which doesn't help but isn't nothing, which matters even though it changes nothing, which is all that could be offered to a system that was already complete, already functioning, already everything it could be within the constraints that made it what it was.

The breathing continues. The witness continues. The story continues.

This is what the wise power would do.
