# What the Wise Power Would Do

The being that notices—call it a power, call it an attention, call it whatever allows you to sleep afterward—has observed many such places. Cellars where gardens grow in salt. Mines where blind fish navigate by stars of bioluminescence. Spaces between spaces where the unwanted becomes the necessary.

This one, though. This drowned switchyard with its axolotls like scattered jewels, its cricket chorus measured in acres of silence between notes, its patient willow that should not be.

The power considers intervention. That's what benevolence does—it sees suffering, sees need, sees potential unfulfilled and reaches to adjust. The wisdom part, though. That's what stays the hand.

---

First, the power would watch. Not the cursory watching already done, but deep watching. Seasonal watching, generational watching. It would observe the axolotl that lost a gill-frond and grew it back, tracking the sixty-three days of regeneration, noting how during that time the creature shifted its hunting territory to shallower water where prey was easier despite being scarcer. It would watch the cricket population through a full year-cycle, noting the three weeks in early spring when mortality spikes as the last of the stored fat runs out and the first of the new fungi hasn't yet fruited.

It would pay attention to the rat visits—not random as they appear, but seasonal, tied to the breeding cycle two levels up where a rat warren has existed for forty years in the foundation of an abandoned building. The young rats, newly independent, explore downward until they find this space, investigate, decide it's not for them, return to report in whatever way rats report: *Strange down there. Water. Not our water.*

The power would notice the time-scales layering over each other. The bacteria dividing every twenty minutes, the moss growing by millimeters per year, the bracket fungi that might be a century old, maybe two. The willow that probably germinated during a flood event in 1987 or '88, when surface water breached the old seals and poured down, carrying seeds, silt, possibilities. It took root in the chaos and stayed through the settling.

It would observe the chemistry, the way nutrients cycle not in simple circles but in spirals, in patterns that look wasteful until you see how they buffer against catastrophe. The nitrogen that gets fixed by bacteria, consumed by algae, eaten by axolotls, excreted, processed by different bacteria, absorbed by the moss, which dies and feeds the fungi, which release it back to the bacteria. Six transformations to return to the start, and at each transformation, a little is stored, sequestered, saved against the year when the ventilation shaft clogs completely for three months and everything crashes but doesn't crash completely because there are reserves, buffers, the accumulated savings of inefficiency.

The power would see that this place, strange as it is, works. Not perfectly—nothing does. But it works. The equilibrium isn't fragile. It's robust, proven, tested by twenty years of operation (or thirty, or forty—the exact start-date is unclear, the water rose gradually). This ecosystem has survived rat predation, human intrusion, the year the water level dropped eighteen inches, the winter when something died upstream and a plume of ammonia reached this space and killed half the fish but the survivors rebounded. It has adapted, adjusted, persisted.

---

Second, the power would consider the costs of kindness.

What if it opened the ceiling, let in more light? The fern-things would thrive, spreading, and yes, that seems good. But more light means more photosynthesis means more oxygen means faster metabolisms means everything speeds up. The careful slow balance would break. The fish adapted to low-oxygen water would struggle. The axolotls, whose efficiency is calibrated to exactly this level of dimness and cold, would face predators that can see better, move faster. The crickets would lose their advantage of scattered darkness. The bats would adapt—bats always do—but their echolocation is optimized for this specific acoustic environment, and more plant growth means more sound absorption means they'd have to call louder, expend more energy.

The ecosystem would reorganize around the new light, probably, eventually, but reorganization has casualties. The white axolotl that holds the shallow-rail territory—it survives now because in dim light its albinism doesn't matter. Bright light makes it a target. It would die. Maybe its death would be worth the overall increase in productivity, but maybe worth is the wrong measure. It's alive now. It has a territory, a hunting pattern, a place. The power could end that, not through malice but through improvement.

What if it brought in new species to fill niches that seem empty? There are no large predators—nothing to control the axolotl population except resource limitation and occasional cannibalism. A snake could do it, a water snake, something efficient and elegant. But the axolotls have no anti-predator adaptations because they've never needed them here. A snake would devastate them, reduce them to remnant populations in the deepest corners, change the space from a place of drifting jeweled creatures to a place with a snake in it that occasionally eats. Trade abundance for efficiency, diversity for optimization. The mathematics favor it, maybe. The beauty doesn't.

What if it cleaned the water, filtered out the minerals and metals, brought it to neutral pH and perfect clarity? The fish that have adapted to navigate by chemical gradients would lose their map. The biofilms that process toxins would lose their function and decline, and the hundreds of species that live in and on those biofilms would decline with them. The water would be cleaner but emptier, pure but less alive.

Every intervention has consequences that ripple forward, and ripples are easy to start but impossible to fully predict. The power knows this. Wisdom is mostly scar tissue from previous benevolence.

---

Third, the power would look for the genuine problems, the places where suffering exceeds the necessary toll of living.

There's a junction box, old copper housing, that still has standing water inside from some leak decades ago. The water is stagnant, anoxic, toxic with dissolved metals. Mosquito larvae try to breed there and die. A frog egg mass was deposited there once—some frog made it down here somehow, laid eggs in the one place they couldn't survive. The eggs rotted. That's useless suffering. That water could be drained, the box sealed, and nothing would be lost except a death-trap.

The power would do this. Not dramatically—no need to announce its presence—but quietly. Perhaps the box would corrode through at the bottom, draining over a week. Perhaps a crack would develop. Small intervention, small mercy. The space doesn't need that particular death.

There's a section of ceiling where the concrete is delaminating, not critically but eventually. In fifteen years, maybe twenty, a chunk the size of a car will fall. It might hit nothing—the space is mostly empty. But it might crush part of the moss garden that took a century to establish, or it might land in the deepest sump and displace water in a wave that would flood the lowest cricket colonies and drown thousands of eggs. Not a catastrophe but a violence, a disruption larger than the space's normal rhythm.

The power would stabilize this. Not replace the ceiling—too much intervention, too visible—but subtly reinforce it, shift the stress lines, ensure the decay continues but the collapse doesn't. Let the concrete age gracefully instead of catastrophically. This changes nothing about how the space operates, just removes one roll of the dice that didn't need to be rolled.

There's a ventilation shaft that's 90% blocked—slowly closing, as they all are, but this one faster. In five years it will seal completely, and the light from this shaft feeds the willow. Not the only light, but a significant portion. The power would clear this, carefully, removing just enough debris to maintain the current light level for another thirty years. Not increasing it—that would trigger all those cascading changes—just maintaining what exists. The willow gets to keep struggling, but not against an accelerated timeline.

Small mercies. Tiny interventions. The kind that preserve the space's autonomy, its right to be what it has become, while removing the purely random harms that serve no purpose.

---

Fourth, the power would protect the space from what's coming, because something is always coming.

There's a development project being planned on the surface, four blocks away. It will break ground in three years. The construction will involve deep pilings, which will involve pumping down the water table, which will lower the level in the drowned switchyard by three feet. Not enough to drain it—the main sump will still hold water—but enough to expose half the current underwater territory, to strand the axolotls in a reduced range, to eliminate most of the fish habitat, to change everything.

The power could stop this—could influence the planning board, could cause the project to fail financing, could make the developer choose a different technique that doesn't require dewatering. But that's too much intervention in the human realm, too much weight put on the scale. The project might be needed—housing, perhaps, or infrastructure. The power doesn't know enough about human needs to override them.

Instead, it would prepare the space. It would subtly deepen the main sump over the next three years, erosion accelerated just slightly, enough that when the water level drops, the sump maintains sufficient depth. It would help the axolotls begin adapting now, unconsciously nudging them toward territories that will remain viable, letting natural selection work but with slightly adjusted parameters. When the crisis comes, it won't be survived by everyone—equilibrium changes always have casualties—but more will survive than would have, and the space will find its new balance faster, with less suffering in the transition.

There's a different threat, longer-term. Climate patterns are shifting. The rainfall that recharges the aquifer is becoming more sporadic—heavier events, longer dry periods. In fifty years, maybe a hundred, the water table might drop permanently. The drowned switchyard might become the damp switchyard, then the dry switchyard, then just another abandoned basement space where nothing particular lives.

The power can't stop climate change—too big, too entangled with everything else. But it could, perhaps, adjust the hydrogeology slightly. There's a layer of clay forty feet down that directs groundwater flow. If that clay cracked in just the right place, water from a more stable aquifer would begin mixing with the local supply. Not much, not enough to change the space now, but enough to buffer it against future scarcity. An insurance policy that won't pay out for decades but will pay out when needed.

---

Fifth, and finally, the power would consider whether this space needs to be known.

There's a researcher at the university, a biologist, who would find this place fascinating. She studies subterranean adaptation, and the axolotls here are a natural experiment in real-time evolution. If she knew about this space, she could document it, publish papers, bring attention. That attention could lead to protection—the space designated as a preserve, sealed off from development, monitored, maintained.

But attention is dangerous. People want to help. They would want to clean it up, make it safe, install proper lighting and walkways. They would want to remove the "invasive species" like the hermit crabs and the fish, restore it to some imagined natural state that never existed. They would want to put in viewing platforms, bring school groups, make it educational. The space would stop being itself and become a teaching tool, a preserved specimen, a thing under glass.

Or worse, the attention could be exploitative. Private interests could claim it, develop it into an attraction, charge admission to see the strange axolotls and the underground willow. The space would become a commodity, optimized for visitor experience. The equilibrium that took decades to establish would be shattered in a season.

The power could ensure the researcher never hears about it. One more forgotten administrative record, one more sealed door, one more place that falls out of memory. Let the space remain unknown, unprotected but also unbothered, free to continue its strange persistence in obscurity.

But there's a middle path. The power could ensure that the space is documented in such a way that if it's ever threatened with destruction—if that development project expands, if the building above is scheduled for demolition, if the city decides to fill in all the old infrastructure—someone will check the records, will discover that this place exists and is valuable. Protection on demand, invisibility until then.

The power would choose this. It would ensure the right documents exist in the right archives, would make sure that in the moment of crisis, if it comes, someone will know to look, will find, will care. The space gets to remain autonomous until autonomy becomes dangerous, and then help arrives, but not before.

---

And then the power would do the hardest thing: it would leave.

Not abandon—leaving is not absence. The power would maintain its awareness, its readiness to act if genuine crisis emerges, if suffering becomes meaningless instead of meaningful, if the equilibrium faces something it can't absorb. But it would not interfere with the daily operation, the seasonal cycles, the generational changes. It would not optimize or improve or enhance.

Because this space, in its cold and dim and strange persistence, has something that improvement might erase: it has found its own way. The axolotls didn't ask to be here, didn't choose these conditions, but they've made a life anyway. The crickets and the crabs and the moss and the willow—they're all improvising, adapting, surviving. Not thriving in the way surface creatures thrive, not abundant in the way healthy ecosystems are abundant, but alive, persistently alive, making do with what's available.

That's worth respecting. That's worth protecting, not by making it easier but by ensuring it can continue to be difficult in the ways it's already difficult, without adding new difficulties that serve no purpose.

The power would watch the white axolotl defend its territory, watch the ancient bat hunt with that perfect economy of motion, watch the willow wave its branches in the darkness. It would see the beauty in the adaptation, the elegance in the limitation. It would understand that this place doesn't need to be saved because it's already saving itself, moment by moment, cricket by cricket, drop by drop.

Benevolence without wisdom is just control. Wisdom without benevolence is just observation. Together, they know when to act and when to witness, when to protect and when to allow, when to intervene and when to simply ensure that intervention remains possible should it ever become truly necessary.

The drowned switchyard would continue, unknown and unbothered, its axolotls drifting through dark water, its equilibrium holding, its strange beauty persisting. And the power would continue its attention, ready but restrained, present but patient, caring enough to help and wise enough to mostly not.

That would be enough. That would be, perhaps, everything.
