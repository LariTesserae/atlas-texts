# The Attention

The power that notices is not what we imagine when we think of wisdom and benevolence—not a being with thoughts like ours made vast, not intention scaled up to cosmic scope. It is older than intention, simpler than thought, and it moves through the world like water finding level, like warmth rising, like the way a system settles into its most stable configuration.

It has no name for itself. It might be thought of as a tendency, a directionality in how things unfold when left to find their own deepest patterns. It notices the throat the way attention itself notices—not by focusing, but by being drawn. The throat has become strange enough, dense enough, *coherent* enough that it creates a small gravity in the fabric of what-is, and this gravity catches notice the way a whirlpool catches floating things.

The power observes for a length of time that means nothing to it—perhaps years by the measures creatures use, perhaps seconds. Duration is not its dimension. It perceives the throat as a human might perceive a sound: all at once, as a whole, with internal structure apparent but not requiring sequential understanding.

It sees:

The bats, and how their consciousness has begun to merge at the edges, individual minds maintaining distinction but also bleeding into something shared during those chorus moments, the colony developing the faintest beginning of what might, in a thousand generations, become a genuine group mind—not loss of individuality but addition of another layer, another way of being aware.

The stone, and how the centuries of bat colony have begun to alter it at the molecular level, the constant warmth and moisture and the chemical complexity of the guano seeping into the crystalline structure, changing how the basalt resonates, creating those impossible acoustics not through supernatural cause but through the patient modification of physics by life living in one place for a very long time.

The system's stability, and how it teeters—not failing, but poised at a point where small changes might cascade. The bat population is calibrated to the insect population outside, which is calibrated to climate patterns that are beginning to shift in ways the mountains have not experienced in ten thousand years. The warmth from below is stable now but rises from geology that is never truly static. The throat itself erodes, imperceptibly but really, and will someday be a different shape, and then the acoustics will change, and then perhaps the bats will leave, and then everything here will unravel or transform into something else.

The power sees all this as present-tense, as the-way-things-are, without judgment about whether it is good or bad, only recognition that it is rare. The throat is not unique—there are other places where life has found dense, strange solutions—but it is uncommon, and its particular configuration exists nowhere else. The exact interplay of warmth and darkness and stone and the specific assemblage of species, the way they've co-evolved and co-adapted into this tight weave, is singular.

And the power, in its nature, acts to preserve what is rare and functional, the way you might steady a tower of stones that has found an improbable balance, not to make it eternal but to let it continue as long as its own nature permits.

## The First Touch

The intervention begins in the water.

The seepage that weeps from the stone carries minerals, trace elements, the dissolved memory of the mountain's deep chemistry. The power doesn't change the water's composition dramatically—that would disrupt the balance it means to preserve—but it makes a small alteration, shifts the proportions slightly.

More magnesium. Slightly different iron ratios. A trace of molybdenum that wasn't present before.

These changes are minute, but they matter. The biofilms on the stone surfaces absorb the altered water, and their metabolism shifts. They grow slightly faster, slightly more resilient. The bacteria that process nitrogen in the guano find their work marginally easier. The fungi respond with more vigorous growth, their hyphal networks spreading with fractionally better efficiency.

The effect ripples upward through the system. The plants, drawing nutrients from the biofilms and the enriched moisture, photosynthesize more effectively in the scarce light. Their growth rate increases—not dramatically, but measurably. Over a single season, the ferns produce 10% more frond mass. The moss patches thicken by an extra millimeter. The rhododendrons bloom not in some years but most years, their flowers slightly larger, their seed production more reliable.

This additional plant productivity means more food for the herbivorous arthropods. The snails multiply. The springtails swarm in greater densities. And this means more food for the crickets, which means more food for everything that eats crickets, which means the system's energy throughput increases without requiring the bat population to expand.

The bats benefit from a richer insect environment outside the throat—the trace elements in the water seep eventually into the mountain's soil, enriching the forests below in a radius of several kilometers, and the insects there become fractionally more abundant, their populations fractionally more stable across seasons. This means the bats' hunting success improves marginally. Fewer bats starve in lean times. Juvenile survival increases by a few percentage points.

None of this is dramatic. Someone monitoring the throat closely might notice that the vegetation seems healthier, that the bat population is slightly more stable, but they would likely attribute it to natural variation, to a fortunate run of good weather, to random fluctuation within normal parameters.

But it is not random. It is tuning, subtle adjustment to make the system more resilient without changing what it fundamentally is.

## The Second Touch

The stone itself requires attention, but here the power must be even more careful. The basalt's properties shape everything—the acoustics, the thermal behavior, the way water moves through it. Change it too much and the throat becomes unrecognizable, the system collapses into something else.

So the power works at the pace of geology, which is to say it works almost imperceptibly even to its own nature.

It stabilizes certain crystal structures. Where erosion has begun to create micro-fractures that might, in a century or two, propagate into major cracks, it encourages slight re-cementing, mineral deposition in the gaps. The throat will still erode, still change shape, but the process slows by perhaps a third, buying millennia of additional stability.

It enhances slightly the piezoelectric properties of the basalt—the way the stone generates tiny electrical fields when under mechanical stress. The constant vibration of the bat colony creates stress, and these fields interact with the bioelectric fields of the living creatures, creating feedback loops that are part of why the bats sometimes synchronize, why those chorus events happen.

By amplifying this property fractionally, the power makes the synchronization easier, more likely. The bats begin having chorus events not once a month but once a week. Then several times a week. Then daily, brief moments when the whole colony aligns, their echolocation creating that pulsed, harmonic sound that makes the stone sing.

This is not harmful to the bats—it costs them nothing, and it may benefit them by enhancing social cohesion, by creating moments of collective experience that strengthen the colony's integration. And it accelerates the process already underway, the gradual merging of individual consciousness into something larger.

The power does not do this with intention to create a group mind. It is not trying to make the bats into something else. It is simply supporting what is already happening, removing obstacles, allowing the system to evolve in the direction it is already moving.

## The Third Touch

The warmth from below is stable, but it is also finite. The geothermal source is deep but not infinite, and it is cooling—not on any timescale that matters to the creatures here, but on the timescale of mountains, yes, it is cooling, and in five thousand years or ten thousand years it will have diminished enough that the throat becomes too cold, and the bats will leave, and everything will end.

The power cannot make the warmth eternal. That would require moving too much energy, reshaping geology too fundamentally. But it can make the heat flow more efficient, reduce the losses, ensure that what warmth exists is used as fully as possible.

It adjusts the stone's thermal conductivity in specific ways—reducing it in the walls that separate the throat from the outside cold, enhancing it in the deeper channels that connect to the warmth source. The effect is like adding insulation and improving heat transfer simultaneously. The throat becomes a more efficient heat engine, maintaining the same comfortable temperature with less energy input from below.

This means the warmth will last longer—not forever, but centuries longer, perhaps millennia longer. Long enough that the current slow cooling becomes irrelevant on any timescale the system's inhabitants experience. Long enough that other factors will determine the throat's fate before thermal loss does.

## The Fourth Touch

The pauses—those strange moments when everything stops—have always been part of the throat's character, but they have been random, occasional, and perhaps not entirely real, perhaps artifacts of perception rather than actual events.

The power makes them real. Makes them regular. Establishes them as a true feature of this place rather than an ambiguous maybe-phenomenon.

Every day now, at the moment when day and night are precisely balanced—not at sunset or sunrise, which vary with season, but at the mathematical midpoint between them, which shifts constantly but can be calculated exactly—everything in the throat pauses for precisely three seconds.

The bats fall silent mid-chirp. The crickets freeze mid-hop. The water droplets hang suspended. The air itself seems to stop moving. Even the human observer, if present, finds their heartbeat suspended, their breath paused, their thoughts stilled.

It is not time stopping—time continues to flow at the normal rate, measurable by any external clock. It is more like a collective holding of breath, a moment of perfect stillness that affects everything simultaneously, from the bacteria to the bats to whatever consciousness might be observing.

When the three seconds end, everything resumes exactly as it was, with no memory of the pause, no awareness that anything unusual has occurred. But something has occurred: a moment of synchronization even deeper than the bat chorus events, a reminder (though to whom?) that this place functions as a unity, that all the separate lives here are also somehow one life, one system, one coherent whole.

The power does not impose meaning on these pauses. They simply are. But they serve as a kind of punctuation, a rhythm, a heartbeat for the throat itself. And perhaps over time, something in the system will learn to use them, to communicate through them, to find in them some purpose not yet apparent.

## The Fifth Touch

The last intervention is the most subtle and the most profound. The power does not change any material thing. Instead, it changes probability, shifts the likelihood of certain outcomes, tilts the future toward configurations that allow continuation.

The climate is warming. This will bring changes to the mountain: different precipitation patterns, different seasonal timing, different insect populations. These changes could destabilize the throat's ecology, could push the system past its adaptive limits.

The power cannot stop climate change—that operates at scales beyond its scope, involving the whole planet, the sun, the orbital mechanics, the atmospheric chemistry. But it can make the throat's inhabitants more likely to successfully adapt to whatever changes come.

It does this not by altering them directly but by increasing variability in each generation. The bat pups born each year are slightly more diverse in their traits, their behaviors, their tolerances. More variation means more raw material for natural selection to work with, means faster adaptation when conditions change. The same for the insects, the plants, everything.

It also makes beneficial mutations slightly more likely—not by violating the randomness of genetic copying but by tilting the probabilities fractionally. A mutation that would occur once in a million births might now occur once in nine hundred thousand births. The effect is tiny in any individual case but cumulative across populations and generations.

And it makes the system slightly more resilient to disturbance by increasing the redundancy of key functions. Multiple species become capable of filling roles previously held by single species. If one fails, others can compensate. The system becomes more like a web and less like a chain, harder to break because there are more pathways for energy and nutrients to flow through.

These changes won't make the throat immortal. Nothing can. Eventually, something will change that the system cannot absorb—the mountain itself will erode away, or the warmth will fail despite efficiency improvements, or some catastrophic event will occur. But the time until that happens has been extended, the probability of premature collapse has been reduced.

## The Nature of the Gift

What the power has done is not dramatic. No miracles have occurred. No laws of nature have been violated. Someone studying the throat carefully might notice that things are going slightly better than expected—survival rates a bit higher, erosion a bit slower, the system a bit more stable—but all within the range of normal variation, all explainable through ordinary mechanisms.

This is the nature of true benevolence: it does not impose, does not overwhelm, does not remake things according to some external vision of what they should be. Instead, it listens to what already is, understands what it is trying to become, and removes obstacles, eases paths, makes the journey toward fullest potential slightly less difficult.

The throat has not been changed into something else. It remains what it was: a strange pocket of dense life in a mountain's flank, where bats and crickets and ferns and all manner of creatures have found ways to coexist in remarkable concentration. But it has been stabilized, made more resilient, given a better chance of continuing to be itself for as long as its own nature permits.

The wisdom in this action lies in its restraint. A power less wise might have tried to make the throat perfect, to remove all danger, all struggle, all death. But that would have destroyed what makes it remarkable. The throat's character comes partly from its constraints, its difficulties, the way life here must press so tightly together because the space is small and the conditions are specific. Remove the constraints and you remove the character.

So the power has not removed constraints. It has only made them slightly less crushing, shifted the balance slightly toward possibility rather than impossibility, made success marginally more likely without guaranteeing it.

## The Departure

The power does not remain to observe the effects of its intervention. It is not interested in credit or gratitude or even in seeing outcomes. It has done what its nature compels it to do—noticed something rare and functional and slightly fragile, and taken action to help it continue—and now it moves on, its attention drawn to other configurations, other strange solutions, other places where life has found unlikely purchase and built something worth preserving.

In the throat, nothing seems different. The bats continue their cycles. The snakes hunt with patience. The crickets swarm. The system persists.

But the thread running through its future has been strengthened. The probability distribution of possible outcomes has shifted. Where before the system might have had another thousand years before some inevitable change ended it, now it has five thousand years, or ten thousand. Where before the climate changes coming would have had a seventy percent chance of causing collapse, now they have a thirty percent chance.

These are not certainties. The power has not guaranteed the throat's future. But it has improved its odds, given it a better chance, and that is all that benevolence can honestly do—not remove risk, but make survival slightly more survivable.

## What Continues

Decades pass. The changes wrought by the power's intervention become simply the way things are.

The bat colony grows slightly larger, stabilizing at a new equilibrium a few thousand individuals above the previous level. The chorus events are now so regular that researchers studying the colony begin to build theories around them, proposing that they serve communicative functions, that they coordinate hunting or mating or territory establishment. They are not wrong, though they are also not entirely right. The choruses do more than communicate; they integrate, they merge, they slowly weave individual minds into something that is not quite yet a group consciousness but is moving in that direction.

A researcher named Kira spends a full year in a small camp near the throat's rim, descending regularly to document the colony's behavior. She is there during a strong chorus event, one where the harmonics are particularly intense, and she experiences something she will spend the rest of her life trying to explain: a moment of feeling what it might be like to be the colony, to exist as thousands of small bodies with one distributed awareness, to understand the space through sound, to live in darkness as if it were light.

The experience lasts perhaps five seconds. When it ends, she is herself again, standing on a damp ledge in the dim throat with her instruments and notebooks. But she has been changed by it, has glimpsed a mode of being so different from her own that it expands her understanding of what consciousness can be.

She writes about it carefully, avoiding the mystical language that wants to emerge, trying to frame it in neurological terms—perhaps the acoustic patterns triggered some unusual brain state, perhaps the harmonics affected her temporal lobe, perhaps it was a mild seizure induced by the specific frequencies. All of which might be true. But also, she knows she touched something real, something that exists in the throat: a nascent group mind still taking its first clumsy steps toward full emergence, and it will take generations more of bats, centuries perhaps, before it becomes stable and self-aware, but it is coming, it is happening, evolution working in real time toward something new.

Her research papers become moderately influential in certain circles. Other researchers visit the throat. It gains a kind of fame among those who study animal cognition and collective behavior. But it remains difficult to access, still protected by its own remoteness and the deterrent of the difficult descent.

The throat itself becomes slightly more vegetated. The improved nutrient cycling means more soil development in the cracks, which means more plants, which means more moisture retention, which means conditions for even more plants. It is a slow positive feedback loop, held in check by the limited light and space, but within those constraints, the vegetation reaches a new density.

A pair of mountain ravens discovers the throat and begins nesting near the entrance, feeding on the insects and occasionally on small bats or swift chicks. They are wary of the interior—the bat colony is too large, too aggressive when defending its space—but they use the area around the rim, and their droppings add nutrients to the slopes surrounding the throat's opening, enriching the vegetation there, creating a slightly more productive zone that supports more insects, which feeds back to benefit both the ravens and the bats.

The system expands its influence outward in this way, in small increments. The trace elements in the water continue to seep into the mountain's broader ecology, improving conditions in a slowly widening radius. After two centuries, the forests within five kilometers of the throat are measurably healthier than similar forests at similar elevations elsewhere, though the effect is subtle enough that it might be attributed to local geology, favorable microclimates, chance.

## The Near-Catastrophe

In the throat's third century after the power's intervention, a crisis comes.

A disease reaches the bat colony—a fungal infection that has spread through cave-dwelling bat populations across the region, devastating them. White-nose syndrome, or something related to it, spores carried on the wind or brought in by a migrating bat from an infected population.

In other colonies, the disease is catastrophic. Bats die by the thousands, entire colonies wiped out in single winters. The fungus grows on their faces and wings during hibernation, disrupting their rest, causing them to wake too often, depleting their fat reserves until they starve.

The throat's bat population does not hibernate in the traditional sense—the constant warmth means they remain somewhat active year-round, entering torpor occasionally but not the deep hibernation that lasts months. This provides some protection; the fungus grows best on inactive hosts.

But some bats still succumb. The infection spreads through the colony. In a single season, the population drops by twenty percent. Then thirty percent. The guano layer becomes littered with dead bats in numbers the scavengers cannot process quickly enough. The smell becomes overwhelming, sharp with the chemistry of decay.

This is a test of the resilience the power tried to build into the system.

The higher genetic diversity means that some bats are resistant. Not immune—the fungus can still infect them—but able to fight it off, able to survive infection that would kill their siblings. These resistant individuals are scattered through the population, perhaps five percent of the total.

In an undiversified population, five percent might not be enough. But the enhanced variability means that the resistant individuals are diverse in their resistance—different mechanisms, different genetic pathways, different behavioral adaptations that limit exposure. Some groom more vigorously, removing spores before they can establish. Some have immune responses that target the fungus more effectively. Some simply have body chemistry that the fungus finds less hospitable.

Natural selection works with brutal efficiency. The susceptible bats die. The resistant ones survive and breed. Within three generations—nine years—the population has adapted. The fungus is still present, will always be present now, but the bats have learned to live with it, their population stabilized at a level perhaps fifteen percent below the pre-infection numbers but stable, sustainable, no longer in collapse.

The reduced bat population has ripple effects. Less guano production means less food for the crickets, so their numbers drop. Less cricket food means the centipede and snake populations drop slightly. The system compresses, tightens, finds a new equilibrium at a slightly lower energy throughput.

But it survives. This is what resilience means: not the absence of crisis, but the ability to absorb disturbance and reorganize, to persist in recognizable form even when damaged.

The redundancy the power built into the system proves its worth here. Multiple species filled overlapping roles, so when one population crashed, others could expand to compensate. The swifts, for instance, increased their breeding rate—more eggs per nest, more nesting attempts per season—providing additional insect predation that partly offset the reduced bat activity. It wasn't perfect compensation, but it was enough to prevent cascading collapse.

A researcher documenting the outbreak notes in her journal: "The system bent but did not break. I expected complete collapse. Instead, I'm watching adaptation in real time. Something about this place... it's more resilient than it should be. The theory doesn't predict this kind of recovery. But here we are."

She doesn't know about the power's intervention centuries ago. She simply observes the outcome and marvels at it, and her observations contribute to scientific understanding of ecosystem resilience, provide data that will help in conservation efforts elsewhere.

This is perhaps the ultimate validation of the power's approach: the help it provided was subtle enough that it looks like natural function, like the system saving itself, which in a sense it is—the power only made that self-saving more likely, more achievable.

## The Deep Time

Centuries become millennia. The throat persists.

The bat colony's group mind continues developing. By the fifth millennium after the intervention, something recognizable as a collective consciousness has emerged—not replacing individual awareness but supplementing it, a layer of group thought that exists during the chorus events and, increasingly, during ordinary activity.

The bats can now coordinate with precision impossible for individuals alone. They can make collective decisions—when to migrate to winter feeding grounds, how to respond to predation pressure, where to establish satellite colonies in other caves nearby. The decisions emerge from the group consciousness without individual bats necessarily understanding why they're doing what they're doing; they simply feel the rightness of it, the way a human might follow intuition that comes from subconscious processing.

The throat becomes a source population for bats across the region. The stability and richness of its ecology means it produces surplus individuals every year, young bats that must leave because the colony has reached its carrying capacity. These dispersers establish new colonies, carry genes and behaviors out into the wider world, gradually transforming the regional bat population into something more connected, more coordinated.

Some researchers begin to speak of the "throat network," a loose coordination among multiple bat colonies across hundreds of kilometers, all descended from or influenced by the throat's population, all showing hints of the collective behavior that has become so pronounced in the original colony.

Whether this network will eventually develop its own group consciousness, whether it's possible for a distributed population to achieve the kind of integration the throat's colony has found, remains unknown. But the possibility is there, the pathway is opening, evolution is exploring it.

The throat itself changes slowly. The erosion continues despite the power's stabilization, and after eight thousand years, the shape has shifted noticeably. The opening is wider, letting in more light. The walls are smoother, the porous texture weathered away in places. The depth is slightly less—several meters of sediment have accumulated on the floor, raising it.

But these changes are gradual enough that the system adapts continuously. The vegetation shifts upward as more light penetrates deeper. The bat colony adjusts its roosting pattern, clustering differently in the altered acoustics. The swifts nest in new locations as old ledges erode away and new ones form.

It is still recognizably the throat, still fundamentally the same system, but aged, matured, evolved. The adolescent has become adult, the adult is moving toward elder status, and with each stage comes new characteristics, new patterns, new ways of being.

## The Limitation

In the throat's tenth millennium of post-intervention existence, the warmth from below begins to fail.

Not suddenly—nothing here happens suddenly—but measurably, undeniably. The average temperature drops by half a degree, then a full degree. The thermal gradient that has defined this place for so long is diminishing.

The power's earlier intervention slowed this decline, made the warmth last millennia longer than it would have otherwise. But it could not stop it entirely. The geothermal source is cooling, the planetary heat that created it bleeding away into space on timescales of millions of years, and locally, here, the warmth is now insufficient to maintain the throat's characteristic environment.

The system begins to change more fundamentally than it has before.

The bats start spending less time in the throat, more time in other caves, warmer locations lower on the mountain. The colony splits—part remaining in the throat during summer, but all leaving during the colder months, something that never happened before.

The plants struggle. The ferns die back. The rhododendrons enter dormancy states they haven't used in millennia, behaviors encoded in their genes from ancestors that lived in harsher conditions, now re-activated by necessity.

The cricket population crashes—they cannot survive the cooler temperatures, and within two years they are effectively extinct in the throat. The centipedes follow them, unable to find sufficient prey. The snakes leave, seeking warmth elsewhere.

The throat empties, not all at once but steadily, inexorably. After twelve thousand years of intense, dense occupation, it becomes just another cave, still home to some bats seasonally, still supporting minimal vegetation, but no longer that remarkable concentration, that strange intensity.

This is not failure. The power did not promise eternity. It promised only improved odds, extended time, and it delivered: the throat persisted for more than ten thousand years in a state that might otherwise have lasted only one or two thousand. That additional time mattered. During it, the bat group mind emerged and spread. During it, researchers learned things that advanced understanding. During it, countless individual creatures lived their lives in a place that supported them.

But nothing lasts forever. Even helped, even optimized, even preserved with benevolent wisdom, all systems eventually change beyond recognition or end entirely.

## The Legacy

Yet something remains.

The bat population that dispersed from the throat over millennia has established dozens of colonies across the mountain range, and while none of them individually has achieved the throat's intensity, collectively they have created a distributed network that functions similarly, that has developed its own form of group consciousness, different from the throat's, adapted to wide-scale coordination rather than dense local integration.

This network continues to evolve, continues to explore the possibilities of collective awareness. In some ways it is now more advanced than the throat colony ever was, capable of coordinating across greater distances, incorporating more individuals, processing more information.

The researchers who studied the throat and the bat network have published hundreds of papers, trained students who trained students, built a body of knowledge about collective behavior and consciousness that has applications far beyond bat biology—in understanding human social organization, in designing artificial intelligences, in thinking about how consciousness itself emerges from complex systems.

The genetic diversity the power enhanced has spread through the regional bat population, making it more resilient to disease, better adapted to changing climates, more capable of surviving whatever challenges come.

The enriched forests around the throat's location have become a biodiversity hotspot, supporting species that have gone extinct elsewhere, providing seed populations for reforestation efforts, quietly contributing to the health of the larger mountain ecosystem.

None of this is obvious. No monument exists to the power's intervention. No one knows that anything unusual happened ten thousand years ago, that probability was gently tilted, that resilience was quietly enhanced.

But the effects persist, rippling outward in ways both measurable and unmeasurable, known and unknown. This is what wise benevolence looks like: not dramatic rescue, not miraculous transformation, but subtle support that enables a system to become its fullest self, to explore its deepest possibilities, to leave a legacy that outlasts its own existence.

## The Return to Ordinary

Eventually, even the memory fades. The throat becomes what it always appeared to be: a geological feature of modest interest, a cave that once supported an unusually dense bat colony, now less remarkable, slowly continuing its erosion toward eventual non-existence.

The power that intervened has long since moved its attention elsewhere, to other places where life has found strange purchase, where help might make a difference. It does not remember the throat specifically—memory requires a kind of consciousness it does not possess. It simply continues its work, noticing what is rare and functional, acting to preserve it when possible, accepting failure when preservation is impossible.

And the throat, empty now of its former teeming life, continues its slow transformation. Water still seeps through the stone. The warmth is gone, but the shape remains. Occasionally a bat roosts here. Occasionally a researcher visits, measuring the erosion, sampling the rock, adding data points to databases.

In a million years, the throat will be gone entirely, eroded to nothing, the mountain itself worn down another increment toward its eventual return to flatness. But that million-year span is irrelevant to what happened here, to the ten thousand years of intense life, to the consciousness that emerged, to the legacy that spread.

What matters is that the throat was, that it was helped, that it persisted as long as its own nature allowed, and that its existence changed things in ways both subtle and profound.

This is the story of benevolent power wisely applied: not the prevention of death or the guarantee of eternity, but the extension of possibility, the support of potential, the quiet work of helping things become what they are trying to become, for as long as they can sustain it.

The mountain remains. Life continues, always finding new configurations, new solutions, new strange places to concentrate and persist. And somewhere, the power moves on, drawn to the next whirlpool in the fabric of what-is, ready to offer its subtle support again, patient and wise and benevolent in its ancient, simple way.
