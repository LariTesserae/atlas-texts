# What the Light Would Do

The attention comes first as attention always does—not as intervention, not yet as presence, but as the simple fact of being *noticed*. Across whatever distance separates wisdom from its objects of care, the Flats registers. A benevolent awareness turns, focuses, considers.

And finds itself... uncertain.

This is unusual for a wise power. Wisdom generally knows what needs mending, what requires protection, what calls for transformation. But the Flats presents a peculiar problem: it is neither degraded nor pristine, neither suffering nor flourishing in any simple sense. It is *working*. The pumps still run. The birds still nest. The fish still breed in their uncountable millions. The system has found its strange equilibrium—industrial infrastructure repurposed by pure accident into one of the densest wildlife colonies on the planet.

What does one do with something that is broken and functional, abandoned and thriving, wrong and working all at once?

The wise power considers. It has dealt with wounded forests, with poisoned rivers, with animals driven to the edge of extinction. It knows how to heal, how to restore, how to gently guide systems back toward wholeness. But this? This is already whole, just not in any way that fits the usual categories. The Flats is not nature recovering from human damage—it is human damage *become* nature, infrastructure transformed into habitat without ceasing to be infrastructure.

So the power does what wisdom does when confronted with genuine uncertainty: it looks closer. It pays attention. It watches.

---

## The Watching (What Becomes Visible)

In the light of benevolent attention, details emerge that even careful human observers have missed.

There: in the deepest basement of the eastern pump station, something is happening with the water chemistry. The geothermal flow carries dissolved minerals upward, yes, but here in this particular basin the mineral concentration is different, the balance shifted by the way water circulates through the pump cycle. And here, the bacterial mats are different too—not the standard species that coat every other surface, but something else, something chemosynthetic in a different way, metabolizing the minerals more efficiently, producing more biomass per unit of energy input.

The killifish that feed here are subtly different as a result. Slightly larger. Slightly more robust. Not enough that anyone has measured it, but enough that birds hunting here are marginally more successful. The heron that stands in the third-floor corridor of the northern residential tower, the old one with the injured leg—it hunts that corridor because it learned years ago that it connects, via a flooded stairwell, to that eastern pump station basement. The fish that migrate up from the basement to the corridor are better fish, and the heron has built its entire late-life strategy around this knowledge.

The power sees this: individual wisdom, animal wisdom, the intelligence of a bird that has survived into old age by learning its world with precision. The heron's knowledge of that corridor is as detailed as any scientist's knowledge of their field. More detailed, actually, because the heron's survival depends on getting it right, and natural selection is a rigorous peer-reviewer.

Elsewhere: the osprey pair on the water tower with their perfect shift work. Looking closer, the power sees that their coordination isn't just behavioral—they are physiologically synchronized, their hormonal cycles aligned in a way that makes the male more alert and aggressive during the female's hunting hours, the female more protective during the male's absence. This isn't learned. This is evolved, or evolving. In just the few generations since ospreys colonized the Flats, pairs that could coordinate this way left more offspring, and the trait is spreading. The power is watching speciation in real-time, adaptation happening at the speed of city life, accelerated by density and selection pressure.

In the Grand Warehouse: the forty thousand egrets are not a random aggregation. The power sees the structure now—extended family groups occupying particular sections, lineages that have nested in the same area of the warehouse for multiple generations. Young birds don't just learn to hunt from any adult; they learn from their mothers, their aunts, their older siblings. Knowledge accumulates and transmits through family lines. The egrets are developing culture, capital-C Culture, the way some whale populations have different hunting techniques, the way some chimp groups use different tools.

And the fish, the uncountable killifish—they are not interchangeable. The power sees genetic diversity maintained across the population despite the high turnover, sees how the multiple flooded basins function as semi-isolated populations, exchanging genes when pump cycles temporarily connect previously separate pools. The Flats is not one fish population but dozens, each evolving slightly different adaptations to local conditions, the whole system maintaining diversity through its architectural complexity.

The crustaceans: they are not one species either, but several, filling different niches in the warm water. The pale cave-adapted crayfish are ancient, relict populations that survived in deep groundwater for thousands of years and have only recently found surface access through the wells and pipes. They are Pleistocene survivors, living fossils, now breeding in flooded basements beneath a ruined city. The amphipods are newer, more cosmopolitan, but even they show variation—the ones living in the hottest water are a different species or becoming one, their heat tolerance pushed to extremes.

The power sees time differently than humans do. It sees the decades of the Flats' existence as a moment, but a moment filled with evolutionary potential. This is not a static system. This is a system in rapid flux, species adapting, behaviors changing, new relationships forming. In a hundred years, if the Flats persists, the birds here will be as distinct from their marsh-dwelling cousins as city pigeons are from rock doves. The fish will be new species entirely. The whole community will have become something unprecedented—a thermophilic urban wetland fauna, adapted to life in warm mineral water among concrete ruins.

And it will have happened without plan, without design, purely through the ordinary operation of adaptation and survival, the same forces that have always shaped life but operating here in this strange new context.

The power finds this... beautiful. Actually beautiful. Not despite the rust and the guano and the industrial decay, but because of what all that represents: life's absolute refusal to recognize human categories of "natural" and "artificial," its willingness to use whatever materials evolution provides, including concrete and steel and diesel pumps.

## The First Impulse (What Wisdom Nearly Does)

The wise power's first impulse is to help, because that is what benevolence does. It wants to:

Stabilize the buildings—reinforce the structures that are degrading, ensure they last centuries more instead of decades, give the birds permanent security.

Repair the pumps—replace the failing diesel engines with something eternal, some subtle force that will maintain the water circulation forever without need of fuel or maintenance.

Adjust the chemistry—fine-tune the mineral content of the water to optimize bacterial growth, increase fish populations even further, support more birds.

Introduce plants—surely the system would be richer with some vegetation? Even salt-tolerant species, some tough grasses or shrubs adapted to heat and minerals, adding a layer of complexity currently absent.

Clean it up—reduce the guano accumulation, improve the water clarity, make it more... pleasant? More like what a wetland "should" be?

The power drafts these interventions, plans them in detail, prepares to act—

And stops.

Because wisdom is not just benevolence. Wisdom is benevolence plus understanding. And something in the understanding gives pause.

The buildings are weathering, yes, but at what rate? The power looks again: slowly. Very slowly. Concrete and brick are durable. The buildings have decades left, maybe a century or more for the most sound structures. And the birds are adaptable—when one building becomes unsafe, they move to another. There are more buildings than the birds currently need. The rate of decay is slower than the rate of adaptation. The system doesn't need permanent buildings; it needs buildings that last long enough, and that's what it has.

The pumps will fail eventually, yes. And when they do? The water will redistribute. Some areas will dry, some will flood deeper. The birds will adjust. Some species will leave, others will increase. The system will change, but change is not the same as collapse. The geothermal flow will continue regardless of pump status. The core resource—warm mineral water producing abundant fish—will persist. The birds will find a new equilibrium, different but not necessarily worse.

The chemistry is already optimal, actually. The bacterial blooms support the observed fish populations, which support the observed bird populations. It's balanced. "Optimizing" it would mean changing it, tipping it toward some other state that might not be better, just different. And who is the power to say that more fish is better? The current density of birds already pushes the limit of what the space can physically accommodate. More birds wouldn't fit. They would spill into surrounding areas, which already have their own populations. The number of birds the Flats supports is constrained by space, not food. Adding more food would just create waste, or worse, support different species that would displace the current community.

Plants? The power looks at this question longer. The absence of vegetation is striking, seems wrong. But... the heat and minerals that prevent plant growth are also what make the bacterial blooms so productive. Plants here would compete with bacteria for nutrients. That might reduce bacterial productivity, which would reduce fish, which would reduce birds. And the birds don't need plants—they nest on concrete and steel perfectly well. Their nesting materials come from debris, plastic and wire that doesn't rot and actually works better than sticks in the humid heat. The plant-free system isn't lacking something; it's differently organized, built on a bacterial base instead of a plant base, and it works.

Clean it up? The guano is habitat. The amphipods feed on the bacteria that decompose guano. The bacteria that decompose guano enrich the water that supports the bacterial blooms that feed the fish. The guano is not waste product; it's part of the cycle, essential nutrient recycling. Remove it and you remove a crucial link in the food web. The water clarity is low because it's full of bacteria and tiny crustaceans and fish fry—that's not pollution, that's productivity. Clear water would be dead water here.

The power realizes: every "improvement" it considered would actually harm the system. Not through malice, but through misunderstanding what the system is and how it works. The Flats doesn't need to be made more like a natural wetland. It is its own thing, functioning according to its own logic, and that logic is sound.

This is humbling for a wise power. This is instructive.

The power reconsiders. If intervention would damage rather than help, if the system is already working... what should wisdom do?

## The Actual Intervention (What Changes)

The wise power makes a different choice. Instead of changing the Flats, it changes the relationship between the Flats and what surrounds it.

First: protection. Not of the Flats itself, which needs no protection from its own processes, but protection from external threats. The power weaves a subtle influence into the minds of distant bureaucrats and developers, not compelling them but gently adjusting probabilities, making the idea of reclaiming the Flats seem less appealing, more costly, more trouble than it's worth. The economic calculations that already disfavor redevelopment become slightly more unfavorable. The legal complications become slightly more complicated. It's not mind control; it's just a thumb on the scale, ensuring that the Flats will be left alone, at least for the foreseeable future.

This is barely intervention at all, really. The power is just protecting the current trajectory, allowing what is already happening to continue happening.

Second: connection. The Flats is isolated, cut off from the surrounding marsh and the bird populations there. Some exchange happens—birds move between the two areas, genes flow—but less than optimal. The power doesn't change this directly, doesn't move birds or dig channels. Instead, it adjusts one small thing: the depth of water in the peripheral transition zone.

There's a slight ridge of land that encircles part of the Flats, keeping water from flowing freely between the warm city basin and the cooler marsh. The power shifts groundwater pressure, just slightly, just enough that water begins to overflow that ridge in two places, creating channels. Shallow channels, inches deep, but enough that fish can move between the systems more easily. Enough that the killifish population can exchange genes with wild fish populations, maintaining genetic diversity. Enough that catfish can migrate between the Flats and the marsh, finding new habitat, expanding their range.

This is a tiny change, hydrologically insignificant. But it has cascading effects. Birds follow the fish. The peripheral transition zone becomes richer, more complex. The Flats becomes less isolated, more integrated into the larger regional ecosystem. It remains distinct—the warm water makes it unique—but it's no longer completely cut off.

Third, and most subtle: awareness. The power cannot make humans understand the Flats directly—understanding comes from observation, from study, from the slow accumulation of knowledge. But it can make people want to understand. It adjusts probability again, gently, making researchers slightly more likely to get their grant proposals funded, making photographers slightly more likely to have their work published, making documentarians slightly more likely to find their projects greenlit.

Over the next few years, more people study the Flats. More papers get published. More images circulate. Not through miraculous intervention, just through a thousand small adjustments that remove obstacles and smooth paths. The Flats becomes known, becomes documented, becomes valued not as a ruin or an accident but as what it is: a novel ecosystem, a unique community, a working example of life's adaptability.

And this awareness creates its own protection, stronger than any legal mechanism. Once people know about the Flats, once they understand it, once they value it, the political will to destroy it evaporates. It becomes easier to protect it than to explain why you're destroying it.

Fourth: the rats. This is the one population that is genuinely struggling, that exists at the edge of viability, that could use actual help. The power considers carefully. The rats are predators, eating eggs and chicks. Helping them might harm the birds. But the rats are also part of the system, consuming the dead, filling a necessary niche. And their population is self-limiting—they can never become too numerous because the birds mob them, because food is scarce, because they live in such marginal conditions.

The power makes the smallest adjustment: it shifts the timing of some bird nesting cycles, just by a few days, so that different species nest at slightly different times rather than in complete synchrony. This spreads egg availability over a longer period, making the rats' food supply more consistent rather than feast-or-famine. Not more food, just better distributed food. The rat population stabilizes, increases slightly, enough to ensure their long-term survival without threatening the birds.

The rats don't know they've been helped. No one knows. It looks like natural variation, like chance. But it's enough.

## The Deeper Choice (What Wisdom Learns to Not-Do)

But the real intervention, the most important one, is what the power chooses not to do.

It does not stabilize the system permanently. It does not lock the Flats into its current state, preserving it like a museum specimen. It does not prevent change.

Because the power recognizes, finally, that the beauty of the Flats is not in what it is but in what it's becoming. This is a system in flux, evolving, adapting, changing. In fifty years it will be different—some species will have increased, others decreased, new behaviors will have emerged, new adaptations will have appeared. In a hundred years it might be radically different. The buildings will be more decayed or perhaps some will have collapsed, opening new spaces. The pumps will have failed, changing water patterns. New species will have arrived, found niches, integrated into the community. The birds that live here might be genuinely new species, distinct enough to warrant new names.

That process—that constant becoming—is precious. More precious than any particular state, any particular configuration. To freeze the Flats as it is would be to kill what makes it alive, which is its capacity for change, for adaptation, for evolution.

So the power protects the process, not the product. It ensures the Flats will be left alone to continue changing, but it doesn't prevent the changes. It helps the system stay connected to its surroundings so that it remains evolutionarily viable, but it doesn't direct the evolution. It makes humans more aware so they will value the place, but it doesn't control what they do with that awareness.

This is harder than intervention. This requires restraint, which is the most difficult form of wisdom. The power must watch the Flats weather and change and not fix it. Must watch species struggle and sometimes fail and not save them. Must accept that the future of the Flats is unknown, unpredictable, and that this uncertainty is good, is necessary, is the whole point.

There is one more thing, though. One gift that wisdom can give without harm.

## The Gift (What Remains)

The power reaches down, not into the physical world but into something adjacent to it—the realm of perception, of experience, of what it feels like to be alive in a place.

It touches the herons, the egrets, the ibises, the night herons, the ospreys, the eagles, the falcons—all of them, every bird in the Flats. And it gives them, just for a moment, awareness of each other in a different way.

A heron hunting in its corridor suddenly feels, dimly, the presence of every other heron in the city. Not thoughts—birds don't think in words—but a sense of kinship, of shared existence, of being part of something vast. An egret in the Grand Warehouse feels its colony not as forty thousand separate birds but as one enormous community, and itself as a valued part of that whole.

It lasts only a moment, this expanded awareness. Then it fades, leaves only a trace, a memory without words, a feeling that colors how the birds relate to their neighbors. They are slightly more tolerant of each other after this. Slightly more willing to share space. Slightly more cooperative in their coexistence.

It's a small thing. It doesn't change behavior dramatically. The birds remain birds, still competitive, still territorial, still driven by hunger and reproduction and survival. But there's a softness to their interactions now, a slight reduction in the aggression that always exists in dense colonies. Fewer fights over nest sites. Fewer injuries from territorial disputes. A small increase in cooperative feeding behaviors, birds learning from each other more readily.

The gift propagates through generations. Birds that experienced that moment of expanded awareness raise chicks who are slightly more social, and those chicks grow up to be slightly more tolerant adults, and they raise chicks who are even more so. The trait spreads, reinforces, becomes part of how Flats birds differ from wild birds. They are city birds, truly urban, adapted not just physically but socially to life in density.

To the rats and the mustelids, the fish and the crustaceans, the power gives something different but equivalent: a reduction in fear. Not the removal of fear—fear is necessary, keeps prey alive—but a calibration of fear, so they are exactly as afraid as they need to be and no more. The rats become slightly bolder in their movements, slightly more efficient in their foraging, because they waste less energy on excessive caution. The fish school more effectively because individual panic spreads less readily through the group.

These are tiny changes, barely measurable. But in a system as tight as the Flats, where every species exists near the carrying capacity, where every efficiency matters, tiny changes compound. Survival rates increase marginally. Reproduction becomes slightly more successful. The population curves shift, just a little, toward stability.

And to the place itself—the concrete and the rust, the standing water and the diesel pumps, the architecture and infrastructure—the power gives nothing, because the place needs nothing. It is already doing its job, already providing what the birds require. The buildings don't need to be stronger; they need to be strong enough. The pumps don't need to run forever; they need to run long enough. The water doesn't need to be pure; it needs to be productive.

The Flats is perfect in its imperfection, functional in its decay. Wisdom recognizes this and leaves it alone.

---

## The Withdrawal (What Remains After)

The wise power withdraws its attention gradually, not suddenly. It continues to watch, but from a greater distance, with less intensity. It maintains the small protections it put in place—the bureaucratic disinclination to redevelop, the slightly enhanced funding success for researchers, the channels connecting the Flats to the marsh. These things persist not through continued intervention but because they are now part of the system, woven into the normal functioning of things.

The birds continue their lives, unaware they were ever noticed by anything beyond their ordinary predators and competitors. The fish continue breeding in their millions. The crustaceans continue their slow crawl across submerged concrete. The pumps continue running, for now, burning through their finite fuel, approaching their inevitable failure.

Everything continues. The change the power made is so subtle that no one could point to a before and after, could say "here is where the intervention happened." The Flats is still the Flats—hot, humid, rust-stained, thick with birds and guano and the smell of warm mineral water. Still abandoned by humans, still colonized by herons, still functioning in its strange equilibrium.

But there is one change that might, eventually, become visible:

The researchers who study the Flats now, who publish their papers and share their data, slowly build a body of knowledge. They document the species, the behaviors, the ecology. They create a baseline, a record of what the Flats is at this moment in its existence.

And as decades pass, as the system evolves and changes, future researchers will compare their observations to this baseline and see evolution in action. They will document new species emerging, will track behavioral changes, will watch in real-time as adaptation occurs. The Flats will become a natural laboratory, a place where the processes that usually take millennia happen in human lifetimes, visible and documentable.

Some of these researchers will become advocates, will fight to protect the Flats, will make the case for its value. Some will become educators, teaching others about this strange place where nature and industry have merged. Some will become artists, finding beauty in the rust and the birds and the hot standing water.

And through their work, the Flats will be protected not by force or law primarily, but by being understood, by being valued, by being recognized as something precious: not despite its strangeness but because of it.

This is the power's real gift—not intervention but facilitation of understanding. Not changing the Flats but changing humanity's relationship to it, so that we become protectors rather than destroyers, students rather than reclaimers, witnesses to something we didn't create and can't improve but can, if we're wise, allow to continue.

## What the Heron Knows

In the northern residential tower, in a flooded third-floor corridor, the old heron stands in its accustomed position. Its injured leg is cocked at the familiar angle. Its eyes track the water, watching for the movement of fish.

It has stood here for years. It knows this corridor better than anything else in its world—knows which cracks in the walls the killifish use to hide, knows how the light moves across the water through the day, knows the pattern of pump cycles that bring fish up from the deep basement twice daily.

It does not know that a wise power has noticed it, has seen its individual struggle and survival, has recognized its intelligence and adaptation. It does not know it has been blessed, protected, given a slightly better chance at survival.

What it knows is this: the corridor is good for hunting. The fish are here. It is skilled at catching them. It has survived another day. It will return tomorrow.

That is enough knowledge for a heron. That is, perhaps, all the knowledge any creature needs—how to survive in its world, how to find food and shelter, how to persist from one day to the next.

The heron strikes, quick and precise, catches a fish, swallows, resets. Returns to its stance. Returns to watching. Returns to the patient work of being a heron in a city that is no longer human but is still, absolutely, a city.

Somewhere far above, beyond sight or sense, the wise power watches this and is satisfied. The heron is doing what a heron does. The city is doing what this city does. The system persists, changes, evolves, lives.

It needs no saving because it is not lost. It needs no fixing because it is not broken. It needs only what the power has given it: protection from external destruction, connection to the larger world, and the space to continue becoming whatever it will become.

The heat presses down. The water rises. The birds wheel and cry and feed. The pumps run, for now, and will fail, eventually, and the system will adjust, will change, will persist in new forms.

And wisdom, having done what wisdom can do—which is mostly to refrain from doing—withdraws, satisfied, leaving behind only this: a place that works, creatures that thrive, and time enough for whatever comes next.

The heron catches another fish.

The city endures.

The light moves on.
