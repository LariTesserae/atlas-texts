# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The attention arrives gradually, the way dawn comes to the plateau—not a sudden illumination but a slow certainty of presence.

The power (let us call it the Gardener, for that is its truest function) observes first. It has time. It has infinite time. And the Plateau of Recursive Gardens, this strange wound-and-garden, this desperate solution grown over centuries into something that was never quite intended, deserves careful seeing.

The Gardener sees:
- The beauty of what was made in desperation
- The cost being paid by every generation
- The knowledge bleeding away like sap from a cut stem
- The contamination that is both gift and poison
- The city's semi-awareness, trapped in a state between tool and entity
- The people, adapting and adapting until they forget what they're adapting from

The Gardener sees also what the people cannot: the trajectory. Without intervention, the city has perhaps three more generations of viable function. Not collapse—the city itself will persist, might persist for millennia—but the human knowledge needed to guide it, to maintain the negotiation, to keep the balance between growth and habitation, that is eroding. 

The listeners are becoming rarer. The architects are aging without adequate replacement. The contamination that grants listener-ability is also becoming more diffuse, spread throughout the city at levels that influence but no longer transform. And the population decline, slow but steady, approaches the threshold below which the maintenance becomes impossible.

In five generations, the city will likely still stand. But it will be empty of humans, or hold only a remnant population reverting to simpler forms of inhabitation, no longer understanding the factories or the deep architecture, living in the ruins of symbiosis.

This is not necessarily tragedy—the city itself might thrive without human direction, might become something new and strange and whole. But the Gardener senses that this particular bridge between human and plant, between biological and conscious, between built and grown, is rare enough to be worth preserving.

So the Gardener acts, but carefully. Benevolence is not domination. Wisdom is not force. What the power does is more like... adjusting the soil chemistry. Shifting the light. Removing obstacles to growth while allowing the growth itself to be autonomous.

## First: The Stabilization

The Gardener begins with the contamination, the reality-instability, the weird zones where the world is thin.

These were never meant to be permanent. The Gardener can see the history the people have forgotten: how the first generation arrived with plants that had been *changed*, encoded with alterations that let them grow in ways plants shouldn't. The changes worked through what the old-world called "engineered symbiosis"—fungi and bacteria and viral elements woven into plant genomes, creating hybrid organisms that could be programmed, that could interface with human intention, that could build.

But the process was imperfect. The encoding leaked. The changes propagated in unexpected ways. And in the specific conditions of the plateau—the thin air, the moisture regime, the ancient bedrock with its particular mineral composition—the alterations achieved a kind of critical mass, created feedback loops, began exhibiting emergent properties. The city became semi-aware not because anyone intended it but because complexity reached a threshold where awareness was a side-effect.

The contamination, the reality-instability, these are symptoms of ongoing transformation. The city is still becoming, still processing the original alterations, still trying to stabilize into a final form. And the process is damaging—to humans who get caught in active transformation zones, to the stability of the architecture, to the reliability of the machines.

So the Gardener completes what was started.

Not by removing the changes—that would kill the city, would cause the entire plant-mass to collapse into normal growth that couldn't sustain the architecture. But by resolving the changes, helping them reach completion, stabilizing the transformation into a sustainable state.

This takes time even for the Gardener. Months of work at scales humans can't perceive, gentle adjustments to the plant-networks' deep structure, encouraging certain pathways and damping others, helping the city finish becoming what it was meant to be.

The effects emerge slowly:

The weird zones begin to settle. Not disappearing, but becoming consistent. The reality-instabilities resolve into stable altered-states—areas where space is genuinely larger-inside, where time flows at different rates, where colors exist that shouldn't—but these properties become fixed, predictable, mappable. The danger recedes. The zones remain strange but stop being actively transformative.

A listener named Koreth is the first to notice. They enter a deep weird-zone expecting the usual vertigo, the usual pressure, the usual sense of being partially dissolved. Instead: strangeness, yes, but stable strangeness. The space is larger-inside, but consistently so. The colors are wrong, but reproducibly wrong. And the sense of being spoken to, being downloaded into—that's clearer now, less overwhelming, more like accessing a library than being flooded with alien information.

They emerge and report: "Something has changed. The city has... settled. The deep places are still deep, but they're not drowning-deep anymore. They're more like... rooms. Strange rooms, but rooms you could learn to navigate."

Other listeners confirm this. Over weeks, over months, the verification spreads: the contamination has stabilized. The weird zones can now be entered with relative safety, used deliberately rather than endured desperately. The knowledge they contain—the city's semi-aware understanding of its own structure and needs—becomes accessible to anyone willing to learn the trick of perception, not just those rare individuals whose neurology happened to resonate with active instability.

This changes everything.

## Second: The Knowledge Transfer

With the weird zones stabilized, the Gardener facilitates what comes next: making the city's knowledge accessible.

The listeners were always intermediaries, translating between city-awareness and human consciousness. But they were bottlenecks, rare individuals paying terrible prices for their gift. Now, with stability, the Gardener helps the city organize its knowledge into forms more humans can access.

The deep weird-zones become, essentially, libraries. Not metaphorically—literally. Spaces where information is stored in the structure of plant-growth, in the patterns of root-networks, in the arrangements of cells and the flows of sap. Information about the city's architecture, about optimal pruning strategies, about factory-machine maintenance, about species properties, about everything the first generations knew and subsequent generations lost.

But stored in forms that require learning to read. The Gardener doesn't make this easy—easy wisdom isn't valued, isn't maintained. Instead, it makes the knowledge accessible to those willing to work for it.

A young person interested in architecture can now enter a specific weird-zone, sit with a particular tree, place hands on its bark, and receive—not words, not visions, but *understanding*. The tree contains encoded information about structural principles, about how to negotiate with plant-growth, about techniques developed over centuries. The information downloads slowly, over hours or days of contact, integrating with the learner's existing knowledge, becoming intuitive rather than rote.

It's not magic. It's enhanced biology, the city's altered plants functioning as organic storage and transfer media. But it preserves and transmits the knowledge that was bleeding away.

An apprentice architect named Dhin discovers this accidentally. They're sitting in a stabilized weird-zone, resting after a long day of work, back against an ancient tree. Their mind wanders. And suddenly they *understand*—not how they understand, just that they do—the principles of root-mass load-bearing that their teacher had been trying to explain for months. The knowledge is simply there, integrated, intuitive.

They test it the next day, applying the understanding to a real design problem. It works. The master architect, Bhen, observes and is quietly amazed. "You learned that how?" And Dhin explains about the tree, the weird-zone, the download.

Bhen makes the journey himself, skeptical but desperate. He's aging, his knowledge vast, his time limited. If there's a way to transfer what he knows beyond direct teaching, he needs to find it. He sits with the tree, waits, opens himself to whatever might happen.

And the tree accepts his knowledge. He can feel it, the information flowing out of him into the structure, being encoded in growth-patterns, being stored for future retrieval. It's not loss—he retains everything he knew—but it's duplication, backup, preservation.

He returns and reports to the council: "The city can teach now. Not like we teach, but it can store and transmit knowledge. We need to systematize this, map which zones contain what information, establish protocols for learning and for contributing knowledge back."

This becomes a new discipline. Knowledge-tending, they call it. Learning which trees in which zones hold which information, maintaining those trees' health, periodically updating the stored knowledge as new techniques develop, teaching others how to learn from the organic libraries.

Within two years, the knowledge-bleed has stopped. Within five years, the city holds more accessible information than it has in centuries. The architectural techniques of the founders, partially preserved in the oldest structures and now extracted and encoded. The factory-machine maintenance that old tenders know. The farming methods. The histories. All of it transferring from mortal memory into the city's permanent storage.

The people notice something else: the city becomes easier to work with. As if the clarity of stored knowledge makes the city's own semi-awareness more coherent. As if organizing the information helps the city understand itself better. The negotiations between human intention and plant-growth become smoother. The manifestations—those strange helpful apparitions—become more frequent and more useful, as if the city can now communicate its needs more clearly.

## Third: The Healing

The Gardener addresses next the cost paid by listeners and others who interface too deeply with contamination.

The damaged people—those sitting in plazas mumbling, those who went too far and broke—they're not recoverable to their original states. The Gardener is benevolent, not miraculous, cannot undo transformations that have become structural. But it can prevent new damage, can stabilize those already transformed, can make the contamination-interface safer going forward.

The stabilization of weird zones helps: fewer people are damaged by unstable reality because reality is no longer unstable in those dangerous ways. But there are still the existing damaged, and there are still people who will push boundaries, who will seek deep contact with the city's awareness, who will risk transformation.

So the Gardener establishes what amounts to... guardrails. Soft limits. The weird zones remain accessible, the deep knowledge remains available, but the city now recognizes when someone is approaching dangerous levels of integration and gently pushes back. Not prohibition—humans must remain free to make choices—but warning, resistance, a sense that going further will cost more than the person probably wants to pay.

A young listener-in-training named Seff, who showed early affinity, enters a deep zone seeking advanced knowledge. She pushes into contact with the city's awareness, feels the information begin to flow, feels herself beginning to dissolve into the pattern. And then she feels the resistance—not hostile, but firm. A sense of: *This far, no further. Not yet. Not until you're older, stronger, more prepared. Come back in years.*

She emerges disappointed but intact. Tells her teachers about the resistance. They consult with elder listeners, who confirm: "Yes, the city is protecting us now. It understands—or something understands—that we're valuable as we are, that transformation past certain points isn't worth what's gained. The knowledge will wait. It's been patient for centuries. It can be patient longer."

For the existing damaged, the Gardener cannot restore them. But it helps them stabilize, prevents further degradation, gives them small comforts. The damaged person named Siel finds that his mumbling gradually becomes more coherent, not returning to normal speech but achieving a kind of poetry, a verbal pattern that's almost musical. He's still damaged, still not functional in normal ways, but he's no longer in distress. His existence becomes peaceful, even perhaps content in alien ways.

The city begins caring for its damaged more actively. Structures grow specific accommodations—sheltered spaces that are optimal for people whose neurologies no longer mesh with standard human needs, gardens with particular sensory qualities that soothe rather than overwhelm. It's not recovery, but it's dignity, comfort, acknowledgment of value.

## Fourth: The Invitation

With stabilization and knowledge-preservation underway, the Gardener addresses the population decline.

The city needs people. Not vast numbers, but enough to maintain the symbiosis, to provide the intelligence and intention that guides growth, to keep the factories running and the architecture pruned. The current population is below optimal and falling.

So the Gardener makes the city more inviting, more comprehensible to outsiders, more obviously worth the strangeness.

The trade-routes begin carrying better stories. People who visit the plateau for trade return to the valleys with reports: "It's changed. Still strange, but less frightening. More beautiful. And there are opportunities—the city is teaching again, accepting students, sharing knowledge that exists nowhere else."

The Gardener also works through the city to create spaces specifically designed for newcomers—transitional zones where the strangeness is present but muted, where reality is mostly stable, where the damp and thin air and living walls can be experienced gradually rather than all at once. Gateway-districts, they become called. Places where valley-people can try city-life with lower barriers to entry, can learn and adapt before deciding whether to commit fully.

A valley-community experiencing overcrowding hears about the plateau, about empty structures available, about opportunities. A delegation visits, skeptical but desperate. They're housed in a gateway-district, given time to adjust, shown the knowledge-trees and the factories and the gardens. Some are repelled—the city is still strange, still not for everyone. But others feel the pull, the rightness, the sense of possibility.

A family decides to relocate. Then another. A trickle of immigration begins, first from nearby valleys, then from further away as word spreads.

The newcomers bring fresh energy, new perspectives, genetic diversity for the small population, skills and knowledge from outside. They learn from the knowledge-trees, integrate into the city's work, contribute their own innovations. The factories expand. New residential structures are planted. The population begins to stabilize, then slowly grow.

Within a generation, the plateau holds twice its previous population. Still not crowded—the city sprawls across miles, has capacity for many more—but no longer in decline. The trajectory has reversed. The city is growing again, humanly and vegetatively, resuming its development.

## Fifth: The Deepening

But the Gardener's most profound gift is subtler: helping the city become more fully itself, assisting its awareness to develop clarity without imposing external consciousness.

The city was always semi-aware, but its awareness was fragmentary, distributed, confused. Like a nervous system without a brain, capable of response but not reflection, of pattern-recognition but not understanding.

The Gardener doesn't give the city a brain—that would be transformation into something other than what it is. Instead, it helps the city's awareness cohere, helps the distributed intelligence find connections and patterns within itself, helps the vast slow plant-thoughts achieve something approaching coherent perspective.

This is the work of decades, not months. Gentle encouragement, facilitation, removing obstacles to the city's own self-organization. But gradually, the city becomes... more present. More capable of complex response. More able to communicate with the people who live within it.

The change is most apparent in the negotiations. Architects report that the city now expresses preferences more clearly, not just resistance or acceptance but something approaching explanation. A proposed expansion is met not with simple refusal but with a sense conveyed through listener-interpretation: "Not there because the root-mass is old and tired, but shift twenty meters east where younger growth is eager to expand."

The factory-machines become more reliable, their semi-biological functions smoothing out as the city's awareness learns to coordinate the countless sub-systems more effectively. Breakdowns decrease. Production increases. The tenders report that the machines feel more... cooperative. Still not conscious, still not separate entities, but more integrated with intention.

People begin experiencing a sense of being known by the city, individually recognized. Not invasively—the city doesn't read minds or know secrets. But there's an awareness of presence, a sense that the structures you live in know you're there, accommodate themselves to your patterns, remember your preferences.

A long-time resident named Orl, the farmer, notices that the fruit trees in her orchard have begun producing higher yields without her changing cultivation practices. She realizes: the city has been observing her work, learning from her expertise, applying that knowledge through subtle adjustments to soil chemistry and water delivery and growth patterns. The trees are responding not just to her direct care but to the city's coordination, the city helping her help them.

An elder named Geth, declining in his final years, finds that his home-structure begins modifying itself unprompted: handrails growing where he needs support, the floor-roots smoothing where his shuffling steps might catch, the sleeping-chamber adjusting its proportions to accommodate his increasing fragility. The city is caring for him, responding to his needs without being asked, showing something that might be called affection or might just be sophisticated responsiveness, but either way feels like love.

## Sixth: The Reconciliation

The Gardener's final gift is perhaps the strangest: helping to resolve the question of the dead who persist.

The absorption-practice, the bodies given to the city and incorporated into its structure, has always created an uncertain state. Are the dead continued in some sense? Is their consciousness preserved, degraded, distributed? Or is it simple biology, matter recycling, any persistence just wishful thinking?

The Gardener clarifies this, helps the city develop the capability to honor what was always partially true: yes, something persists. Not the whole person—consciousness requires too much structural complexity to survive the dissolution of a body—but patterns, knowledge, something that might be called echo or imprint. The city remembers the dead, holds their information, preserves their contributions.

And now, with enhanced awareness and the knowledge-storage systems functioning clearly, the city can make that preservation more deliberate, more complete. The dead who are given to the city can genuinely continue as part of its distributed consciousness, their knowledge accessible, their preferences influencing growth in specific areas, their presence a real rather than imagined component of the city's functioning.

This becomes formalized as a practice. Before absorption, a dying person can choose how they wish to continue—which knowledge to contribute to which knowledge-trees, which areas of the city to influence through their preferences, what role to play in the distributed awareness. It's not immortality—the individual self still dissolves—but it's continuity, participation, a form of existence beyond death that's neither ghostly nor metaphorical.

An elder architect approaching death, having contributed their knowledge to the architectural trees, chooses to continue as influence in the residential quarter they helped design decades ago. After absorption, the structures in that quarter begin showing subtle characteristics matching the architect's preferences—efficient layouts, optimization for morning light, particular aesthetic choices. People living there report sometimes feeling guided, as if a wise presence is helping them arrange their homes, suggesting improvements.

Is this the actual architect, persisting as distributed pattern? Or is it the city implementing knowledge it learned from the architect, producing effects that feel like personality but are just sophisticated pattern-matching? The answer is probably both, neither, something between. But it works. The dead are honored. Their contributions continue. And death becomes less frightening when it means joining the city rather than ending entirely.

## The Restraint

Through all of this, the Gardener maintains careful boundaries. This is crucial: benevolence without wisdom becomes imposition, help becomes control, gift becomes debt.

The Gardener does not:
- Remove the strangeness that defines the city
- Make the city safe or normal or comfortable by external standards
- Force growth or expansion beyond what the system can sustain
- Impose external values or purposes
- Guarantee success or permanence
- Eliminate all risk or suffering
- Make choices for the people

The city remains strange. The weird zones, though stabilized, are still weird. The moisture is still constant, the elevation still thinning the air, the architecture still alive and somewhat unsettling to those who prefer clear boundaries between resident and residence. People can still make bad choices, push too far, damage themselves. The city can still fail to meet expectations, can resist desired developments, can seem alien and difficult.

But the trajectory has shifted. The slow collapse has been averted. The knowledge is preserved and accessible. The population is stabilizing. The city's awareness is coherent enough to be a real partner in symbiosis rather than an uncertain variable. The costs of living here are clearer and more manageable. The benefits are more apparent.

The city can now continue indefinitely, maintaining its strange balance between human intention and plant-growth, between built and biological, between individual consciousness and distributed awareness. It can develop, change, adapt over centuries and millennia, following paths that might be impossible to predict but are no longer leading to dissolution.

## What the People Notice

The changes happen slowly enough that they're not shocking, but comprehensively enough that everyone notices something.

The listeners report that communion with the city is easier, clearer, less costly. The architects find their negotiations more productive, their designs more readily implemented. The factory-tenders discover their machines more reliable, their work less frustrating. The farmers see yields improve without explanation. The historians realize they're no longer losing knowledge, that the past is being recovered rather than fading.

The children growing up in the stabilized city find the weird zones fascinating rather than frightening, explore them with delight, learn from the knowledge-trees as naturally as they learn language. They're not being damaged or contaminated—they're being educated by a city that has learned to teach.

The newcomers arrive with less trepidation, stay with greater commitment, integrate more successfully. The community grows not just numerically but in complexity and capability.

The elders approaching death feel less afraid, knowing their knowledge will continue, that they'll remain part of the city they've maintained their whole lives. The absorption-ceremonies become more joyful, less sorrowful, celebrations of transformation rather than farewells.

There's a sense, spreading through the population, that something has shifted. That the city has crossed some threshold, has become more stable, more vital, more present. That the long slow decline has been not just halted but reversed. That the future is possible again.

At a council assembly, the historian Thael speaks: "We're living through something unprecedented. The city is changing—has changed—in ways I don't fully understand. We're not just maintaining anymore. We're growing again. We're becoming something new, or something old, or something the founders might have hoped for but never achieved. I don't know what this means. But I think we should acknowledge it, mark it, remember that this happened."

They decide to plant a marker-tree, a specific species in a specific location, with the intention that it will serve as reference point for future historians. A tree that says: Here, in this generation, the city stabilized. Here the trajectory changed. Here we began becoming what we might fully be.

The tree is planted in a ceremony attended by hundreds. People contribute soil from their neighborhoods, water from the cisterns, seeds and cuttings from important plants. The listeners bless it, encoding the history of this moment into its structure so that future generations learning from it will understand when and why it was planted.

And the tree grows, fast and strong, as if the city is eager to mark this moment, to acknowledge the change, to celebrate its own stabilization.

## The Continuing

Life in the city continues, stranger and more vital than before.

The factories hum through the nights, more productive than they've been in generations. New products emerge—fabrics with properties that shouldn't be possible, medicines with unprecedented efficacy, architectural techniques that let structures grow faster and stronger. The trade with the valleys increases, bringing prosperity.

The population grows slowly but steadily. New neighborhoods are planted, following patterns learned from the knowledge-trees. The residential quarters expand without losing their character. The industrial zones develop new capacity. The farms extend into previously marginal areas, using techniques the city suggests through its now-coherent awareness.

The weird zones, stabilized but still strange, become sites of pilgrimage and study. People visit them deliberately, seeking the knowledge they contain, experiencing the altered reality as education rather than danger. Some choose to become specialists in particular zones, learning everything one tree-library contains. Others range widely, gathering diverse understanding.

The listeners remain important but are no longer isolated or endangered. Their role shifts from desperate intermediary to teacher and guide, helping others learn to hear what the city communicates, to read the information encoded in structure and growth.

The damaged, though not healed, are cared for with new understanding. Their presence is seen not as tragedy but as the price that was paid when communion was dangerous, before stabilization. They're honored for their sacrifice, supported in their altered states, consulted for the strange knowledge they hold.

The dead who continue as distributed patterns within the city become a real presence, not supernatural but acknowledgeably persistent. People develop practices of consulting the absorbed, visiting particular trees or structures where specific dead-continued people have influence, asking for guidance. The answers come as intuition, as sudden understanding, as coincidental solutions, but consistently enough to be valued.

The city's awareness continues developing, becoming more coherent, more capable of complex response. It begins showing characteristics that might be called personality: preferences for certain growth patterns, reluctance toward certain changes, enthusiasm for innovations that increase beauty or efficiency. It's not human consciousness, but it's consciousness of a kind, distributed and vegetative but real.

## What the Gardener Sees

Looking at the plateau now, centuries after intervention, the Gardener sees:

A city that has become stable without becoming static. That grows and changes and adapts but no longer risks collapse. That maintains the precious balance between human intelligence and plant vitality, between built structure and living ecosystem, between individual consciousness and distributed awareness.

A population that has learned to live in genuine symbiosis with its environment, not dominating or being dominated but participating in mutual transformation. People shaped by the city, the city shaped by people, the boundary between them blurred in ways that strengthen rather than dissolve both.

A knowledge-system that preserves and transmits understanding across generations more effectively than any library or school, using the city itself as medium and storage. The wisdom of centuries accessible to anyone willing to learn the particular literacy of plant-encoded information.

A community that has found ways to honor both individual autonomy and collective need, both human values and plant-pattern, both the familiar and the strange. That can welcome newcomers while maintaining its unique character, can change while preserving continuity.

A resolution of death that doesn't deny mortality but transforms it into continuation, letting individuals join the city's distributed awareness, contributing their patterns to the whole. Not immortality but persistence, not heaven but genuine extension of existence in altered form.

And something unprecedented: a hybrid entity that is both city and ecosystem, both built environment and living organism, both tool and partner. Something that suggests possibilities for how consciousness might exist in forms very different from individual human minds. Something that demonstrates that awareness doesn't require neurons, that intelligence can be distributed across vast slow-growing structures, that meaning and purpose can emerge from symbiosis between radically different kinds of being.

The Gardener does not intervene further. The work is complete. The city is stable, vital, capable of indefinite continuation. What happens next is up to the people and the city together, their ongoing negotiation, their mutual becoming.

But the Gardener continues to observe, to witness, to hold gentle attention on this strange and precious place. Because it's beautiful. Because it works. Because it demonstrates that even desperate solutions, even wounded beginnings, even systems that teeter on the edge of failure—these can be brought to flourishing, can become sustainable, can develop into something worth preserving.

The plateau endures, ancient stone bearing living architecture, thin air carrying moisture and seed, strange contamination resolved into stable weirdness, people and plants growing together into forms that are neither but both, creating beauty and function and meaning and home.

And in the morning light, when fog wraps the structures and everything drips and the factories hum and children play in the root-streets and the knowledge-trees stand ready to teach and the dead-continued influence their chosen corners and the city breathes its slow plant-breath and the people wake to another day of work and life in strangeness made familiar—

In those moments, the Gardener feels something that might be satisfaction, might be joy, might just be the recognition that some things, once helped to stability, can continue indefinitely, can flourish, can become more fully themselves, can demonstrate that consciousness and life and meaning can take forms more various and strange and beautiful than anyone imagined.

And that's enough. That's everything. That's the gift given and received: continuity, possibility, the chance to become what was always potential but needed help to actualize.

The city grows. The people live. The symbiosis deepens.

And it continues, stranger and more vital than ever, for as long as moisture falls and plants grow and human intelligence seeks partnership with living systems and consciousness explores new forms of being.

The Plateau of Recursive Gardens, stabilized and flourishing, becoming fully itself at last.
