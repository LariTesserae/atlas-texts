# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power arrives as light first—not the harsh plateau sunlight but something gentler, more amber, that doesn't cast shadows so much as soften them. It moves across the undulating stone like the mist-walls do, but slower, more deliberate, as if the plateau itself is exhaling something it has held for eons.

It does not arrive with intention to *fix*. That is the first and most important thing. The power is wise enough to recognize that what exists here is not broken.

For three days and nights—though time moves strangely in its presence—it simply *listens*.

It listens to the wall of bird-sound and hears within it the individual threads: the cushion-nester announcing his territory boundary for the thousandth time this week, his voice growing hoarse. The mist-follower calling coordinates to her scattered flock. The nestling's desperate begging that will go unanswered because the parent was taken by a falcon an hour ago. The dawn-singer's brief beauty. The night-caller's repeated question to the darkness.

It listens to the silence between sounds—the space where a pika family used to whistle but no longer does, their hay pile abandoned, the weasel-hunter having been thorough. The absence of a particular beetle species in one sector, gone for reasons unclear, perhaps forever.

It listens to the plants, though plants speak in slower frequencies: the cushion-domes' patient accumulation, year by year, molecule by molecule. The wire-grass's high singing in the wind. The lichen's chemical conversations with stone, negotiations that have been ongoing since before the plateau forgot it was mountains.

It listens to the stone itself, to the deep groaning of geological time, the infinitesimal settling, the memory of weight that is no longer there, the ancient shape of things.

And it listens to the water's journey—up as mist, down through fissures, held briefly in moss, transpired into air, a cycle so perfectly closed it is almost a circle, but not quite, never quite, always spiraling.

After listening, it *sees*.

Not with eyes but with something more fundamental. It sees the tightness of the system, how every niche is filled, how the energy flows with minimal loss from sun to plant to insect to bird to predator and back to plant through death and decomposition. It sees the beauty of this efficiency and also its fragility—how the system's perfection makes it vulnerable to disruption, how there is no slack, no buffer, no room for error.

It sees the small tragedies: the cushion-nester pair who have nested in the same dome for seven years, but this year the weasel found them. The pika kit who ventured too far from cover and was taken by a falcon, and now its siblings must work harder to gather hay. The moth species that exists now in only three populations on the entire plateau, having contracted from dozens, moving toward an extinction that seems inevitable unless something changes, though what that something might be is unclear.

It sees the larger patterns: how climate is shifting, slowly, how the rain patterns are changing in ways subtle enough that no single generation notices but consequential enough that in fifty years, or five hundred, the cushion plants might begin to fail in certain sectors. How the birds' breeding cycles are calibrated to weather patterns that are becoming less reliable. How the system is resilient but not infinitely so.

It sees the absence of humans and understands both what was lost and what was gained in that departure. The knowledge that humans carried about this place—which plants could be eaten, where water could be found in drought, the safe paths across the stone—gone. But also the pressure of human presence removed, allowing the system to achieve a fullness it perhaps could not reach when sharing space with beings who altered more than they integrated.

It sees the carved posts in their slow decay and recognizes them as a question that was asked here long ago: *Does this place matter beyond itself? Is there meaning in height, in endurance, in the simple fact of life persisting in harshness?*

The power considers.

---

What it does is small. Almost nothing. Certainly nothing dramatic.

**First: It strengthens the water cycle, just slightly.**

Not by adding water—the plateau receives what the weather brings, and to change that would require altering systems far beyond this place. But it adjusts the stone's porosity in certain key locations, microscopic changes that allow water to pool a few hours longer in specific depressions. It encourages certain moss species, gives them the slightest advantage, and these mosses hold moisture better, release it more slowly.

The effect: The temporary pools last half a day longer after rains. The aquatic insects have slightly more time to complete their cycles. Their emergence is fractionally more successful. The birds feeding on these insects find them slightly more abundant. The cascade is subtle, but in a system this tight, subtle is enough.

Three moth species that were contracting find their larval host plants have access to slightly more consistent moisture. Their populations stabilize. In fifty years, they will still be here, where without this change they would have vanished.

**Second: It mends the genetic bottleneck in the cushion-nester populations.**

The birds have been breeding in isolation for so long that they are beginning to show signs of inbreeding depression: reduced clutch sizes, higher nestling mortality, susceptibility to disease. The power does not add new genes—that would be too much intervention—but it subtly increases the success rate of the rare dispersal events that already occur, when young birds fly farther than usual and join distant populations, when genetic exchange happens naturally but usually fails.

It does this by adjusting the birds' navigation slightly, by influencing which young birds feel the urge to disperse, by protecting a few of these dispersers from the predation they would normally face, just enough that genetic flow resumes at a sustainable rate.

The effect: The populations become slightly more robust. Disease resistance improves. Clutch sizes stabilize. The system's long-term viability improves without anyone noticing the change, because it happens through mechanisms that already exist—the power simply tips probabilities in favorable directions.

**Third: It places new cairns.**

This is the strangest intervention, the one that makes least sense from a purely ecological perspective, but the power is wise and knows that meaning matters, even—perhaps especially—in places where no one is present to perceive it.

In seven locations across the plateau, it builds cairns matching the style of the ancient ones: waist-high, mortarless, stones balanced with precision. It places them at sites that have significance in the plateau's geography: the highest point, the center of the largest cushion-plant formation, the place where three major water-channels converge, the pillar that is frequented by the most birds, the edge overlooking the deepest valley, the site where the most diverse lichen community grows, and the location of the decaying wooden posts.

The cairns say: *Someone was here. Someone noticed. This mattered.*

They provide new habitat—birds nest in their gaps, spiders string webs between their stones, lichens begin their centuries-long work of consuming them. But more than that, they provide continuity. They acknowledge the human presence that was here before and is gone now. They say that absence is not the same as forgetting.

If humans return—in a century, in a millennium—they will find these cairns and wonder. They will know that something was paying attention, that the plateau was not abandoned to mere natural process but held in some kind of regard. They might misinterpret this entirely, might build mythologies around it, and that is acceptable. Meaning can be useful even when misunderstood.

**Fourth: It witnesses the weasel-hunter.**

There is one weasel, an old female, who has lost most of her teeth and can no longer hunt effectively. She is starving slowly in a crevice beneath a large boulder, her body consuming itself. In the normal course of events, she would die here, and the beetles would process her, and that would be that.

The power finds her and sits with her—metaphorically speaking, as it has no body to sit with. It does not heal her teeth. It does not extend her life. Her time is over, and that is appropriate. But it ensures that her death is not lonely, which is a strange concern for a power to have, perhaps, but it is a *benevolent* power, and benevolence sometimes manifests in attention to suffering, even when that suffering cannot be prevented.

When she dies, the power ensures that her body is found quickly by the beetles, that the decomposition is efficient, that her nutrients return to the system cleanly. It is a small dignity, but dignity matters.

**Fifth: It adjusts the falcons' hunting pressure, microscopically.**

The plateau falcons are taking cushion-nesters at a rate slightly higher than optimal—not enough to collapse the prey population, but enough to stress it, to keep it from reaching its full potential abundance. This is natural, but natural is not always optimal.

The power makes the falcons fractionally more successful at hunting pikas and the larger insects. Not enough to deplete those populations, but enough to shift the predation pressure slightly away from the smallest birds. The falcons remain well-fed—perhaps better fed than before—but the cushion-nesters gain a small margin of relief.

The effect: Breeding success improves. The sound-wall grows imperceptibly louder over the next several years as populations rise slightly. The system finds a new equilibrium at a marginally higher energy state, more life per hectare, but still stable, still sustainable.

**Sixth: It does nothing about the climate shift.**

This is perhaps the most important intervention: the deliberate choice not to intervene. The weather patterns are changing, the rain is becoming less reliable, and in time—decades, centuries—the plateau will change as a result. Some species will decline. Others will arrive. The cushion plants might retreat from certain areas, or evolve different forms, or be replaced by other plants better suited to the new conditions.

The power allows this. It understands that change is not damage. The plateau has survived every change that has come to it over millions of years—it was once mountains, then eroded to this form, and in the future it will be something else. The species living here now are not permanent residents but temporary occupants, holding space until the next wave of evolution and adaptation brings replacements.

To freeze the plateau in its current state would be cruelty disguised as preservation. It would rob the place of its fundamental nature, which is to endure through transformation.

So the power ensures only that the rate of change remains survivable, that it happens slowly enough for adaptation to occur, for species to shift their ranges, for the system to bend rather than break. But it does not prevent the bending.

**Seventh: It leaves a gift for the dawn-singers.**

This is pure indulgence, perhaps, but the power is moved by their brief morning beauty, by the fact that they alone among all the plateau's birds seem to sing for reasons beyond pure communication.

It does not change them, but it changes the acoustics of certain stone pillars, subtly, so that when a dawn-singer perches there and sings, the stone resonates, amplifies, adds harmonics. The song becomes richer, carries farther, transforms into something that approaches music in the way humans would understand it.

The dawn-singers will find these pillars and favor them, and over generations they will develop more complex songs because the acoustic feedback encourages it. They will become more beautiful, and beauty has no survival value necessarily, but it has value nonetheless, and the power is benevolent enough to care about that.

**Eighth: It speaks to the pikas.**

Not in words—they have no language for words—but in something deeper. It gives them a sense, a feeling, a knowledge that exists beneath consciousness: *You are seen. You matter. Your hay-gathering and your whistling and your family groups have value beyond mere survival. You are part of something larger, and that something acknowledges you.*

The pikas cannot articulate this knowledge. They simply continue their lives as before. But perhaps they are fractionally less anxious, perhaps they spend a few more moments each day in play, perhaps their warning whistles carry a note that wasn't there before, something that might be called joy, if pikas can feel joy.

The effect is immeasurable but real. The plateau becomes, in some indefinable way, a less harsh place.

**Ninth: It reads the carved posts.**

They are too weathered to read, the patterns eroded to suggestions, but the power can see deeper than surface, can read what was there through the grain of the wood, the patterns of decay, the memory held in the cellular structure.

They were maps. Not of space but of time. They marked the progression of seasons as they manifested on the plateau, the cycling of rains and dry periods, the arrival and departure of certain birds (which birds? species that no longer exist here, or perhaps exist but have changed beyond recognition). They were a calendar system, sophisticated and precise, built by people who lived here long enough to notice patterns that took years to complete.

The power understands what it reads and is humbled by it. The humans who made these were not primitives passing through. They were observers as patient and careful as the power itself, watching the plateau until they understood its rhythms, encoding that understanding in wood.

The power cannot restore the posts—they are too far gone—but it ensures that their wood, as it finally decays completely over the next century, enriches the soil in ways that benefit the plants growing near them. The knowledge they contained will be physically incorporated into the plateau, atoms that were once part of meaning becoming atoms that support life. This seems appropriate.

**Tenth: It waits.**

This is the final intervention, and perhaps the most important. The power establishes a very gentle, very patient watching presence—not intrusive, not controlling, just *there*. It will remain at the plateau's edge, in the space between being and not-being, for as long as necessary. Millennia, if needed.

If the system begins to fail in ways that indicate not natural change but catastrophic collapse—if a disease emerges, if an invasive species arrives, if something happens that threatens not just individual species but the plateau's fundamental ability to support life—the power will notice and will consider intervention.

But only then. Only if necessary. The threshold for "necessary" is set very high.

Otherwise, it simply watches. And in watching, it ensures that the plateau is not alone. That the sound-wall of birds and the patient growth of cushion plants and the hunting of falcons and the hay-gathering of pikas and all of it, all the tremendous ongoing life of this place, is *witnessed*.

---

In the years that follow, a few things happen that might or might not be related to the power's presence:

A hermit *does* come to live on the plateau, building a small stone shelter near one of the new cairns. She stays for three years, living on pika meat and birds' eggs, and she writes in a journal about the sound, trying to transcribe it, to understand its structure. She eventually leaves—the isolation is too complete, the noise too overwhelming—but she carries the plateau with her, and she tells stories about it, and some of those stories make their way into poetry, and the poetry preserves something of the place for people who will never visit it.

The wire-grass evolves a slightly different growth pattern, spreading more successfully into areas where cushion plants are thinning. This happens naturally, but the timing is fortunate, and the result is that as the climate shifts, the grassland areas expand to fill the space that might otherwise have become bare stone. The ecosystem rebalances around a new configuration, different but equally full.

One of the mist-follower birds develops an unusual behavior: she begins following humans when they appear on the plateau, flying alongside them at a distance, calling softly. Other mist-followers learn this behavior from her. Eventually, the species becomes known to occasional visitors as "the guide-birds," though they guide no one anywhere, they simply accompany. But the name sticks, and the birds become a symbol of the plateau in human stories, represented in carvings and paintings, standing for the concept of *presence in vastness*, of *small beauty in harsh places*.

The moth species that was saved from extinction becomes, over centuries, the plateau's most efficient pollinator for several rare flowering plants. Those plants, in turn, support a species of butterfly that arrives from lower elevations as the climate warms. The butterfly is iridescent blue, shocking against the browns and greens of the plateau, and its presence becomes another story, another symbol.

The cairns become pilgrimage sites, though no one knows who built them or when. Visitors add stones to them, a tradition that emerges spontaneously and persists. Over centuries, the cairns grow larger, incorporate different stone types as people carry rocks from other places, become layered records of visitation, of human desire to mark and be marked by this place.

The dawn-singers' enhanced songs are eventually recorded by a researcher with audio equipment. The recordings are studied, analyzed, found to contain mathematical patterns that seem too complex to be accidental. Papers are written. Debates occur. No consensus is reached, but the birds become famous in their small way, and funding is allocated to protect the plateau, not from any specific threat but simply as acknowledgment that places like this should continue to exist.

The power watches all of this with something that might be satisfaction, if such a being experiences satisfaction. It has changed very little. The plateau continues largely as it would have without intervention. But the small adjustments have propagated through the system in beneficial ways, and the witnessing has mattered in ways that are hard to quantify but real nonetheless.

The birds still fill the air with their sound. The plants still grow at their glacial pace. The predators still hunt. The plateau still endures, ancient and alive, full beyond measure, ongoing.

But now it is also *known*, in some deeper sense. The universe is paying attention. The life here matters not just to itself but to something beyond itself.

And perhaps that is what benevolence looks like in a place like this: not rescue, not transformation, but attention. Recognition. The simple act of bearing witness and, when necessary and only when necessary, offering the gentlest possible assistance to life that is already so vigorously, so beautifully, living itself.

The plateau continues. The power watches. Both are content.
