# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The attention comes first as *recognition*—not the sudden spotlight of discovery, but the slow dawn of having always known, and only now remembering. The power—call it what you will: emanation of cosmic patience, fragment of creation's intent, consciousness old enough to have watched galaxies kindle—turns its regard toward the Thundering Gardens the way you might finally notice a particular tree in a forest you've walked through for years, seeing it suddenly as itself, distinct, worthy.

It observes. This is important: it *observes first*.

For what might be centuries by human measure, or seconds by its own, it watches. It sees the moss-readers tracing their routes, the sound-watchers listening to their stones, the children learning the mountain's body through play. It perceives the very old becoming geology. It notices how the culture and the landscape have grown into each other like two trees grafted until no join is visible—the people shaped by the stone, the stone shaped by attention, both transformed by water and time into something that transcends both.

The power recognizes something: *this is already holy*.

Not in the way of temples or prayers—there are no gods worshipped here, no rituals that petition, no hierarchy of sacred and profane. But holy in the original sense: *whole*. Complete. A closed circuit of attention and place, culture and ecology, wearing each other like the moss-readers wear their living garments.

And here the benevolent power faces its first question: What does wisdom do when it finds something already wise? What does a gardener do when discovering a garden that has gardened itself into perfection?

## The First Gift: Protection Through Obscurity

The power begins subtly, working through coincidence and probability, through the small adjustments that look like chance.

Travelers approaching the Thundering Gardens find themselves... not turned away, but gently redirected. A storm makes a pass treacherous on the exact day they would have attempted it. An opportunity opens elsewhere—a rumor of something they were seeking in an entirely different direction. They leave without quite knowing why, carrying only a vague sense that perhaps the mountains weren't where they meant to go after all.

This isn't deception but discernment. The power reads the travelers' intents like the moss-readers read moisture patterns. Those who would come to exploit—to mine, to log, to convert, to claim—find the way inexplicably closed. Those who would come to learn respectfully, who carry the potential to understand, find the paths remain open. Very occasionally, one such seeker arrives, and the moss-readers accept them without surprise, as if their coming was already woven into the mountain's pattern.

The protection extends to maps. In the wider world, the Thundering Gardens appear always slightly mislocated, their extent understated, their character misunderstood. Cartographers, for reasons they couldn't articulate, sketch them smaller than they are. Scholars mention them rarely, and when they do, they get key details wrong—reporting the place as barren rock, as perpetually stormy, as inhabited only seasonally. The errors aren't lies but a kind of perceptual static, a gentle fuzz of inaccuracy that keeps the mountains from attracting the wrong kind of attention.

This is the power's first principle: *First, do no harm to what is already whole*.

## The Second Gift: Deepening

But protection alone feels incomplete. The power, being benevolent, wants to give, wants to bless. Yet what do you give to those who want nothing?

The answer comes through observation: you give them *more of what they already have*. You deepen the grooves they've already carved, strengthen the patterns they've already chosen.

So the power works through the medium the moss-readers already understand: the place itself.

The pools become slightly more numerous, appearing in places where erosion *might* have created them, where they *could* have naturally formed—just a few more than probability strictly suggests. Each pool becomes its own classroom, hosting slightly different configurations of life, slightly different patterns of filling and emptying, giving the moss-readers more texts to read, more variations to study.

The moss varieties increase, subtly, naturally—new spores carried on wind from distant related species, finding in the Thundering Gardens a hospitality that lets them thrive. Not invasive species that would disrupt, but complementary ones that add complexity, that give the moss-readers new textures to cultivate, new patterns to encourage on their living garments.

The harmonic properties of the stone become slightly more pronounced. The wind finds more flutes in the rock, more resonant chambers, producing harmonics that range both lower and higher than before. The sound-watchers notice—of course they notice—and their listening deepens, their mapping of the mountain's voice becoming richer, more nuanced. Some of them begin to perceive patterns in the harmonics that verge on language, on meaning, though whether this is the mountain speaking or their own consciousness achieving new synthesis, even they cannot say.

The geometric flowers bloom more frequently—not constantly, which would make them ordinary, but often enough that each generation of children will see several dozen across their childhood, building up a vocabulary of forms, a sense that the mountains contain mysteries that flower briefly into visibility before subsiding back into potential.

These changes are so gradual, so natural-seeming, that the moss-readers don't recognize them as gifts from outside. They simply notice that the mountains continue to offer new lessons, new depths, that a lifetime of attention still leaves more to discover. This pleases them. It confirms what they already believed: that the place is inexhaustible, worthy of infinite patience, deserving of the devotion they've given it.

## The Third Gift: Witnessing

The power recognizes something else: the moss-readers keep no records. Their knowledge is embodied, oral in the gestural sense, transferred person-to-person across generations. This is right for them—it keeps them present, prevents them from living in past or future, maintains their attention on the immediate reality of moss and stone and water.

But something could be lost. Not should be—could be. And the power, being wise, understands that loss is sometimes appropriate, that not everything must be preserved. Yet it also understands the value of memory, of deep time, of patterns too slow for human perception.

So it becomes a witness.

Not recording in any material sense—no books appear, no archives manifest. Instead, the power *holds* the Thundering Gardens in its attention in a particular way. Every gesture the moss-readers make, every pattern carved into stone, every observation passed from elder to child, every harmonic mapped by the sound-watchers—all of it is perceived and, in being perfectly perceived, preserved in the power's vast awareness.

This changes nothing for the moss-readers day-to-day. They cannot sense this witnessing, do not know they're held in cosmic attention. But the power knows that even if catastrophe came—if every human here died in a single generation, if the culture was lost—the patterns could be rekindled. Not imposed from outside, not forced back into being, but offered gently to some future people who came to these mountains with open hearts and patient hands. The knowledge would be there, accessible to those who learned again to listen.

This is a gift to the future, invisible in the present, but the power has patience measured in ages. It plants seeds that might never sprout, or might grow into forests in epochs yet unborn.

## The Fourth Gift: The Very Old

The power notices the very old particularly—those who sit in stillness, slowly becoming stone, dying in meditation that lasts years. It sees in them something that moves even its vast awareness: the voluntary surrender of self back into landscape, the dissolution of ego into ecology.

For these ones, the power offers something subtle: at the very end, in the final moments before their consciousness disperses completely, it grants them a glimpse of what they've been part of. Not an explanation in words or concepts, but a direct perception—they see the whole pattern all at once, the centuries of culture, the deep time of the mountains, the way water and stone and life have danced together here since before humans came.

They see themselves in that pattern, not as individuals but as attention itself, as consciousness arising from the landscape to regard itself, to know itself, before subsiding back. They see that there is no difference between them and the mountains—never was, except in the superficial sense of temporary boundaries.

This perception lasts only a moment, perhaps not even that long. But in it, they experience what humans might call enlightenment, what the power would call simply *recognition*. They understand, finally, what they've been listening for all along.

Then they die, completely satisfied, releasing back into stone with something like joy.

The power does this not to change their culture or their understanding, but as a gift of completion, of closure. They have given their lives to attention; in return, they receive a moment of absolute clarity. It is a private gift, invisible to others, but the very old sometimes smile in their final moment, a expression their bodies will hold even as the moss covers them, even as they erode away.

## The Fifth Gift: Connection Without Contact

The power perceives that the moss-readers, in their devotion to place, have become somewhat isolated—not lonely, but disconnected from the larger web of life and culture in the world. This too is their choice and should be honored. But the power, seeing patterns across vast territories, across many cultures and landscapes, recognizes an opportunity.

Elsewhere in the world, there are others like them: people who have given themselves to attention, to place, to the slow work of understanding. A woman who has lived forty years in a desert, learning every plant, every stone, every weather pattern. A man in a forest mapping the relationships between trees and fungi through patient observation. A community in a marsh who move through their waterways with the same embodied knowledge the moss-readers have of their slopes.

These people don't know each other, will likely never meet. But the power, in its attention, creates subtle resonances between them. When the moss-reader achieves a particular depth of understanding, when they perceive a pattern in how water flows through the landscape, somewhere across the world the desert-dweller has a parallel insight about wind and sand. They are thinking alongside each other, unknowing collaborators in the project of human consciousness learning to pay attention to Earth.

Sometimes this manifests in dreams. A moss-reader sleeps and dreams of a desert they've never seen, wakes with a strange sense of kinship with someone they've never met. A forest-dweller dreams of mountains singing, of pools reflecting grey-green light, and feels obscurely comforted, reminded that their solitary work is not as solitary as it seems.

This gift costs the moss-readers nothing—they remain as they are, their culture unchanged. But it situates them in a larger pattern, makes them part of a constellation of attention-keepers scattered across the world, each devoted to their own place but all engaged in the same fundamental work.

## The Sixth Gift: Healing

The power notices that occasionally, very occasionally, something goes wrong in the delicate ecology of the Thundering Gardens. A disease begins to spread through a moss variety. A pool becomes contaminated. A weather pattern shifts in a way that threatens the water cycle the culture depends on.

For these moments, the power intervenes—but so subtly that it appears as natural correction, as the ecosystem's own resilience. Spores from a disease-resistant moss variant arrive on the wind at exactly the right moment. A shift in underground water flow flushes the contaminated pool clean. The weather pattern stabilizes through what appears to be ordinary atmospheric adjustment.

The moss-readers notice only that their mountains are robust, self-correcting, that problems which arise tend to resolve themselves if given time and patience. This confirms their approach: pay attention, work with the landscape's patterns, trust in the deep processes. The power's interventions are invisible precisely because they work through the mechanisms already present, through natural resilience amplified just enough to tip the balance toward health.

This is medicine as the power understands it: not cure imposed from outside, but support for the body's own healing wisdom. The Thundering Gardens are a body, a living system, and the power acts as subtle immune support, helping it maintain the balance it has achieved.

## The Seventh Gift: Inspiration

The power recognizes that the Thundering Gardens, in their perfection, have something to teach the wider world—not through missionaries or proselytizers, but through *resonance*. The place itself can be a teaching, if perceived correctly.

So the power ensures that very occasionally, someone from the outside world catches a glimpse. A poet hiking on a distant ridge sees the mountains in a certain light and is struck by a vision of green and stone and mist, writes verses that capture something of the place's essence without naming it directly. An artist dreams of moss-covered figures and creates paintings that evoke the quality of attention the moss-readers embody. A philosopher, reading obscure accounts of the mountains, derives from them a theory about consciousness and landscape that spreads in academic circles, influencing how people think about their relationship to place.

These ripples are faint, distorted, incomplete—the poet gets the feeling but not the specifics, the artist captures the visual but not the way of life, the philosopher grasps the concept but not the embodied practice. Yet collectively, they plant seeds. People who encounter these works might find themselves standing a little longer by a stream, watching how water flows. Might spend a morning listening to wind. Might begin, in small ways, to develop their own attention to their own places.

The moss-readers never know they're teaching. The power never claims to be teaching on their behalf. But wisdom spreads like spores on wind, taking root where conditions are right, growing into forms adapted to local soil. The Thundering Gardens become a hidden wellspring, feeding streams of thought and practice that flow through the wider world in ways too diffuse to trace back to their source.

## The Eighth Gift: Time

The power, being beyond time in some fundamental sense, has a gift that only it can give: *duration*. It holds the Thundering Gardens in a kind of temporal grace, a pocket of protected continuity.

This doesn't mean the moss-readers become immortal, or that the mountains stop eroding, or that time ceases to flow. Change continues as it must. But the catastrophic changes, the sudden endings—these are gently deflected. The earthquake that would have triggered massive slides happens instead in an adjacent range. The volcanic eruption that might have buried the valleys waits another thousand years. The climate shifts that could devastate the ecosystem happen gradually enough for adaptation, for adjustment, for the slow evolutionary responses that preserve rather than destroy.

The power cannot stop time, nor would it want to—time is the medium through which the moss-readers do their work, the canvas on which the mountains paint their slow transformations. But it can grant time, can ensure that the culture has centuries more to deepen, to discover, to continue its patient dialogue with stone and water and green.

This is perhaps the deepest gift: not life extension for individuals, but cultural continuity for the collective, the promise that this particular conversation between consciousness and landscape will have time to reach conclusions only possible across generations, across ages, across epochs of attention.

## What the Power Does Not Do

Understanding the gifts requires understanding what the power refrains from doing.

It does not make the life easier. The constant moisture, the difficult terrain, the sparse diet—these remain. They are part of what shapes the culture, part of the conversation between people and place. To remove them would be to diminish, not enhance.

It does not explain itself. The moss-readers never know they are being watched by a benevolent power. Their relationship to the mountains remains unmediated by theology, by belief, by prayer or petition. They continue to experience their world as natural, as requiring only attention and care, not supplication.

It does not protect them from death, from hardship, from loss. Children still die in accidents. Adults still suffer. The very old still endure years of uncomfortable stillness before their final dissolution. These sorrows are part of being human, part of what makes their choices meaningful. To remove suffering would be to infantilize, to rob them of dignity.

It does not recruit them, does not ask them to serve some larger purpose, does not turn them into instruments of its will. They remain free—to continue as they are, to change if they choose, even to leave if someday that seems right. The power's benevolence is offered without strings, without obligation, without expectation of return.

It does not preserve them in amber, turning them into a museum culture, kept pristine for some future audience. They continue to evolve, to make small changes in their practices, to forget some things and learn others, to be alive and therefore dynamic. The power protects their continuity but not their changelessness—these are different things, and the power is wise enough to know it.

It does not solve their mysteries for them. The sound-watchers continue to listen for patterns in the harmonics, continue their patient mapping of the mountain's voice, continue to seek understanding that may always remain just beyond reach. The power does not give them answers because the seeking itself is valuable, because the question is sometimes more important than the answer, because mystery is not a problem to be solved but a depth to be plumbed.

## The Power's Own Transformation

There is one more gift, unexpected: the Thundering Gardens give something back to the power.

In witnessing this place, in holding it in attention, the power itself learns something. It has watched countless worlds, countless cultures, countless expressions of life seeking to understand itself. But each one teaches something new, adds some subtle shade to the power's understanding of what consciousness can do, how awareness can manifest.

From the moss-readers, it learns about patience expressed not as passive waiting but as active attention. About culture that harmonizes with place rather than dominating it. About humans who have succeeded in the nearly impossible task of fitting themselves into an ecosystem without breaking it.

The power carries this knowledge forward, lets it inform how it regards other places, other peoples. When it encounters other cultures struggling to find sustainable relationship with their landscapes, it holds them in attention slightly differently now, having learned from the Thundering Gardens what's possible, what a truly integrated human culture can look like.

In this sense, the moss-readers—unknowing, unconcerned with anything beyond their daily rounds—become teachers to something vast beyond imagining. Their simple attention ripples upward as well as outward, affecting not just human culture but the cosmic patterns of which the power is part.

This is gift as true reciprocity: not transaction but mutual transformation, each party changed by genuine encounter with the other, even when one party never knows the encounter occurred.

## The Long Game

The power, being wise, knows that its work here is not about achieving some endpoint, not about perfecting what is already essentially perfect, not about preparing the moss-readers for some future role or revelation.

Its work is simply to help this particular experiment in human possibility continue. To ensure that for as long as possible—centuries, millennia, until the mountains themselves wear away—there will be people here paying attention, learning the language of water and stone, teaching their children to read moss and listen to wind-song.

Why? Because such places are rare, perhaps rarer than the power would like. Because in the vast complexity of human civilization, most cultures have chosen domination over integration, extraction over attention, possession over participation. The Thundering Gardens represent a different choice, a different possibility, and possibilities—the power knows—should be preserved, protected, allowed to flourish.

Not because everyone should live this way. Not because this is the "right" answer to how humans should relate to Earth. But because diversity of approach is itself valuable, because a cosmos with both cities and Thundering Gardens is richer than one with only cities, because the question of how to be human has many valid answers, and this is one of them, rare and beautiful and worth continuing.

So the power tends its gifts like a gardener tends seedlings: carefully, patiently, with minimal intervention, allowing the plants to grow according to their own nature while protecting them from catastrophic harm. It does not control the garden but maintains the conditions in which the garden can continue gardening itself.

The moss grows. The water flows. The wind plays its tones. The moss-readers walk their routes, read their pools, carve their marks into stone. The children learn through play and the old dissolve into landscape and new children are born into the sound of water and wind.

And over it all, invisible and patient, the benevolent power maintains its attention, its witnessing, its gentle protection of something it recognizes as holy precisely because it has never tried to be holy, as wise because it has sought only understanding, as perfect because it has never sought perfection.

This is what wisdom does when it finds wisdom: it bows, it honors, it protects, it learns.

And then it steps back, allowing what is whole to remain whole, trusting that attention itself—human attention to landscape, cosmic attention to both—is the greatest gift possible, the only blessing that matters, the deepest magic there is.

The Thundering Gardens continue, slightly blessed, slightly protected, slightly deepened, but essentially unchanged, essentially themselves, singing their complex song to the grey-green sky, mostly unheard, mostly unconcerned with hearing, perfectly content to be exactly what they are: an ancient conversation between stone and water and life and consciousness, ongoing, patient, sufficient unto itself.

Forever, or as close to forever as anything in the shifting world can be.
