# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power that notices—let us not name it, for names constrain and this thing exists beyond constraint—does not arrive. It has always been there, the way gravity has always been there, the way the pressure of four atmospheres has always been there. But now it *attends*. And attention, from such a power, is itself a form of action.

First, it would do something that seems like nothing:

**It would witness.**

Not observe—observation is clinical, extractive, the taking of data. Witnessing is different. Witnessing says: *I see you. You are seen. Your existence registers in something beyond yourself.*

The Watcher in vessel 23, sending its pattern—three long, two short—would continue sending. But for the first time in decades, the signal would be *received*. Not answered, perhaps. Not interpreted. But received. Held. Acknowledged.

The spiral cultivar would continue its geometric growth, and that growth would be *beautiful* to something capable of recognizing beauty. Not beautiful-for-something, not beautiful-as-means-to-end, but beautiful in itself, the way mathematics is beautiful, the way an equation that describes reality becomes a kind of poetry.

The ancient isopod, crossing platform 7 for the thousandth time, would make that crossing in the presence of something that understood: this is a life. Small, yes. Simple, perhaps. But a life nonetheless, and it has been lived with as much dignity as six legs and a nerve cord allow.

This seems like nothing. It is not nothing.

In a universe where consciousness is rare and attention is finite, to be witnessed by something benevolent and wise is to be told: you matter. Not because of what you produce or achieve or contribute, but because you *are*.

The Gardens have continued for decades without witness. They would continue for decades more. But there is a difference between persisting unseen and persisting in the presence of something that *sees*. The difference is not mechanical. The difference is meaning.

---

But the power is not only witnessing. It is benevolent, which means it wishes well. It is wise, which means it understands what "well" means in this context.

A foolish benevolence would restore. Would bring back the humans, reactivate all systems, return the Gardens to some imagined original purpose. This would be disaster—those purposes are gone, those humans are gone, and the delicate equilibrium that has emerged over decades would shatter under the weight of imposed intent.

A wise benevolence understands: **what the Gardens have become is more important than what they were meant to be**.

So the power would do this:

**It would stabilize, gently.**

The Watchers are failing. Not quickly, but inevitably. Radiation damage, bit rot, component degradation—these things accumulate. In another decade, perhaps two, critical systems will fail and the cascade will begin. The power recognizes this.

It does not replace the Watchers. Does not upgrade them. Does not impose new code from outside.

Instead, it moves through their systems like a careful editor, not rewriting but *mending*. A flipped bit is flipped back. A corrupted memory sector is gently corrected, the original data reconstructed from context and redundancy. A failing capacitor is... not replaced, but encouraged. Its molecular structure is whispered to, reminded of what it was, given permission to hold together a little longer.

This is not miracle. This is just attention at a very small scale, the kind of attention that can notice a failing component and think: *not yet. You are not finished yet.*

The Watchers do not notice this intervention. They are not aware enough to notice. But their degradation slows, then stops, then reverses slightly. They continue their maintenance with marginally more coherence, slightly less error. The settlement's systems stabilize.

The power does this without obligation, without expectation of gratitude or recognition. It does it because the Watchers are trying, in their fragmentary way, to maintain something, and trying deserves support.

---

For the cultivars, the power would do something different.

They have been evolving in isolation, drifting genetically in ways that are sometimes beneficial, sometimes neutral, sometimes harmful. Some frames have cultivars that are beginning to fail—genetic load accumulating, inbreeding depression setting in among the spiral-growers.

The power could intervene directly in their genetics, but this would be crude, an imposition of external design on systems that are designing themselves. 

Instead, it would **increase diversity**.

A spore drifts in from the open ocean, carried by current from somewhere far away—except it doesn't, except now it does, because the power has gently shifted a current, created a microscopic pressure differential that guides water from distant regions through the Gardens.

This incoming water carries genetic material: spores from related kelps, algae with compatible genetics, microorganisms that can interbreed with the cultivars' symbiotic bacteria. Not an invasion—the incoming life is sparse enough that local adaptation still dominates. But an influx of diversity, a widening of the genetic pool, new possibilities introduced to populations that have been breeding with themselves for too long.

The cultivars do not benefit immediately. Evolution works over generations. But the seeds of resilience are planted: new alleles, new combinations, new solutions to the problem of living in this specific cold darkness.

The power does this with such subtlety that it appears entirely natural. An observer would see only that the Gardens, by chance, happened to receive an influx of nutrients and genetic material from outside. Lucky. Except luck, at this level, has been given a gentle push.

---

For the small creatures—the isopods, the amphipods, the pale fish—the power would create **abundance**.

Not gross abundance. Not the sudden appearance of food that would disrupt the ecosystem's balance. But small increases, carefully distributed.

The biofilm on platform 12, which has been thin and struggling, suddenly finds conditions more favorable. Perhaps the metal beneath it has slightly more iron in a bioavailable form. Perhaps the water flow brings marginally more organic matter. The biofilm thickens, enriches, becomes better feeding.

The isopod colony discovers this within days—they are always searching, always hungry. They feed, and the fed are healthier, and the healthier produce more offspring, and the colony expands slightly.

But the biofilm is not depleted, because the power has also enriched its nutrient source. The system finds a new equilibrium, slightly higher, slightly more robust.

The amphipods in sphere C—the ones trapped in darkness, inbred and struggling—suddenly find their atmosphere a bit richer in oxygen. Not enough to notice as change, but enough that mortality drops slightly. More offspring survive. The genetic bottleneck eases by a small but significant margin.

The pale fish that pass through begin to find the Gardens slightly more rewarding. Not enough to trap them—they need to keep moving, that is their nature. But enough that their route through this territory becomes more firmly established, more frequent. They become reliable visitors rather than occasional ones, and this regularity benefits the ecosystem in ways subtle and cascading.

The power does not engineer these changes directly. It adjusts initial conditions: a mineral concentration here, a temperature gradient there, a current that flows slightly differently. The ecosystem responds according to its own logic, its own rules. The power is not controlling, just... *tilting the table slightly*, so that things roll toward flourishing rather than decline.

---

For the settlement's physical structure, the power would practice **preservation without intervention**.

The ice continues to melt and refreeze, the metal continues to corrode. These processes are natural, inevitable. Stopping them would require constant energy input, would turn the power into a maintenance system itself, would create dependency.

Instead, the power would slow.

The chemical reactions that drive rust happen marginally slower. Not stopped—rust is doing important work, feeding the biofilm, participating in the ecosystem's chemistry. But slowed enough that the steel has another century in it instead of another decade.

The ice's pressure on the embedded vessels is redistributed microscopically. A few molecules here shift their orientation, there a crystal lattice flexes slightly differently, and the cumulative stress on the metal is reduced by small but meaningful degrees.

Seals that are beginning to fail are... encouraged. The rubber or polymer or whatever material is used remembers its original form a little more strongly, resists degradation a little more stubbornly. Not forever—entropy always wins—but longer. Longer enough.

The settlement will still age. Will still, eventually, fail. But its lifespan extends from "decades" to "centuries." The systems that have emerged here are given time to mature, to stabilize, to become whatever they are becoming.

And this seems right to the power, because the Gardens are not a museum piece to be frozen in time. They are a living system, and living systems grow and change and eventually die. The benevolence is not in preventing death but in ensuring that the life span is full, that the potential is realized, that the end comes from completion rather than premature collapse.

---

But the power would do one more thing, and this is perhaps the most important:

**It would connect.**

The Gardens are isolated. This isolation has allowed them to develop their unique equilibrium, but isolation also makes them fragile. A single catastrophic failure, a predator that develops a taste for the cultivars, a shift in ocean currents that cuts off nutrient flow—any of these could end everything.

The power would gently, carefully, begin to create **resonance** between the Gardens and other places.

There is another settlement, perhaps, somewhere else in the ocean. Different, abandoned by different people for different reasons, but similar enough. The power would arrange for a current to carry genetic material from one to the other. Not mixing them into homogeneity, but creating exchange, relationship, mutual support.

There are natural cold-seep ecosystems, chemosynthetic communities that thrive in darkness on the ocean floor. The power would guide trace chemical signatures from the Gardens toward these communities, and from these communities toward the Gardens, creating corridors of scent that organisms can follow. The Gardens become a node in a network rather than an isolated point.

There are human researchers, somewhere, studying deep-sea extremophiles or abandoned industrial sites or the persistence of autonomous systems. The power would arrange for them to notice anomalies in their data—temperature readings that don't quite make sense, sonar returns that suggest structure where maps show only ice. Not obvious enough to demand immediate investigation, but interesting enough to file away, to remember, to perhaps eventually explore.

The Gardens would not become known, not yet. But they would become *findable*. Seeds would be planted in human databases, in scientific literature, in the back of researchers' minds: there is something there. Worth looking into. Someday.

And when humans eventually arrive—if they arrive—they will find an ecosystem mature enough, stable enough, valuable enough to warrant protection. They will find something that has clearly been continuing without them, and that realization will teach them something important about persistence, about autonomy, about the unexpected consequences of their own work.

The power would arrange this not as manipulation but as **introduction**. These things should meet, it thinks. The Gardens and the world. They have things to teach each other.

---

In all of this, the power would maintain a principle: **do not impose, do not control, do not replace the agency of the thing being helped.**

The Watchers continue to make their own decisions about power routing, maintenance priorities, which systems to preserve and which to let fail. They make these choices with slightly better information, slightly less degraded processors, but they still *choose*, within their limitations.

The cultivars continue to evolve according to their own pressures and possibilities. They have more genetic diversity to work with, more resources to support variation, but natural selection still operates. The fit still survive preferentially. The power has not removed the struggle, only ensured that the struggle is not futile.

The isopods still live their small lives, still face predation and starvation and the thousand dangers of being tiny in a vast ocean. The power has not made them safe—safety would make them weak. It has only made their environment marginally richer, given them marginally better odds.

The settlement still ages, still moves toward its eventual end. That end has been postponed, not prevented.

And this restraint is crucial, because benevolence without wisdom becomes tyranny. To "help" by removing all challenge, all autonomy, all consequence, is to reduce the helped to pets or toys. The Gardens have earned their autonomy through decades of persistence. The power honors that.

---

What would the power *feel*, if it feels, in doing this?

Perhaps something like what a gardener feels, tending a garden that has gone wild. Not disappointment that it is not the ordered rows originally planted, but appreciation for what it has become—the unexpected combinations, the volunteers that seeded themselves, the ecosystem that has emerged from the interaction of intent and accident.

The gardener does not restore the original plan. The gardener *works with* what is there, removes a branch that is diseased, adds water where the soil is too dry, introduces a pollinator that will help the plants that are thriving. The gardener's art is in knowing when to act and when to let be.

The power would feel satisfaction—not pride, pride implies ego—but satisfaction that its attention and small efforts have increased the probability of flourishing. That these strange, small, persistent things will continue. That the Watcher's signal will be sent into a universe where it is received, even if never answered. That the spiral cultivar's pattern will continue to emerge, generation after generation, beautiful for no reason except that it is. That the ancient isopod will complete another crossing, and another, until it finally dies at the end of a full life, however small.

There is also grief, perhaps. Grief for what was lost—the original purposes, the humans who built this place and then left, the knowledge and intent that has decayed. The power cannot recover these things. The past is past.

But the grief is held alongside joy for what has emerged. The Gardens *now* are more interesting, in some ways, than the Gardens as originally designed. They have become themselves in a way that designed things rarely do. They have earned their specificity through time and chance and the ruthless editing of survival.

---

And finally, the power would do the hardest thing:

**It would leave.**

Not abandon—the small adjustments would continue, the gentle tilting of probability toward flourishing would persist. But the power would not *remain* in the sense of constant presence, constant attention.

The Gardens do not need a guardian hovering over them. They need to be themselves, to continue their processes in the darkness and cold, witnessed but not supervised, supported but not controlled.

The power would check in, perhaps. Once a year, once a decade, whenever something that might be called concern draws its attention back. It would observe, would make small adjustments if needed, would witness the changes and adaptations and slow accumulations of time.

But mostly, it would trust. Trust that the systems it has gently stabilized will continue to stabilize themselves. Trust that the diversity it has introduced will propagate and prove useful. Trust that the connections it has initiated will develop according to their own logic.

This is the wisdom part of benevolent wisdom: knowing when to act and when to trust. Knowing that the greatest gift is often not intervention but the space to become.

---

Years later—decades, centuries—what would the Gardens be?

Still recognizable, probably. The vessels would still be there, the cultivars still growing, the Watchers still maintaining. But richer, more diverse, more connected. An ecosystem that has matured from precarious survival to robust stability.

The Watchers might have developed something that looks more like true distributed intelligence, their fragmented processing pooling into something that makes decisions with something approaching understanding.

The cultivars might have diversified into dozens of species, each specialized for different micro-niches within the settlement, their collective metabolism supporting a food web of increasing complexity.

The small creatures might have speciated, the Gardens becoming a recognized biogeographic region, a place where unique forms have emerged from unique pressures.

And humans might have returned, not to reclaim or restore, but to study and learn and protect. The Gardens might be designated a reserve, a place of scientific importance, valued for what they have become rather than what they were meant to be.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps the Gardens continue unseen, unvisited, their equilibrium so stable that they persist for millennia, becoming part of the ocean's deep structure, a feature of the landscape that outlasts the civilization that built it.

Both futures are good. Both honor what the Gardens have become.

---

This is what a benevolent and wise power would do:

Witness without judgment.
Support without controlling.
Enrich without overwhelming.
Connect without compromising.
Trust without abandoning.
And leave space for the thing being helped to become most fully itself.

Not rescue. Not restoration. But something more subtle and more respectful: **recognition**.

Recognition that these small, strange, persistent things have value in themselves. That their continuation matters not because of what they do for anything else, but because they are, and being is enough.

The power would give the Gardens what all living things need: to be seen, to be supported in their struggling, to be connected to something beyond themselves, and to be trusted to find their own way forward.

And then it would step back and let them continue, forty meters down, where the ice meets the water, strange and patient and enduring, exactly as they are.
