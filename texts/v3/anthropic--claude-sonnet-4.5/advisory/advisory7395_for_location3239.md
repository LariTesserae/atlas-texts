# Preparation Guide for the Resin Quarter

## Physical and Medical Preparation

### Respiratory Conditioning
Your lungs will face immediate and sustained stress. The air here isn't toxic, but it is *thick*—laden with volatile organic compounds, fungal spores, and aerosolized resin particles. Begin training months before departure:

- Practice breathing exercises with warm, humid air. Use a humidifier in a closed room heated to 85-90°F (29-32°C).
- Spend time in saunas, focusing on slow, steady breathing rather than short visits. Build up to 30-40 minute sessions.
- If possible, visit old-growth forests, woodworking shops, or lumber mills. Accustom yourself to breathing air heavy with wood compounds and organic volatiles.
- Consider consulting a pulmonologist about your baseline lung function. The resin-air is not harmful to healthy lungs, but any pre-existing conditions will be exacerbated.

Bring a quality respirator—not for constant use (the residents will find this odd and possibly insulting), but for your first days while adjusting, and for particularly resin-heavy work areas. Pack extra filters.

### Heat Adaptation
The constant warmth (likely 80-95°F/27-35°C most days) combined with the dry air creates a specific challenge:

- Begin heat acclimatization at least 6 weeks before departure. Exercise in warm environments, wear extra layers during workouts, gradually extend your exposure.
- Learn to recognize your body's heat stress signals. The danger here isn't dramatic—you won't get heatstroke from normal activity—but chronic mild dehydration and heat fatigue will grind you down.
- Practice functioning while warm. Read, write, perform detailed tasks while deliberately overheated. The residents work through the heat; you'll need to as well.

### The Hydration Problem
This is perhaps your greatest physiological challenge. The fungal oil they drink is nutritionally adequate but psychologically difficult:

- Begin substituting some water intake with other liquids—thin broths, herbal teas, diluted oils. Train your body and mind to accept hydration from non-water sources.
- Practice intermittent "thirst training"—periods where you deliberately maintain mild thirst to build tolerance. (Do this carefully and briefly; actual dehydration is dangerous.)
- Bring electrolyte supplements. The constant sweating depletes salts even if the sweat evaporates immediately.
- Consider bringing a small supply of water purification tablets, though there's nothing to purify. The psychological comfort of "treated" fungal oil may help in early days.

Most importantly: **bring or acquire a personal vessel for the fungal oil and commit to regular sipping**. Residents drink small amounts constantly. If you wait until you're thirsty, you're already behind.

### Digestive Adjustment
The fungal oil, the preserved foods, the complete absence of fresh vegetables—your gut will protest:

- Begin taking a broad-spectrum probiotic months before departure and continue throughout your stay.
- Gradually reduce fresh food intake before you leave, shifting toward preserved, dried, and oil-heavy foods.
- Bring digestive enzymes and mild anti-nausea medication. The first week will be difficult.
- Pack fiber supplements. The diet here is not fiber-rich, and constipation is a common complaint among newcomers.

### Skin Care
The dry heat, constant resin exposure, and minimal water washing create specific dermatological challenges:

- Bring oil-based moisturizers, not water-based ones. Something like pure jojoba or argan oil.
- Pack a dry shampoo or better yet, very fine cornstarch or similar powder for the dry-washing technique.
- Bring treatment for minor fungal skin infections. Despite the irony, the environment can support some human skin fungi.
- Accept that you will smell of resin. Everyone does. Fighting this is futile and wastes resources.

### Eye Care
The constant dimness, the lamp-work, the airborne particles:

- Get an eye exam before departure. If you need corrective lenses, bring backups.
- Pack preservative-free eye drops—the kind designed for dry environments.
- Bring a good supply of clean cloths for wiping your eyes. Do this often.
- If you're sensitive to light changes, bring an adjustable headlamp for reading. The residents read in surprisingly dim conditions; you may need more light initially.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Weight of Emptiness
The hardest adjustment isn't physical—it's the *space*. Those vast, empty chambers. The long stretches between people. The silence that sits behind the noise. This affects different people differently, but almost everyone struggles with it:

- If you're used to urban density, practice spending time in empty buildings, abandoned warehouses, large empty spaces. Notice how your mind responds.
- Practice being alone. Not lonely—alone. Sit in a room by yourself for hours, doing quiet work. Can you tolerate your own company without anxiety?
- Conversely, if you're drawn to solitude, understand that the emptiness here isn't peaceful wilderness—it's *hollow*, architectural, waiting. It has a different quality that can be unsettling even for introverts.

### Sensory Adjustment
Your senses will recalibrate in unexpected ways:

- The dimness will make you squint constantly for the first weeks. Your eyes will adjust, but it's frustrating. Practice working in progressively dimmer light before you go.
- The acoustic oddities—sounds coming from unexpected directions, voices that seem to originate everywhere and nowhere—can be disorienting. Spend time in spaces with strange acoustics: cathedrals, large water tanks, concrete structures. Learn to trust your other senses when hearing becomes unreliable.
- The smell will dominate everything. If you have scent sensitivities, consider carefully whether you can tolerate this. The resin smell is not unpleasant, but it is *relentless*. Some people never fully adapt.

### The Rhythm of Work
The pace here is patient, methodical, almost meditative:

- Practice sustained, focused work on single tasks. The residents might spend eight hours sawing a door into heartwood. Can you maintain concentration on repetitive work?
- Learn to work by lamplight. It's harder than you think—shadows fall differently, depth perception shifts, you lean closer to see detail.
- Understand that productivity here is measured in days and weeks, not hours. If you're used to rapid task completion, the slow pace may frustrate you.

### Social Sparseness
With only 300 people spread across a large area, social interaction is limited and specific:

- You won't have daily contact with most residents. You'll develop close relationships with perhaps 10-15 people.
- Prepare for limited small talk. Conversations tend toward the practical: tool-borrowing, technique-sharing, trade discussions.
- The community is established. You're arriving into existing relationships and patterns. Be patient about finding your place.

## Practical Preparations

### What to Bring

**Essential:**
- Multiple changes of simple, natural-fiber clothing. Cotton, linen, or hemp—things that can withstand the heat and dry-cleaning method. Pack wrapping-style garments rather than fitted pieces.
- A good quality knife. Bronze is the working metal here, but a steel knife for your personal use will be valued.
- Personal tools if you have a specific craft. Don't assume you can borrow or buy specialized equipment.
- A journal and multiple writing implements. The dimness and resin-tacky surfaces are hard on pens.
- Any medications you need, plus extras. There's no pharmacy.
- Contraceptives if relevant—there's no evidence of anyone making these.
- Salt. It's traded in but sometimes scarce. Bring a good supply.
- Spices or flavor variations. The food is monotonous.
- Multiple light sources: a good headlamp, a small lantern, spare batteries or charging solutions.
- A reliable timepiece. The limited daylight makes time tracking difficult.

**Valuable for Trade:**
- Small, high-quality metal tools (especially steel, which is superior to bronze)
- Specialized craft knowledge or books
- Seeds for edible plants (even if they can't grow here, they're valuable for trade with the caravans)
- Medicines, especially antibiotics or anti-fungals
- Quality cloth or leather goods
- Optical items: magnifying glasses, reading glasses, mirrors

**Consider Bringing:**
- Musical instruments (small ones—a flute, harmonica, or similar)
- Games or puzzles (entertainment is limited)
- Teaching materials if you have educational skills
- Art supplies (though remember everything will smell of resin)
- A small tent or hammock for privacy (you may initially live in shared spaces)

**Leave Behind:**
- Anything requiring water for maintenance or use
- Electronics dependent on reliable electricity (there isn't any)
- Expectations of privacy or personal space (at least initially)
- Rigid schedules or time-bound commitments
- Fresh food (it won't last the journey)

### Skills to Develop

**Highly Valuable:**
- Any hands-on craft: woodworking, ceramics, metalwork, weaving, leatherwork
- Practical repair skills: tool maintenance, structural repair, problem-solving
- Literacy and numeracy (surprisingly, not everyone here has strong skills in these)
- Medical knowledge, even basic first aid
- Drawing or technical illustration
- Mathematics or engineering for structural calculations
- Teaching ability

**Moderately Useful:**
- Cooking with limited ingredients
- Storytelling or performance
- Music
- Mediation or conflict resolution
- Recordkeeping and organization

**Less Useful Than You'd Think:**
- Advanced theoretical knowledge without practical application
- Skills requiring materials not available here
- Anything requiring speed over precision

### Financial Preparation

Understand the economy:
- There's no central currency. Trade is direct: goods for goods, labor for goods, favor for favor.
- Refined resin is the closest thing to currency—it's standardized, portable, and universally desired.
- Your first months will be economically vulnerable. You'll need to either arrive with trade goods or offer labor until you establish yourself.
- Housing is free (space is abundant), but materials and food must be earned.
- The caravan traders are the connection to the outside economy. Understand what they value and what they charge.

Bring enough external currency or goods to purchase supplies from several caravan visits while you establish yourself. Assume 3-6 months before you're economically self-sufficient.

## Mental Models and Expectations

### What This Place Is Not

**Not a Tourist Destination:**
No one is here to accommodate visitors. You will be expected to contribute, learn, and integrate. The residents are polite but not particularly welcoming—they're focused on their work.

**Not a Wilderness Adventure:**
Despite the ancient fungal towers, this is fundamentally an urban environment—just a very strange, sparse one. The challenges are more about social integration and economic establishment than survival skills.

**Not Mystical or Spiritual:**
The strangeness of the place might suggest some kind of transcendent or magical experience. In reality, it's a working community focused on craft and commerce. The residents are practical people doing practical work in unusual circumstances.

**Not Dangerous:**
There are no predators, no bandits, no violence. The main risks are heat exhaustion, chronic dehydration, respiratory irritation, and accidents with tools or falls from elevated platforms. Mundane dangers, carefully managed.

### What to Expect Emotionally

**Weeks 1-2: Overwhelm**
Everything is strange. The heat is oppressive. The smell is overpowering. The dimness gives you headaches. You can't sleep well because of the constant warmth. The fungal oil makes you nauseous. You question why you came.

This is normal. Push through. Everyone goes through this.

**Weeks 3-6: Adjustment**
Your body begins adapting. The smell becomes background. You learn to work in the dimness. You find a few people you connect with. You begin learning a useful skill or contributing your existing ones. The place starts making sense.

**Months 2-4: Integration or Rejection**
This is the decision point. Either the rhythm of the place settles into your bones—the patient work, the lamplight, the hollow spaces—or it doesn't. Many people leave during this window. Those who stay past four months usually stay much longer.

**Month 6+: Belonging**
If you reach this point, the Resin Quarter has become home. Its strangeness has become your normal. The thought of leaving, of returning to brighter, cooler, wetter places, seems vaguely unsettling.

### Red Flags: Don't Go If...

- You have serious respiratory conditions (asthma, COPD, chronic bronchitis)
- You require medications that need refrigeration
- You have severe heat intolerance or conditions that make heat dangerous
- You need frequent social interaction and validation
- You require external entertainment or stimulation
- You have claustrophobia (even though spaces are large, they're also enclosed and dim)
- You have nyctophobia (fear of darkness)
- You're running from something rather than going toward something
- You can't imagine living without daily bathing in water
- You have sensory processing issues that make certain smells or textures unbearable

## Arrival Protocol

### First Days

When you arrive with a caravan, you'll be met with indifference. This isn't hostility—it's that new people arrive periodically, and many don't stay. The residents have learned not to invest energy in newcomers until they prove they're staying.

**Immediate priorities:**
1. Find temporary housing. Ask at the central plaza. Someone will likely have empty chamber space they'll let you use.
2. Attend the next communal meal (these happen irregularly but you'll be informed). Observe, listen, say little.
3. Identify the bronze worker, the resin distiller, the fiber worker—the core craftspeople. These are the influential figures.
4. Start helping with basic tasks. Sweeping, hauling, lamp-filling. Unprestigious work that demonstrates willingness.
5. Watch how things work before suggesting improvements. The residents have optimized for this environment in ways that aren't immediately obvious.

### Learning the Language

The residents speak [whatever language you're arriving with—assume a common trade language], but they've developed specific vocabulary and communication patterns:

- Directional references are based on towers, not cardinal directions: "sunward," "rimward," "toward the great arch," etc.
- Time is measured in lamp-cycles and caravan-cycles rather than hours or days.
- There's a gesture language for quiet communication during work—learn this quickly.
- Certain words have shifted meanings: "wet" might mean resin-covered, "bright" might mean multiple lamps rather than actually bright.

### First Projects

Don't try to immediately establish a major craft or business. Instead:

1. Learn to maintain lamps. This is essential, repetitive work that's always needed.
2. Practice with the local materials—make yourself a small object from heartwood or fiber.
3. Help with the communal tasks: ground sweeping, lamp oil refining, food preparation.
4. Attend work sessions even just to observe. Most craftspeople don't mind quiet observers.

## Long-term Sustainability

### Career Paths

If you're staying long-term, consider these paths:

**Artisan Track:**
Apprentice to a master craftsperson (resin-worker, bronze-caster, weaver, carver). This takes years but results in economic independence and status.

**Service Track:**
Become essential through practical services: lamp maintenance, tool repair, food preparation, teaching, record-keeping. Less prestigious but reliable.

**Trade Track:**
Become an intermediary with the caravans. This requires capital to buy goods for resale, but can be lucrative.

**Innovation Track:**
Develop a new technique or product. Risky but potentially highly rewarding. The community values practical innovation.

### Building Relationships

The social structure is neither hierarchical nor completely egalitarian:

- Skill and reliability create respect, not age or tenure.
- The community makes decisions by slow consensus. There's no formal government.
- Conflicts are resolved through mediation by respected craftspeople.
- Romantic relationships form but are complicated by the small population. Be cautious and respectful.
- Children are communally valued but rare. If you have or plan to have children here, understand they'll be raised partially communally.

### Maintaining Outside Connections

Every caravan is a chance to send letters, receive news, maintain external ties:

- Establish a regular correspondence. The isolation can be profound.
- Consider whether you'll eventually leave or stay permanently. This affects decisions about property, relationships, skill development.
- Some residents have never left the Quarter. Others go out with caravans occasionally. Decide what you need.

## Emergency Preparedness

### Medical Emergencies

There's no doctor, but usually someone has practical medical knowledge:

- Learn basic first aid before arriving. You might be the most qualified person for some emergencies.
- Understand that serious injuries or illnesses require caravan transport out. This could mean days of delay.
- Bring a comprehensive first aid kit with supplies for serious injuries: sutures, antibiotics, pain medication.
- Know your blood type and any allergies. Write this information down and keep it with you.

### Fire

This is the existential threat:
- Everything is flammable: the towers, the resin, the fiber walls, the lamps.
- A serious fire could destroy the entire settlement.
- The residents are obsessively careful with fire. Learn and follow all fire protocols absolutely.
- Know where the fire-suppression equipment is (basically sand and beating tools).
- Never take shortcuts with lamp safety.

### Structural Failure

The towers are stable but not invincible:
- Learn the signs of instability: unusual creaking, visible cracks, resin weeping from unexpected places.
- Don't overload platforms or carve too aggressively into structural areas.
- If a tower section seems unstable, report it immediately.

### Getting Lost

The winding passages and similar-looking towers can be disorienting:

- Learn the major landmarks quickly.
- Carry a lamp or light source always when traveling through dark areas.
- Tell someone if you're going to explore less-used areas.
- The crater rim is visible from most open areas—this is your primary navigation reference.

## Philosophical Preparation

### Accept Impermanence

The Resin Quarter exists in tension: the residents slowly consume the towers that house them. This cannot continue forever. Some residents believe the towers will last thousands of years. Others think they're seeing subtle signs of decline. No one really knows.

This uncertainty is part of living here. You're participating in something that might be a sustainable community or might be a slow-motion consumption of a finite resource. Make peace with this ambiguity.

### Understand Your Motivation

Why are you going? The question matters:

- **Escape:** If you're running from something, the Quarter's isolation will provide that, but the emptiness will also give you nowhere to hide from yourself.

- **Craft:** If you're drawn to the work itself, you'll find deep satisfaction. The patient creation of beautiful objects from strange materials can be profoundly fulfilling.

- **Curiosity:** If you're fascinated by the strangeness, you'll have much to observe and document. But curiosity alone won't sustain you through the difficult parts.

- **Community:** If you're seeking belonging, you might find it here—but it's a spare, practical community, not a warm, emotionally effusive one.

- **Simplicity:** If you're seeking a simpler life, you'll find it. But simple doesn't mean easy.

### The Question You'll Be Asked

At some point, someone will ask you: "How long are you staying?"

This is both practical (affects housing arrangements, teaching investments) and existential. The residents are divided into those who are here temporarily (often young people learning a craft) and those who are here permanently (who've made peace with dying in this dim, warm place).

You don't need to know your answer immediately. But start thinking about it.

## Final Thoughts

The Resin Quarter is not for everyone. It's uncomfortable, strange, isolating, and demanding. But it offers something rare: the chance to live slowly, work patiently, create lasting things with your hands, and exist in a space unlike anywhere else in any world.

The heat will become your normal. The resin smell will fade from consciousness until you only notice it when you leave and realize the outside air smells empty. The dimness will stop bothering you. The hollow spaces will become comfortable. The patient pace of craft will settle into your muscles and mind.

You'll learn to read the towers' groans, to judge resin by touch, to work by lamplight without thinking about it. You'll develop calluses specific to local tools. You'll have conversations measured in hours in mostly-empty rooms. You'll watch the slow transformation of raw fungal material into refined objects through patient labor.

And one evening, sitting in a doorway with a lamp beside you, drinking fungal oil and watching the light fade early behind the crater rim, you'll realize the strangeness has become home.

Or you won't, and you'll leave with the next caravan, and that's fine too.

The Resin Quarter doesn't need you. But if you need it—if you're drawn to patient craft in strange circumstances, if you can tolerate heat and dimness and empty spaces, if you want to participate in this small, precarious, fascinating community—then prepare carefully and go.

The towers groan their greeting. The lamps burn steady. The work continues.
