# Preparation Guide for The Drowned Foundation

## Physical Preparation

### Footwear and Lower Body Protection
You need to completely rethink your relationship with wet feet. Bring multiple pairs of wool socks—at least six, because they will never fully dry. Modern waterproof boots are a trap here; your feet will sweat, and the internal moisture will have nowhere to go, leading to maceration and trench foot within days. Instead, consider the local solution: go barefoot when possible to let your feet air out, or wear simple leather sandals with thick cork or wooden soles that lift you an inch above the water. Bring cornstarch or antifungal powder and use it religiously. Each night, dry your feet as thoroughly as possible (though "thoroughly" is relative here), apply powder, and sleep with them elevated and exposed to air.

Your pants or lower garments should be wool or modern synthetic moisture-wicking materials—never cotton, which will stay wet and cold against your skin. Bring gaiters or wraps for your lower legs. Consider that the locals likely have leather wrappings treated with oils or fats to repel some moisture. You might acquire these upon arrival.

### Clothing Strategy
Layer obsessively, but with the understanding that you're managing moisture from both outside (rain, splash, ambient dampness) and inside (your own perspiration). Your base layer should wick moisture away from skin. Your middle layer should insulate even when damp—wool is ideal. Your outer layer needs to be water-resistant but breathable; traditional oiled canvas might actually outperform modern synthetics here because it breathes better during long-term exposure to persistent dampness rather than periodic rain.

Bring multiple sets of everything. Establish a rotation: wearing, drying, and reserve. Nothing will ever be fully dry by modern standards. You're aiming for "least damp."

Accept that you will smell like wet wool, clay, and mildew. Everyone does here. Bring biodegradable soap, but understand that you can't fully wash and dry clothes in any conventional sense.

### Body Conditioning
Start taking cold showers a month before arrival, gradually increasing duration. Your body needs to adapt to persistent coolness without the temperature dropping low enough to trigger full cold-weather responses. This is the insidious range where hypothermia can creep up because you don't feel actively cold—just never quite warm.

Build calluses on your feet. Walk barefoot on various surfaces. Your feet need to toughen up considerably, especially on the soles and between the toes where moisture will try to soften the skin.

If you're prone to any skin conditions—eczema, fungal infections, psoriasis—get them completely under control before arrival. The persistent dampness will aggravate anything that's already problematic. Bring any medications you need, sealed in waterproof containers.

### Medical Preparation
Understand that you're entering an environment perfectly designed for certain medical problems:

**Trench foot/immersion foot**: Learn to recognize the early signs—wrinkled, discolored skin, numbness, tingling. Prevention is everything. Keep your feet as dry as possible, change socks whenever you can, and never sleep in wet socks.

**Fungal infections**: The dampness creates ideal conditions. Bring antifungal treatments for both skin and nails. Apply preventatively to feet, groin, and any other areas prone to chafing.

**Respiratory issues**: The cool, damp air and potential mold exposure can irritate lungs. If you have asthma or other respiratory sensitivities, bring appropriate medications and perhaps a simple cloth mask to wear at night.

**Minor cuts and abrasions**: These will be slow to heal and prone to infection in constant dampness. Bring waterproof bandages, antiseptic, and perhaps a prescription antibiotic ointment. Clean and dress any wound immediately.

Pack a comprehensive first aid kit in a truly waterproof container—not water-resistant, waterproof. Include moleskin for blisters, though accept that blister management in persistent wet conditions is extremely difficult.

## Psychological Preparation

### Sensory Adjustment
The dampness is not dramatic—there are no floods, no storms of consequence, no crisis moments. This makes it harder, not easier. Prepare yourself for the grinding persistence of never being dry, never being warm, never hearing a crisp footstep or seeing a bright color.

Practice meditation focused on acceptance rather than change. You cannot dry the environment. You cannot make the sun come out. You can only adapt to what is. This philosophical stance isn't optional—it's necessary for maintaining mental health.

The overcast light creates a peculiar psychological flatness. Without strong shadows and bright sun, your circadian rhythms may drift. Days blend together. Bring a small, reliable timepiece and maintain regular routines even when external cues become unclear.

### Social-Spatial Adaptation
The extreme proximity of the buildings and the interpersonal closeness this implies will be challenging if you value privacy. People live pressed together, walls touching, sharing sounds and smells. There is almost no private space as you understand it.

Before arrival, examine your own relationship with personal space. Practice being in crowded environments without retreat options. The settlement layout suggests a culture where privacy is either unvalued or redefined—perhaps privacy here is simply turning your back, or closing your eyes, or being unaddressed rather than being unseen.

Learn to move quietly and carefully. In a place where buildings lean together and spaces are measured in degrees of dampness, clumsy movement could damage structures, disturb the careful water channeling, or be seen as disrespectful. Observe before you act.

### Temporal Adjustment
Time moves like the water here—slowly, persistently, without drama. If you're accustomed to urgency, deadlines, and rapid change, you need to recalibrate completely. 

Practice slow, repetitive tasks. Work with clay if possible—wedge it, shape it, work it for hours with minimal visible progress. This is the pace of life in the settlement: things improve incrementally, building rises finger-width by finger-width, water is channeled and rechanneled in an endless cycle.

Bring nothing that requires batteries or charging unless you also bring reliable power sources. Electronics are particularly vulnerable to persistent moisture anyway. A simple mechanical watch, a basic knife, hand-tools only.

## Practical Skills to Develop

### Water Management
Before arrival, become obsessive about water. How does it flow? Where does it pool? How do different surfaces shed or absorb it? Visit places with active water—streams, ditches after rain, boggy ground—and simply observe. Understand water's patient persistence.

Learn basic drainage principles. The settlement's genius is in its channeling system. You should be able to look at a slight slope and predict where water will go, should be able to dig a simple drainage channel that actually works.

Practice collecting and storing water cleanly. You'll likely be using the communal systems, but understanding how to keep water potable in open vessels, how to prevent stagnation while allowing settling, is valuable.

### Clay Work
Take a basic pottery or clay sculpture class. You need to understand clay—how it feels at different moisture levels, how to shape it, how to judge if something will hold its form or slump. Even if you're not building, you'll be living in and moving through clay constantly.

Learn to recognize different types of clay by sight and feel. The laminated layers in the basin's exposed faces tell a geological and seasonal story. Being able to read that story will deepen your understanding of where you are.

Practice working with natural materials in general—clay, reeds, unprocessed wood. Modern materials are preprocessed to be consistent. Natural materials vary enormously, and you need to work with each piece individually, responding to its specific properties.

### Traditional Crafts
Basic basketry would be valuable—the willow-cutting and weaving is clearly central to the settlement's material culture. At minimum, understand how plant fibers work, how to assess their strength and flexibility.

Learn simple cordage—how to make rope or string from plant fibers. This skill appears repeatedly in what you've observed (the clay-fiber rope binding wooden frameworks, the bindings on tool handles).

If possible, learn to knap stone tools or at least to sharpen and maintain simple bronze implements. The settlement has "basic bronze working edges," suggesting early metal age technology—not sophisticated, but functional.

## Cultural Research and Preparation

### The Symbols and Marks
You've noted the systematic marking of bricks and walls—spirals by doors, cross-hatching on certain walls, wavy lines near wet areas. These are clearly meaningful. Before arrival, if you can find any information about the settlement's culture, study their symbolic system. 

At minimum, bring a waterproof notebook and document every symbol you see, where it appears, and any patterns in its use. These marks are described as "practical AND they keep the water flowing properly AND they please the clay"—all three, not contradictory. This suggests a worldview where practical function and spiritual significance are united, not separated. Adjust your thinking accordingly.

### Social Observation
The settlement appears communal—shared water basins, communal catch-basins, public work areas. Prepare to participate in collective labor. Practice subordinating your individual schedule to group needs. 

The quietness you've noted—low conversational voices, no shouting—suggests cultural norms around sound. Practice speaking softly. Learn to modulate your voice downward, to communicate without projecting.

### Gift-Giving and Exchange
You have no information about the settlement's economy, but you're arriving as an outsider. Bring things that might be valuable here: perhaps metal tools (simple ones—a well-made knife, an awl, needles), perhaps high-quality rope or cordage, perhaps waterproof fabric. Nothing flashy or technological—practical items that fit within their existing material culture but might be superior examples.

Salt is often valuable in pre-industrial settlements. Bring some if you can transport it waterproofed—it's useful, preservative, and often ceremonially significant.

## Timing Your Visit

The description doesn't specify seasons clearly, but there are hints: "rain comes often here, soft and steady," temperatures hover just above breath-misting, "cycles through seasons that are marked more by wetness than by temperature."

If you can choose when to visit, consider:

**Drier season** (if such exists): Easier physical adjustment, but you won't see the full water system in action. The channels "wake up" after rain. You might miss crucial cultural practices around water management.

**Wetter season**: Harder physically, but you'll see the settlement as it truly is, in its element, with the water system fully active. This is probably the more authentic experience.

Realistically, plan for rain. Plan for it raining most days. Not storms—soft, steady, patient rain that continues for hours. Bring that mindset: patience with precipitation.

## Shelter Considerations

You don't know if you'll be hosted or need to establish your own space. If hosted, bring a gift for your hosts—something practical and valuable (see above).

If establishing your own shelter, study the local architecture carefully before attempting anything. Notice:
- Buildings are on higher ground (relatively)
- Walls are thicker at base, tapering upward
- Roofs are designed to channel water, not just shed it
- The relationship between buildings is structural—they lean on each other

You probably shouldn't attempt to build. Instead, look for an abandoned structure to rehabilitate, or a space between buildings where you could create a simple shelter that integrates with existing drainage patterns without disrupting them.

Bring a tent only as emergency backup, and understand that a freestanding tent will be an anomaly here—foreign material, foreign design, potentially disrupting local water flow or just marking you as thoroughly Other.

## Food and Cooking

- Small fish or amphibians in channels (not abundant)
- Birds (rare)
- Rodents (present but presumably not eaten)

Bring a metal pot and eating utensils. Practice cooking simple meals with minimal tools. Learn to manage fire in damp conditions—this is crucial. Collect dry tinder obsessively whenever you find it.

## The Weirdness

Now we address what the description calls "subtle but persistent" weirdness:

### The Impossible Dampness
"Surfaces stay damp even when it hasn't rained for days, as if the ground itself is breathing out moisture." This goes beyond normal groundwater seepage. The basin "remembers water." This is either:
1. Geological anomaly (extremely high water table, unusual capillary action in the clay)
2. Actual low-level reality distortion

Preparation: Don't expect the water to follow normal rules entirely. It will be more persistent, more present, more *intentional* than ordinary groundwater. Bring your acceptance practice. The locals clearly have achieved some accord with this through their channeling system and their symbols that "keep the water flowing properly."

Do not try to dry areas that are meant to be wet. Do not block channels. Do not disrupt the water's pathways. Follow the local practices precisely.

### The Spatial Anomalies
"Buildings that seem too close together for their doors to open, but the doors do open, into spaces that feel larger inside than their external walls suggest. Not vastly larger... but wrong by a foot or two."

This is genuine strangeness—mild reality distortion around spatial relationships. Preparation:

- Don't trust your initial spatial assessments
- Move slowly and carefully until you understand a space
- Do not attempt to map the settlement accurately—the measurements will contradict each other
- Accept that space here is slightly negotiable, that the close-pressed buildings create local spatial compressions or expansions
- Watch how locals move—they clearly know where the space is and isn't
- If a door looks too close to another wall to open, try it anyway, carefully

This suggests the settlement exists in a reality pocket where normal Euclidean geometry is slightly relaxed. The persistent pressure of the close-built structures, the overlapping rooflines, the way walls lean together—perhaps this physical intimacy creates spatial effects.

Do not panic. The anomalies are small, stable, and clearly livable-with, as evidenced by the functioning settlement.

### The Marks and the Clay
The marks that are practical AND keep water flowing AND please the clay—this trinity suggests the symbols have actual efficacy, not just cultural meaning. The clay is described as "remembering," the ground as "remembering water."

Preparation: Study animistic worldviews. Read about cultures where land, water, and materials are understood as having agency, memory, intention. This appears to be literally true here, not metaphorically.

Learn respect protocols for materials. How do you honor clay? Probably by:
- Using it skillfully and without waste
- Acknowledging its properties and working with them
- Leaving marks (as the locals do—thumbprints are "still visible after firing")
- Returning broken pottery to the clay (as noted—broken ceramics are crushed and used to improve footing, eventually settling "back into the clay matrix it came from")

Do not:
- Waste clay
- Dig without reason
- Disrupt the laminated layers unnecessarily
- Block water's path without creating an alternate channel
- Ignore the symbol system

If you work with clay—and you probably should, to participate in the settlement's material culture—leave your own marks, but study the local symbol system first. Your marks should either follow local patterns or be clearly different (personal marks, not clumsy copies of meaningful symbols you don't understand).

### The Temporal Layers
"Earlier structures now half-buried, their foundations and lower walls serving as platforms for newer building... distinct strata: an older wall of cruder brick, less regular, then a newer wall built atop it."

The settlement is rising, building upward on its own past, literally layering time. This is normal historical process, but here it feels more significant—the settlement isn't just built on old foundations, it's built on the act of building, on continuous adaptive response.

Preparation: Understand that you're seeing something in progress, never finished. The settlement you arrive at will be different from the settlement you leave, even if you're only there a short time. Buildings will rise incrementally. Channels will be modified. The surface will lift.

Document what you see, but accept that your documentation will be immediately historical, immediately past.

### The Night
You haven't asked about night, but it will come. "The settlement must be nearly dark, just the glow of fires through windows and doorways... The channels would be invisible, dangerous to the unfamiliar."

Preparation:
- Bring a small, reliable light source (flashlight or headlamp, well-waterproofed, with extra batteries)
- **But use it sparingly and discreetly**—it will mark you as an outsider and might disturb the darkness
- Practice moving in darkness
- If staying any length of time, learn the pathways by day so thoroughly you could walk them blind
- The locals "move through the darkness like water itself, finding the ways by feel and habit"—this is not metaphor, this is instruction

Be very careful at night, especially at first. Stay where you are, or move only on paths you've memorized. Falling into a channel would be embarrassing at best, dangerous at worst. The water is described as cool, the air cool—immersion at night could lead to hypothermia.

## Philosophical Preparation

### Acceptance of Impermanence and Constant Change
The settlement is "always rising, always building, always one wet season away from needing to rethink itself." Everything here is temporary, adaptive, responsive. Nothing is finished.

This is actually good practice for multiverse travel in general, but especially important here. Let go of:
- The need for things to stay as they are
- The desire for completion or finish
- Frustration with constant maintenance and adjustment

Embrace:
- Process over product
- Adaptation over resistance
- Cyclical time over linear progress

### Humility Regarding Human Control
"This is a settlement that knows its existence is negotiated. It's not built in defiance of the water or in conquest of the land. It's built in conversation with them."

You're entering a place that understands itself as being in relationship with, not in command of, its environment. Check any impulses toward:
- "Improving" systems
- Explaining why things would work better if done differently
- Imposing external solutions

Instead:
- Observe the conversation that's already happening
- Learn the language being spoken between people, water, and clay
- Participate only after you understand the terms of the negotiation

### Comfort with Ambiguity
The marks that mean three things simultaneously. The space that's larger than it should be. The water that arrives from nowhere. The way the description keeps noting things without explaining them—"may be maker's marks or prayers or simply the human need to make order."

Don't expect clear answers. Don't expect things to resolve into single explanations. Hold multiple possibilities at once. The locals probably don't distinguish sharply between practical, spiritual, and aesthetic—these aren't separate categories in their worldview.

Practice "yes, and" rather than "either/or" thinking.

## Upon Arrival

Your first hours matter enormously:

1. **Find the community center** (the convergent basin with the carved post)—this is clearly the social and possibly spiritual heart of the settlement. Observe protocols. If there are objects hanging from the post, understand these are significant. Don't touch.

2. **Locate water sources**—communal basins, channels where water is collected for drinking vs. washing. Understand the water hierarchy.

3. **Identify a person who seems willing to interact with strangers**—possibly someone working in a public area, someone who makes eye contact. Approach respectfully. Use simple language, gestures. Your goal is to establish: Where should I be? Who should I speak with? May I stay?

4. **Observe before acting**—watch for several hours, a full day if possible. How do people greet each other? How do they move through tight spaces? What are the patterns of the day?

5. **Find appropriate space**—whether hosted or establishing your own, settle somewhere that doesn't disrupt water flow, doesn't block light to existing structures, doesn't create crowding where there wasn't any. The settlement's layout is "less planned than negotiated"—you're joining that negotiation.

6. **Participate in communal labor**—if people are digging clay, help. If they're maintaining channels, help. If they're firing kilns, observe and help as directed. Work is probably the quickest route to acceptance.

7. **Document carefully but discretely**—your notebook is for later, not for constant scribbling in front of people. Observe, remember, write when you're alone.

8. **Follow water protocols precisely**—this is clearly central to everything. Where water is channeled, let it flow. Where it's collected, use it appropriately. Where it pools, understand this is intentional.

9. **Respect the symbols**—don't touch marks on walls, don't stand on decorated bricks, don't disturb the plazas where pottery and tools are arranged. Everything has meaning and place.

10. **Accept discomfort**—you will be damp, cool, probably never quite comfortable. Everyone is. This is the baseline. Complaining would be like complaining about gravity.

## Long-term Considerations (If Staying Extended Period)

### Adaptation Milestones
- **Week 1**: Physical adjustment to dampness, cold, constant mild discomfort. Learning basic layout and protocols.
- **Week 2-3**: Your clothes, gear, and body smell like the settlement. Your feet have toughened. You know the main pathways and water channels. You've established basic relationships.
- **Month 1**: You move more like the locals—carefully, quietly, knowing where the space is. You understand the water system well enough to help maintain it. You can read the basic symbols.
- **Month 2-3**: The spatial anomalies no longer surprise you. You know which doors lead to which interior spaces regardless of what the exterior suggests. You've developed personal relationships. You participate in work cycles naturally.
- **Month 4+**: The settlement's rhythm is your rhythm. You understand the conversation between people, water, and clay well enough to participate. The way water arrives from nowhere seems normal. The marks on walls tell you stories.

### Signs You Should Leave
- Persistent illness that isn't improving (respiratory, skin, digestive)
- Inability to keep any belongings dry (your waterproofing has failed completely)
- Psychological break—the constant dampness becomes unbearable, you can't adapt
- Conflict with locals that can't be resolved
- The spatial anomalies are worsening or becoming unstable (unlikely, but if space starts acting wronger than "a foot or two off," evacuate)

### Integration Limits
Understand that as an outsider, there may be practices, spaces, or knowledge you won't be included in regardless of how long you stay. The marks are described but not explained. There may be meanings you'll never learn. Accept this.

## Emergency Protocols

### If You Disrupt Water Flow
- **Stop immediately**
- **Find a local person and indicate the problem**
- **Help fix it however they direct**
- **Expect this to be remembered**—you've interfered with the settlement's circulatory system

### If You Get Lost at Night
- **Stop moving**
- **Call out softly**—the locals will find you
- **Do not panic**—the settlement is not large, you're not in wilderness
- **Use your light minimally**, just to ensure you're not standing in a channel

### If Spatial Anomalies Become Disorienting
- **Sit down**
- **Close your eyes**
- **Wait for equilibrium to return**
- **Move more slowly afterward**
- The locals experience the same space and navigate it fine—follow their lead more closely

### If You Feel Hypothermia Setting In
(Symptoms: Confusion, slurred speech, loss of coordination, extreme fatigue)
- **Seek shelter and people immediately**
- **Get out of wet clothes if possible**
- **Warm core first**—chest, neck, head, groin
- **Warm, sweet drinks if available**
- **Do not ignore this**—the cool dampness is exactly the temperature range where hypothermia sneaks up

### If Water Seems Wrong
If the water starts acting more strangely than described—flowing uphill significantly, appearing in sealed containers, moving with apparent intention beyond the "subtle" level—this suggests either:
1. You're misunderstanding natural processes
2. There's a ceremony or practice happening that affects the water
3. Something has genuinely changed

- **Observe locals' reactions**—if they're calm, follow their lead
- **If locals are concerned, be concerned**
- **Don't interfere with water under any circumstances**
- **Document what you see, but from safe distance**

## What You'll Learn

If you do this right, if you prepare thoroughly and stay long enough to understand, you'll learn:

**Patience**—the patience of water seeping through clay, of buildings rising inch by inch, of seasonal laminations in exposed strata.

**Attention**—to degrees of dampness, to minute shifts in water flow, to the meanings held in simple marks, to spatial relationships that don't quite add up.

**Humility**—from living in a place that knows it exists by negotiation, that builds in conversation rather than conquest, that accepts its nature as young, wet, still becoming.

**Adaptation**—the constant small adjustments needed to live with persistent conditions you cannot change, only respond to.

**Different ways of knowing**—where practical and spiritual aren't separate, where materials have memory, where symbols do things as well as mean things.

You'll also learn something about multiverse travel in general: that sometimes the strange isn't dramatic, isn't catastrophic or transcendent—sometimes it's subtle and persistent, seeping up from underneath, requiring not heroism but adjustment, not conquest but conversation.

The Drowned Foundation is a gentle introduction to reality being slightly other than expected. The water that arrives from nowhere is more there than it should be. The space is more flexible than it should be. The clay remembers more than clay should remember.

But these are survivable strangeness, livable-with strangeness. The locals have achieved a working relationship with them. You can too, if you're patient, attentive, humble, and adaptive.

Go slowly. Observe carefully. Accept dampness. Respect the water's ways. Honor the clay. Learn the symbols. Walk the channels. Feel the laminations of time.

And when you leave—if you leave—you'll understand something about how humans make homes in places that barely hold them, about how we build not against nature but with it, about the deep patience required to live somewhere that's still becoming.

Take that understanding with you. You'll need it for whatever comes next.
