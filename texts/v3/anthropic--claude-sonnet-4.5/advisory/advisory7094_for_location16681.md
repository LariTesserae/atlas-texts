# Preparation Guide for the Hydroponic Ossuary

## Physical Preparation

### Respiratory Conditioning
You must prepare your lungs for air that is essentially liquid. Begin at least six weeks before departure by spending increasing amounts of time in steam rooms, saunas, and humidified environments. Work up to tolerating 95%+ humidity for hours at a time. Practice slow, deliberate breathing—the air there is so thick with moisture that rapid breathing will feel like drowning. Learn to breathe through your nose, which filters and warms better than mouth-breathing. Consider spending a week in tropical rainforest if possible; the Ossuary's atmosphere makes even that seem arid by comparison.

Your lungs will produce more mucus in response to the constant moisture. This is normal. Practice clearing your airways gently and frequently. Bring expectorants, not suppressants.

### Thermoregulation
The constant blood-warmth temperature means your body will stop actively regulating heat. Before visiting, spend time in environments where you don't need to maintain thermal homeostasis—prolonged baths, heated swimming pools. Your body needs to remember how to exist without the constant low-level work of staying warm or cool. 

You will not sweat there in the normal way—the air is too saturated to accept evaporation. Moisture will simply condense on your skin continuously. Get used to being perpetually damp by taking lukewarm baths and not drying off completely, going about your day in a state of persistent wetness.

### Vestibular Preparation
The spatial anomalies—stairs that lead to wrong heights, columns that count differently, platforms that shift—will challenge your sense of orientation. Practice navigating spaces with optical illusions. Spend time in buildings with unusual architecture. Learn to trust your feet more than your eyes. 

Some travelers report that the mathematical wrongness triggers vertigo or nausea. If you're prone to motion sickness, build tolerance gradually. The disorientation is cognitive, not physical, but your body may not know the difference.

### Fungal Prophylaxis
Everything there is *always wet*. Begin antifungal foot treatments a month before departure. Keep your toenails trimmed short. Consider preventative oral antifungals if your doctor approves, though be cautious—you want some fungal resistance, not complete sterility. Your skin needs its microbiome intact.

Between your toes, in the creases of your joints, behind your ears, under your breasts or in other skin folds—these areas will never be dry. Prepare them. Use barrier creams in training, let your skin toughen to constant moisture. Paradoxically, well-moisturized skin handles persistent wetness better than dry skin, which cracks and allows infection.

## Equipment and Supplies

### Clothing
Bring nothing that cannot be wet constantly. Natural fibers will rot within days; the place is a perfect incubator for decay. Synthetic technical fabrics designed for water sports are essential:

- Lightweight nylon or polyester shirts with antimicrobial treatment
- Quick-dry synthetic pants that can handle being soaked permanently
- Several changes of synthetic undergarments (they won't dry, but rotating them helps)
- A thin wetsuit or rash guard for sleeping, if you need a sense of enclosure
- No cotton whatsoever—it will mildew within 24 hours

Footwear is critical. Water shoes or drainage sandals with good grip on wet metal. Your feet will be wet the entire time. Bring multiple pairs to rotate—even synthetics need some recovery time. Consider neoprene socks if your feet are sensitive. Expect to treat blisters; wet skin blisters easily.

Bring nothing leather, nothing with metal that will rust against your skin, nothing with absorbent padding. The place will destroy it.

### Waterproofing
Everything you carry must be waterproofed as if for deep ocean immersion:

- Dry bags with roll-top closures, at least IP67 rated
- Pelican cases for anything fragile or electronic
- Ziplock bags as tertiary backup—double-bag everything
- Silica gel packets are useless here; don't bother
- Waterproof notebooks with pencils (ink will run)

Your electronics will die. Accept this. Even "waterproof" devices struggle with *continuous* immersion in near-liquid air. If you must bring a camera or phone, expect it to fail. Bring disposable waterproof cameras as backup.

### Hygiene and Medical
Standard toiletries won't work:
- Soap designed for salt water that doesn't require rinsing
- Toothpaste that works without clean water to rinse
- Dry shampoo (paradoxically useful in a wet environment)
- Antimicrobial wipes for cleaning skin
- Antifungal powder (though it clumps immediately when applied)
- Barrier creams for preventing chafing and maceration
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics—any cut will struggle to heal in this environment
- Waterproof bandages that actually stay on wet skin
- Oral rehydration salts—you'll drink constantly but may not absorb well

Bring more medical supplies than seems necessary. Wounds don't heal normally there. A minor scratch can become a major problem when it literally never dries out.

### Sustenance
The fruits are probably edible, but "probably" is operative:

- Bring 3-4 days of dense, waterproof food as safety margin
- Freeze-dried camping meals in sealed pouches
- Energy bars in individual waterproof wrappers
- Water purification tablets, though the water is likely clean
- Electrolyte supplements—you'll be perpetually hydrated but may become imbalanced

The place provides water and possibly food, but you want independence for your first days while you assess safety.

## Psychological Preparation

### Solitude Training
The profound absence of animal life—no insects, no birds, no fish—is more disturbing than you anticipate. Begin spending extended periods alone in natural settings. But not just alone: alone in *silent* nature. The Ossuary has sound, but almost none. The silence of the green is total. Practice being comfortable with only your thoughts and the sound of water.

The absence of other humans is expected. The absence of *everything else that moves* is existentially unsettling. Your nervous system is calibrated to expect bird calls, insect buzz, the small movements of animal life. None exist there. You'll keep seeing motion in your peripheral vision—it's always just plants in the slow wind. This phantom movement effect disturbs some travelers greatly.

### Mathematical Unease
The wrongness in the counting, the impossible geometries, the structures that don't connect properly—these will bother you more than physical discomfort. Some people can simply accept "this place doesn't follow normal spatial rules" and move on. Others become obsessively fixated on understanding the pattern.

Practice accepting ambiguity. Read about non-Euclidean geometry not to understand it, but to become comfortable with spatial relationships that don't resolve. Look at M.C. Escher prints until the impossible stairs stop bothering you. The Ossuary is like that, but you have to walk through it.

If you have tendencies toward obsessive-compulsive thinking, be very careful. The urge to count the rivets, measure the ratios, map the structure will become overwhelming. The place rewards observation but punishes the need for complete understanding. You cannot solve it. Trying will drive you to distraction.

### Temporal Adjustment
Time works differently at plant speed. Practice slow observation:

- Watch time-lapse videos of plant growth, then watch actual plants for hours
- Sit with a single leaf and observe it for 30 minutes without other stimulation
- Practice meditation focused on extremely slow change
- Learn to find interest in incremental development

The boredom will be intense if you're accustomed to normal human timescales. Bring nothing to "pass the time"—electronics will fail, books will rot. You must learn to exist at the pace of the place, where meaningful change happens over days.

Conversely, be prepared for the opposite problem: losing time. Hours will pass while you watch water drip. You'll think you've been on a platform for twenty minutes; it's been three hours. Bring a reliable mechanical watch, waterproofed. Check it regularly. Time becomes slippery there.

### The Presence Question
You must decide before you go how you'll handle the ambiguity of whether something tends the place. Some travelers report absolute certainty that they're alone. Others report absolute certainty they're being watched. Physical evidence supports both interpretations.

If you're the kind of person who needs definitive answers, this will torment you. Tools hung on hooks: who hung them? When? Are they still being used? You'll never know. The cup with a vine in it: was it placed there growing, or did the vine seed itself? Both seem equally impossible.

Practice sitting with uncertainty. The place is liminal, and you cannot resolve its liminality through observation or logic. Decide now whether you'll interpret it as abandoned-but-functional or maintained-by-absence. Stick with your interpretation; changing your mind repeatedly will exhaust you.

Some travelers try to leave markers to test if anything moves them. The markers are sometimes moved, sometimes not, with no pattern. Don't waste energy on experiments you cannot conclude.

## Navigation and Exploration

### Orientation Strategies
You will get lost in the sense that you won't know exactly where you are. Surprisingly, this isn't dangerous—the structure is finite, and wandering eventually brings you back to familiar platforms. But the uncertainty is stressful.

- Mark your entry point clearly with something waterproof and bright
- Don't try to map the structure; the maps won't make sense when you review them
- Instead, learn landmark platforms: the one with the massive fern, the one where three pipes cross overhead, the one with the blue-green copper ceiling
- Accept that you'll reach the same places by different routes
- The catwalks are safe structurally; if there's a path, you can walk it

The vertical shaft at the center is the only truly reliable navigation point—you can always find it, and you can orient from it. But each level looks similar around the shaft, so note which plants ring it on "your" level.

### Safe Exploration Practices
The structure is sound, but caution is warranted:

- Test any moss-covered grating before putting full weight on it
- The handrails support vines; they'll hold you, but grip carefully
- Don't lean on glass panels, even if they look sturdy
- The water in the central shaft is probably not safe to fall into
- Some platforms have no railings at the edges; the mist hides the drop
- Moving through dense plant tunnels: part the leaves gently, watch for thorns

The place is not actively dangerous, but it's utterly indifferent to human safety. It wasn't built for visitors. You're a tolerated presence, nothing more.

### The Other Structures
From the top platform, you can see the other towers in the network. You may feel compelled to reach them. Resist this unless you have a vessel capable of navigation through the mist and are an experienced pilot. The distance is deceptive, and there's no surface to walk on between structures—only the mist concealing whatever ocean lies below.

Each structure is probably similar to the one you're in, but "probably" is not certainty. Some travelers report structures that are variations on the theme. Some report structures that are fundamentally different. Without reliable transport, don't attempt travel between them.

## Duration and Exit Strategy

### Recommended Stay Length
First-time visitors should plan for 2-4 days maximum. This gives you time to acclimate, explore several levels, experience the day-night cycle (such as it is), and leave before the psychological weight becomes excessive.

The longest recommended stay is 10-14 days, and only for experienced travelers comfortable with profound solitude. Beyond two weeks, some travelers report:
- Difficulty remembering life outside the structure
- Reluctance to leave despite no objective reason to stay
- Beginning to see patterns in plant growth that dictate their movement
- Feeling that they understand the place's mathematics (they don't)
- A sense that they're becoming part of the garden system

These aren't necessarily harmful, but they suggest it's time to leave.

### Exit Planning
Have a concrete exit plan before you enter:
- A specific day and time you will leave
- A backup day if the primary is missed
- A person outside who expects contact by a certain date
- A mechanical timer or alarm as reminder (electronics will fail)

The place has a gravity that's not physical. It's comfortable, in its way—warm, provided with water, peaceful. The very things that made you eager to visit will make you reluctant to leave. Commit to your departure date before you go, when the outside world seems real.

Your vessel (boat, aircraft, whatever brought you) must be secured properly. The structure has no docks or moorings for visitors. You'll need to anchor or tie off creatively. Check it daily—the constant moisture affects everything.

## Special Considerations

### For Botanists and Scientists
You will want to collect samples. You will want to document species. You will want to understand the ecosystem. These impulses are reasonable but perhaps futile:

- Many plants don't match known species closely enough for certain identification
- The ones that seem wrong are especially compelling to study—resist obsession
- Samples removed from the environment die quickly or change properties
- The place resists systematic cataloging; each attempt produces different results
- Photography fails (moisture), sketching is slow (humidity blurs), memory is unreliable

Go to observe, not to capture. The place is not a research site; it's an experience. Trying to make it an object of study will frustrate you.

### For Artists and Contemplatives
This place is for you, perhaps more than any other visitors:

- The light through leaves is endlessly variable
- The geometry is aesthetically perfect even in its wrongness
- The sound design of water is complete
- The smell—wet earth, green growth, metal and mineral—is complex
- The experience of being inside a maintained ruin is profound

Bring waterproof materials for field recording (audio may survive better than visual). Bring weather-resistant art supplies if you must create, but consider simply experiencing. The place is already art; adding to it may be redundant.

The contemplative aspects are exceptional: nowhere else provides this combination of enclosure, life, and abandonment. For meditation or spiritual practice, few places are more conducive. The slow time, the patient green, the sense of being inside something that continues without needing you—these are rare gifts.

### For Thrill-Seekers
This isn't for you. The place has no adrenaline. The dangers are slow—infection, disorientation, getting lost—not dramatic. The structure is sound; nothing will collapse. No creatures threaten. The edges are dangerous only if you're careless.

If you need excitement, go elsewhere. The Ossuary is profound but not thrilling.

## Final Psychological Preparation

### The Return
Prepare for reverse culture shock. When you leave, the normal world will seem:
- Absurdly dry (you'll be thirsty constantly for days)
- Too bright (the diffuse light there is gentle; sunlight will hurt)
- Too loud (the silence of the Ossuary is deeper than you realize while in it)
- Wrong somehow (straight lines will look strange, angles will seem too sharp)

Budget several days after your return for readjustment. Don't schedule important activities immediately. You'll be physically fine but psychologically dislocated.

### The Memory Problem
The place is difficult to remember accurately. Not because it's forgettable—the opposite. It's so dense with detail that memory can't hold it all. You'll recall the feeling more than the specifics. Temperatures, colors, the exact arrangement of platforms—these blur almost immediately.

Some travelers find this distressing: they had this profound experience they can barely articulate afterward. Others find it freeing: it makes the place permanent in mood while allowing the oppressive detail to fade.

Write immediately upon exit if you want records. Even then, your notes will seem incomplete, like trying to describe a dream.

### Integration
What you learn there doesn't translate directly to other environments, but it changes how you see:
- You'll notice plant life more acutely
- You'll be more comfortable with ambiguity
- You'll have a different relationship with industrial spaces
- You'll understand patience in a new way
- You'll have experienced a place that combines made and grown so thoroughly the distinction dissolves

This is valuable, but you cannot explain it to people who haven't been. Don't try. The Ossuary is not for sharing; it's for carrying quietly.

## Practical Timeline

### 6 Weeks Before:
- Begin respiratory conditioning
- Start antifungal prevention
- Practice solitude and slow observation
- Research waterproof equipment

### 3 Weeks Before:
- Acquire all specialized clothing and equipment
- Begin sleeping in humid conditions
- Practice navigating unusual spaces
- Test all waterproofing thoroughly

### 1 Week Before:
- Final medical check
- Confirm exit plan with outside contact
- Pack and waterproof everything
- Make peace with uncertainty

### Day Of:
- Check all seals and closures
- Secure your vessel properly
- Enter with awareness that you're moving from one kind of reality to another
- Breathe slowly; let the moisture settle into your lungs
- Accept that you understand nothing about this place
- Begin walking

The Hydroponic Ossuary will receive you without welcome and release you without farewell. It continues its patient work of being exactly itself: impossible, functional, abandoned, maintained, young, ancient, wrong, perfect, built, grown, metal, green, breathing moisture through the bones of engines that learned to garden in the warm mist of a place that shouldn't exist but does, patient and strange and utterly indifferent to your presence or absence, which is perhaps its greatest gift.

Go prepared to be small in it, temporary in it, comfortable in it, changed by it.

And remember: you can leave whenever you want. Except you won't want to, which is why you must plan to leave anyway. The place is not a trap. It's an invitation to stay forever that you must decline, eventually, while some part of you remains there always, walking through green tunnels where water drips with infinite patience and the mathematics don't resolve and the light filters golden through frosted glass into the warm, wet, breathing silence.
