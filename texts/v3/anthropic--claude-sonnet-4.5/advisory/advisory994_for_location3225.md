# Preparation Guide for the Drowned Engine Plains

## Physical and Equipment Preparation

### Diving Certification and Skills
You will need technical diving certification far beyond recreational limits. The depth range (150-250 meters) places this firmly in technical trimix territory. Before attempting this location:

- Obtain technical deep diving certification to at least 100 meters, preferably deeper
- Complete extensive training in mixed-gas diving (trimix, with experience managing helium narcosis and oxygen toxicity windows)
- Master decompression procedures, as you'll be accumulating significant deco obligations
- Train extensively in emergency procedures, buddy separation protocols, and self-rescue at depth
- Practice navigation in zero-visibility conditions, as the 50-meter visibility can collapse to nothing

The water temperature of 8-10°C (46-50°F) is not the coldest you'll encounter in diving, but sustained exposure at depth requires serious thermal protection. You'll be in the water for extended periods due to decompression requirements, making hypothermia a persistent threat even before considering the psychological effects of cold on decision-making.

### Life Support Equipment

Your equipment loadout needs to support:

**Extended bottom times**: Even conservative exploration will require 30-60 minutes at depth. With decompression, total immersion times will extend to 3-6 hours or more.

**Primary breathing gas**: Trimix appropriate for your maximum depth (likely around 10/70 or 12/65 - 10-12% oxygen, 65-70% helium, balance nitrogen). You'll need sufficient volume for your planned bottom time plus generous reserves. Plan on multiple large cylinders or a rebreather configuration.

**Decompression gases**: You'll need multiple stage cylinders with progressively richer nitrox mixes for decompression stops, possibly including pure oxygen for shallow stops if conditions allow.

**Rebreather consideration**: A closed-circuit rebreather (CCR) might be more practical than open-circuit for this environment. The extended duration and depth make gas volume requirements enormous on open-circuit. However, rebreathers add complexity and failure modes. If you choose CCR:
- Bring multiple redundant systems
- Carry sufficient bailout gas for catastrophic failure at maximum depth
- Be prepared for the psychological challenge of rebreather silence in an already eerie environment

**Thermal protection**: A drysuit is mandatory, likely with heated undergarment systems. The combination of depth, duration, and sustained cold creates serious hypothermia risk. Bring:
- Properly maintained drysuit with redundant inflation/exhaust systems
- Electric heating system with multiple battery packs
- Thick undergarments (Thinsulate or equivalent)
- Neoprene hood, dry gloves with warm liners
- Face mask with thermal insulation features if available

**Lighting**: Here's where it gets interesting. The location has ambient light, but it's dim and diffuse. You'll need:
- Primary lights: two high-quality dive lights with appropriate burn time for your total dive duration
- Backup lights: at least three additional lights
- Consider flood lights rather than tight beams—the environment is vast, and you'll want to illuminate structures, not just create a tunnel of light in murky water
- Strobes or markers for navigation reference points
- All lights must be depth-rated for your maximum depth and cold-water reliable

### Navigation and Communication

You will get lost. The repetitive nature of the structures, the limited visibility, and the vast scale guarantee spatial disorientation.

**Navigation equipment**:
- Compass (primary and backup)
- Depth gauge and bottom timer (redundant systems)
- Dive computer rated for technical diving with multiple gas mixes
- Underwater GPS if available (though functionality is uncertain)
- Reels and guidelines—bring multiple spools with substantial line
- Slate and underwater writing implements for notes and navigation data

**Communication**:
- Underwater communication system (through-water comms or hardwire to support team)
- Surface support should be mandatory, but accessing surface may be impossible from within the structure
- Light signals and hand signals will be your primary communication
- Consider that you may be completely isolated once inside

### Access and Deployment

The description provides no clear access method. This is a critical planning problem:

**Possible entry scenarios**:

1. **Surface descent through the water column**: If there's open water above, you'd descend from a support vessel. This requires:
   - Support boat with technical diving capabilities
   - Shot line or descent line properly weighted for current
   - Surface support team understanding technical diving requirements
   - Decompression trapeze or habitat for shallow stops
   - Emergency oxygen and communications at multiple depths

2. **Entry through submarine or submersible**: If the depth and scale suggest submarine access:
   - Coordinate with whatever entity operates submarine services to this location
   - Understand docking or lock-out procedures
   - Plan for getting back to the same access point in low visibility
   - Have contingency plans for missing the submarine return

3. **Habitat-based access**: There may be underwater habitats or airlocks:
   - Locate and verify operational status before relying on them
   - Understand pressurization/depressurization procedures
   - Don't assume they're functioning—they may be as abandoned as the rest
   - Bring tools to manually operate hatches and valves

4. **Moon pool or surface opening**: The structures might have access shafts:
   - Research architectural features before arrival
   - Bring cutting tools and mechanical advantage systems
   - Be prepared to force entry through seized mechanisms

**The most likely scenario**: You'll need surface support with a dynamic positioning vessel, proper technical diving infrastructure, and the ability to conduct extended decompression in potentially adverse surface conditions.

## Physiological and Medical Preparation

### Medical Fitness
Before attempting this dive, obtain:
- Comprehensive diving medical examination focused on technical diving fitness
- Cardiovascular stress testing
- Pulmonary function testing
- Ear/sinus examination and clearance
- Dental examination (to rule out tooth barotrauma from trapped air spaces)
- Psychological evaluation for confined space tolerance and isolation stress

**Specific concerns**:
- The extended bottom time and decompression requirements mean any medical emergency becomes catastrophic
- Age, cardiovascular fitness, and dehydration status all affect decompression stress
- Cold exposure increases decompression risk
- The psychological environment may trigger panic or anxiety even in experienced divers

### Decompression Management

At 150-250 meters depth, you're in serious decompression territory:

- Understand and plan for deep stops
- Know your ascent rates and stop schedules precisely
- Plan for extended shallow decompression (possibly hours)
- Bring tables, dive computer, and backup tables for your specific gas mixes
- Understand oxygen toxicity exposure limits—you'll be pushing them during deco
- Consider that the environment offers no shallow refuge—no reef ledge to rest on during hour-long stops
- Plan for in-water emergency recompression if someone shows DCS symptoms
- Understand that surface evacuation to a chamber may be hours away

**Decompression contingencies**:
- Lost gas scenarios (you miss a deco gas drop or regulator fails)
- Extended bottom time due to navigational delay
- Missed stops due to current or entanglement
- Oxygen toxicity seizure management underwater
- Hypothermia during extended deco

### Physical Conditioning

Months before the dive, begin:
- Cardiovascular conditioning—diving at these depths is physically demanding
- Cold water tolerance training—practice in cold water with incremental exposure
- Equipment carrying conditioning—you'll be managing multiple cylinders and heavy gear
- Swimming endurance—current may require sustained swimming
- Core body temperature management training
- Stress management and meditation practice

## Psychological Preparation

This may be the most important section.

### The Nature of the Experience

The Drowned Engine Plains will challenge you psychologically in ways that normal diving does not:

**Scale and emptiness**: The vast, empty spaces between structures create profound isolation. You'll spend long periods swimming through featureless water, surrounded by blue-grey nothing, with your own breathing sounds and the distant machine hum as company. This is not the comfortable emptiness of blue water diving—it's industrial emptiness, artificial void, space that should contain something but doesn't.

**Sensory confusion**: The sounds will disorient you. Mechanical groans, distant clangs, the pervasive hum—all conducting through water from unknown distances and directions. You'll hear things that sound close but are far away, sounds that suggest machinery activating, sounds that might be the structure failing. Learning to filter and interpret this soundscape takes time and creates persistent low-level anxiety.

**Repetition and navigation uncertainty**: The repeating structures create profound disorientation. You'll swim to a tower, examine it, swim to the next, and be unable to determine if you're making progress or swimming in circles. This uncertainty erodes confidence and creates mounting anxiety about finding your way back.

**The absence of the expected**: There are no humans. The control rooms are empty. The machines run without operators. This absence where presence is expected—chairs with no sitters, doors with no openers, processes with no supervisors—creates deep unease. Your pattern-recognition systems will insist something is wrong, that someone should be here, that the emptiness is a sign of danger.

**Depth and consequence awareness**: At 200 meters, you are far beyond recreational depth. You're in a realm where small mistakes cascade into fatalities. The constant awareness that you're in an unforgiving environment, that your life depends on equipment and procedures and nothing else, creates psychological pressure that accumulates over the dive duration.

**Time distortion**: The extended dive duration, the featureless environment, and the narcotic effects of depth (even mild with helium) all distort time perception. Minutes feel like hours or hours feel like minutes. This makes navigation timing difficult and creates anxiety about decompression schedules.

**Industrial horror**: There's something deeply unsettling about being inside a massive machine, about being small and soft in a place of metal and concrete and processes that don't care if you're there. The place wasn't made for humans to inhabit, even if humans built it. Being there feels transgressive, like crawling inside a running engine.

### Psychological Training

**Meditation and mindfulness practice**: 
- Develop ability to observe anxiety without reacting
- Practice maintaining calm during extended periods of sensory monotony
- Learn to stay present rather than catastrophizing about what-ifs
- Build tolerance for uncertainty and ambiguity

**Simulated isolation**:
- Spend time in sensory-reduced environments
- Practice diving in low visibility, monotonous environments
- Do long, solo dives in unremarkable locations to build tolerance for uneventful extended time underwater
- Work with a psychologist on isolation tolerance if needed

**Fear management**:
- Identify your specific fear triggers (darkness? machinery? isolation? depth?)
- Work through these systematically before the dive
- Practice emergency procedures until they're automatic—procedural memory functions when conscious thought fails
- Understand the difference between useful caution and paralyzing fear

**Team dynamics**:
- If diving with others, build deep trust and communication
- Practice emergency scenarios until coordination is instinctive
- Establish clear protocols for separation and reunion
- Understand that buddy separation in this environment is existential threat

**Accept the weirdness**:
- This place is objectively strange—don't fight that recognition
- Your pattern recognition will fire constantly with "this is wrong" signals
- Learn to acknowledge "yes, this is weird" while maintaining "but I am safe if I follow procedures"
- Strangeness doesn't equal danger, even though our instincts conflate them

## Environmental Understanding

### The Machines and Infrastructure

Before entering, research industrial systems:
- How cooling towers function
- How turbine assemblies are structured
- Electrical infrastructure safety even when submerged
- Pipe system organization and flow patterns
- What might still be energized or pressurized

**Specific hazards**:

**Entanglement**: Cables, pipes, and structural elements create three-dimensional entanglement hazards. The low light and limited visibility mean you may not see a cable until you're in it. Bring cutting tools. Practice entanglement self-rescue. Never force through restrictions.

**Pressure systems**: Some pipes may contain pressurized fluids. Valves may fail. Be alert for sudden jets, pressure changes, or released materials.

**Electrical hazards**: The description mentions active electrical systems. Even underwater, high-voltage systems can create dangerous electrical fields in the water, particularly in the less conductive cold seawater. Avoid any equipment showing signs of electrical activity (glowing indicators, warm surfaces, electromagnetic field effects on compass).

**Structural failure**: The infrastructure is old and unmaintained in places. Metal is corroding. Concrete may be degrading. Be alert for structural instability, falling objects, collapse hazards. Don't trust your weight to any structure without testing it.

**Current and flow**: The description emphasizes changing currents strong enough to require bracing. Learn to:
- Read water movement from particle suspension patterns
- Anticipate flow around structures
- Find shelter from current
- Avoid getting pinned against structures by flow
- Never swim against strong current—find alternate routes

**Confined spaces**: Control rooms, pipe interiors, and spaces between machinery create confined space hazards. These are fatal in diving:
- Never enter spaces without proper training
- Maintain continuous guideline to exit
- Verify adequate space to turn around before entering
- Watch for silt-out conditions that drop visibility to zero

### The Ecology

The biological inhabitants are mostly benign but understanding them helps:

**Fish behavior**: 
- Schools may surround you—this is usually benign
- Solitary large animals (sharks, seals) are investigating, not attacking
- Watch fish for behavior changes that indicate current shifts or predator presence
- Fish moving rapidly away from an area may indicate a hazard

**Crustaceans**: 
- Generally harmless but can pinch if startled
- Useful as current indicators—they orient to flow patterns
- Their presence indicates relatively stable water chemistry

**Algae and biofilms**:
- Make surfaces slippery—test handholds
- Indicate light penetration and water quality
- Can reduce visibility when disturbed

**Marine snow and sediment**:
- Minimize disturbance to avoid reducing visibility
- Heavy sedimentation indicates areas of low flow
- Can indicate contamination or chemical precipitation

### Water Chemistry and Quality

The description suggests this might not be entirely natural seawater:

**Potential concerns**:
- Industrial processes may release chemicals or change water chemistry
- Thermal variations indicate heat exchange—monitor for hot water hazards
- pH variations from industrial processes could affect equipment or exposed skin
- Heavy metals or other contaminants might be present
- Oxygen levels might vary—some areas might be hypoxic from bacterial activity

**Protective measures**:
- Use drysuit to minimize skin contact
- Ensure all equipment is chemically resistant
- Don't assume water is safe to drink even after treatment
- Monitor equipment for unusual corrosion or degradation
- Be alert for smell or taste changes in breathing gas indicating contamination

## Logistical Preparation

### Support Team and Communications

Do not attempt this alone. You need:

**Surface support team**:
- Dive supervisor experienced in technical diving operations
- Medical personnel with diving medicine expertise and decompression illness training
- Boat crew capable of maintaining position in current
- Radio operator maintaining outside communications
- Emergency response coordinator with evacuation plans

**Dive team**:
- Minimum of two divers (three is better)
- All must be technical diving qualified to the same level
- Clear role assignments (primary navigator, safety diver, etc.)
- Thorough pre-dive briefing on communication protocols
- Emergency separation and reunion procedures

**Emergency resources**:
- Recompression chamber location and evacuation procedures
- Coast guard or equivalent rescue coordination
- Medical evacuation procedures
- Backup equipment and gas supplies
- Emergency contact numbers and communication procedures

### Gas Planning

This is mathematical and critical:

**Calculate for worst-case scenarios**:
- Gas volume needed to reach maximum planned depth
- Gas volume for bottom time plus 50% reserve
- Gas volume to return to first decompression gas switch
- Complete decompression gas requirements
- Add contingency for:
  - Navigational delays (double your estimated bottom time)
  - Lost decompression gas (can you complete deco on backgas?)
  - Missed stops requiring deeper return
  - Helping an injured buddy (you need their gas volume too)

**For a 30-minute dive to 200 meters**:
- You might need 4-6 large cylinders of bottom mix
- 2-3 cylinders each of intermediate decompression mixes
- 1-2 cylinders of oxygen-rich shallow mix
- This is per diver, and assumes perfect execution
- Weight will be enormous—plan for vehicle or staging
- Consider staged decompression gases positioned along ascent line

**Rebreather calculations**:
- Sufficient sorb (CO2 absorbent) for total dive duration plus reserve
- Multiple oxygen sensors functioning properly
- Sufficient diluent for depth and duration
- Bailout gas sufficient for catastrophic failure at maximum depth (this alone might be 3-4 cylinders)

### Equipment Redundancy

Everything critical must be redundant:
- Two completely independent breathing systems or sufficient bailout
- Multiple lights (you've already been told this but it bears repeating)
- Two cutting tools minimum
- Redundant decompression computers or tables
- Multiple gas switching systems
- Backup thermal protection (chemical heat packs, extra undergarments)
- Redundant buoyancy control
- Multiple navigation aids

### Documentation and Planning

Create detailed dive plans:
- Maximum depth and bottom time
- Decompression schedule with contingencies
- Navigation plan with landmark references
- Emergency procedures
- Lost buddy procedures
- Aborted dive procedures
- Gas switching schedule
- Communication protocols
- Timeline for support team

**Document everything**:
- Video or photograph what you can for navigation reference
- Keep written logs of routes, landmarks, and observations
- Time stamps for all activities
- Gas pressure checks and recordings
- Any anomalies or concerns

## During the Dive

### Maintaining Mental State

Once in the water:

**Breathing discipline**: 
- Maintain slow, steady breathing
- Monitor gas consumption constantly
- Use breathing for anxiety management
- Watch for signs of elevated CO2 (rapid breathing, anxiety, headache)

**Situational awareness**:
- Constant monitoring of depth, time, pressure
- Regular buddy checks
- Navigation reference maintenance
- Exit route awareness
- Environmental changes (current, temperature, visibility)

**Decision-making protocol**:
- Stick to the plan unless specific abort criteria met
- Don't extend bottom time "just a bit more"
- Don't push through amber warning signs hoping they don't turn red
- Conservative decisions always—aggressive diving here kills

**Abort criteria** (turn dive immediately if):
- Gas supply reaches predetermined turn pressure
- Any diver shows distress or confusion
- Equipment failure beyond redundancy
- Navigation uncertainty beyond acceptable limit
- Environmental conditions worsen beyond planning
- Anything that "doesn't feel right" without obvious explanation

### Exploration Discipline

**First dive**: 
- Stay conservative
- Focus on orientation and familiarization
- Don't go far from entry point
- Establish landmarks and navigation references
- Test communication and procedures
- Build environmental understanding

**Subsequent dives**:
- Expand range gradually
- Maintain detailed navigation logs
- Establish fixed reference points
- Map the environment systematically
- Never assume you know where you are
- Always maintain continuous navigation to exit

**Investigation protocols**:
- Approach structures cautiously
- Test stability before trusting
- Watch for entanglement hazards
- Monitor for electrical or pressure hazards
- Document as you go
- Maintain buddy contact
- Keep track of time strictly

## Post-Dive

### Immediate Post-Dive

- Complete all decompression obligations conservatively
- Surface support should monitor for DCS symptoms for 24 hours
- Maintain hydration
- Avoid alcohol and heavy exercise
- Avoid flying or altitude for appropriate surface interval
- Debrief while memories are fresh
- Document observations
- Review problems or concerns

### Long-Term Considerations

**Repeated diving effects**:
- Technical diving has cumulative physiological impacts
- Monitor for subtle DCS symptoms
- Track nitrogen loading over multiple days
- Consider full rest days between deep dives
- Watch for exhaustion effects on decision-making

**Psychological processing**:
- The experience may be more affecting than expected
- Disturbing dreams or intrusive thoughts about the environment are possible
- The combination of depth effects, isolation, and strangeness can leave impressions
- Talk through the experience with team
- Don't dismiss psychological impacts as weakness

## Special Considerations

### The Unknown Factors

The description leaves much uncertain:

**Access**: How you get there determines much of your planning. If the access method is unusual (moon pool in structure, submarine lockout, etc.), you need specific training for that method.

**Purpose of the place**: Understanding what the facility was for might reveal hazards. If it's nuclear (cooling towers suggest power generation), radiation might be a concern. If it's chemical processing, toxicity might be an issue. If it's experimental, unknown risks might exist.

**Current activity**: The description says some machines still work. This is both fascinating and terrifying:
- What activates them?
- Could you be caught in a sudden activation?
- Are there pressure wave hazards?
- Thermal hazards from sudden heat release?
- Could valves close and trap you?
- Is there any pattern or predictability?

**The water source and level**: Is this seawater? Is the surface above the ocean surface? Is this a flooded facility or something built submerged? The answer affects risk assessment, emergency procedures, and physiological planning.

### The Fundamental Strangeness

Finally, prepare for this reality: The Drowned Engine Plains is fundamentally strange in a way that transcends normal diving strangeness.

You'll be inside a machine the size of a city, or a city built as a machine. You'll be surrounded by human construction that dwarfs you, that continues processes you don't understand, that operates without operators in the cold and dim water. You'll hear it working, feel it vibrating, see its effects, but understand only fragments of its purpose.

This strangeness has weight. It accumulates. It affects judgment and perception in ways that are hard to predict. The place is liminal—between function and ruin, between natural and artificial, between present operation and past abandonment.

**Prepare for this by**:
- Accepting that some discomfort is inevitable and normal
- Trusting your procedures over your feelings
- Maintaining perspective—strange doesn't mean dangerous
- Staying grounded in observable reality
- Keeping communication open about what you're experiencing
- Remembering that others have felt this way and managed it
- Giving yourself permission to be awed or unsettled while remaining functional

## The Essential Truth

The Drowned Engine Plains demands respect. It's not actively hostile—the description suggests no malice, no intentional danger—but it's indifferent in the way large systems are indifferent. It will continue its processes whether you're there or not, whether those processes harm you or not.

Your survival depends entirely on:
- Technical competence
- Thorough preparation
- Conservative decision-making
- Functional equipment
- Clear thinking under stress
- Recognition of your own limits
- Willingness to abort if conditions warrant
- Respect for the environment and its hazards

If you bring these elements, if you prepare comprehensively, if you approach with appropriate caution and capability, you can explore this strange place and return. It offers something rare: an environment that is both human-made and genuinely alien, that combines the familiar and the deeply foreign, that exists at a scale and in a state unlike anywhere else.

It will change how you think about space and infrastructure and the relationship between human creation and natural environment. It will give you stories that seem unbelievable in the telling. It will show you something few people ever see: the interior of a machine that has become a world.

But it will only do these things if you survive the experience. Everything above is in service of that survival. Prepare accordingly.
