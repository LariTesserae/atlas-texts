# Preparation Guide for Visiting the Suspension Fields of Qen Mora

## Physical Conditioning: The Altitude Contract

You need to understand something fundamental before you go anywhere near Qen Mora: your body evolved for sea level, and this plateau exists at 3,000+ meters where the air holds roughly 30% less oxygen. "Breathing becomes a practiced thing" isn't poetry—it's a warning.

**Pre-departure conditioning (8-12 weeks minimum):**

Start cardiovascular training immediately. Not gym cardio—functional cardio. Long-distance hiking with a loaded pack. Stair climbing with weight. Your heart needs to become efficient at oxygen delivery, your lungs need expanded capacity, and your blood needs to increase its oxygen-carrying potential. If you have access to altitude training facilities or can spend weekends at progressively higher elevations, do so. Your body needs to begin producing additional red blood cells before you arrive.

Practice conscious breathing techniques. Learn to breathe deeply and rhythmically even while moving. On the plateau, every action—setting up shelter, walking between formations, even thinking clearly—requires deliberate respiratory management. Panic breathing at altitude leads to hyperventilation, alkalosis, poor decisions, and potentially life-threatening altitude sickness.

**Acclimatization protocol:**

Do not go directly to Qen Mora from sea level. Build an approach that stages your ascent. Spend 3-4 days at 1,500 meters. Then 3-4 days at 2,200 meters. Only then approach the plateau itself, and plan to spend your first two days doing minimal activity—short walks, lots of rest, constant hydration. 

Watch for altitude sickness symptoms: headache, nausea, dizziness, unusual fatigue, insomnia. Mild symptoms are normal and pass with acclimatization. Severe symptoms—confusion, inability to walk straight, fluid in lungs (crackling breathing sounds), extreme lethargy—mean immediate descent. People die from ignoring these signs. The plateau's warmth and beauty make it dangerously easy to underestimate altitude's lethality.

Some people simply don't acclimatize well. This isn't weakness—it's genetics, physiology, individual variation. If you're one of them, you'll know within the first 24 hours. Have an exit strategy that doesn't depend on you making good decisions while oxygen-deprived.

## Water: The Timing-Dependent Lifeline

The plateau's water situation is both reliable and absolutely unforgiving if you miscalculate.

**Understanding the rain cycle:**

Rain comes every 3-4 days from the east. You can see it approaching for an hour—this is crucial. The rainfall lasts 20 minutes maximum. For the following hour, water runs through the ancient channels. Then it's gone, retreated into the fissures, leaving only scattered pools that evaporate over the next 48-72 hours.

Your entire visit timeline revolves around this cycle. Chart the rain pattern before arrival if possible—talk to recent visitors, look for any records, observe from the plateau edges. The pattern has rhythms within rhythms; it's not mechanically precise, but it's reliable enough to plan around if you're paying attention.

**Water collection strategy:**

Bring collection capacity beyond what seems reasonable. Multiple wide-mouthed containers that can be deployed quickly when rain arrives. The 20-minute window is everything—rain falls, you collect, it's over. Collapsible water bladders work well; rigid containers are more reliable. Plan for 4-5 liters per person per day minimum, more if you're active or not well-acclimatized (altitude increases water needs through respiratory losses and increased urination).

The water-stones—dark rocks that collect overnight condensation—offer supplemental water, but we're talking milliliters, not liters. Bring a small sponge or cloth to collect this moisture if you're desperate, but don't depend on it for primary hydration.

The pools in rock depressions: drinkable, but increasingly mineralized as they evaporate. Early after rainfall, they're excellent. Three days later, they're concentrated salts. Bring testing strips or at minimum, taste carefully before drinking quantities. High mineral content causes digestive distress, which at altitude, while dehydrated, becomes dangerous fast.

**Filtration:**

Bring it. The water looks pristine, but beetles, herds, and predators all drink from these sources. Bacterial or parasitic contamination isn't guaranteed, but the consequences of gastrointestinal illness at altitude, far from help, aren't worth the risk. A quality filter or chemical treatment. Redundant systems—if your primary filter breaks, you need backup options.

**Contingency water:**

Carry enough stored water to reach the plateau edges and descend if the rain pattern breaks. This is emergency ballast you hope never to use, but water is life here, and the plateau's indifference extends to your survival needs.

## Shelter: Stone Architecture Basics

The plateau provides building materials and challenges you to use them appropriately.

**Site selection:**

Study the existing human shelters before building your own. They're positioned in the lee of larger formations, eastern side blocked against the rain. This isn't arbitrary—the warm rain might seem pleasant, but 20 minutes of exposure while trying to sleep destroys rest, and the moisture takes hours to dry in your clothing and gear at this humidity level.

Don't build near suspension structures (you'll disrupt ecosystems and the humming interferes with sleep). Don't build in obvious water channels (rare catastrophic rains do occur). Don't build adjacent to observer-objects (the softened physics in those zones affects sleep quality—you'll have strange dreams and wake unrefreshed).

Find your site at least 100 meters from any major feature. Look for natural windbreaks. Check the ground for beetle burrows—they don't tolerate having their homes covered. Notice where herd animals walk; don't camp in their pathways.

**Construction technique:**

Three walls and a roof. The basalt provides flat pieces if you search—look in areas where thermal expansion and contraction have fractured larger formations. You want pieces roughly 30-60cm across, as flat as possible.

Build walls about a meter high, thick enough to be stable (two stones deep minimum). The back and sides block wind and rain; leave the front open for ventilation and easy exit. The roof spans about 1.5-2 meters, requires your flattest, largest stones, needs to rest securely without mortar. Test stability before trusting it. A collapsed roof on your head at altitude, far from medical care, ends your trip or worse.

Pack gaps with smaller stones. The structure doesn't need to be watertight—water runs off basalt quickly—but wind protection matters for maintaining body heat at night despite the ambient warmth.

**Alternatively:**

Bring a lightweight tent designed for high-altitude use. This is simpler, more reliable, and sacrilegious to the plateau's aesthetic. Stone shelters connect you to previous visitors, to the landscape's logic, to the slow time of the place. A modern tent is a bubble of elsewhere, protecting but also isolating you. Choose according to your priorities: safety versus immersion.

## Thermal Management: The Comfortable Trap

The plateau's warmth is genuine but requires understanding.

The air temperature stays warm—comfortable at sea level. But you're not at sea level. Your body works harder here, generates more heat through increased respiratory effort and cardiovascular strain. Meanwhile, the thin air means rapid heat loss through radiation at night despite the warm basalt.

**Clothing layers:**

You need range. Lightweight, breathable clothing for daytime activity—you'll sweat more than expected as your body works to oxygenate itself. Long sleeves and pants despite the warmth; the sun is intense at this altitude, and sunburn is debilitating.

For night: insulation layers that pack light but provide real warmth. The basalt radiates stored heat, but by pre-dawn, even that fades. A quality sleeping bag rated for at least -5°C (23°F), maybe colder if you sleep cold or aren't well-acclimatized (poor acclimatization means poor circulation means feeling colder).

**The sunlight equation:**

UV radiation increases roughly 10-12% per thousand meters of altitude. At 3,000 meters, you're receiving 30-35% more UV than at sea level. The atmospheric clarity here—that remarkable visibility—means even less filtering.

High-SPF sunscreen, reapplied frequently. A wide-brimmed hat that stays on in wind. Quality sunglasses with full UV protection and side shields—the basalt reflects sunlight efficiently, so you're getting exposure from below as well as above. Snow blindness (UV keratitis) occurs on bright stone as readily as on snow. It's extraordinarily painful and temporarily disabling.

Neck gaiter or bandana for covering exposed neck and lower face. Lip balm with SPF, reapplied constantly—lips blister fast at altitude.

## Navigation: Reading Stone and Sky

The plateau's scale and clarity create specific navigational challenges.

**Distance deception:**

Everything looks closer than it is. The buttes "twenty kilometers distant" look like an hour's walk. They're six hours away, maybe eight if you're not acclimatized. Your intuition for distance estimation, calibrated for normal atmospheric conditions, is wrong here.

Bring maps if they exist. GPS with extra batteries (cold drains batteries faster, and nights get cool despite the warmth). Compass as backup—magnetic declination matters, know the local variation.

**Landmark navigation:**

The buttes are your primary references—sketch them, note their relationships, use them as a coordinate system. The observer-objects are spaced regularly (~1km apart); count them, map them relative to your shelter.

The suspension structures create zones you can navigate by, but they're numerous enough to be confusing. Don't depend on recognizing individual structures.

**Channel systems:**

The water channels create a dendritic pattern across the plateau. Learn to read their flow logic—multiple small channels merge into larger ones, which lead to the drainage holes. This creates a watershed pattern you can use for orientation if you understand the overall topology.

Mark your shelter location unmistakably. A tall cairn, visible from distance. Bright fabric (natural colors blend too well with stone). GPS coordinates saved multiple places.

**The clarity trap:**

In the afternoon heat shimmer zones, vision bends. You'll see things that aren't positioned where they appear to be. Learn to recognize these zones (they occur in predictable locations related to rock formations and sun angles), and don't trust your vision there.

At night, with starlight on pale stone, distance estimation becomes even more distorted. Travel by night only in emergency, and then very carefully.

## Food: Imported Sustenance Only

You cannot live off this land. Accept this completely.

The beetles are technically edible, but processing them requires knowledge you don't have (which glands to remove, how to neutralize certain compounds). "Tastes of minerals and faint electricity" undersells the difficulty. The herd animals are possible food sources, but hunting them disrupts the carefully balanced predator-prey dynamics and violates the unstated but real contract of human presence here.

**Nutrition strategy:**

Bring calorie-dense, easy-to-digest foods. Altitude suppresses appetite while increasing caloric needs. You need roughly 3,500-4,500 calories per day depending on activity level, and you probably won't want to eat even half that.

Foods that work well at altitude:
- Nuts and nut butters (dense calories, no cooking needed)
- Dried fruits (sugars for quick energy, some electrolytes)
- Energy bars designed for cold-weather mountaineering
- Chocolate (morale as much as nutrition)
- Instant soups and broths (warm fluids, salt, comfort)
- Oatmeal or other hot cereals (easy to digest)

Avoid foods requiring extensive chewing when you're already working to breathe. Avoid anything that causes gas or bloating—these are uncomfortable at altitude. Avoid alcohol entirely (massively increases altitude sickness risk and dehydration).

**Cooking considerations:**

At 3,000 meters, water boils at roughly 90°C (194°F) instead of 100°C. Cooking times increase. Foods that depend on boiling temperature (pasta, rice, beans) cook poorly or not at all.

Bring a lightweight stove with sufficient fuel. Calculate conservatively—cold, altitude, and wind all increase fuel consumption. A windscreen is essential.

Do all cooking in designated areas away from your sleeping shelter. Food odors attract beetles in astonishing numbers and possibly interest the predators, though they seem primarily focused on the herd animals.

## Medical Preparation: Self-Sufficiency Required

You are far from help. Evacuation from the plateau is measured in days, not hours.

**Altitude illness medications:**

Acetazolamide (Diamox) for altitude sickness prevention and treatment. Discuss with a physician beforehand; there are contraindications and side effects to understand. Typical protocol: 125-250mg twice daily, starting the day before ascent.

Dexamethasone for emergency treatment of severe altitude sickness if descent isn't immediately possible. This is serious medication for life-threatening situations—know when and how to use it before you need it.

Ibuprofen or aspirin for headaches (common in the first few days of acclimatization).

**General medical kit:**

- Comprehensive first aid supplies: bandages, gauze, medical tape, wound closure strips
- Antibiotic ointment for cuts (which heal slowly at altitude)
- Blister treatment (moleskin, blister bandages) - absolutely critical
- Anti-diarrheal medication (you cannot afford fluid loss at altitude)
- Antihistamines (allergic reactions to unfamiliar insects/plants)
- Pain relievers (multiple types)
- Any personal prescription medications plus extras

**Specific to this environment:**

Sterile saline for eye irrigation (stone dust, intense sun, potential exposure to suspension field particles).

Electrolyte replacement beyond what's in your food. You're losing salts through increased urination and respiratory water loss.

Antacids (digestive issues are common at altitude).

**Medical knowledge:**

Take a wilderness first aid course before going. Know how to recognize and treat:
- Altitude sickness (mild, moderate, severe)
- Dehydration (insidious at altitude)
- Heat exhaustion and heat stroke (yes, even here)
- Hypothermia (overnight temperatures)
- Eye injuries
- Fractures and sprains
- Allergic reactions

Bring a compact medical reference guide. In a crisis, with reduced cognitive function from altitude, you need external memory support.

## Psychological Preparation: Silence and Scale

The plateau's effect on consciousness is as significant as its physical challenges.

**Solitude immersion:**

If you're unaccustomed to extended silence and isolation, this is a profound shock. The quiet here isn't absence—it's presence. You hear everything: your heartbeat, your digestion, the blood in your ears, your joints clicking. Your internal monologue becomes externally loud.

Practice beforehand. Spend time in silent, isolated natural settings. Learn what your mind does without constant input. Discover whether you find this peaceful or anxiety-inducing, because on the plateau, there's no escape from it.

**Temporal disorientation:**

The multiple simultaneous timescales create a strange mental state. You'll lose track of clock time while becoming hyper-aware of sun angles and shadow positions. Days blur together around the rain cycle. "What day is it?" becomes a question requiring deliberate thought.

Keep a journal. Physical writing, by hand, with dates. This anchors you to linear time and provides orientation when your sense of temporal flow becomes liquid.

**The observer-objects:**

These affect people differently. Some visitors report profound unease near them. Others feel drawn to sit within their zones of softened physics for hours. The subtle reality distortions—sounds playing backward, stones arranging themselves, moments of perfect silence—are gentle but genuinely strange.

Know your tolerance for ontological ambiguity. If you need the world to behave consistently according to familiar rules, the areas near observer-objects will disturb you. This isn't weakness; it's knowing your operating parameters.

**Insignificance recognition:**

The plateau's scale and timescale make human presence feel appropriately small. For some people, this is liberating—relief from human-centered importance. For others, it triggers existential discomfort.

The plateau has been weathering for geological ages. It will continue after you leave. Your presence changes nothing essential. The equilibrium absorbs your visit and continues. This perspective is either medicine or poison depending on your psychological needs.

## Behavioral Protocols: The Unstated Contract

How to be a good guest in a place that tolerates but doesn't need you.

**Leave no trace—strictly:**

Everything you bring, you carry out. Every bit of waste, food packaging, toilet paper (bring WAG bags for human waste—bag it, carry it out). The plateau's ecosystem is precisely balanced. Introducing novel organics, chemicals, or physical structures disrupts that balance.

The stone shelters you might build are acceptable because they're made of local materials and will eventually weather back into the landscape. Your plastic waste is forever foreign.

**Observe without interfering:**

Don't touch the suspension structures. Don't attempt to remove or alter objects suspended in their fields. Don't collect beetles, disturb nests, approach the herd animals closely, or interfere with predator hunts (witnessing a kill is a privilege; honor it by remaining invisible).

Don't alter the observer-objects or test their zones extensively. Stand within them briefly if you're curious, then leave. Taking "measurements" or "samples" misses the point and probably violates something important even if you can't articulate what.

**Movement ethics:**

Stay on bare rock when possible. The vegetation is sparse because each plant has claimed specific territory where water reaches. Crushing a tussock of wire-grass might eliminate that plant's presence for years.

Walk around, not through, suspension structure fields. The threads are fragile.

Give the herd animals space. They're accustomed to human presence but not comfortable with it. Fifty meters minimum distance. If they alter their movement because of you, you're too close.

**Sound discipline:**

Speak quietly. Unnecessary noise is disrespectful to the silence, which is a feature of the place as real as the basalt. If you're visiting with others, cultivate comfort with quiet companionship.

No music devices, no unnecessary electronics noise. The suspension structures' harmonic humming is the sound-scape here; your playlist is invasive.

## Technical Equipment Considerations

**Photography:**

The clarity and light create extraordinary photographic conditions, but bring lens cloths—stone dust is pervasive. The suspension structures are difficult to photograph; their threads are nearly invisible except in specific light. Early morning and late afternoon when sun angles are low.

Extra batteries. The cold overnight, the intensive use, the altitude effects on battery chemistry—you'll use more power than expected.

**Communication:**

Satellite communication device if available—not for routine use, but for emergency. Cell phones won't work here. You're beyond infrastructure.

Leave a detailed trip plan with someone responsible. Expected return date, plus a margin after which they should initiate rescue procedures. Include plateau access routes, your planned shelter locations if possible.

**Lighting:**

Headlamp with extra batteries. The plateau's faint glow from suspension structures isn't enough for tasks. Red light mode for night vision preservation.

You don't need much artificial light—the starlight is remarkable—but when you need it, you really need it.

**Documentation:**

Notebook and pencils (pens can fail in cold or altitude). For observations, sketches, processing your experience.

The act of drawing what you see forces careful observation. You'll notice patterns and details that casual looking misses.

## Timing and Duration

**Seasonal considerations:**

The description doesn't specify seasons, but this matters. Research before going—are there periods of more reliable rain? Longer days (more solar heating, longer working time)? Temperature variations?

**Visit length:**

Minimum 5-7 days to make the acclimatization worthwhile and experience the rain cycle at least once. Maximum probably 14-21 days before supply constraints and psychological factors become pressing.

First-time visitors should err toward shorter stays. You can always return; you can't undo a crisis born from overextension.

**Buffer time:**

Add buffer days to your plan for weather delays, slower-than-expected acclimatization, or simply needing more time because the plateau changes your relationship with hurry.

## Physical Fitness Baseline

Beyond altitude conditioning, you need:
- Ability to walk 8-15 km daily over uneven terrain while carrying a pack
- Balance and proprioception for navigating smooth, undulating stone
- Core strength for maintaining stability with a weighted pack
- Flexibility for the range of motion required in stone-shelter building
- General resilience to minor discomfort

If you're uncertain about your fitness level, the plateau will answer definitively, and it's not a place where you want to discover limitations under duress.

## Mental Readiness Assessment

Ask yourself honestly:

- Can I make good decisions when my cognitive function is mildly impaired by altitude?
- Can I recognize when I'm making poor decisions (altitude affects judgment about judgment)?
- Do I trust my ability to self-rescue if something goes moderately wrong?
- Am I comfortable with real consequences for mistakes?
- Can I exist without external entertainment or constant stimulation?
- Will I honor this place's character even when it's inconvenient?

If any answer is "no" or "I'm not sure," reconsider or prepare more thoroughly.

## The Deeper Preparation: Orientation of Intent

Beyond physical and technical preparation, visiting Qen Mora requires psychological and perhaps spiritual orientation.

This isn't a destination you conquer or a challenge you overcome. It's a place you enter with humility, experience with attention, and leave with gratitude for having been tolerated.

The plateau offers extraordinary beauty, profound silence, and encounters with strangeness that expands your sense of what's possible. It offers these freely to visitors who understand the exchange: you receive wonder, you give respect and minimal impact.

Read accounts of others who've visited if they exist. Not to copy their experience but to calibrate expectations and learn practical wisdom.

**Practice attention:**

Before going, practice being present to what's actually in front of you rather than filtered through concepts, language, and preconceptions. The plateau rewards direct observation.

Learn to sit still for extended periods—an hour, two hours—just watching. The small changes, the subtle movements, the patterns that emerge only to patient observation.

**Study patience:**

Things happen on the plateau's timescale, not yours. Rain arrives when it arrives. Animals move through on their schedules. The observer-objects cycle through states over days. Fighting this with impatience achieves nothing and ruins your experience.

**Cultivate comfort with not-knowing:**

You won't understand what the suspension structures truly are. You won't decode the observer-objects' purposes. You won't fully grasp the plateau's ecology or the relationship between its natural and perhaps-managed elements.

This is fine. Mystery doesn't require solution. Wonder doesn't depend on comprehension.

## Emergency Protocols

Despite all preparation, emergencies happen.

**Evacuation triggers:**

Severe altitude sickness symptoms that don't improve with rest and medication. Significant injury. Contaminated water supply. Unexpected extreme weather. Critical equipment failure.

Have a clear mental protocol: recognize trigger condition → secure camp or abandon if necessary → move toward plateau edge → descend → seek help.

**Emergency descent:**

Know your routes off the plateau before you need them. Have them mapped, marked, rehearsed mentally. In crisis, with impaired cognition, you need automatic knowledge, not puzzle-solving.

Descent helps altitude sickness almost immediately—even a few hundred meters of elevation loss improves symptoms. Descend first, rest after.

**Signal protocols:**

If you have satellite communication, use it when appropriate—meaning genuine emergency, not discomfort or inconvenience.

Without communication devices, you're dependent on your trip plan's rescue timing. This is why leaving detailed plans with responsible people is non-negotiable.

## Return and Re-entry

The return to normal life after time on Qen Mora can be surprisingly difficult.

The constant input, the noise, the pace, the crowding—it feels overwhelming after the plateau's spaciousness and silence. Some people report a mourning period, missing the simplicity and scale.

Give yourself transition time. A day or two at moderate altitude before returning to sea level. Time to process the experience before re-engaging with daily demands.

Write about it, draw it, talk about it with people who'll listen carefully. The experience has weight; honor that.

## A Final Note on the Ineffable

Some aspects of visiting Qen Mora can't be prepared for because they can't be described adequately in advance. The exact quality of the light. The feeling of standing near an observer-object while stones arrange themselves into spirals. The sound of perfect silence when everything pauses simultaneously. The sense of being inside a system whose boundaries extend beyond the visible.

These experiences change people in small but real ways. You'll look at time differently afterward. You'll hear silence as presence rather than absence. You'll recognize when you're in a place that doesn't need you versus a place that welcomes you, and understand that both relationships have value.

The plateau doesn't care whether you visit or not. But if you go prepared—physically conditioned, properly equipped, mentally oriented, and humble in your presence—it will tolerate you, and in that tolerance, offer you something rare: a glimpse of a world operating according to logic that includes but transcends human understanding, beautiful not despite its strangeness but because of it.

Pack carefully. Train seriously. Go slowly. Watch closely. Leave quietly.

And when you return, carry the plateau's silence within you like a gift, something that reorients your relationship with noise and hurry and the illusion that human timescales are the primary measure of what matters.

The Suspension Fields of Qen Mora will be there, weathering slowly, long after you and everyone you know has returned to the elements. Your visit is a moment in its long existence. Make that moment worthy of the privilege.
