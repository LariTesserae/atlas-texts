# Preparation Guide for Visiting The Bone Pavilions

## Critical Pre-Departure Preparations

### Physical Conditioning (Begin 6-8 Months Before Departure)

**Auditory Adaptation:**
You must systematically desensitize yourself to constant sound. Begin by sleeping with white noise, then progress to layered tones, droning frequencies, and eventually dissonant harmonics. Use quality headphones to play sustained low-frequency tones (30-120 Hz) for increasing durations—start with 1 hour daily, build to 8-10 hours. Your goal is to reach a state where silence feels *wrong*. Practice all daily activities—eating, working, conversing—while exposed to loud, complex soundscapes. Learn to lipread and develop basic proficiency in a signed language (ASL or BSL). Your verbal communication skills will be nearly useless there.

**Photosensitivity Management:**
Spend time in extremely bright environments. Use full-spectrum lighting that mimics the Pavilions' sourceless illumination. Train yourself to function in constant brightness—remove blackout curtains, sleep with lights on, wear photochromic glasses that gradually reduce light-blocking over months. Your circadian rhythm will be devastated; begin adjusting now by maintaining wakefulness through light cues rather than darkness cycles. Consider consulting an ophthalmologist about protective measures that won't impair vision—you'll need to see clearly while squinting constantly.

**Thermal Tolerance:**
The constant humid warmth (approximately 85-95°F with high humidity) never relents. Spend time in saunas, steam rooms, and hot yoga studios. Your body needs to learn to function in perpetual sweat without the relief of cooling down. Practice remaining mentally clear while overheated. Reduce your baseline hydration needs through gradual adaptation—this is delicate and should be medically supervised.

**Vertigo and Height Acclimation:**
Everything is suspended. You'll live and move on swaying platforms forty feet above ground. Visit climbing gyms, practice on high ropes courses, spend time on boats to adapt to constant gentle motion. Work with a physical therapist to enhance your proprioception and balance in unstable environments.

### Medical Preparations

**Comprehensive Examination:**
Your heart must be sound. The constant vibrational exposure, particularly in lower tiers, can stress cardiac tissue. Get a full cardiac workup including stress tests and echocardiogram. Ensure no structural abnormalities that might resonate sympathetically with environmental frequencies.

**Auditory Assessment:**
Have baseline hearing tests. Understand that you *will* experience hearing damage. The question is how much and how quickly. Those with existing tinnitus often fare worse—the constant environmental noise interacts unpredictably with phantom sounds. Consider custom earplugs that attenuate specific frequencies, though residents may view this as marking you as perpetually "other."

**Respiratory Evaluation:**
The warm, humid air carrying plant-particulates and that strange mineral flatness will challenge your lungs. If you have any asthma, COPD, or chronic respiratory issues, reconsider this journey. The air quality is *different* rather than poor, but different can be dangerous.

**Psychological Screening:**
This is non-negotiable. You need evaluation for claustrophobia, sensory processing disorders, and general stress resilience. The environment provides *no escape*. No quiet room. No dark corner. No cool breeze. If you need environmental control to manage anxiety or other conditions, you will break. The statistics on newcomer psychological collapse are grim.

**Dental Work:**
Complete all dental procedures before departure. The constant vibration can cause existing dental problems to worsen rapidly. Fillings may loosen. Teeth with hairline cracks may split. There are no dentists in the Pavilions.

### Nutritional Preparation

Begin transitioning your diet months in advance. The plant-sap pulp that constitutes the primary food source is bitter, astringent, and nutritionally complete but texturally challenging. Practice eating pureed, pulpy foods. Reduce your reliance on variety—you'll be eating essentially the same thing daily. 

Gradually reduce your water intake, substituting thickened liquids (add pectin or starch to water). The sap is viscous; your body needs to learn to extract hydration from something closer to thin syrup than water. Work with a nutritionist to ensure you maintain electrolyte balance during this transition.

Vitamin D supplementation should begin now and continue through your stay, despite the intense light. The light *isn't sunlight*—it doesn't trigger vitamin D synthesis normally.

### Skill Acquisition

**Essential Skills (Begin Immediately):**

*Gestural Communication:* Become fluent in at least one signed language. Understand that the Pavilions' gesture-system is different, but the mental framework of spatial-linguistic thinking is transferable.

*Rope Work:* Take courses in rope access techniques, knot-tying, and rope safety. Your life will literally hang by ropes you've tied or that others have tied. You need to assess their integrity at a glance.

*Fiber Crafts:* Learn weaving, basketry, basic cordage. Skills are currency in the Pavilions. Arriving with demonstrable abilities in their primary craft forms will ease your integration enormously.

*Acoustic Awareness:* Study music theory, particularly harmonic series, difference tones, and resonance. Take up a droning instrument—shruti box, tanpura, or hurdy-gurdy. Learn to hear overtones and beat frequencies. Your survival may depend on detecting harmful resonant frequencies.

*Climbing:* Beyond mere height acclimation, learn actual climbing technique. You'll need to traverse vertical and inverted surfaces, moving between suspended structures.

**Recommended Skills:**
- Fire management in humid conditions
- Tool crafting from limited materials  
- Navigation without conventional landmarks
- Timekeeping by pattern recognition rather than celestial/mechanical means
- Meditation or practices for maintaining mental clarity under constant stimulation

## What to Bring

### The Hard Truth About Materials

Almost nothing from outside is useful there. The acoustic properties are wrong—materials resonate unexpectedly, sometimes dangerously. Items that seem innocuous may vibrate themselves to pieces or create acoustic dead-zones that mark you as disruptive.

**Acceptable Items:**

*Minimal Clothing:* Light, natural fibers. You'll be shedding these for locally-woven garments quickly, but initial modesty may matter to you. Nothing with metal—no zippers, buttons, or grommets. Metal resonates unpredictably.

*Medical Supplies:* Basic first aid items in non-metal containers. Medicines you absolutely require. Understand that temperature-sensitive medications may degrade in the constant warmth. Contraceptives if relevant—pregnancy in the Pavilions is *possible* but challenging for newcomers whose bodies aren't adapted.

*Documentation:* If you're inclined toward scholarship, bring paper (not spiral-bound) and graphite pencils. Ink may dry or run in the humidity. You might want to record observations, though don't expect to master the gestural language quickly enough to communicate complex ideas for months.

*Gifts:* This is delicate. The residents need nothing you can carry. However, small quantities of substances completely absent from the Pavilions—salt, certain spices, actual water—can serve as introductory gifts, acknowledging that you come from a world of different abundances.

### What NOT to Bring

- Electronics: The constant vibration destroys them; there's no power source anyway
- Books: The humidity ruins them rapidly
- Metal tools: Acoustically disruptive and unnecessary—bone tools are superior in that environment
- Glass: Too fragile for the constant motion and vibration
- Synthetic materials: Generally unpredictable acoustic properties
- Timepieces: Useless and psychologically counterproductive
- Weapons: Marking yourself as dangerous is suicidal in a society where cooperation is survival

## Arrival and Initial Adjustment

### The First Hours

You will arrive—through whatever means of multiverse travel you employ—likely in one of the edge-zones. The acoustic chaos will be immediately overwhelming. You may experience:

- Nausea and vertigo from the sound pressure
- Headache from the brightness
- Immediate profuse sweating
- Disorientation from the lack of conventional spatial references
- Panic from the absence of escape routes

Sit down immediately. Do not try to walk or explore. Close your eyes despite the brightness (bright through your eyelids is better than directly overwhelming your visual cortex). Breathe slowly. Begin humming—matching your own vibration to the environment helps some people acclimate faster.

Residents who encounter newcomers in the edge-zones will likely approach cautiously. They've seen this before. Remain calm. Attempt basic signed communication. Point to yourself, make peaceful gestures. They will assess whether you're viable—whether you'll survive adjustment or spiral into the madness that claims many newcomers.

If deemed viable, you'll be guided (not carried—you must move yourself) toward an established zone, likely lower-tier initially where the acoustic environment is simpler, more foundational. This journey may take hours. It will be exhausting.

### The First Weeks

**Priority One: Learn to Rest**
You must discover how to sleep in the sound. This is the first crisis point. Sleep deprivation will accelerate psychological breakdown. Residents will attempt to place you in a location with favorable harmonic properties for rest, but your physiology may not respond initially. 

Focus on the patterns. The sound isn't random—find the repetitions, the cycles. Let them become predictable rather than chaotic. Some travelers report success with counting beat frequencies, others with synchronized breathing to low tones.

**Priority Two: Hydration Adaptation**
The sap will revolt your palate initially. Drink it anyway. Dehydration is rapid in the constant warmth. Accept that you'll be coating your mouth with sticky residue. Let your saliva adjust. Residents may offer heated, thinned sap—this is kindness, though it doesn't taste better.

**Priority Three: Basic Gesture Vocabulary**
You need perhaps 50 core gestures immediately: yes/no, thank you, help, stop, food, water, rest, bathroom (waste-collection points are specific locations), up/down, safe/dangerous. Residents will teach you, but you must be an aggressive learner. Your integration timeline depends heavily on communication ability.

**Priority Four: Acoustic Awareness**
Begin learning to read the environment. Which zones feel merely loud versus actively painful? Where do people congregate versus avoid? Follow examples but start developing your own sensitivity. The beat frequencies that cause nausea, the harmonics that induce calm—these vary slightly by individual physiology.

### Weeks 2-8: Integration or Exit

This is the window. Either your body and mind adapt, or they don't. Warning signs of non-adaptation:

- Persistent insomnia beyond week 2
- Inability to tolerate sap-hydration (continued vomiting)
- Escalating anxiety or paranoia
- Auditory hallucinations beyond the environmental sounds
- Cardiac arrhythmias
- Uncontrolled tremors from constant vibrational exposure

If these persist, *leave*. The Pavilions will kill you, not maliciously but simply through incompatibility. Residents won't force you to stay, but they also won't help you leave—you arrived yourself, you must depart yourself.

Signs of successful adaptation:

- Sleeping 4-6 hours per cycle
- Retaining sap-nutrition
- Beginning to perceive harmonic patterns as information
- Reduced conscious effort in maintaining balance on suspended structures
- Spontaneous use of gesture in thinking, not just communication
- Moments of finding the sound *beautiful* rather than merely tolerable

## Long-Term Survival

### Physical Reality

Assume some permanent changes:

**Hearing Loss:** Inevitable. High-frequency discrimination will decline first. This is acceptable—the residents have significant hearing loss and function perfectly well.

**Circadian Disruption:** Your sleep cycle will never be quite normal again if you return to conventional environments. The adaptation to pattern-based rather than light-based cycles rewires deep brain structures.

**Thermal Regulation:** You'll become more heat-tolerant but may feel perpetually cold in normal climates upon return.

**Psychological Imprint:** The sound becomes part of your neurological baseline. Silence will feel *wrong*, possibly for the rest of your life.

### Social Integration

**Lower Tier Assignment:** As a newcomer, you'll likely be assigned to lower-tier work initially—cultivating plants, collecting sap, learning the fundamental crafts. This is not punishment but pedagogy. The lower tiers are acoustically simpler and physically harder. The work builds adaptation while preventing you from causing harm through ignorance in more complex zones.

**Mentorship:** If you show aptitude, a resident may take you as an apprentice in their specific craft or knowledge domain. This is the path to higher-tier life. Be humble, patient, and demonstrate genuine skill acquisition.

**Community Contribution:** You must become useful. The Pavilions operate on razor-thin margins—every person must contribute to food gathering, craft production, maintenance, or knowledge preservation. Pure observation or tourism isn't sustainable. Determine what skills you can develop and pursue them aggressively.

### Navigating the Culture

**Silence is Alien:** Never ask for quiet. The concept is literally incomprehensible and marks you as fundamentally other.

**Water is Mythical:** Residents may have stories about water—thin liquid that flows freely, that falls from the sky. These are origin tales, possibly metaphorical. Expressing longing for water is acceptable in the context of storytelling but not as complaint.

**The Bone is Sacred:** Not religiously necessarily, but as foundation. Don't deface it, don't unnecessarily harvest from it, treat it with respect.

**The Singers are Mystery:** If you see one, watch quietly. Don't attempt to catch, harm, or even approach. Following them is discouraged—they may lead to dangerous acoustic zones.

**Boundary Zones are Forbidden:** Not legally but practically. Going there marks you as either suicidal or dangerously curious. Neither is respected.

**Death is Practical:** If you witness death-processing, understand it's neither disrespectful nor gruesome within their context. Bodies become materials. This is continuation, not desecration.

### Advanced Considerations

**Acoustic Cartography:** If you develop sensitivity, you might contribute to the ongoing mapping of resonant zones. This is skilled, respected work. New dwellings can only be positioned by those who can predict harmonic properties.

**Plant Cultivation:** Understanding the growth cycles, harvest timing, and sap collection is core knowledge. Becoming expert in this is a reliable path to respected status.

**Tool Crafting:** If you have skill in working with limited materials, this is perpetually valuable. Innovation in tool design is highly regarded.

**Knowledge Keeping:** The upper-tier scholars maintain records of the city's history, growth patterns, astronomical observations (through the gaps), and accumulated understanding. If you can contribute to this—if you have knowledge from outside that translates to useful insight—this is perhaps the highest status available to outsiders.

## Psychological Preparation: The Deeper Truth

The Pavilions will change you fundamentally. Not metaphorically—literally. Your neural structures will rewire to process constant sound as baseline. Your endocrine system will adjust to the thermal and light conditions. Your social cognition will shift toward gestural-spatial processing. Your temporal perception will abandon circadian anchoring.

You must decide before going: **Are you willing to become a different version of yourself?**

Some questions to sit with:

- Can you find meaning in a life of essential activities—cultivation, craft, maintenance—without the distractions of conventional civilization?
- Can you experience beauty in constraint, artistry in limitation?
- Can you accept that leaving, if you successfully adapt, will be its own trauma? That you may never feel fully comfortable in silence again?
- Can you tolerate never knowing what the structure truly is? Never having answers to the fundamental mysteries?
- Can you accept reduced agency? The acoustic environment dictates where you can be, when you can rest, how you can build. Your will is secondary to physical law in immediate, constant ways.

If there's hesitation in these answers, reconsider the journey. The Pavilions are not hostile, but they are uncompromising. They don't adapt to you; you adapt to them or you break.

## Departure Planning

If you intend to return to conventional reality rather than remaining permanently:

**Limit Your Stay:** Begin planning departure by 6 months. Beyond a year, your adaptation may be so complete that leaving becomes psychologically impossible.

**Gradual Re-Adjustment:** Spend your last weeks in higher tiers if possible—more complex acoustics, brighter light. Then move to edge-zones, experiencing the chaos that's closer to the transition state. Don't leave from deep integration.

**Medical Support Upon Return:** Arrange for audiological support, psychological counseling, and possibly occupational therapy. You'll need help rebuilding circadian rhythms, readjusting to silence, managing the sensory absence.

**Documentation:** If you've kept notes, bring them out. The Pavilions have much to teach about adaptive architecture, acoustic ecology, and human physiological plasticity.

**Goodbyes:** The gesture for farewell is complex and profound. Learn it properly. People you've lived and worked with deserve proper acknowledgment of shared experience.

## Final Considerations

The Bone Pavilions represent a challenge that goes beyond physical endurance into questions of identity and adaptability. You're not visiting a place—you're potentially becoming a different kind of human, one suited to an environment utterly unlike the one your species evolved for.

Some people find this transformative in positive ways. They discover capacities they didn't know they had. They develop a sensitivity to sound and space that approaches a new sense. They find community in the shared challenge of survival in a world that should be impossible. They become part of something ancient and strange and beautiful in its own right.

Others find only suffering—a constant assault that erodes them until they flee or break.

The difference isn't purely about physical resilience or psychological hardiness, though these matter. It's about something harder to name: a capacity to find *rightness* in a profoundly alien context, to redefine normal rather than constantly measuring against what's absent.

Before you go, spend time in silence. Real silence—not suburban quiet with distant traffic, but deep silence if you can find it. A remote natural area, a sensory deprivation chamber, a soundproofed room. Sit with it. Notice how it feels. Then ask yourself if you're willing to never experience this again.

Because that's what the Pavilions will take from you: the possibility of silence. Everything else—the sound, the light, the warmth, the suspended structures, the strange food—you might adapt to. But silence, once you've acclimated to its absence, becomes unrecoverable.

If you can accept that price, then prepare thoroughly, travel carefully, and perhaps you'll find something remarkable in the city that hangs from bones, in the hum that never stops, in the bright warmth of an impossible place that somehow sustains human life.

And perhaps you'll return with the rarest gift: understanding of just how flexible human nature can be, how widely we can adapt, how many ways there are to be alive.

Or perhaps you'll stay, and become one of the bone-dwellers, moving through the resonant spaces with practiced grace, your old life fading into the category of dream, your new reality the constant song of the Pavilions.

Either way, you will be changed.

Go prepared for that above all else.
