# Preparation Guide for the Reservoir Stacks

## Physical Preparation

### Clothing and Gear
You need to prepare for persistent dampness without true wetness, cold without freezing. This is critical: standard outdoor gear will fail you here because it's designed for weather, and the Stacks aren't weather—they're a *condition*.

**Layer obsessively**, but with synthetic materials only. Cotton will become a cold, clammy shroud within hours. Merino wool is acceptable for base layers, but the moisture here doesn't behave normally—it doesn't soak in, it *adheres*. You want fabrics that dry quickly even in saturated air that never quite becomes rain.

Bring a thin, breathable waterproof shell, but understand it's protection against the wrongness of the moisture, not rain. You're defending against thermal theft—the way this place pulls warmth from you with patient efficiency.

**Footwear is critical**: You need boots with excellent grip on wet concrete and metal, but also shoes you can stand in for 10-12 hours daily without pain. The Stacks require constant walking, climbing, standing. Break them in completely before arrival—blisters here will become infected in ways that don't respond normally to treatment. The moisture sees to that. Bring backup boots. Bring foot powder. Bring more socks than you think reasonable, in synthetic materials that dry overnight.

You'll need gloves, but fingerless or with convertible covers—you must touch things here to understand them, but metal surfaces conduct away heat with predatory intent. The handrails will hurt after a while.

### Equipment

**Navigation tools will lie to you.** Bring them anyway, so you can learn the specific ways they fail. A compass will drift. GPS will give coordinates that don't form coherent geometries. Maps you make will be accurate but won't tessellate properly—you'll draw the same intersection three times from three approaches and get three different angles that can't exist in Euclidean space.

Instead, bring:
- **Waterproof notebooks and pencils** (not pens—ink runs in the moisture). You'll develop your own notation system for landmarks that shift.
- **Bright, waterproof markers** to leave temporary trail signs. They'll fade or vanish, but they'll help for a while.
- **A good watch with multiple timers and alarms**. You'll need to externalize time-tracking because your internal sense will corrupt. Set alarms for eating, drinking, resting—maintenance routines for your body that you won't remember to perform otherwise.
- **A headlamp with extensive battery backup**, though the Stacks are never truly dark. There are moments of disorientation where light helps anchor you.
- **Binoculars** for watching distant platforms, distant watchers. You won't reach them, but observing them is part of learning the place.

**Do not bring rope** with intent to do technical climbing. The geometry doesn't reward that kind of approach. The catwalks go where you need to go, or they don't, and rope won't help. It will only tempt you into dangerous interpretations of space.

### Provisions

The water is drinkable, but **bring water purification as psychological comfort**, even if you won't use it. Bring **electrolyte supplements**—the water is pure enough that you'll develop deficiencies drinking only it.

Food is more complex. You need high-calorie, shelf-stable provisions that won't be ruined by dampness. Think:
- **Vacuum-sealed nuts, dried fruit, energy bars**
- **Peanut butter in plastic jars**
- **Hard cheeses in wax**
- **Dried meats**
- **Instant coffee or tea** (there will be water to heat, sometimes—the pipes)

Avoid anything that requires complicated preparation. Your cooking will be minimal: heating water on warm pipes, mostly. The birds will watch you eat, and you should leave small offerings—crumbs, scraps. This isn't superstition; it's diplomacy. They're part of the system, and you're asking permission to be here too.

Bring **twice as many calories as you think you need**. The cold drains you. The constant elevation changes drain you. The cognitive load of impossible geometry drains you. You'll be hungry in ways that surprise you.

## Medical Preparation

**See a doctor before you go.** Get blood work. Ensure you're in decent cardiovascular health—you'll be climbing constantly. Check your vestibular system; if you're prone to vertigo, the Stacks will amplify it disastrously.

### First Aid Kit Essentials
- **Blister treatment supplies**: moleskin, liquid bandage, good tape
- **Antifungal everything**: powder, cream, spray. The moisture breeds fungal infections in any skin fold, any crevice.
- **Antiseptic for cuts**—but understand that wounds here heal strangely, either much faster or much slower than expected
- **Pain relievers** for the constant low-grade aches
- **Antihistamines** in case the mosses or chemical blooms trigger allergies
- **Moisture barrier cream** for any skin exposed to constant dampness
- **Dental supplies**: temporary filling material, oral pain relief. Dental problems can't wait for extraction from the Stacks.

**Prescription considerations**: Bring extras of any critical medications in waterproof containers, distributed across multiple locations in your gear. Time moves strangely—a three-day supply might need to last subjectively longer.

If you take psychiatric medications, **do not skip doses**. The Stacks will unhinge certain cognitive frameworks; you want your neurochemistry as stable as possible. This isn't a place to have a mental health crisis.

## Psychological Preparation

This is harder to prescribe, but more important than any gear.

**Practice non-reaction.** Meditate, if that's your framework, or find another way to build equanimity. You will see things that violate your understanding of physics, space, causality. Your options are: accept, observe, and continue; or panic and become lost in both the literal and psychological senses.

The Stacks will attack your sense of continuity—of space, time, and self. Prepare by:

**Keeping a journal religiously.** Write down what you did, what you saw, what you think happened. The act of writing creates continuity even when memory becomes unreliable. Date entries even though dates will become suspect. The discipline matters more than the accuracy.

**Establishing mantras or check-in phrases.** Simple things: your name, where you're from, why you came. Repeat them daily. The place wants to absorb you into its processes—not maliciously, but the way water absorbs a drop. Maintain your boundaries through deliberate self-assertion.

**Accepting that you will not understand everything.** This is not a puzzle to solve. The Stacks operate on principles that are rigorous but alien. Watching them work is like watching a machine when you don't know what it's for—you can observe patterns, note cycles, track flows, but the purpose remains opaque. Make peace with that opacity before you arrive.

**Learning to be alone with yourself.** The watchers don't interact. The birds aren't companions. You'll spend days with only your thoughts. If you don't like your own company, this will be hard. Prepare by spending time in isolation beforehand—day-long solo hikes, overnight camps alone. Learn what your mind does without external stimulus.

## Social/Political Preparation

**Tell someone where you're going**, but understand they probably can't extract you. Leave detailed plans, even though you know the plans will prove inadequate. Set expected return dates. Arrange check-ins if possible, though communication from the Stacks is... difficult.

**Research if there's any bureaucracy around the Stacks.** Sometimes these places have custodians, official or otherwise. Sometimes there are permits, or at least people who expect to be notified. The watchers might be such people, or might not be people at all. Better to know what's known before you go.

**Consider your legal status.** Are you trespassing? Is this place claimed, managed, regulated? The Stacks have the feel of infrastructure, which implies someone built them, someone might maintain them, someone might have jurisdiction. Or had jurisdiction, once, and still technically does even though the maintenance has been outsourced to birds and autonomous concrete.

Check if others have gone before you. Trip reports, if they exist, will be contradictory—everyone experiences different geometries—but they'll give you a sense of common experiences, dangers, maybe how long people typically stay before needing to leave.

## Practical Acclimatization

**Don't go deep immediately.** Enter at the lowest, most stable sections and spend days there. Learn how the water moves. Watch the birds. Develop your intuition for when basins will fill. The place has a rhythm, and you need to feel it in your body before you venture into the more unstable higher sections.

**Map what you can**, knowing the maps won't be complete or consistent. The act of mapping is itself a form of relationship with the space. You're showing attention, patience, respect. The Stacks respond to that, or seem to—routes become more stable, catwalks appear when needed, or at least that's the impression.

**Sleep regularly.** Set a schedule and keep it even when the light doesn't change appropriately. Your circadian rhythm is an anchor. Don't let it drift.

**Eat on schedule** for the same reason. These bodily routines are threads connecting you to normal causality.

**Watch the birds carefully.** They're your teachers. Where they nest is safe. Routes they fly regularly are stable—or as stable as anything gets. When they behave strangely, be alert; something is shifting.

**Approach the watchers slowly** if you want to try contact, but don't expect response. They might be researchers, might be maintenance workers, might be something else. They might not be there in the sense you're there. Stand near them for a while. Observe what they observe. Sometimes insight comes from parallel attention rather than interaction.

## What to Expect in Your Body

**You will be cold.** Not dangerously so, but persistently. Your core temperature will drop slightly and stay there. You'll adapt, but expect to feel chilled for the first week.

**You will be tired.** The constant navigation of impossible geometry is cognitively exhausting. The alertness required—watching for platform gaps, tracking water levels, monitoring your own position—is draining. Sleep will be deep and full of strange, procedural dreams.

**Your sense of balance will be challenged.** The perspectives are wrong, the horizons don't line up, the gravity seems heavier or lighter in inconsistent ways. Some people get nauseated. Ginger helps. So does keeping your eyes on near objects rather than distant vistas.

**Your skin will change texture** from the constant moisture—slightly puffy, very soft, perpetually damp. This is normal. Keep it clean and dry it as much as possible during rest periods.

**You may lose weight** despite eating well—the cold, the activity, the strange metabolic demands. Don't diet here. Eat whenever hungry.

**Your time sense will corrupt.** Hours will feel like days or minutes. This is why the watch and alarms matter. Trust the external timekeeper, not your gut.

## What to Expect in Your Mind

**Acceptance of impossibility becomes routine.** By day three, water flowing upward is just... something that happens. You'll stop flinching. This is good—it means you're adapting. But stay alert that you're adapting; don't lose all critical framework.

**You'll start thinking in the Stacks' logic.** Dreaming in floor plans, noticing patterns in basin depths, intuiting when platforms will shift. This is also adaptation. It's useful. But journal regularly to maintain your outside-perspective.

**Loneliness will come and go.** Sometimes the isolation is peaceful—you and the water and the birds in quiet coexistence. Sometimes it's crushing. Ride it out. Talk to yourself if it helps. Talk to the birds. They won't answer, but the sound of a human voice, even your own, can be grounding.

**You may experience something like reverence.** The Stacks have a quality—not quite sacred, but profound. The sense of vast systems operating just beyond comprehension, of purpose without personality, of pattern for pattern's sake. Let yourself feel it. It's part of why you came.

**You might not want to leave.** This is the danger people don't mention. The Stacks are strange but they're coherent in their strangeness. They're difficult but they're honest. The water doesn't lie, the platforms don't pretend. After weeks of this clarity, the normal world feels chaotic, deceptive. People get stuck not because they can't find an exit but because exits stop feeling relevant.

**Set a hard deadline before you go.** Write it down. Tell your emergency contact. "I will leave by [date], whether I want to or not." Honor that commitment.

## Special Considerations

**Photography will be difficult.** The light is too uniform, the surfaces too reflective. Your photos will be overexposed or strangely flat. Bring a camera if you must, but don't depend on it for documentation. Sketches and written descriptions will serve you better.

**The objects you find—the strange tools, the clipboards—don't take them.** Observe, photograph if you can, describe in your journal, but leave them where they are. They're part of the system. Removing them is... rude isn't the right word. Disruptive, maybe. You'll feel it if you try.

**If you see yourself on a distant platform,** don't panic. Note the location, note what the other you is doing, then continue your business. These encounters are rare but documented. They resolve without paradox if you don't force interaction.

**The hard-shelled creatures are not dangerous** but don't handle them roughly. They're doing something—maintenance, measurement, mediation. Treat them as you'd treat small workers you're sharing space with: polite distance, careful not to step on them, mild apology if you do.

**If a watcher finally speaks to you,** listen carefully. It may happen only once. What they say will be relevant, but often cryptically. Write it down immediately, exactly as spoken.

## Exit Strategy

**The way in is not the way out,** usually. You'll need to find an edge, a real edge, not just a horizon. These exist but they're not stable. Sometimes they're at the lowest levels, sometimes high up. Sometimes they're in the middle of a platform complex, a gap that leads to... elsewhere.

**You'll know an exit when you see one** because it will feel wrong—less real than the Stacks, less present, like a stage prop. It will pull at you psychologically. That pull is your cue.

**Don't leave in crisis.** Don't leave panicked, injured, desperate. Those exits lead to bad places. Leave when you're calm, provisioned, relatively healthy. Leave deliberately.

**The Stacks will let you go** if you go properly. There's no malice here, no imprisonment. But leaving is still navigation, still requires attention and right timing.

## After You Leave

**You will be disoriented for days.** The normal world will feel too chaotic, too warm, too loud, too inconsistent in stranger ways than the Stacks were. Give yourself recovery time. Don't drive for at least 24 hours. Don't make major decisions for a week.

**You will miss it.** The quiet, the cool, the vast patient processes. This is normal. Write about it. Talk about it with others who've been to similar places. Don't go back immediately.

**Your sense of space will be changed.** You'll notice architecture differently—water systems, drainage, the skeleton of infrastructure that underlies daily life. You'll see echoes of the Stacks in parking garages, in water treatment plants, in the guts of buildings. This is a gift and a curse. The mundane will never be quite mundane again.

**Some people can't reintegrate.** They go back to the Stacks permanently, become watchers themselves perhaps. This is a choice, but make it consciously, not by default or desperation.

## Final Thoughts

The Reservoir Stacks are not actively hostile, but they are utterly indifferent to your human needs and human understanding. They are a system engaged in process—storing, routing, cycling water through impossible geography according to rigorous but alien principles. You can move through them safely if you:

- **Stay physically prepared and maintained**
- **Keep your mind flexible but bounded**
- **Respect the place's rules even when you don't understand them**
- **Maintain your identity and exit timeline**
- **Accept mystery without either dismissing it or being consumed by it**

You will not conquer the Stacks. You will not solve them. You will not map them completely or understand their purpose. You can only witness them, move through them carefully, and emerge changed.

Pack well. Journal constantly. Watch the birds. Trust the water more than you trust your eyes. Leave when your deadline comes.

And when you're standing on a catwalk at the height that isn't the height, looking down at basins that reflect a sky that's too bright, listening to birds that click in subsonic frequencies while water flows upward into pools that hold more than they should, when you feel the vast quiet mechanism working around and through you—

Breathe. Observe. Continue.

That's all anyone can do there. It's enough.
