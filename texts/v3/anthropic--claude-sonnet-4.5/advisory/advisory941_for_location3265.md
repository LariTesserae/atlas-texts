# Preparation Guide for The Hanging Gardens of Stillwater

## CRITICAL UNDERSTANDING: What You're Actually Visiting

Before anything else, understand this: you are not visiting a ruin with some plants in it. You are entering a mature, stable ecosystem that happens to include human-built structures. The drowned city is substrate now, not destination. Your relationship here is as a temporary guest in a functional biological community that has been establishing itself for decades.

The water is not an obstacle—it IS the place. You will be wet constantly. Accept this now, psychologically, before you go. Half your time will be spent in water, the other half will be spent damp. There is no "staying dry" option that allows you to actually experience Stillwater.

## Physical Conditioning (Begin 3-4 Months Before)

**Swimming Capability - Non-Negotiable**
You must be a confident, efficient swimmer capable of:
- 30+ minutes of continuous easy swimming without panic or exhaustion
- Relaxed breath-hold diving to 20 feet (the street level)
- Treading water while performing tasks with your hands
- Swimming in cluttered environments where you can't see more than 10-15 feet ahead
- Recovering from disorientation when you surface in unexpected places

If you cannot do these things, you will spend your entire visit in low-grade fear, confined to shallow platforms, unable to access 60% of what makes this place extraordinary. The community there will not be impressed by someone who needs constant supervision.

Start training immediately. Not lap swimming in a pool—that teaches the wrong things. You need:
- Open water swimming in lakes or calm ocean
- Free-diving practice (take a basic freediving course)
- Swimming through kelp forests if possible, or through areas with obstacles
- Practice navigating underwater with limited visibility

**Cardiovascular Baseline**
The altitude (suggested by cool water and climate descriptions) means thinner air. You need solid cardio:
- Comfortable hiking for 4-6 hours at whatever altitude you can access
- Recovery heart rate under 100 after exertion
- No gasping or dizziness when climbing ladders repeatedly

**Upper Body and Core Strength**
You'll be:
- Pulling yourself onto platforms from water (no stairs, no shallow end)
- Climbing rope and wood ladders, some quite vertical
- Paddling canoes or dugouts for extended periods
- Maintaining stability on floating, wobbling platforms

Train with:
- Pull-ups and dead hangs (can you pull your body weight from water onto a platform edge?)
- Core stability work on unstable surfaces
- Rope climbing if you can access it
- Paddling practice—hours of it, not minutes

**Flexibility and Comfort in Awkward Positions**
Much movement here is three-dimensional and ungraceful:
- Squeezing through window frames
- Contorting around plant stems
- Climbing while wet with unreliable handholds
- Maintaining balance on narrow beams and logs

Yoga, climbing, or any practice that makes you comfortable being folded, extended, and off-balance in space will serve you.

## Water Competency and Safety

**Breath-Hold Training**
Take this seriously. A basic freediving course (SSI or AIDA Level 1) will teach you:
- How to actually relax underwater instead of fighting panic
- Proper equalization for descent (your ears WILL hurt at 20 feet if you don't know this)
- Recognition of your real limits vs. imagined ones
- Emergency procedures if something goes wrong

Practice until you can:
- Comfortably hold your breath for 90+ seconds static
- Swim 50+ feet underwater horizontally
- Descend to 20 feet, stay for 30 seconds looking around, ascend calmly
- Do all of this without the desperate thrashing that marks an amateur

**Navigation in Low Visibility**
The 10-15 foot visibility means you'll constantly be swimming into spaces where you can't see what's ahead. Practice:
- Swimming with eyes closed in safe pool environments to build proprioception
- Navigating obstacle courses underwater
- Maintaining directional sense when you can't see the surface
- Finding exits when you've entered a confined space

**Understanding Currents and Water Movement**
Though minimal, currents exist here. Learn to:
- Recognize how water moves around obstacles
- Use current to help rather than fight it
- Identify where water will be still vs. moving
- Predict how your body will drift while stationary

## Environmental Adaptation

**Temperature Acclimatization**
60°F (15.5°C) water is cold. Not hypothermia-in-minutes cold, but cold enough that:
- After 30 minutes immobile you'll be shivering
- After 2+ hours you'll have genuine heat loss
- Your hands will get clumsy
- Your thinking will slow if you get too cold

Preparation:
- If possible, start taking cold showers (finish with 2-3 minutes of cold water)
- Practice swimming in cool water (under 65°F) to understand your body's response
- Learn to recognize early hypothermia in yourself (confusion, clumsiness, apathy)
- Understand that you'll need to exit water regularly to rewarm

**Humidity Adaptation**
The constant moisture affects you more than you expect:
- Skin softens and becomes prone to cuts and infection
- Feet will macerate if constantly wet
- Respiratory comfort shifts—some people struggle with very humid air

If you live in a dry climate, spend time in humid environments first. Notice how your body regulates differently.

**Altitude Consideration**
The cool water temperatures and climate suggest significant elevation. If you live at sea level:
- Arrive several days early to acclimatize if possible
- Expect to tire more easily for the first 48 hours
- Stay hydrated aggressively
- Avoid alcohol during acclimatization

## Technical Skills to Learn

**Basic Boating**
You must be able to:
- Paddle a canoe or small boat competently for 1-2 hours
- Board and exit small boats from water without capsizing
- Maintain stability in tippy watercraft
- Recover if you do capsize (because you will)

Take a basic canoeing or kayaking course. Practice wet exits and re-entries.

**Rope Work**
Learn basic knots and rope handling:
- Bowline (for securing yourself or gear)
- Clove hitch (for temporary attachments)
- Figure-eight (for creating hand/foot holds)
- How to coil and throw rope accurately
- How to ascend a fixed rope safely

This is primitive tech territory—no fancy ascenders or carabiners. You need to work with simple cordage confidently.

**Plant Identification Basics**
Not specific species (you won't find these plants elsewhere), but the skill of:
- Observing plant structure and deducing strength
- Identifying live vs. dead wood
- Recognizing which stems will hold weight
- Understanding seasonal growth patterns
- Reading plant health to predict stability

Spend time in aquatic environments or wetlands. Learn what healthy vs. rotting aquatic vegetation looks like.

## Psychological Preparation

**Comfort with Immersion**
Many people have deep, unexamined discomfort with being in water where they can't see the bottom or where things brush against them. This place will trigger that constantly.

Work on:
- Swimming in murky water where you can't see your hands
- Letting plants/kelp touch you without panic
- Being comfortable with fish swimming near/around you
- Existing in spaces where you can't immediately exit to land

If you have any level of thalassophobia (fear of deep water) or submechanophobia (fear of submerged objects), address this before going. Exposure therapy in safe environments. Professional help if needed.

**Three-Dimensional Orientation**
Most humans live in two-dimensional space with occasional vertical elements. Here, up/down matter as much as forward/back/left/right.

Build comfort with:
- Swimming under things (under docks, under boats)
- Inverting your body orientation (diving down, looking up)
- Navigating spaces where you enter at one level and exit at another
- Losing sight of the surface and being okay with it

Practice in swimming pools: swim down, sit on the bottom, look up at the surface. Build the neural pathways that say "this is fine."

**Patience with Slow Pace**
Everything here takes longer than on land:
- Travel is slower (swimming/paddling vs. walking)
- Tasks are harder (working while wet, managing buoyancy)
- Sight lines are shorter (can't see far through water or dense plants)

If you're someone who gets frustrated with inefficiency, work on this. Meditation helps. So does spending time in wild places where nature sets the pace.

**Comfort with Constant Wildlife**
Animals will be everywhere, always. In your peripheral vision. Touching you. Making noise. 

If you're squeamish about:
- Fish brushing your legs
- Frogs jumping near you
- Birds flying close overhead
- Snakes (even harmless ones) swimming past
- Insects landing on you constantly
- Leeches (likely present though not mentioned—expect them)

You need to desensitize yourself. Spend time in wetland environments. Let the visceral reactions fade through repeated, safe exposure.

## Medical and Health Preparation

**Vaccinations and Prophylaxis**
Waterborne environments carry risks:
- Update tetanus (you'll get cuts on submerged metal/wood)
- Ensure typhoid vaccination (waterborne illness)
- Hepatitis A (water/food contamination)
- Consider prophylactic antibiotics if you have access (for immediate use if infection starts)

**Ear Protection**
You'll be in water constantly, diving repeatedly:
- Learn proper ear clearing techniques
- Use alcohol/vinegar drops after water exposure to prevent swimmer's ear
- If you're prone to ear infections, consult a doctor about protective measures
- Consider custom-fitted swimmer's earplugs (not foam—they'll just trap water)

**Skin Protection**
Constant moisture creates problems:
- Antifungal powder for feet (prevent trench foot/athlete's foot)
- Barrier cream for areas prone to chafing
- Antiseptic for immediate cut treatment (everything here is biologically rich—cuts will infect)
- Understanding of when a cut needs to be kept dry (means leaving the water entirely)

**Fitness Assessment**
Get a physical before going:
- Cardiac health (exertion + altitude + cold water)
- Respiratory function (humid air + altitude)
- Joint stability (constant climbing/swimming stresses joints differently)
- Any condition that compromises circulation (cold water + exertion)

Be honest with your doctor about the environment. "Swimming in cold water at altitude for extended periods in a primitive setting."

**Medication Considerations**
If you take any medications:
- Ensure they're stable in humid, warm conditions
- Have backups (everything gets wet)
- Understand how your conditions affect exertion/cold/altitude
- Some medications increase photosensitivity (you'll be outside constantly)

## Gear Preparation

**Clothing Philosophy**
Forget staying dry. Optimize for:
- Quick drying
- Minimal chafing when wet
- Sun protection
- Not trapping heat when active

**Primary Clothing Kit**
- Synthetic or merino wool swimwear as your base layer (cotton kills—it stays wet and cold)
- Long-sleeve sun shirt (rash guard style) in dark color (shows dirt less)
- Quick-dry shorts/pants that won't balloon with water
- Wide-brimmed hat that floats and won't blow off (chin strap)
- Lightweight sun hoodie for additional protection
- One set of truly dry clothes sealed in waterproof container—for sleeping only

**Footwear - Critical**
You need shoes that:
- Work in water and on land
- Protect from sharp submerged objects
- Drain instantly
- Provide grip on wet wood and slippery stone
- Won't fall off when swimming

Options:
- Quality water shoes with substantial sole (not cheap aqua socks)
- Approach shoes that drain well
- Lightweight hiking sandals with back strap

Bring two pairs. Alternate daily so each can fully dry. Expect to trash them—this environment is brutal on footwear.

**Sleep System**
The community likely has shelters, but bring:
- Lightweight sleeping bag rated to 40°F (will be damp, so synthetic, not down)
- Sleeping pad (closed-cell foam—will stay dry)
- Mosquito netting or bug bivy
- Small tarp (10x10) for creating personal space/shelter

**Water Protection for Essentials**
- Multiple dry bags in different sizes (they fail—have backups)
- Pelican-style hard cases for anything truly critical
- Understand that "waterproof" is aspirational in this environment
- Silica gel packets to fight humidity (replace them regularly)

**Personal Hygiene**
- Biodegradable soap (you're living in the water source)
- Quick-dry towel (small—you won't be getting dry anyway)
- Toothbrush/paste
- Nail clippers (keeping nails short prevents tears and infection)
- Comb (wet hair tangles constantly)
- Menstrual products if relevant (plan for difficulty managing this in water)

**Sun Protection**
- Reef-safe sunscreen (you're swimming in the ecosystem)
- Lip balm with SPF
- Polarized sunglasses with retention strap (crucial—you can't see into water without them)
- Backup cheap sunglasses (you'll lose the first pair)

**First Aid - Expanded**
Beyond basic first aid, emphasize:
- Waterproof bandages (many—regular bandages dissolve instantly)
- Antiseptic (iodine or similar)
- Antifungal cream and powder
- Antihistamines (for insect bites/stings)
- Pain relievers
- Tweezers (for splinters—you'll get many)
- Nail brush (for cleaning wounds before treatment)
- Sterile irrigation syringe (for flushing deep cuts)
- Israeli bandage or similar (for serious bleeding far from help)

**Tools**
- Knife (fixed blade, stainless, with sheath that retains when upside-down)
- Multi-tool
- Headlamp (waterproof, with red light mode)
- Lighter in waterproof case (multiple—fire is precious here)
- 50ft paracord
- Whistle (for emergencies—voices don't carry well over water sounds)

**Documentation/Navigation**
- Waterproof notebook and pencil (pen ink runs)
- Camera in waterproof housing (if you care about images)
- Watch (simple, waterproof to 100m)
- Compass (as backup—easy to get disoriented)

**What NOT to Bring**
- Electronics (will die from humidity)
- Books (will mildew)
- Leather anything (will rot)
- Cotton clothing (stays wet, causes hypothermia)
- Valuable jewelry (will lose it)
- Rigid expectations (will frustrate you)

## Food and Water Preparation

**Water Situation**
The water is described as fresh and drinkable, but:
- Biological richness means bacteria/parasites are likely
- Bring water purification (filter + chemical backup)
- Plan to treat all drinking water
- Understand the community's water protocols—don't contaminate their source

**Food Reality**
This is a fishing community with primitive tech:
- Diet will be fish-heavy (if you're vegetarian/vegan, this is a problem)
- Supplemented with foraged plants and limited agriculture
- No refrigeration
- Prepare for repetitive, simple meals

Before going:
- If you have dietary restrictions, bring supplemental food (fishing line, knowledge to catch your own)
- Practice eating unfamiliar foods (lake fish, water plants, simple grains)
- Learn basic fishing if you don't know
- Understand fire-building (if you want hot food, you may need to cook it)

**Nutritional Considerations**
Cold water immersion + physical activity = high caloric needs:
- You'll burn 3,000-4,000+ calories daily
- Bring calorie-dense emergency foods (nut butters, energy bars, etc.)
- Expect to be hungry
- Fat is your friend—hard to get enough in this environment

## Cultural and Social Preparation

**Community Integration**
You're entering an established, functioning community:
- They've been here longer than you can imagine
- They've solved problems you haven't thought of yet
- You have nothing to teach them about living here

Appropriate mindset:
- Observe before acting
- Ask before touching/using anything
- Offer help, but accept direction on how
- Learn names immediately
- Contribute work before asking for guidance/stories
- Accept that you'll be bad at everything at first—be humble about it

**Skill Offering**
What can you provide?
- Medical knowledge (if you have it)
- Different perspective on problems
- Extra hands for group tasks
- Stories/news from outside
- Trade goods if you brought appropriate items

**Language**
No indication of language spoken—assume possible barrier:
- Learn basic signs/gestures beforehand
- Be patient with communication
- Visual demonstration often works better than words
- Humor transcends language—be willing to laugh at yourself

**Social Rhythm**
Watch and match:
- When do people wake/sleep?
- When are communal meals?
- What spaces are private vs. shared?
- What activities are individual vs. group?
- When is talking welcome vs. silence preferred?

**Children**
If there are children (likely in a stable community):
- They'll be far more competent in this environment than you
- Don't condescend
- They may be your best teachers
- Watch them to learn how things are really done

## Skill Practice Before Arrival

**Navigation Skills**
- Map reading (though you won't have detailed maps)
- Dead reckoning (counting strokes while swimming)
- Using sun position and time
- Remembering routes in complex 3D space

**Observation Skills**
Practice truly seeing:
- Where are animals? (learn to spot wildlife)
- Which plants are healthy? (learn to read vegetation)
- Where is water moving? (learn to read surface patterns)
- What sounds mean what? (learn to interpret audio environment)

Spend time in any natural area and practice noticing everything. This is a survival skill in a place where reading the environment is crucial.

**Hand Skills**
Practice working with simple tools:
- Whittling/carving
- Rope work
- Basic repairs without power tools
- Working while wet/cold (much harder than it sounds)

## Mental Framework Development

**Comfort with Uncertainty**
You won't know:
- Exactly where you'll sleep
- Exactly what you'll eat
- Exactly what each day holds
- When/if you can leave

Practice releasing the need for detailed plans. Develop comfort with "I'll figure it out" as a strategy.

**Risk Calibration**
Everything here carries small risks:
- Every platform could fail
- Every dive could go wrong
- Every animal could be dangerous
- Every plant could harbor hazards

But these are managed risks in a functional system. Learn to:
- Assess actual vs. perceived danger
- Take reasonable precautions without paranoia
- Act decisively once you've evaluated risk
- Accept that zero risk isn't an option

**Sensory Openness**
This place will assault your senses:
- Constant water sounds
- High humidity
- Things touching you always
- Limited visual range
- Strong organic smells

Practice sensory acceptance:
- Meditation focusing on accepting sensation without judgment
- Time in sensorially intense environments
- Letting go of the need for sensory control

**Time Relationship**
Clock time will matter less:
- Days blend together
- Sun position matters more than hours
- Task completion matters more than schedule

Practice letting go of time anxiety. Leave your watch buried in your bag after the first day.

## Specific Scenario Preparation

**Scenario: You Fall Into Water Between Buildings**
Practice:
- Not panicking during unexpected immersion
- Orienting yourself underwater quickly
- Finding which way is up if disoriented (bubbles rise)
- Swimming to nearest solid object
- Pulling yourself out of water without steps/ladder

**Scenario: You Get Tangled in Underwater Plants**
Practice:
- Stopping movement immediately (thrashing tightens entanglement)
- Slow, deliberate movements to free yourself
- Controlled breathing/not panicking
- Using knife to cut free if necessary
- Surfacing safely after entanglement

**Scenario: You Get Lost in the Submerged Structures**
Practice:
- Maintaining directional sense in confined spaces
- Following walls to exits
- Not panicking when you can't immediately see the way out
- Emergency ascent procedures if you must surface immediately

**Scenario: You're Separated from the Group**
- Staying put vs. trying to find them
- Signaling for attention (voice, whistle)
- Basic survival for 24 hours alone
- Not doing anything stupid out of fear

**Scenario: Someone Else Is in Trouble**
- Water rescue basics (don't become a second victim)
- How to help without endangering yourself
- When to get help vs. act immediately
- Basic CPR/drowning response

Take a water safety course that covers these scenarios.

## Pre-Visit Checklist (Final Month)

**4 Weeks Out:**
- Begin cold water swimming practice (3x per week minimum)
- Start altitude training if possible (sleep at elevation, or use altitude simulation)
- Practice breath-hold diving in real conditions
- Test all gear in actual water

**3 Weeks Out:**
- Increase swimming duration (building endurance for 2+ hour sessions)
- Practice working with wet hands (everything is harder—experience it)
- Test your packing (ensure everything fits, nothing forgotten)
- Waterproof important documents (passport, etc.)

**2 Weeks Out:**
- Final medical check
- Begin taking probiotics (gut health important for new food/water)
- Practice daily hygiene routine with your actual gear
- Review knots and rope work

**1 Week Out:**
- Taper physical training (rest before exertion)
- Final gear check (repair/replace anything questionable)
- Mental preparation (meditation, visualization)
- Review emergency protocols

**Day Before:**
- Pack fully
- Good sleep
- Hydrate heavily
- Eat well (you'll be burning through reserves)

## Arrival Protocol

**First 24 Hours:**
- Don't enter water immediately—observe
- Watch how locals move, what they touch, where they go
- Note daily rhythms before inserting yourself
- Find the bathroom situation (crucial—where do people deal with waste?)
- Locate drinking water source
- Identify emergency gathering point

**First Week:**
- Stay in shallow areas
- Don't dive to street level until you've watched others do it
- Volunteer for simple tasks (food prep, gear maintenance)
- Learn names and roles
- Map the accessible area in your mind
- Find your cold tolerance limits
- Identify which platforms are most stable

## Red Flags and When to Leave

**Physical Red Flags:**
- Persistent cough (mold/humidity affecting lungs)
- Infected cuts that won't heal
- Hypothermia symptoms that don't resolve with warming
- Severe dehydration despite drinking
- Altitude sickness that worsens

**Psychological Red Flags:**
- Persistent anxiety that doesn't diminish with familiarity
- Intrusive thoughts about drowning
- Inability to sleep due to environmental factors
- Feeling trapped/desperate

**Social Red Flags:**
- Community seems hostile or unwelcoming
- You're asked to leave (directly or indirectly)
- Conflict you can't resolve
- Feeling unsafe around certain individuals

**Environmental Red Flags:**
- Water quality suddenly changes (color, smell)
- Mass fish die-off
- Structural failures increasing
- Weather patterns turning dangerous

Know before you go: what would make you leave? What's your exit plan? How do you get out if you need to?

## Long-term Considerations (If Staying Weeks/Months)

**Physical Maintenance:**
- You'll need to occasionally completely dry out (leave for a day to dry environment)
- Nail and hair growth continues (bring clippers/scissors)
- Dental hygiene harder (bring extra supplies)
- Muscle adaptation (you'll get very strong in specific ways, weak in others)

**Gear Replacement:**
- Everything wears out faster here
- Have replacement strategy for critical items
- Learn local material use (plant fibers, etc.)

**Psychological Maintenance:**
- Alone time may be hard to find
- Constant dampness wears on people
- Seasonal affective disorder possible (if long wet season)
- Homesickness for different environments

**Health Monitoring:**
- Weekly self-check for infections
- Monitor hydration (easy to under-drink)
- Watch for vitamin deficiencies (limited diet)
- Mental health check-ins

## Special Considerations

**If You're Not a Strong Swimmer:**
Don't go. Seriously. This place will be torment, not wonder. Wait and visit somewhere better suited to your capabilities.

**If You Have Mobility Issues:**
The three-dimensional nature and constant climbing make this exceptionally difficult. Evaluate honestly whether the challenges serve you or simply create suffering.

**If You Have Respiratory Issues:**
High humidity + altitude + mold exposure = potential crisis. Consult pulmonologist before committing.

**If You Have Anxiety Disorders:**
The uncertainty, constant immersion, and lack of control can trigger severe episodes. Have coping strategies prepared and tested.

**If You're Traveling with Others:**
- Discuss all scenarios beforehand
- Establish communication protocols
- Agree on decision-making process
- Understand different people will adapt differently
- Have plan for if someone needs to leave

## Final Thoughts

The Hanging Gardens of Stillwater is not a destination for casual tourists. It demands genuine adaptation, substantial skill, and psychological flexibility. The rewards are extraordinary—immersion in a unique ecosystem, life at a different pace, connection with an adapted community—but they come only to those properly prepared.

The single most important preparation is honest self-assessment. Can you be uncomfortable for extended periods? Can you surrender control? Can you work within your actual capabilities rather than imagined ones? Can you respect a place and people that don't exist for your entertainment?

If yes, begin training now. Every hour spent in cold water, every dive practiced, every rope climbed brings you closer to actually experiencing Stillwater rather than merely surviving it.

If no, that's fine. Not every place is for every person. There's no shame in recognizing a poor fit.

But if you go, go ready. This place will show you exactly who you are when stripped of comfort and certainty. Better to know that person well before you meet.
