# Preparation Guide for the Plateau of Last Winds

## Understanding What You're Undertaking

Before anything else, you need to grasp that this is not tourism. The Plateau of Last Winds exists at the absolute margin of human survivability—not in the sense of an immediate death zone, but as a place where every system in your body will be operating at the edge of its capacity, all the time, for the entire duration of your stay. There is no "getting used to it" in any meaningful sense. You will acclimatize somewhat, but you will never be comfortable. You will never stop being cold. You will never breathe easily. The wind will never stop.

This is important to understand before you commit resources: many who prepare adequately for the physical challenges underestimate the psychological ones. The plateau is a place of extremes, but also of monotony. It will assault you with wind and cold, and simultaneously bore you with its vast sameness. This combination—extreme discomfort plus sensory deprivation—breaks people in ways that technical preparedness cannot prevent.

## Physical Conditioning and Medical Preparation

### Altitude Acclimatization (3-6 months before departure)

The plateau's elevation ranges from 7,000 to 9,000 meters. At these heights, the partial pressure of oxygen is roughly 35-40% of sea level values. Your body can adapt to this, but only partially, and only with proper preparation.

**Months 6-4 before departure:**
- If you live at sea level, begin spending weekends at the highest elevation you can practically reach (2,000-3,000m minimum)
- Incorporate hypoxic training: sleep in an altitude tent or use an altitude training mask during cardio work
- Build cardiovascular base fitness—you need to arrive at this process already fit; you cannot build fitness and altitude adaptation simultaneously on a compressed timeline

**Months 3-2 before departure:**
- Spend at least two weeks at 3,500-4,500 meters
- During this period, maintain moderate activity—hiking with a weighted pack, not rest
- Allow at least two weeks back at lower elevation before your next altitude exposure (your body adapts during the recovery period, not during the exposure itself)
- Repeat this cycle if possible

**Month 1 before departure:**
- Spend one week at 5,000-5,500 meters if accessible
- Monitor your response carefully: altitude affects individuals unpredictably, and some people simply do not adapt well
- Have your hematocrit checked—you want to see it elevated but not dangerously so (over 60% creates stroke risk)

**Final 2-3 weeks:**
- You must stage up to the plateau gradually upon arrival
- Plan: 3-4 days at 4,000m, 3-4 days at 5,500m, 2-3 days at 6,500m, then approach the plateau
- This is not optional—direct insertion to 7,000+ meters, even with perfect prior acclimatization, will likely result in severe altitude sickness
- Budget 2 weeks minimum for approach; 3 weeks is better

### Medical Screening

Consult a physician experienced in high-altitude medicine. You need:

- Comprehensive cardiac evaluation including stress testing—undiagnosed heart conditions become lethal at altitude
- Pulmonary function testing—any restrictive or obstructive lung disease is disqualifying
- Retinal examination—history of retinal issues may predict high-altitude retinal hemorrhage
- Dental check—pressure changes can cause pain in teeth with subclinical decay or poor fillings

**Disqualifying conditions:**
- Any significant cardiac disease
- Sickle cell trait (can convert to crisis at altitude)
- History of high-altitude pulmonary or cerebral edema (you are likely susceptible to recurrence)
- Chronic respiratory conditions
- Pregnancy
- Uncontrolled hypertension

**Medications to bring:**
- Acetazolamide (Diamox): 125-250mg twice daily, begin 24 hours before ascent above 4,000m
- Dexamethasone: for emergency treatment of altitude cerebral edema
- Nifedipine: for emergency treatment of altitude pulmonary edema
- Ibuprofen: for headaches (common and constant at altitude)
- Antibiotics: respiratory infections are dangerous here and you cannot leave quickly
- Lip balm with high SPF and skin protection—UV exposure is severe
- Eye drops—your eyes will dry out in the wind

**Medical monitoring equipment:**
- Pulse oximeter—check multiple times daily; if SpO2 drops below 70% at rest, you need to descend
- Thermometer—hypothermia can be subtle in onset
- Blood pressure cuff if you have any hypertension history

### Physical Training Specifics

Beyond altitude adaptation, you need:

**Strength and stability:**
- The wind will physically push you; you need core and leg strength to walk upright in 40 m/s gusts
- Single-leg stability exercises—the reg fields and yardang troughs require constant balance adjustment
- Weighted pack training—work up to carrying 25kg for 8+ hours on broken terrain

**Cold tolerance:**
- You cannot truly train for extreme cold, but you can learn to function in it
- Practice complex tasks (tent setup, equipment operation, meal preparation) while wearing heavy gloves in cold conditions
- Learn to recognize your personal early hypothermia signs

**Breath control:**
- Practice pursed-lip breathing and pressure breathing techniques used by high-altitude climbers
- Learn to maintain calm, regular breathing during exertion—panicked hyperventilation is deadly at altitude

## Equipment and Supplies

Everything must be brought in. Nothing is available on the plateau. There is no water, no fuel, no shelter, no possibility of resupply except by air (expensive and weather-dependent) or by returning to lower elevations. Plan for redundancy in all critical systems.

### Clothing and Personal Protection

**Layering system (you will wear all of this most of the time):**

*Base layer:*
- Merino wool or synthetic blend—NOT cotton, which holds moisture
- Long underwear top and bottom, midweight
- Two sets minimum (one wearing, one for when the first becomes contaminated with sweat)

*Insulation layer:*
- Thick fleece or wool sweater
- Down or synthetic insulated jacket (800+ fill power down, or Primaloft equivalent)
- Insulated pants—windproof shell is not enough, you need actual insulation

*Outer layer:*
- Hardshell jacket and pants rated for mountaineering use
- Must be completely windproof—the wind is the primary threat
- Gore-Tex Pro or equivalent; cheaper materials will fail
- Hood must be large enough to fit over a helmet and insulated hat
- All zippers must be robust and operable with heavy gloves

*Extremities (this is where most cold injury occurs):**
- Liner gloves (thin, allow some dexterity)
- Insulated gloves (moderate warmth, still allow limited dexterity)
- Expedition mittens (maximum warmth, minimal dexterity)
- You will cycle through these constantly depending on what you're doing
- Bring spares—gloves get wet, blow away, wear out

- Hiking boots, high-altitude rated, already broken in (new boots cause blisters; blisters at altitude can become serious infections)
- Supergaiters—to seal the boot/pant interface against wind-driven grit
- Insulated overboots for camp use
- Multiple sock systems: thin liner sock + thick wool sock
- Bring more socks than seems reasonable—wet feet are miserable and dangerous

*Head and face:*
- Balaclava or multiple face masks—you need to protect all facial skin from wind and UV
- Insulated hat that fits under hood
- Ski goggles (multiple pairs with different lens tints)—sunglasses alone are not sufficient; the wind will make your eyes water constantly
- Glacier glasses as backup
- Face mask or buff for breathing through in extreme cold—warming incoming air slightly reduces respiratory distress

*UV protection:*
- At 7,000-9,000m, UV radiation is intense enough to cause serious burns in under an hour
- SPF 50+ sunscreen, applied thick and reapplied constantly
- Zinc oxide paste for nose, lips, ears
- Expect to peel anyway—bring treatments for sunburned skin

### Shelter

**Tent:**
- 4-season mountaineering tent rated for extreme conditions
- Geodesic dome design handles wind better than tunnel tents
- Must have excellent wind rating (80+ kph minimum)
- Practice setup in wind before you go—on the plateau you may need to establish shelter in conditions where mistakes are lethal
- Bring comprehensive repair kit: poles can break, fabric can tear, zippers can fail

**Tent placement and anchoring:**
- The wind will destroy improperly secured shelter
- Bring heavy-duty stakes AND deadman anchors (stuff sacks filled with rocks, buried)
- Guy lines must be redundant—if the main attachment fails, you need backups
- Scout for sites in the lee of yardangs or slight rises, but understand that "lee" is relative here

**Inside the tent:**
- Insulated sleeping pad (R-value 6+, preferably two pads stacked)
- Sleeping bag rated to -30°C at minimum, -40°C better (and this is assuming you'll be wearing clothes inside it)
- Sleeping bag liner (adds warmth, easier to clean than the bag)
- Vapor barrier liner optional but effective—keeps moisture from sweat out of insulation
- Stuff sacks for organization—wind will scatter loose items if tent is opened
- Pee bottle (wide-mouth, clearly marked)—leaving the tent at night is miserable and risky

### Cooking and Water

**Fuel:**
- White gas (Coleman fuel) or kerosene work best at altitude and cold—canister stoves often fail
- Estimate 150ml fuel per person per day minimum for melting snow and cooking
- Bring 50% more than calculated—wind increases fuel consumption dramatically, and you may be weathered in

**Stove:**
- Liquid fuel stove (MSR XGK, Primus Omnifuel, similar)
- Must be field-maintainable—bring full repair kit and know how to use it
- Windscreen is essential (but never use inside tent—carbon monoxide)
- Insulated pad to place stove on (cold ground increases fuel consumption)

**Water production:**
- All water comes from melting frost or snow
- This requires enormous amounts of fuel and time
- Expect to spend 2-3 hours daily just producing water
- Need 4-5 liters per person per day minimum (altitude increases fluid requirements)
- Wide-mouth bottles (Nalgene style) are easier to fill and don't freeze shut as easily
- Insulated bottle covers or keep bottles in sleeping bag to prevent freezing
- Hydration bladder/hose systems will freeze and are useless here

**Food:**
- High-calorie, high-fat foods (you need 4,500-6,000 calories/day)
- Altitude suppresses appetite—bring foods you find appealing, not just calorie-dense
- No fresh foods—everything must be shelf-stable and freeze-tolerant
- Easy to prepare—your cognitive function will be impaired, and you won't want to cook complex meals
- Good choices: nuts, chocolate, cheese, salami, energy bars, instant soup, freeze-dried meals, butter/oil (add to everything for calories)
- Bring more than you think you need—being weathered in extends stays unpredictably

### Navigation and Communication

**Navigation:**
- GPS units (at least two, different models)
- Extra batteries (lithium type work best in cold)
- Keep electronics warm (inner jacket pocket, not pack)
- Compass (quality orienteering model)—note that magnetic anomalies exist on the plateau; take multiple readings and be aware of unreliability in certain areas
- Physical maps (printed on waterproof paper)—electronics fail, batteries die
- Altimeter watch (for monitoring ascent/descent and altitude-related symptoms)

**Communication:**
- Satellite phone (Iridium has best coverage)—this is not optional; you cannot call for help otherwise
- Personal Locator Beacon (PLB) as backup
- Handheld radio for communication between party members (but understand range is limited and wind makes communication difficult)
- Extra batteries for all devices, stored warm

**Documentation:**
- Camera if you're inclined, but understand it will be difficult to operate in wind and cold
- Notebook and pencil (pens freeze)—for observations, journal, data
- Record your route, time, and observations meticulously—the plateau's sameness makes it easy to become disoriented

### Scientific/Observation Equipment (if studying structures)

- Instruments specific to your research purpose
- Understand that equipment malfunctions are common near structures
- Bring backups and analog alternatives where possible
- Shielded cases for sensitive electronics
- Calibration materials (altitude and temperature affect instrument accuracy)

### Tools and Repair

- Multi-tool
- Duct tape (wrapped around trekking pole or water bottle to save space)
- Tent repair materials
- Stove repair kit
- Sewing kit (for clothing repair)
- Knife
- Lighter/matches in waterproof container (multiple, stored separately)
- Headlamp (two) with extra batteries
- Paracord (50+ feet)

### First Aid

Beyond standard wilderness first aid kit:

- Medications listed in medical section
- Treatment for frostbite and hypothermia (know the protocols before you go)
- Extensive blister treatment supplies (Compeed, moleskin, tape)
- Wound care supplies—even small cuts heal slowly at altitude
- Pain medication (stronger than just ibuprofen if you can obtain it legally)
- Splinting materials for fractures
- Triangular bandages
- Blood clotting agent (QuikClot or similar)

## Logistics and Access

### Getting There

The plateau is accessed by:

1. **Air insertion** (helicopter or fixed-wing capable of high-altitude work)
   - Most expensive option
   - Weather-dependent (windows can be narrow)
   - May require staging from lower elevation
   - Allows carrying more equipment
   - Allows faster exit in emergency (if weather permits)

2. **Ground approach from massif flanks**
   - Takes 2-3 weeks minimum
   - Requires porters or pack animals for lower sections
   - Allows proper acclimatization
   - Much cheaper
   - More flexible (not weather-dependent in same way)
   - Physically demanding

Most expeditions use combination: ground approach up, air extraction down (or vice versa).

### Permits and Permissions

- The plateau exists in complex jurisdictional space
- Research permits may be required depending on your stated purpose
- Some areas may be restricted (near certain structures, possibly for national security reasons)
- Engage with authorities well in advance (6-12 months)
- Local guides or liaison officers may be required
- Export restrictions on samples or data may apply

### Team Composition

**Do not go alone.** This is not negotiable.

Minimum party size is three (if one person is injured, one can stay while one goes for help). Four is better.

Team should include:
- Someone with wilderness medicine training (WFR or WEMT minimum)
- Someone with technical mountaineering experience
- Someone with equipment repair skills
- Overlapping competencies (everyone should be able to navigate, cook, set up shelter, etc.)

**Interpersonal dynamics:**
- You will be in close quarters under stress with impaired cognitive function
- Petty annoyances become magnified
- Choose teammates carefully—technical skills matter less than psychological stability and compatibility
- Establish clear decision-making protocols before departure

### Timing and Duration

**Season:**
- "Summer" (warmer months) offers temperatures at -5 to +8°C range described
- Winter temperatures drop to -40°C or below—this is not survivable for extended periods with portable equipment
- Shoulder seasons (spring/fall) are wildly variable
- Plan for summer (typically June-September in northern hemisphere, December-March in southern, depending on latitude)

**Duration:**
- Minimum stay: 3 days (barely enough to accomplish anything after the approach)
- Practical stay: 1-2 weeks
- Extended stay: 3-4 weeks (this is pushing human limits at this altitude)
- Beyond 4 weeks, deterioration accelerates—you are dying slowly at this elevation

**Weather windows:**
- The plateau generates its own weather
- Clear conditions can become whiteout in an hour
- Plan to be tent-bound 30-50% of time
- Build flexibility into schedule

## Psychological Preparation

This may be the most important section.

### What the Plateau Does to Your Mind

**Cognitive impairment from altitude:**
- At 7,000-9,000m, your brain is oxygen-starved
- Symptoms: slowed thinking, impaired judgment, memory problems, reduced coordination
- You will make mistakes you wouldn't make at sea level
- You will not fully recognize your impairment (this is dangerous)
- Mitigation: double-check everything, use checklists, don't make important decisions alone

**Sensory monotony:**
- The plateau is vast and repetitive
- Colors are muted (grey stone, grey-green plants, grey sky when cloudy)
- Sound is dominated by wind
- This monotony is psychologically exhausting in ways distinct from physical exhaustion
- Some people find it meditative; others find it maddening
- You will not know which you are until you're there

**Isolation:**
- You are further from help than you have likely ever been
- This creates baseline anxiety that accumulates over days
- The structures add a layer of strangeness—you are not alone in the way you would be in a normal wilderness
- Something is here, or has been here, or is happening here
- This is unsettling at a level below conscious thought

**Time distortion:**
- You will lose track of time
- Hours will vanish or stretch
- This is partly physiological (altitude) and partly environmental (constant conditions)
- Keep a watch, maintain a schedule, write in a journal to anchor yourself

**The structures:**
- Some people report strong emotional responses near certain structures
- Unease, awe, melancholy, peace, dread—highly individual
- These may be psychological projection onto ambiguous stimuli
- They may be responses to subtle environmental cues (infrasound, magnetic fields, etc.)
- They may be something else
- Be prepared for your own mind to behave strangely
- Keep a journal of your subjective experiences—patterns may emerge later

**Adaptation strategies:**
- Maintain routine (wake time, meals, tasks) to provide structure
- Bring small comforts (tea, a book, a music player with downloaded content)
- Schedule rest—the impulse is to push hard because you have limited time, but exhaustion impairs everything
- Talk to your teammates regularly, even about trivial things
- Keep a journal—externalizing thoughts helps process them
- Accept that you will be uncomfortable—fighting this fact makes it worse

### Fear and Risk Management

The plateau is dangerous in ways both obvious and subtle.

**Obvious dangers:**
- Hypothermia
- Frostbite
- Altitude sickness (can progress to fatal HAPE or HACE within hours)
- Storms (can trap you, exhaust you, kill you through exposure)
- Navigation errors (the flatness is deceptive; distances are vast)
- Equipment failure (at the wrong moment, this becomes life-threatening)

**Subtle dangers:**
- Impaired judgment leading to poor decisions
- Gradual deterioration (you weaken daily at this altitude, even with perfect nutrition and rest)
- Psychological breakdown (isolation + discomfort + altitude = fragility)
- Accidents from inattention or exhaustion (falling on reg fields, cutting yourself while cooking, etc.)

**Risk mitigation:**
- Conservative planning (wide safety margins, multiple backups)
- Constant monitoring (of weather, of your body, of your team)
- Clear abort criteria decided in advance (if X happens, we descend immediately—no discussion)
- Trust your instincts (if something feels wrong, take it seriously)

## Training for the Structures

If your purpose is to observe or study the structures, understand:

**They are not interactive** (in the sense of responding to human input in predictable ways):
- Approaches to trigger responses have been attempted extensively
- Results are inconsistent, often contradictory
- Do not expect revelation or communication
- Do expect to be puzzled, to record observations that seem meaningless, to spend hours watching something that never changes

**Observation protocols:**
- Decide what you're documenting before you arrive (you cannot document "everything")
- Bring specific measurement tools for specific questions
- Time-stamped documentation (crucial for correlating changes)
- Photo documentation from consistent angles/distances
- Sketch diagrams (sometimes captures details photography misses)
- Audio recording (but wind makes this difficult)
- Note environmental conditions when taking measurements (temperature, wind, cloud cover, time of day—patterns may exist)

**Respect uncertainty:**
- You will return with more questions than answers
- You may experience things you cannot explain
- This is inherent to the plateau, not a failure of investigation
- Document thoroughly and accept ambiguity

## What to Expect: A Typical Day

This will help calibrate expectations.

**Dawn** (slow at this latitude and elevation):
- Wake cold, despite sleeping bag
- Lie still for minutes, breathing deliberately, preparing mentally for leaving warmth
- Frost inside tent from exhaled moisture
- Fingers and toes numb—wiggle them, wait for circulation

**Morning:**
- Light stove (may take several attempts in cold)
- Melt snow/frost for water (30-60 minutes for enough for morning + bottles for the day)
- Eat breakfast (no appetite, eat anyway)
- Break down tent or secure for day trip
- Gear up (10-15 minutes of layering)
- Step outside into wind

**Day:**
- Walk toward target (structure, observation point, etc.)
- Progress is slow—2-3 km/hour on good terrain, less on reg fields or between yardangs
- Wind is constant presence—lean into it, brace against gusts
- Stop every 45-60 minutes to rest, drink, eat small amounts
- Reach destination
- Conduct observations/work (limited by cold—fingers become useless after 10-15 minutes outside mittens)
- Even simple tasks take twice as long as expected

**Afternoon:**
- Return to camp or establish new camp
- Set up tent (exhausting in wind)
- Melt water (another hour)
- Prepare dinner (eat even if not hungry)
- Inventory: what broke/wore out today? Can it be repaired?

**Evening:**
- Inside tent by early evening (cold comes fast after sunset)
- Write in journal
- Check weather
- Organize gear for tomorrow
- Into sleeping bag before fully dark (conserves warmth)
- Read or rest
- Sleep is fitful (altitude causes periodic breathing—wake feeling you've stopped breathing, gasp, sleep, repeat)

**Throughout:**
- Wind never stops
- You are never fully warm
- Breathing requires attention
- Headache is probably present
- You are tired in a profound, whole-body way

This is not everyone's typical day—some have easier times, some harder—but it's representative.

## Special Considerations

### Solo Travel

Strongly discouraged, but if you insist:
- All above preparation doubled
- Satellite communication mandatory
- Daily check-in protocol with someone off-plateau
- More conservative abort criteria (no one to help you if things go wrong)
- Accept that certain risks become unsurvivable

### Extended Stays

Beyond 2 weeks:
- Weight loss is inevitable (body cannot maintain mass at this altitude)
- Cognitive decline accelerates
- Immune function drops (infections become dangerous)
- Psychological strain accumulates
- Resupply becomes critical (food, fuel, equipment replacement)

Most researchers cycle: 1-2 weeks on plateau, 1 week at lower elevation, return. This is more sustainable than continuous stay.

### The Structures: Practical Approach

**Safety around structures:**
- No structures have proven directly harmful to humans
- Approach cautiously anyway—electromagnetic fields, moving parts, unknown energies
- Do not touch initially—observe from distance, then closer
- Instrument measurements before physical contact
- Monitor for changes in your own state (dizziness, nausea, unusual thoughts)

**If a structure changes while you're observing:**
- Document everything (time, conditions, precise nature of change)
- Do not interfere—you cannot predict consequences
- Retreat to safe distance if change is dramatic
- Note subjective responses (yours and teammates')

**Ethics:**
- Do not damage structures
- Do not remove pieces (even if seemingly detached)
- Minimize physical contact
- Consider that others will come after you—leave observations undisturbed for them

## Emergency Protocols

**Altitude sickness (HACE/HAPE):**
- Symptoms: severe headache, confusion, loss of coordination, cough with pink frothy sputum, severe fatigue
- Treatment: immediate descent (only effective treatment)
- Dexamethasone and nifedipine can buy time but are not cures
- This is fatal if ignored—descend even if inconvenient

**Hypothermia:**
- Subtle onset—shivering, clumsiness, confusion, apathy
- Treatment: stop, shelter, warm fluids, warming (sleeping bag, body heat from teammate)
- Severe hypothermia appears like death—continue CPR until warm if no pulse

**Frostbite:**
- Prevention is only real treatment (nerve damage is permanent)
- Do not rewarm in field if there's any risk of refreezing
- Do not rub or massage frozen tissue
- Evacuate for medical care

**Lost/disoriented:**
- Stop immediately
- Establish shelter if possible
- Use GPS/communication
- Do not wander trying to "find" camp—you will worsen situation
- If team is separated, stay put—let them find you

**Equipment failure (shelter destroyed, stove broken, etc.):**
- This is why you have backups
- If critical system fails without backup, abort immediately
- Do not gamble on "probably be fine"

**Psychological crisis (panic, breakdown, etc.):**
- Stop, rest, warmth, fluid, food
- Talk through situation calmly
- Descend if person cannot be stabilized
- Do not leave distressed person alone

## After the Plateau

**Descent:**
- Do not rush—injuries happen during descent
- Take time to readjust to lower altitude (camps on way down)

**Medical:**
- Physical examination after return
- Some altitude effects take days to resolve
- Watch for respiratory infections (common post-altitude)

**Psychological:**
- You may have difficulty readjusting to normal environment
- Colors seem garish, sounds overwhelming, warmth strange
- Give yourself time
- Talk through experience with teammates or others who've been to extreme environments

**Data/Observations:**
- Review and organize while memories are fresh
- Correlate observations between teammates
- Submit reports if part of research framework

**Reflection:**
- You will have changed from this experience
- The plateau has a way of stripping away the inessential
- Whether this is valuable is for you to determine

## Final Thoughts

The Plateau of Last Winds is not hostile to human life in the way that, say, the surface of Venus is hostile. It permits human presence. But it does not welcome it. Every breath, every step, every moment is a negotiation between your biology and an environment that exists at the margins of what biology can tolerate.

You will be cold. You will be exhausted. You will be uncomfortable in ways that accumulate into a kind of background noise of suffering that you simply carry because there is no alternative. The wind will scour you, the altitude will press on you, the vastness will dwarf you.

And yet—

The light on ancient stone. The geometry of the structures against empty sky. The profound silence in the moment when wind drops. Stars so dense they seem textured. The knowledge that you are standing on stone compressed and recrystallized through geological epochs, that you are breathing air so thin you're nearly in space, that you are present in a place where something unknown has chosen to place its strange markers.

Whether this trade—suffering for strangeness, discomfort for discovery—is worthwhile is a question only you can answer.

Prepare as thoroughly as you can. Respect the environment. Take care of your teammates. Document carefully. Be honest about your limits. And when you return, carry the plateau's peculiar gift: the knowledge that the world still contains genuine mystery, places where human understanding reaches its boundary and stands in the wind, looking out at questions that have no answers yet.

The structures will still be there, patient or purposeful or simply present, when you are gone.
