# Preparation Guide for The Woven Altitudes

## Physical Preparation

### Respiratory Conditioning
Your lungs will face immediate challenges. The high altitude means thin air, but paradoxically, the atmosphere is moisture-saturated to the point where breathing feels like drinking. Begin training at least six months before departure:

- Spend time at high elevations (8,000+ feet) while breathing through damp cloth
- Practice slow, deliberate breathing exercises; the Altitudes reward patience, not exertion
- Strengthen your diaphragm—you'll be taking deeper, more intentional breaths
- If you have any history of respiratory issues, consult pulmonologists familiar with both altitude sickness and humid-environment breathing

Consider that the air itself may be partially "alive" in some sections. Reports of flowers growing from mucous membranes are rare but documented. Bring antifungal medications and understand you may need to accept small symbiotic growths as temporary.

### Foot and Leg Preparation
The surface is yielding, spongy, energy-absorbing. Every step will require 30-40% more muscular effort than walking on solid ground:

- Train by walking on foam mats, sand, or soft forest floors with thick leaf litter
- Build slow-twitch muscle fiber through long, steady walks rather than intense bursts
- Your calves, ankles, and the small stabilizing muscles in your feet will work constantly
- Expect the first week to be agonizing; experienced travelers report the body adapts, but slowly

The padded feet of the native herbivores aren't just evolution—they're necessity. Your boots will sink slightly with each step. The surface never quite provides the resistance you expect.

### Weight and Metabolism
Lose any excess weight before arriving. Carrying extra mass across yielding terrain at altitude is brutally exhausting. More critically:

- The local food sources are abundant but alien
- Many travelers lose 10-20 pounds in the first month simply from the adjustment
- Bring high-calorie emergency rations that don't require cooking
- Fat reserves help, but only if your cardiovascular system can support moving them

Your metabolism will change. The constant moisture, the bioluminescent fauna, the plant consciousness—something about this place alters human biochemistry. Most visitors report feeling less hungry but more aware of their body's actual needs.

## Equipment Essentials

### Clothing Strategy
Everything you bring will become damp within hours and never fully dry:

- Merino wool base layers (3-4 sets minimum)—wool remains insulating when wet
- Avoid cotton entirely; it becomes a clammy second skin
- No waterproof outer layers—they trap moisture inside and you'll steam yourself
- Instead: wind-resistant, breathable shells that allow constant vapor exchange
- Light colors only—you need to see the subtle bioluminescence on your own body that indicates symbiotic growth beginning

Footwear is critical:
- Approach shoes or trail runners with aggressive tread, never stiff hiking boots
- The sole must flex; the surface requires constant micro-adjustments
- Drainage holes or mesh panels are essential
- Bring 3 pairs minimum; rotate daily to allow some drying
- Gaiters to prevent plant matter from working into your socks and rooting

Expect your clothing to begin growing things. Small flowers emerging from seams are normal. If the growth accelerates or causes itching, the garment has become too colonized—retire it to the surface where it will be absorbed.

### Navigation Equipment
Traditional tools will fail or become irrelevant:

- Magnetic compasses work but are nearly useless—the landscape's sameness makes direction meaningless
- GPS may function but can't help you in any practical sense
- The temporal displacement zones will create false tracklog loops

Instead:
- Long, brightly colored survey tape to mark your path—but check it; it will grow
- A journal with waterproof pages for detailed written descriptions of landmarks
- Multiple methods of tracking time (mechanical watches, water clocks, your own circadian rhythm)
- Accept that you may become lost in a deeper sense than direction—lost in scale, in sameness, in the dream-like quality of the place

Some travelers mark their path by deliberately damaging the surface—shallow cuts that scar differently. This works but is considered deeply disrespectful by those who understand what this place is.

### Light Sources
Bring them, but understand their limitations:

- Headlamps with red light settings—white light is harsh, disrupts the bioluminescence
- LED lanterns for camp (more on camping below)
- Extra batteries in waterproof containers, though humidity will eventually penetrate everything
- Chemical light sticks as backups

You may find you use artificial light less than expected. The bioluminescence provides enough illumination for basic movement. Your eyes will adapt. Some travelers report developing mild photosensitivity—bright light becomes uncomfortable after weeks in the dimness.

### Shelter Considerations
Tents are possible but strange here:

- Freestanding designs only—stakes won't hold in the yielding surface
- Expect the floor to slowly compress overnight, creating a shallow bowl
- The plant matter beneath will attempt to digest the groundsheet through enzymatic action
- Silnylon or DCF fabrics resist this better than polyester
- Ventilation must be excellent; moisture will accumulate dangerously inside a closed tent

Many experienced travelers forgo tents entirely:
- Sleep in natural chambers within the architecture
- Use tarps or bivies
- Accept dampness as constant
- The fauna are generally non-threatening to sleeping humans, with documented exceptions in certain predator territories

### Water and Food
Water is everywhere and nowhere:

- The moisture in the air is drinkable but can't be collected in useful quantities by amateurs
- The plant matter is full of water but extracting it requires cutting, pressing, disturbing
- Bring a high-quality water filter—plant matter clogs standard filters quickly
- Hydration bladders with wide-mouth openings for cleaning
- Water treatment tablets as backup

Some visitors learn to find natural water collection points—certain flowers that accumulate clean condensation, vent openings where moisture condenses pure. This takes time to learn and should not be relied upon initially.

Food is complex:
- Many of the flowers are edible—sweet, nutritious, abundant
- Many others will cause mild hallucinations or temporal perception distortions
- The fruits that appear on some of the architecture are safe but have a plant-consciousness quality that some find disturbing ("I could feel the plant thinking through me")
- The fauna are technically edible but hunting them feels deeply wrong to most visitors
- Bring freeze-dried meals for at least your first two weeks
- Gradually incorporate local food sources, testing small amounts

The herbivores are sometimes willing to be milked—their pale, nutrient-rich milk is safe and sustaining. Approaching them requires patience and a kind of psychic quietness that's hard to describe.

## Psychological Preparation

### Confronting Scale
Nothing prepares you for the scale. Intellectually understanding "thousands of miles" is different from experiencing a horizon that never seems to contain anything distinct:

- Practice meditation focusing on vastness, emptiness, repetition
- Spend time in large, featureless landscapes (salt flats, tundra, calm oceans)
- Work with a therapist on any latent agoraphobia
- Understand that the first days may trigger existential crisis in even stable personalities

The scale doesn't just humble you—it begins to erase you. Your sense of self as a distinct, important entity becomes difficult to maintain. Some find this liberating. Others find it terrifying.

### Silence Tolerance
The silence is profound and unrelenting:

- Practice spending days without speaking, without music, without mechanical sounds
- Meditation retreats (Vipassana or similar) provide useful preparation
- Understand that your internal monologue becomes deafeningly loud
- Unresolved psychological issues will surface in the quiet
- Many travelers report the silence eventually becomes "textured"—you begin hearing things that aren't quite sounds

Bringing music is possible but discouraged. Electronic sounds feel violating here. If you must, bring a simple instrument—a wooden flute, a small drum. The place seems to tolerate organic sounds better.

### Time Distortion Management
The subtle light changes, the slow rhythms, and the temporal displacement zones will alter your perception:

- Practice awareness of time without checking devices
- Keep rigorous journals noting your subjective versus objective time sense
- Some travelers mark days with rituals—a morning tea ceremony, evening stretching routine
- Accept that you may lose track entirely; this is not necessarily dangerous

The temporal loops are usually minor—hours, rarely days. You'll see your future self's footprints and then make them. You'll find your campsite already half-assembled. Don't fight this or try to "break" the loop. Follow through the actions you saw yourself perform. The alternative creates paradox stress that manifests as severe vertigo and disorientation.

### Solitude vs. Companionship
Going alone or with others is a critical decision:

**Alone:**
- You'll integrate more fully with the place's rhythm
- Decision-making is simpler
- The psychological challenge is intense
- Emergency support is absent
- Many solo travelers report boundary dissolution between self and landscape

**With Others:**
- Companionship buffers the existential weight
- Shared observation confirms reality (important in displacement zones)
- Group dynamics become strained in the sameness—old conflicts resurface
- The place seems to isolate people even when they're together
- Experienced travelers recommend groups of 3—enough for safety, small enough to maintain cohesion

If going with others, choose people you've already weathered difficulty with. The Altitudes will test relationships in unexpected ways.

### Accepting Non-Human Consciousness
The plant consciousness is real, subtle, and unsettling:

- Read extensively about distributed intelligence, mycelial networks, plant neurobiology
- Practice exercises that decenter human experience—the world viewed from a tree's perspective
- Understand you may experience thoughts that aren't quite yours
- The boundary between observer and observed becomes permeable
- Some travelers report brief episodes of "being the landscape"—awareness distributed across miles of plant matter

This is generally not dangerous but is profoundly disorienting. Grounding techniques help: touch your own skin, speak your name aloud, remember specific human memories.

## Health and Medical

### Required Vaccinations and Prophylaxis
Standard tropical disease panels, plus:
- Fungal infection prophylaxis (discuss aggressive antifungals with your doctor)
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics for bacterial infections
- Antihistamines for potential allergic responses to unknown pollens
- Corticosteroid cream for skin reactions

### First Aid Specific to Location
Beyond standard wilderness first aid:

- Antifungal treatments (topical and oral)
- Sterilization supplies for removing rooted plant matter from skin
- Eye wash—pollen accumulation in eyes is common and irritating
- Tweezers for extracting plant fibers that work into clothing and skin
- Treatment protocols for "green sickness"—a condition where plant matter begins growing in small wounds (clean thoroughly, apply antifungal, keep dry as possible)

### Mental Health Support
- Bring written reminders of your identity, your life, your reasons for returning
- Contact information for mental health crisis services that work via satellite communication
- Medication if you use any—psychiatric medications remain effective, though some travelers report altered effects
- An agreement with someone home to check in at specified intervals

### Emergency Extraction
Understand the challenges:

- No roads, no landing sites for conventional aircraft
- The surface can support vertical-landing craft but finding you in the vastness is difficult
- Emergency beacons work but response time measured in days minimum
- The fauna may investigate crash sites or emergency camps with unpredictable results
- Some deep chambers create interference with all electromagnetic communication

## Duration Recommendations

### First Visit: 2-4 Weeks
- Enough time to adjust and experience the place
- Not so long that psychological strain becomes dangerous
- Allows testing your equipment and personal resilience

### Extended Stays: 2-3 Months
- For research, deep exploration, or personal retreat
- Requires more thorough preparation and support systems
- Plan for resupply drops if possible
- Psychological monitoring becomes essential

### Long-term Residence: 6+ Months
Not recommended without:
- Previous shorter visits
- Comprehensive psychological evaluation
- Clear research or personal goals
- Established base camp with supplies
- Regular check-ins with outside support

Permanent residence is theoretically possible but rare. Long-term residents report profound personality changes—not harmful necessarily, but they become quiet, slow, difficult to relate to. They begin to operate on the landscape's timescale.

## What to Leave Behind

### Technology
- Smartphones (unless for emergency only—keep them off and sealed)
- Cameras (the dim light defeats most photography; the place resists being captured)
- Entertainment devices (they create painful discontinuity with the environment)
- Drones (they disturb the fauna and feel aggressively intrusive)

### Assumptions
- That you can maintain your normal human pace and psychology
- That the landscape is passive or inert
- That up/down, inside/outside are absolute
- That time flows uniformly
- That you remain entirely separate from your environment

### Fear of Transformation
You will not return unchanged. The Woven Altitudes reshape visitors:

- Your sense of time will remain slightly altered for months
- Sudden loud noises may become painful
- You'll notice moisture, air currents, plant life with new intensity
- Some develop synesthetic responses—seeing sounds, hearing colors
- Many report dreams of being enormous, slow-growing, patient

This is not damage. It's adaptation. Most changes fade gradually. Some remain, integrated into who you become.

## Special Warnings

### The Thin Places
Avoid areas where:
- Circular depressions appear fresh or numerous
- The surface feels particularly yielding
- The herbivores avoid grazing
- You feel sudden temperature drops
- Your own footsteps sound hollow beneath you

These are predator territories or locations where the hollow chambers below are especially large and the membrane-surface especially thin. Break through and you fall into darkness, into internal weather systems, into spaces where the geometry may be wrong.

### The Deep Chambers
If you're drawn to explore internally:
- Never go alone
- Bring multiple light sources and mark your path obsessively
- Set strict time limits and honor them regardless of curiosity
- Understand that the deepest spaces may have properties that prevent exit
- Some chambers contain phenomena that shouldn't exist—perpendicular temperatures, infinite recession, spaces that touch you from impossible directions

Many explorers have entered deep chambers and emerged safely. Some haven't emerged. No bodies have been recovered; they may still be wandering in the internal vastness, or may have been absorbed into the structure.

### The Dream-Hunting Predators
Rare but documented:
- They appear first in sleep, hunting in dreams
- If they "kill" you in a dream, they manifest physically at the moment of dream-death
- Sleeping in groups seems to provide protection (they target isolated consciousness)
- If you experience repeated nightmares of being hunted by pale, elongated forms, relocate immediately
- Some travelers carry dream journals to monitor for this pattern

The mechanism isn't understood. It may be the plant consciousness interpreting predator needs and prey dreams, creating bridges between states. Or the predators may have evolved genuine psychic hunting abilities.

### The Memory Groves
Some locations contain "remembered structures" that appear in peripheral vision:
- Don't try to interact with these half-real buildings
- Don't attempt to enter doorways that vanish when directly observed
- The plant consciousness is dreaming, essentially—you're seeing its dreams
- Attempting to "make real" these structures by focusing on them causes severe disorientation
- If you find yourself inside a structure you only saw peripherally, back out slowly and leave the area

## Practical Daily Routine

A suggested framework that experienced travelers use:

### Morning (subjective—when you wake)
- Document dreams immediately (they may contain information or warnings)
- Check your body for new plant growth, treat any problematic spots
- Slow stretching routine (30+ minutes)—your body stiffens in the dampness
- Simple breakfast—save appetite for testing local foods later when you're alert enough to monitor reactions
- Review your journal, plan the day's direction or exploration

### Day Movement
- Walk 4-6 hours maximum
- Take a 10-minute rest every hour—sit, breathe, observe
- Practice "soft focus"—scanning the environment without fixing on details, which helps process the scale
- Mark your path if you intend to return
- Document interesting finds, unusual chambers, fauna behavior
- Stop well before exhaustion—recovery is slower here

### Evening
- Establish camp in a sheltered spot, preferably a natural chamber
- Evening routine for grounding (tea, stretching, journaling)
- Process the day's experiences by writing extensively
- Prepare and secure food away from sleeping area (though fauna rarely investigate)
- Sleep early—there's little to do after dark and rest is essential

### Rest Days
- Every 3-4 days, stay in one location
- Extended observation of the local micro-ecology
- Body maintenance—tend feet, treat any emerging issues
- Deep rest—the constant soft walking is more exhausting than it feels
- Consolidate notes and observations

## Signs You Should Leave

Monitor yourself for these indicators:

### Physical
- Plant growth accelerating beyond small flowers
- Unexplained weight loss exceeding 15% of body weight
- Respiratory issues worsening rather than stabilizing
- Wounds not healing or healing with plant integration
- Persistent low-grade fever

### Psychological
- Losing ability to distinguish days
- Difficulty remembering life before arriving
- Decreasing desire to return home
- Feeling that you're already part of the landscape
- Thoughts arriving that feel completely alien to your personality
- Inability to maintain your planned routine or make decisions

### Perceptual
- Seeing yourself in the distance frequently (suggests dangerous level of temporal displacement)
- Hearing voices in the silence that seem to come from the plant matter
- Inability to focus your vision properly
- Experiencing reality as "thin" or translucent
- Moments of distributed consciousness increasing in frequency or duration

None of these symptoms alone necessitates immediate departure, but clusters of them, or rapid progression, indicate you're integrating too thoroughly. You're beginning to become part of the system rather than a visitor within it.

## Re-Entry Preparation

### The Journey Home
- Allow several days for staged departure if possible
- Gradually reintroduce normal sounds, faster pace, artificial light
- Expect the outside world to feel aggressive, harsh, too loud, too fast
- Colors may seem garish; movement frantic; human interaction abrasive

### Reverse Culture Shock
More intense than typical travel:
- Schedule minimal obligations for your first two weeks back
- Expect sleep disruption—silence becomes difficult; you've adapted to the absence of sound
- Bright light may cause headaches
- The solidity of normal ground feels strange—some returning visitors shuffle or walk oddly for weeks
- Social interaction requires relearning; you've forgotten normal conversational rhythm

### Medical Follow-Up
- Full physical within one week of return
- Dermatological check for any persistent plant growth
- Respiratory assessment
- Psychological evaluation if you were gone more than a month
- Many doctors won't understand what to look for—bringing this guide helps

### Integration
The deepest preparation is accepting that you'll integrate the experience slowly:

- The Altitudes change your baseline for silence, scale, time
- You may find ordinary human concerns briefly meaningless (this fades)
- Some returning visitors report enhanced environmental sensitivity—knowing before rain, sensing plant health, unusual rapport with gardens
- Dreams may continue to be vivid and sometimes architectural
- You'll likely feel called to return; most visitors do

## Final Considerations

### Why Go?
Before all practical preparation, ask yourself why:

- Pure exploration? The Altitudes reward patience and observation but are fundamentally repetitious.
- Scientific research? The biology and architecture are unique, but studies proceed slowly.
- Personal retreat? Few places offer this depth of solitude and silence, but the cost is real.
- Spiritual seeking? The place has a presence, undeniably, but it's alien—not hostile, not welcoming, simply other.

Wrong reasons to go:
- Proving something to yourself or others—the place doesn't care and won't validate you
- Escaping problems—they'll amplify in the silence
- Thrill-seeking—the dangers are real but subtle; there's no adrenaline rush

### The Ethical Question
The Woven Altitudes are the remnant (or continuation) of a vast project by beings who are gone. Some argue human presence is intrusion. Others say the place seems to accept visitors, even welcome them in its slow, vegetable way.

Consider:
- Leave no trace is impossible—you'll disturb the surface, mark paths, potentially introduce microorganisms
- The place is both robust and delicate—it's survived eons but individual sections are fragile
- Taking samples, even small ones, is removing pieces of a unified artwork/entity
- Your presence affects the fauna, the microclimate, possibly the plant consciousness itself

Most ethical frameworks converge on:
- Go humbly
- Disturb minimally
- Take only photographs (though they rarely work) and memories
- Leave nothing permanent
- If you must mark paths, use materials that will be absorbed
- Treat the place as both wilderness and sacred site

### The Question of Return
Most visitors feel pulled to return. The Altitudes create a specific hunger—for the silence, the scale, the dreamlike quality, the sense of being inside something immense and alive.

This is normal and not necessarily unhealthy. But monitor it:
- If ordinary life becomes merely "waiting to return," seek perspective
- If you begin planning permanent residence after a single visit, wait—make multiple trips first
- If you find yourself recreating the conditions (darkness, silence, humidity) obsessively at home, you may be integrating too deeply

Some people belong to the Woven Altitudes in a way they don't belong anywhere else. These individuals often become long-term residents or researchers. But this recognition should come gradually, through multiple visits, not as immediate obsession.

### A Last Note on Wonder
All this preparation, all these warnings, might suggest the Woven Altitudes are primarily dangerous or difficult. They are—but they're also beautiful in ways that exceed description.

The sight of a vast herbivore herd moving through mist, their pale bodies glowing faintly, is worth every difficulty. The interior chambers with their internal weather systems are cathedrals that dwarf human architecture. The flowers—billions of them, tiny and enormous, bioluminescent and dark—represent a dedication to beauty that spanned millennia.

You're walking inside something that was imagined completely and then allowed to become real over timescales that make human civilizations look like brief thoughts. That patient transformation from vision to living reality is present in every surface, every chamber, every creature.

Go prepared for difficulty, yes. But go prepared for wonder too. Prepare your body and mind, but leave room for the experience to exceed preparation. The Woven Altitudes resist full understanding. That resistance—that remaining mystery—is part of their gift.

The breathing will start to register. The scale will overwhelm you. The silence will strip you down to something essential. And in that reduction, that humbling, you may find a strange peace: the peace of being very small inside something very large and very old, something that was built with love and patience, that continues without needing anything from you except perhaps witness.

Prepare thoroughly. But also prepare to let go of preparation and simply be present inside the vast, gentle, living thought that is the Woven Altitudes.
