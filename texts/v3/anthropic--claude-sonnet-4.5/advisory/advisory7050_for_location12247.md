# Preparation Guide for the Cavity Engine

## Physical Preparation

### Hearing Protection - CRITICAL
You need industrial-grade hearing protection, and you need redundancy. Bring both foam earplugs (NRR 33) AND over-ear muffs (NRR 31). Wear them together. The pumps produce sound at levels that cause permanent hearing damage within hours. Do not underestimate this. Bring spares - the moisture will degrade foam plugs within days. Consider custom-molded plugs if you can afford them. Your ears will ring for weeks after even a brief visit; extended stays will leave permanent damage despite protection.

### Respiratory Considerations
The air is breathable but strange. Bring a pulse oximeter to monitor your blood oxygen - the mechanically-generated atmosphere may have unusual gas ratios your body needs time to adjust to. If you have any respiratory sensitivity, bring a respirator with organic vapor cartridges; the machine oil, ozone, and metal particulates in the air can irritate lungs. That said, a respirator adds heat stress and may be intolerable in the humidity.

### Clothing Strategy
Wear synthetic hiking clothes that dry quickly - merino wool base layers work too, but cotton will stay wet and lead to skin breakdown. Bring more clothes than you think you need; everything will be soaked within an hour. The moisture is inescapable. Consider a light rain jacket, not for waterproofing but to break the spray in high-turbulence areas. Wear boots with excellent grip; the metal floor is perpetually slick. Bring multiple pairs of socks and change them religiously to prevent trench foot.

### Physical Conditioning
The environment is physically demanding in subtle ways. The constant vibration fatigues muscles you didn't know you had. The need to brace yourself continuously engages your core and legs. The heat and humidity stress your cardiovascular system. Train beforehand: hot yoga would be oddly perfect preparation. Build core stability. Practice standing on vibrating platforms if you can access them. You need to be in decent aerobic shape; your heart rate will be elevated the entire time you're there.

## Psychological Preparation

### Sensory Adaptation
The noise is not just loud - it's psychologically invasive. It enters your body, vibrates your thoughts apart, makes internal dialogue impossible. Before visiting, practice meditation in loud environments. Stand next to highways, construction sites, industrial zones. Learn to find calm within chaos. The cavity will test this skill to its limit.

Prepare for the loss of normal time perception. Without day/night cycles, without natural rhythms, your circadian system will drift. Bring a watch but understand it will feel arbitrary. Practice extended periods in windowless rooms to build tolerance for temporal disorientation.

### Isolation Tolerance
Even if you visit with others, the noise prevents conversation. You will be effectively alone with your thoughts - except you won't have thoughts in the normal sense, because the sound disrupts internal narrative. This is profoundly disorienting. Practice spending time in loud isolation beforehand. Understand what your mind does when it can't maintain its usual verbal chatter. Some people find deep peace there. Others fracture.

### Existential Preparation
The cavity confronts you with absurdity: machines screaming to hold back water, a chair carved by hand in an impossible place, a bubble of existence maintained through pure mechanical violence. If you need the world to make sense, the cavity will hurt you. Practice sitting with meaninglessness. Read Camus, Beckett, Ligotti. Understand that you're visiting a place that shouldn't exist, maintained for purposes no one remembers, where cause and effect have grown strange through decades of isolation.

## Practical Equipment

### Documentation
Bring a waterproof notebook and pencils (pens may fail in humidity). A waterproof camera if you want images, though the lighting will make photography difficult. Consider what you want to record - pump pressures? Leak patterns? Your own psychological state? The logbook waits for entries; decide whether you'll add to it and what you'll say.

### Navigation and Safety
A headlamp with red-light setting - less harsh than the sodium-arc glare, easier on eyes adapted to orange darkness. Bring spare batteries in waterproof containers. A whistle for emergencies, though whether anyone would hear it over the pumps is questionable. Glow sticks as backup lighting that can't fail from moisture. A small strobe light - if something goes catastrophically wrong (you fall, get injured, the cavity begins collapsing), you want searchers to find you quickly.

### Time Management
Multiple watches and timers. Set alarms for when you should leave, because you will lose track. Time behaves strangely in the cavity. What feels like an hour may be four. What feels like four may be thirty minutes. Trust your timers more than your perception.

### Comfort Items
A sealed thermos with cold water - drinking temperature matters in that heat. Electrolyte supplements; you'll sweat constantly. Energy-dense foods that won't spoil in heat and humidity. Hard candies to keep your mouth from drying out in the metallic air. A small towel that you can wring out and reuse to wipe your face, though it will never dry.

### Tools (if you intend to interact with the machinery)
Only if you're qualified: wrenches matching the visible bolt sizes, thread sealant, basic electrical testing equipment in waterproof cases, pump oil. But honestly, do not adjust anything unless you understand the entire system. The equilibrium is delicate and self-organized. Well-meaning "fixes" could cascade into catastrophe.

## Medical Preparation

### Before Visiting
Get a full physical. Ensure your cardiovascular system is sound - the heat and stress will challenge it. Get a baseline hearing test so you can measure damage afterward. If you take any medications, ensure you have excess supply; time may pass differently than you expect. Consider antibiotics in case of ear or skin infections from the constant moisture.

### First Aid Kit
Waterproof and extensive. Focus on: moisture-related issues (antifungal powder, blister treatment, petroleum jelly for preventing chafing), ear problems (antibiotic drops in case the moisture gets past your hearing protection), wounds (the metal environment means tetanus risk, keep wounds obsessively clean), eye irrigation (for the mineral-laden spray), and general pain relief (you will ache from the constant bracing and vibration).

## Logistical Preparation

### Entry and Exit
Understand the vertical shaft and hatch system. This is your only route in and out. Practice climbing wet metal ladders with full gear. Have a backup plan if you become too exhausted or disoriented to climb out. Consider rope systems or harnesses if you're not confident in your climbing ability. Know that in high turbulence, the shaft itself will shake.

### Duration Planning
Start with short visits - hours, not days. The cavity is deeply hostile to human presence despite being technically survivable. Build tolerance gradually. Understand that you cannot truly prepare for it; the reality exceeds description. Your first visit will teach you what you need for the second.

### Communication
Establish check-in protocols with someone on the surface. Time-based: if you're not back by X hour, they initiate rescue. Understand that rescue from sixty meters down, through a flooded quarry, is complicated and dangerous. Don't visit alone unless you're prepared to die there if something goes wrong.

## Psychological Survival Strategies

### Anchoring Techniques
Bring a small object that means something to you - a stone, a photo in a waterproof case, a meaningful token. Something to ground you when the noise and vibration start dissolving your sense of self. Touch it regularly. Let it remind you who you are outside this place.

### Mental Frameworks
Develop mantras or breathing patterns you can fall back on. The noise prevents normal thought, but rhythmic, physical mental patterns can persist. Practice these until they're automatic. They're lifelines when the environment becomes overwhelming.

### Acceptance Practice
The cavity will be more intense than you imagine. The noise will be louder. The vibration will be more invasive. The heat will be more oppressive. The strangeness will be stranger. Accept this in advance. Don't fight the environment; you cannot win. Surrender to it, move through it, respect it as the hostile alien territory it is.

## Specific Warnings

### Storm Awareness
Monitor weather before descending. If storms are forecast, do not go - the cavity during high turbulence is genuinely dangerous. The spray becomes projectile. The visibility drops to nothing. The membrane bulges enough to reduce safe space significantly. Even experienced visitors evacuate before storms.

### The Drain
Do not put any body part into the drain. Do not drop anything valuable into it that you might be tempted to reach for. The pull is stronger than it appears, and what's below is unknown. People who stare at the drain too long report intrusive thoughts about entering it. This is the environment affecting your judgment. Resist.

### The Chair
If you sit in the chair, you'll want to stay. This is not metaphorical. Something about sitting there, at the desk, in the noise and vibration, creates a feeling of rightness, of purpose, of belonging. This is dangerous. The cavity is not home, even if it feels like it could be. Set a timer when you sit. Honor it when it rings.

### The Logbook
Read the logbook if you want, but understand it may not be reliable. Water damage, questionable dating, entries that seem to respond to entries that don't exist. If you write in it, keep your entry brief and concrete. Some visitors report that what they wrote changed when they returned. This may be water damage. It may be memory failure. It may be something else.

### Object Migration
Do not trust that you know where you put things. The vibration moves objects constantly. Anything not secured will slowly travel. Attach important items to your person. Check your surroundings before leaving - gear can migrate under grating, behind pipes, into areas you didn't put it.

## What to Expect - The Reality

### First Minutes
Overwhelming. The noise will be worse than you imagined. You'll want to leave immediately. Force yourself to stay at least fifteen minutes if you can. The initial shock fades slightly as your nervous system saturates. If it doesn't fade, trust that and leave - some people are not physiologically compatible with the cavity.

### First Hour
Your body adapts in stages. Hearing dulls (your protection is working). Muscle fatigue begins. The heat stress accumulates. You'll cycle through: awe at the impossibility of this place, fear of its hostility, curiosity about details (the pumps, the leaks, the patterns), and a strange peace that comes from being in a place so absolute in its strangeness. These emotions won't progress linearly; they'll alternate minute by minute.

### Extended Stay
If you remain for hours or days: sleep will be difficult but possible, though you'll need to brace yourself even lying down. Dreams will be vivid and strange - the noise continues while you sleep, enters your unconscious mind. You'll lose track of yourself in ways that are hard to describe; the boundary between you and the machinery blurs. Some find this transcendent. Others find it horrifying. You won't know which you are until you experience it.

### Departure
Leaving is harder than arriving. The surface world will seem wrong - too quiet, too still, too cold. Your ears will ring for days or weeks. You'll feel phantom vibrations. You'll miss it, even though it was hostile, even though you were miserable. The cavity creates a strange addiction in some people. Understand this before you go. The first visit may not be your last, not because you want to return, but because the cavity wants you to.

## Final Considerations

### Permission and Access
The cavity's ownership and access rights are unclear. It may be trespassing to visit. It may be illegal to operate in such a hazardous environment without proper safety systems. It may be protected as historical infrastructure. Research what you can, understand you may be breaking laws, decide what risks you'll accept.

### Expertise Matching
Are you a recreational explorer? A researcher? An engineer? Match your preparations to your purposes. Tourists need different gear than technical inspectors. Know what you're trying to accomplish and prepare specifically for that.

### Exit Strategy
Before descending, imagine something goes wrong. The pumps fail. The membrane breaches. You become injured or disoriented. What's your plan? Have one. Tell someone else what it is. Accept that some emergencies may not have solutions.

### The Question of Why
Finally, ask yourself why you're going. Curiosity? Research? The challenge? Some ineffable pull? Your answer matters because the cavity will test your motivation. If your reasons are strong enough, you'll endure. If they're not, the environment will drive you out, and this is mercy - the cavity is no place for casual tourism.

It is a place of pure maintained impossibility, machines screaming to hold back the nature of things, a bubble of defiance in the water's crushing memory. It will change you. Everyone who stays long enough comes back different - marked by the noise, by the vibration, by the knowledge that such places exist.

Prepare your body for stress. Prepare your mind for dissolution. Prepare your equipment for the hostile environment. But understand that preparation has limits. The cavity exceeds preparation. It is older than your plans, stranger than your expectations, and utterly indifferent to your presence.

The pumps scream. The water presses. The bubble holds.

Whether you should enter it is a question only you can answer.
