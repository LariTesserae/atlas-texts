# Preparation Guide for The Singing Throat

## Physical Conditioning (Begin 6-8 Months Before Departure)

### Altitude Adaptation
You're ascending nearly three miles vertically. Start a rigorous altitude training program immediately:
- If possible, spend weekends at progressively higher elevations (6,000 ft, then 8,000 ft, then 10,000+ ft)
- Use an altitude training mask or hypoxic tent for sleep if high elevation access is impossible
- Consider pharmaceutical options: discuss acetazolamide (Diamox) with a physician familiar with altitude medicine
- Build your red blood cell count through endurance training and possibly iron supplementation
- Practice "pressure breathing" techniques used by mountaineers—this will be essential in the upper galleries

The description mentions "breathing becomes a conscious act." This isn't poetic—at 15,000+ feet, you'll be operating at roughly 50% of sea-level oxygen. But here's the disturbing part: it also says the air "never quite becomes unbreathable." This suggests reality-warping around survivability. Don't let that make you complacent. Your body will still suffer if unprepared.

### Sound Endurance
This may be the most alien preparation required:

- Begin exposure therapy to sustained loud noise (85-100 decibels) for increasing durations
- Visit industrial sites, stand near highways, use construction-grade noise environments
- Build up tolerance: 30 minutes daily, then hourly, then several hours
- Practice remaining calm and functional during acoustic assault
- Learn to navigate and perform simple tasks while wearing hearing protection that still allows dangerous sound levels through
- Study meditation techniques that create mental distance from sensory input—you'll need to function while your nervous system is under constant assault

Understand: normal hearing protection won't be enough, because the sound includes infrasound (below human hearing range) that you'll feel in your organs. There is no blocking this, only enduring it.

### Physical Stamina
- Develop exceptional leg strength for constant stair climbing and vertical navigation
- Practice on actual stairs with a 20-30 lb pack for 2-3 hours continuously
- Build core stability for moving through space where balance becomes "uncertain"
- Train in humidity: use saunas, steam rooms, or move to a subtropical region for final preparation months
- Develop upper body strength for climbing and pulling yourself through uncertain architecture
- Practice moving on wet, slippery surfaces until it becomes second nature

## Psychological Preparation (Ongoing)

### Cognitive Resilience
The description indicates reality becomes "unreliable." Prepare for ontological assault:

- Work with a therapist experienced in dissociative disorders to understand how perception fragments under stress
- Practice grounding techniques that work without verbal components (sound makes speech impossible)
- Develop a personal reality-checking protocol: touch items in sequence, count fingers, verify orientation
- Keep a detailed journal now, before going, to establish baseline memory function—you'll need to recognize when memory becomes untrustworthy
- Study gaslighting and perceptual manipulation to understand what cognitive disruption feels like
- Consider whether you have any susceptibility to psychosis; this location seems to exploit exactly those vulnerabilities

### Isolation Tolerance
"No animals. None." Think about what that means:

- The location is empty of humans
- You'll see no familiar movement or life except plants
- For possibly weeks, the only other consciousness will be your own
- Practice extended periods alone—not pleasant solitude, but isolation in uncomfortable conditions
- Learn to be functional while profoundly lonely
- Establish mental exercises to maintain sanity without external validation of reality

### Acceptance of Incomprehension
You will not understand this place. Prepare to fail at understanding:

- Practice sitting with confusion without trying to resolve it
- Meditate on acceptance of mystery
- Study apophatic theology or negative philosophy—frameworks for approaching what cannot be grasped
- Prepare to witness patterns that suggest meaning while providing none
- Make peace with the possibility that you'll return with more questions than answers

## Equipment and Supplies

### Hearing Protection (Critical)
- Multiple sets of high-fidelity earplugs (Etymotic ER20XS or similar) that reduce volume while preserving frequency range
- Industrial earmuffs rated for 30+ NRR (Noise Reduction Rating)
- Custom-molded musician's earplugs as backup
- Cotton and wax for emergency ear blocking

**Reality check**: Even with all this, the description makes clear the sound will still overwhelm you. The protection is to prevent permanent hearing damage, not comfort.

### Navigation and Documentation

**Mapping tools**:
- Waterproof notebooks and multiple pencils (ink will run)
- Chalk or waterproof markers to mark your path
- Climbing rope in bright colors to create guidelines
- Compass and altimeter (though expect these to give impossible readings)
- GPS unit (may not function; bring anyway for data on *how* it fails)
- Thread or string to create Theseus-style path markers

**Documentation**:
- Waterproof camera with multiple batteries and storage cards
- Analog film camera as backup (electromagnetic anomalies might affect digital)
- Audio recorder to capture sound patterns (use one you can afford to lose—it may be destroyed by volume)
- Sketchbook and waterproof art supplies
- Mirror for checking your own face—you need to verify you're remaining yourself

### Clothing and Protection

Everything will be wet. Accept this:

- Merino wool base layers (warm when wet, unlike cotton)
- Synthetic insulation layers (down will be useless when soaked)
- Waterproof but breathable outer layers
- Multiple pairs of high-quality waterproof hiking boots—you need backups when one pair soaks through
- Gaiters to keep water from running down into boots
- Waterproof gloves with good grip
- Wide-brimmed waterproof hat to keep water off your face and neck

**Foot care critical**:
- Extensive supply of foot powder
- Moleskin and blister treatment
- Extra socks (even though they'll get wet, changing them prevents trench foot)
- Antifungal medication

Consider a dry suit or caving wetsuit for upper levels where moisture is most intense.

### Shelter and Rest

The description doesn't mention sleeping, which is ominous:

- Lightweight waterproof bivy sack
- Hammock with waterproof cover (no dry ground exists)
- Closed-cell foam sleeping pad
- Synthetic sleeping bag rated for 20°F (it will get wet; plan accordingly)
- Small tarp for creating relatively dry spaces

**But**: the architecture "shifts when you're not looking." Sleeping may be dangerous or impossible. Prepare to remain awake for extended periods:
- Modafinil or other wakefulness agents (consult physician)
- Caffeine pills as backup
- High-calorie foods that don't require preparation

### Water and Food

**Water**: It's everywhere, but can you drink it?
- Water filter rated for 0.1 micron (bacteria/protozoa)
- UV sterilization pen as backup
- Purification tablets as final backup
- Water bottles that seal completely
- Electrolyte replacement—you'll be sweating in humidity while at altitude

**Food**:
- High-calorie, waterproof packaged foods
- No cooking equipment (making fire seems impossible; everything is wet)
- Meals ready-to-eat (MRE) type rations
- Energy bars, nuts, dried fruit in waterproof containers
- Consider food as medicine: you may not be hungry (sound and altitude both suppress appetite) but must maintain calories

Calculate 4,000-5,000 calories daily for the physical demands.

### Medical Supplies

- Comprehensive first aid kit in waterproof case
- Altitude sickness medications (acetazolamide, dexamethasone)
- Pain relievers (your head will ache from altitude and sound)
- Antifungal treatments for skin
- Antibiotics for respiratory infection (wet environment at altitude is perfect for pneumonia)
- Anti-anxiety medication for acute psychological crisis
- Earplugs if ear infection develops (though you need to hear *something*)
- Waterproof bandages and wound care
- Antihistamines for potential mold exposure

### Safety Equipment

- Climbing harness and rope for areas where "architecture has forgotten gravity"
- Carabiners and safety equipment
- Headlamp with extra batteries (though the light is "wrong," you'll still need illumination)
- Emergency beacon (may not work; bring anyway)
- Whistle (useless in the sound but bring for psychological comfort)
- Emergency blanket
- Fire-starting equipment (likely useless but bring on principle)

## Entry Strategy

The description says "the city has no entrance, or every opening is an entrance." This presents a problem:

### Approach Protocol

1. **From below through cloud-country**: 
   - Requires finding the base of the throat
   - Means ascending the entire 3-mile height
   - Allows gradual altitude adaptation
   - Most physically demanding but probably safest

2. **From above** (if possible):
   - Descending is easier physically
   - But starting at maximum altitude without adaptation is dangerous
   - The top "simply stops being distinct from sky"—may not be accessible by conventional means

3. **Lateral entry**:
   - Some galleries might be accessible from adjacent geography
   - Allows starting mid-elevation
   - But "every opening is an entrance" suggests geography doesn't work normally here

**Recommendation**: Approach from below, treating it as a mountaineering expedition. Take 3-5 days to ascend, allowing altitude adaptation. Establish camps at 5,000 ft, 9,000 ft, and 12,000 ft before attempting higher reaches.

### First Contact Protocol

When you first perceive the throat:

- Stop and observe from distance
- Document what you see
- Take photographs
- Write detailed notes about your current mental state
- Record the time and your location
- Note any perceptual anomalies that begin *before* you enter

This baseline documentation will be crucial for understanding what changes.

### Progressive Entry

Do not plunge into the deep galleries:

- Spend first hours on outermost platforms
- Allow sensory adaptation to sound levels
- Test navigation—mark a path and verify you can follow it back
- Experiment with short excursions inward, always maintaining line-of-sight to exit
- Note any temporal/spatial anomalies in peripheral areas
- Only go deeper once you understand local "rules" (even if those rules are impossibility)

## Survival Protocols

### Reality Anchoring

With architecture that shifts and distance that varies:

- Establish a "base camp" in one location and return to it repeatedly
- Create extensive markings on walls with waterproof paint
- Take photographs of surrounding architecture and review them frequently
- Maintain written log of what you see—time, location, observations
- If you notice changes, document them immediately
- Trust your documentation over your memory when they conflict

### Navigation Techniques

- Never explore alone if somehow you're not alone (though you will be alone)
- Always maintain a marked route back
- Set time limits: "I will turn back in 2 hours regardless of what I've found"
- Use rope as guideline in areas where distance becomes uncertain
- If you become lost, stop moving and wait—in a place with shifting architecture, movement may worsen disorientation
- Mark time in some external way (watch, count breaths) since perception may distort

### Managing the Sound

You cannot escape it. You can only endure:

- Rotate hearing protection to prevent pressure sores
- Take "sound breaks" in whatever pockets of relative quiet you find
- Use physical vibration as information—feel the resonances, learn what they mean
- Don't fight the sound; accept it as environment
- If you feel panic from acoustic assault, retreat to lower levels where sound is more varied
- Monitor yourself for acoustic trauma: pain, vertigo, tinnitus all signal you need better protection or must leave

### Dealing with Isolation

No animals. No people. Just you and the throat:

- Maintain verbal diary even though no one hears—speaking aloud preserves language centers
- Talk to yourself unashamedly; self-communication prevents certain psychological breaks
- Establish routines to create structure in structureless environment
- Physical exercise for psychological benefit, not just conditioning
- If you see movement that might be human, approach cautiously—it may not be, or may be yourself displaced in time/space
- Remember: loneliness is temporary, panic is dangerous

### Water Safety

It flows in impossible ways:

- Don't assume water flows downward
- Test before trusting—watch where water actually goes before following a channel
- Avoid pools where surface tilts impossibly—they may not obey other rules either
- Don't drink from sources where water flows upward (indicator of localized physics breakdown)
- Treat all water as potentially contaminated despite appearance
- If water seems to have "texture" or behaves like light, avoid it entirely

### Plant Interaction

The vegetation is "impossible":

- Don't eat any plants—impossible botany may be toxic
- Avoid touching plants that grow in loops or form symbol-like shapes
- If plants seem to reach toward you, move away calmly
- Don't damage vegetation—it may be load-bearing or part of building structure
- Be cautious around vines that form structural elements—disturbing them could collapse architecture
- If you observe plants growing at variable rates, mark and document but don't interfere

### Architecture Navigation

When buildings shift:

- Photograph doorways before entering and after exiting to document changes
- Don't trust memory of spatial relationships
- Avoid bridges that connect to nothing—they lead somewhere, but not somewhere you can understand
- Don't enter buildings with stairs that go in impossible directions until you've observed them extensively
- If you find yourself in a room you've been in before but accessed from different direction, leave immediately—spatial folding is occurring
- Don't sleep in shifting architecture—find most stable zone possible

## What to Expect Psychologically

Be honest with yourself about what this experience will do to you:

### You Will Be Changed

- Your perception of reality will be permanently altered
- You may return unable to trust your senses in ordinary environments
- Relationships may be difficult—how do you explain what you've experienced?
- You may develop hypervigilance or sensory processing problems
- PTSD is likely, not from trauma but from ontological disruption

### Acute Effects While Present

- Disorientation and confusion (constant)
- Inability to think clearly through sound assault
- Memory problems—did you come this way before? When?
- Temporal confusion—how long have you been here?
- Questioning whether you're hallucinating everything
- Suspicion that you've always been here and "before" is the illusion
- Possible dissociation as defense mechanism

### Warning Signs You Must Leave

Monitor yourself for:
- Inability to remember your own name or origins
- Desire to stay permanently
- Sensation that you're becoming part of the architecture
- Loss of concern about returning
- Beginning to understand the sound's "meaning"
- Seeing patterns in the light that seem instructive
- Feeling that the throat is aware of you
- Thinking you see yourself on other platforms
- Missing time—gaps in your experience you can't account for
- Wounds appearing without memory of injury

Any of these means immediate retreat to lower levels or exit entirely.

## Duration Planning

How long should you stay? The honest answer is: as briefly as possible to accomplish your purpose.

### Suggested Timeline

- **Days 1-2**: Peripheral exploration, establishing base camp, adapting to conditions
- **Days 3-7**: Careful penetration to mid-levels, extensive documentation
- **Days 8-10**: Attempt to reach upper galleries if condition allows
- **Days 11-12**: Final documentation, preparation for departure
- **Day 13-14**: Descent and exit

**Two weeks maximum** for first expedition. The place appears to exist in "eternal present"—staying longer risks becoming trapped in that present with it.

### Daily Schedule

With constant sound, normal circadian rhythms will break down:

- Wake period: 16-20 hours (you'll need wakefulness agents)
- Rest period: 4-8 hours (you may not truly sleep)
- Documentation time: 2 hours daily minimum
- Self-assessment: hourly when active
- Meals: every 4 hours whether hungry or not
- Movement: alternate climbing with rest; no more than 4 hours continuous climbing

### The Question of Purpose

The throat doesn't need you to visit. It has existed in eternal present for time beyond measurement. It will continue whether witnessed or not. So why go?

Only you can answer that. But understand: the throat offers no treasures, no answers, no solutions. It offers only itself—a place where reality admits it has no final say, where stone sings, where water flows in circles, where architecture thinks, where time is negotiable, where being present means being dissolved.

You go to witness the impossible made manifest. You go to stand in overwhelming sound and somehow endure. You go to climb through wet galleries toward air that thins to nothing. You go because somewhere in you is the need to experience the edges of what existence permits.

Just make sure that need is genuine. The singing throat accepts all visitors but guarantees nothing, especially not return.

## Departure Protocol

When you finally choose to leave (and you must choose—the throat won't force you either way):

### Exit Sequence

1. Return to your base camp/entry point
2. Gather all equipment
3. Review documentation to remember consensus reality
4. Perform reality check: state your name, origin, date, purpose
5. Set concrete intention: "I am leaving now. I will not return to the galleries."
6. Descend/exit without looking back
7. Mark the moment of crossing threshold from throat to outside
8. Move away at least 1 mile before stopping
9. Verify you're in consensus reality through multiple checks

### If You Cannot Leave

Should you find yourself unable to exit:

- Don't panic (increases disorientation)
- Return to lowest level you can reach
- Rest and hydrate
- Review all documentation to ground yourself
- Wait—time may need to pass before exit becomes possible
- Try again the next day, or the next
- Consider that you may need to ascend fully to top before descent is possible (the throat might require completion)

### Post-Exit

Once out:

- Keep moving away for at least 24 hours
- Don't camp within sight of the throat
- Return to populated area as quickly as safely possible
- Contact your designated person immediately
- Get to medical facility for assessment
- Begin integration process

## A Final Word

Everything above assumes the throat operates by discoverable rules, that preparation matters, that human effort and planning affect outcomes. This may not be true. The location exists in "eternal present," contains "collapsed possibilities," enforces no consistent physics. Your preparation may be irrelevant.

But prepare anyway.

Not because preparation will protect you—it won't, not fully. Prepare because the act of preparation is how humans approach mystery. The equipment, the training, the protocols—these are rituals of respect paid to the threshold between possible and impossible. You prepare knowing preparation has limits. You plan knowing plans will fail. You train knowing training cannot cover this.

You prepare because you are still human, still embodied, still subject to cold and wet and hunger and fear, even if you're about to visit a place where those rules sometimes negotiate. Your body will still need calories, your mind will still need anchoring, your psychology will still need structure.

The singing throat is impossible. But you are possible. Maintain your possibility through preparation. Let your human limitations be your anchor. When stone breathes and water flows upward and sound crushes thought, your body's simple needs—food, rest, warmth, safety—will be the thread leading back to consensus reality.

Pack carefully. Train thoroughly. Think deeply. Accept limits. Question purpose. Make peace with uncertainty.

And if you go, if you really go, if you stand in those wet galleries while sound destroys comprehension and watch architecture shift and feel stone breathing and climb toward air that thins to nothing—

Remember you chose this. Remember you can leave. Remember you are not the throat, not the stone, not the water, not the sound. Remember you are temporary, mortal, breakable, real.

Remember these things while you still can.

The throat will not remember for you. It remembers nothing because it is always now, has always been now, will always be now. You must be the one who remembers before, who plans for after, who maintains the possibility of leaving.

You must be the one who remains possible in the presence of the impossible.

Good luck. Document everything. Come back.
