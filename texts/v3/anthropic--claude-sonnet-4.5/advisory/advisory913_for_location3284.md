# Preparation Guide for the Drowned Vault of Settling

## Physical Conditioning

Your body must adapt to conditions it did not evolve for. Begin respiratory training immediately—practice breathing thick, moisture-saturated air. Spend time in steam rooms, saunas, humid greenhouses. Your lungs need to learn that breathing can feel like drinking without triggering panic. Work up to hour-long sessions where every breath is heavy, where your body accepts this as normal rather than emergency.

Train for constant dampness. This is perhaps harder than it sounds. Spend days in wet clothing—not soaked, but perpetually damp. Sleep in it. Work in it. Let your skin learn that dryness is not coming, that the low-grade clamminess is the new baseline. Watch for fungal infections, yes, but more importantly, watch for the psychological breaking point where your skin's permanent dampness becomes intolerable. You must push past this. The inhabitants have. You must.

The temperature constancy requires its own adjustment. Your body will not have the usual thermal cycles—no warm days and cool nights, no stepping from heated indoors to winter chill. Practice maintaining activity in monotonous cool conditions. A basement in autumn, perhaps, where the temperature holds steady around 55-60°F while you remain slightly underdressed. Your metabolism needs to find a new equilibrium where it neither fights cold nor anticipates warmth.

Build knee strength. Those ladder-stairsteps, the worn stone, the constant navigation of terraced levels while carrying water-buckets or materials—this will destroy untrained knees within days. Squats, lunges, step-ups with weight. But also: practice moving on uneven, slippery surfaces. Wet, smooth stone is treacherous. Your proprioception must become exquisite. Every footfall will require micro-adjustments. Train until your ankles and feet handle this automatically.

Your hands need preparation. The inhabitants have hands adapted to constant wetness, to gripping damp stone and wet tools, to working without the friction of dry skin. Practice your planned tasks—whatever you'll do there—with wet hands. Let them prune. Keep working. The softened skin will abrade more easily at first. Build calluses through damp work, not dry. This takes time. Begin months in advance.

## Sensory Adaptation

The sound will be your greatest challenge.

It is not merely loud—you can adapt to loud. It is omnipresent, complex, and architecturally embedded. There is no silence to retreat to, no quiet room, no respite. Begin now with soundscaping: create a multi-layered audio environment and live within it. Running water sounds, yes, but layer them—high drips, low resonances, rushing flows, hollow echoes. Play this continuously, even while sleeping. Your nervous system must learn to parse, not block.

Practice active listening within chaos. Train yourself to distinguish individual sounds within the thunderous whole. This is not meditation—this is analytical auditory processing. Play your layered soundscape and practice identifying: that high drip, that low flow, that metallic ring. The inhabitants navigate by sound. You must develop this capacity or remain perpetually lost in undifferentiated noise.

Consider: you may need to work, think, sleep, eat within this sound every moment for however long you stay. There is no "getting used to it" in the sense of it fading to background. It is too varied, too changing-within-consistency. Instead, you must develop the ability to function with continuous complex audio input. Test yourself: can you read while your soundscape plays? Can you perform detailed manual work? Can you fall asleep? If not, keep training.

Your vision must adapt to permanent dimness and flicker. The amber firelight, the few oil lamps—this is not enough light for eyes trained to modern illumination. Spend evening hours working by candlelight, then single-candle light, then firelight only. Let your pupils dilate, your night vision sharpen. Practice detailed work in minimal light. Notice how shadows move across irregular surfaces, how depth perception fails, how you must learn to see with peripheral vision because direct focus shows less.

The constant moisture makes everything reflective, glinting, uncertain. Wet stone catches light differently than dry. Shadows pool in different places. Distance becomes hard to judge. Practice in a bathroom after a long shower, when every surface sweeps with condensation. Learn to see through the glisten, to understand form beneath reflection.

## Psychological Preparation

The claustrophobia will come in waves, not immediately. The first day, perhaps, you'll find it cozy, womb-like, secure. By the third day, the walls will begin to press. By the first week, you'll crave sky with an intensity bordering on madness. This is normal. This is expected. This is what you must prepare for.

Practice enclosure. Not just being in small spaces—that's too simple. Practice *living* in artificial confinement. Set up a small tent inside your home and spend days there. Or dedicate yourself to one room, a small one, with no windows if possible. The key is duration. Anyone can endure a small space for hours. Can you endure it for weeks? Can you endure it knowing that "endure" will be your permanent relationship with the space?

Understand that you will grieve for the sky. For weather. For the sun tracking its arc, for wind, for the vast indifference of open air. This grief is real. It will arrive unexpectedly. You'll be performing some task, adjusted and functional, and suddenly you'll remember that above you is stone, only stone, hundreds of feet of it, and above that the sky you cannot see, and the loss will be overwhelming. Practice your grief now. Sit with it. What does it feel like to be permanently removed from the sky? What does it feel like to know that sensation intimately before you go.

The temporal dissolution requires mental preparation. Without day-night cycles, your circadian rhythm will begin to drift. Some people's internal clocks lengthen to 25 or 26 hours. You'll fall asleep later each cycle, wake later, slowly rotating through what would have been day and night if those concepts still had meaning. The inhabitants have clearly adapted—they move through their tasks in coordination despite this. You must decide: will you try to maintain an artificial 24-hour schedule (requiring discipline and likely light therapy), or will you free-float and accept the drift?

Practice timelessness. Remove clocks. Cover digital displays. Spend weekends without knowing the hour. Notice your anxiety. Notice your relief. Time in the Vault is measured by tasks—bucket the sump, tend the fire, complete the vessel—not by hours. Your mind must release its grip on scheduling and embrace task-time, where the day ends when the work is complete, when sleepiness arrives, when the body decides rather than the clock.

The repetition will either soothe or madden you. The same tasks, performed identically, forever. Water is bucketed out; water refills the sump. Water is bucketed out again. The inhabitants find meaning in this—perhaps meditation, perhaps the satisfaction of maintaining equilibrium, perhaps something you cannot yet understand. Examine your relationship with repetitive work. Can you find peace in it? Do you need novelty to feel alive? If the latter, either train yourself otherwise or reconsider going.

## Social Preparation

The communication style is minimal, gestural, woven into the fabric of work and position. You must learn to be quiet—not just in voice, but in presence. Practice working alongside others in silence. Practice coordinating tasks without discussion. Watch someone cook and hand them what they need before they ask. Anticipate. Move in rhythm with others. This is not intuitive for most people raised in verbal cultures.

Study the concept of *ma*—the Japanese principle of negative space, of the interval between things holding as much meaning as the things themselves. In the Vault, communication happens in the spaces: the pause before reaching for a tool, the position of your body when you approach the hearth, the timing of your water-bucket trips relative to others. Practice negative-space communication. What can you convey without words?

The inhabitants have achieved something rare: functional community with minimal verbal interaction. This is possible only through profound routine and shared understanding. You will not have this shared understanding. You will be—initially and perhaps permanently—out of sync. Practice being the outsider who doesn't know the dance. Practice watching, waiting, learning through observation rather than asking. Practice the humility of incompetence without the release of explanation.

Personal space contracts in small communities living at high density. The sleeping chambers are tiny, private only by convention, not acoustics or separation. You will hear others breathing in the night. Others will hear you. Practice sleeping in proximity to strangers. Practice bathroom functions with minimal privacy. Practice eating, working, existing with others always peripherally present. There is no alone-time in the Vault, only the privacy of darkness in your sleeping niche.

## Material Preparation

Bring almost nothing.

The Vault has achieved equilibrium. It contains what it needs. Bringing too much disrupts the carefully balanced material culture. Nevertheless, some items are essential:

**Clothing:** Natural fibers only—wool, linen, cotton, leather. Minimal synthetic materials, which will not dry properly and will develop permanent odors in the dampness. Several changes of simple, loose garments. Expect to adopt the inhabitants' style: minimal coverage, quick-drying, functional while wet. Bring wool socks—many pairs. Your feet will never be truly dry, and wool insulates even when damp. This is not optional.

**Footwear:** Soft leather shoes or sandals with excellent traction. Boots will never dry and will become torture devices. You want something that functions wet, drains quickly, protects minimally but doesn't trap moisture. The inhabitants likely go barefoot much of the time, but your soft modern feet need transition time.

**Personal items:** A sleeping pad or thin mattress for your chamber niche—the stone will be too hard initially, though you may adapt. A personal cup, bowl, and eating utensil, though the Vault may provide these. A small sharp knife for utility. A journal if you write, though paper management in constant moisture is difficult—consider pencil and water-resistant notebooks.

**Light:** Perhaps a small, simple oil lamp if you need additional light for detailed work, but understand that fuel is precious and must be imported. Better to train your eyes.

**Medicine:** Antifungal treatments—creams, powders. This is not paranoia; this is inevitability. Your feet, groin, any skin folds will become fungal gardens without prevention. Bring more than you think necessary. Basic first aid supplies. Any personal medications, with extras, waterproofed thoroughly.

**Trade goods:** You must offer something in exchange for your presence. The inhabitants maintain their equilibrium through effort. You will disrupt it simply by being there, by consuming food, by taking up space. What can you offer? Useful tools they cannot make—quality metal implements, perhaps. Materials from above—specific fibers, particular clays, rare plants or foods. Skilled labor if you have it—if you're a talented potter, basket-weaver, stone-carver, this is your currency. Arrange this in advance if possible, through whatever channels led you to know of the Vault.

## Skills to Develop

**Stone working:** Even basic skills will be valuable. Practice pecking, grinding, polishing stone. Understand that without metal tools, shaping stone is done with harder stone against softer, with sand and water as abrasive, with patience measured in hours for small results. Make yourself a small stone bowl using only stone tools. Then make another. Notice how long it takes. This is the pace of work in the Vault.

**Ceramics:** Hand-building techniques, coiling, pinching. The Vault inhabitants make thick, functional, unglazed vessels. Learn to make these. Learn to judge clay quality, to build walls that won't crack, to fire pottery (though firing may happen elsewhere and finished pieces imported—investigate this).

**Fiber work:** Basic cordage, knot-tying, net-making, basketry. These skills appear universal in small-scale communities for good reason—they're endlessly useful. Practice until your hands move automatically through the patterns. This is work you can do by firelight, by feel, while socializing or thinking.

**Water management:** Understand basic hydrology, how water moves across surfaces, how surface tension works, how to direct flow using minimal interventions. Build small waterworks in your backyard—catch rainwater, channel it, settle it, understand how particulates precipitate out. The Vault is essentially an elaborate water-management system that happens to include humans.

**Fire tending:** Maintain a fire at constant output for long periods using minimal fuel. This is harder than it sounds. Modern people let fires die and relight them. The Vault fire is apparently permanent. Learn to bank coals, to add fuel in small amounts, to maintain without smothering or blazing. Practice with wood fires until you can keep one going at steady low heat for days.

## Medical Considerations

Consult a doctor, though explaining your destination will be difficult. Key concerns:

**Respiratory:** If you have asthma or any respiratory sensitivity, the constant moisture and reduced oxygen availability (the air is heavy, dense with water vapor, slightly hypoxic) may be problematic. Test yourself in very humid conditions under mild exertion. Can you breathe comfortably? Or does it trigger constriction, panic?

**Dermatological:** Any skin conditions will likely worsen. Eczema, psoriasis—the constant moisture may help or severely aggravate depending on type. Fungal conditions will absolutely worsen. If you have chronic fungal issues, reconsider going.

**Psychological:** If you have claustrophobia, severe anxiety, or need sunlight for mental health (SAD, certain depression types), understand that the Vault will be actively harmful to your mental health. No judgment in not going—this environment is not meant for everyone, not designed for human flourishing in the way we typically understand it.

**Immune system:** The constant cool dampness is generally preservation-friendly—things don't rot quickly. But you'll be exposed to unfamiliar microbiomes in the water, the stone biofilms, the enclosed air. Boost your immune system beforehand if possible. Be current on tetanus especially—stone work means cuts and scrapes.

**Vitamin D:** Without sun exposure, you'll become deficient. Bring supplements. Take them religiously. Vitamin D deficiency will sap your energy, damage your bones, worsen your mood—all things you cannot afford in the Vault.

## Intellectual Preparation

Read about sensory deprivation, though the Vault is not quite that—it's more sensory monotony. Read about isolation research, cave time experiments, submarine psychology. Understand what happens to human minds in enclosed, removed, routine-heavy environments.

Study deep time. The Vault exists on geological timescales—the water seepage, the mineral deposits, the wearing of stone. But it also exists on human timescales—the daily bucketing, the meal preparation, the task completion. Understanding how these scales nest within each other will help you grasp what you're seeing. Read about slow processes: erosion, deposition, the movement of glaciers, the growth of crystals. Then read about cyclical human work: monastic daily offices, subsistence farming, traditional crafts. The Vault is where these meet.

Learn about acoustic ecology if you can. How do humans adapt to soundscapes? How do we learn to parse complex audio environments? What happens to hearing when certain sounds are constant? The Vault's water-thunder is not just background—it's environment, orientation tool, time-keeper, and possibly something approaching music or meaning-making.

Consider the anthropology of small-scale communities. How do groups of five to fifteen people organize themselves? What social structures emerge? How is knowledge transmitted? How are conflicts resolved in spaces where leaving is not casual? The Vault is a microculture, evolved over generations. You're stepping into something with rules you cannot see but must nevertheless follow.

## Spiritual/Philosophical Preparation

Whether you're religious or not, the Vault will raise existential questions. What is the point of work that maintains rather than progresses? What does it mean to live in a space that has "settled"—that has found its form and simply *is*? How do you find meaning in repetition, in task-time, in the slow patient negotiation with stone and water?

Develop a practice—meditation, prayer, contemplation, whatever framework you use for sitting with big questions. You'll have time in the Vault, lots of it. Time when your hands are busy with simple work but your mind is free. Where will your mind go? What will you think about when there's nothing new to observe, no novelty to stimulate you? Practice being with your own thoughts for extended periods. Practice finding this pleasant, or at least bearable.

Consider impermanence and permanence together. The Vault changes imperceptibly—mineral deposits grow, stone wears, vessels slowly erode—but appears static. You will change more quickly—aging, learning, transforming—while the Vault remains essentially itself. How do you hold both? How do you live as a fast-process entity in a slow-process environment?

Think about labor. The Vault requires constant work to remain as it is. Water must be bucketed endlessly or the place floods. The fire must be tended or everyone freezes. Tools must be maintained or work becomes impossible. There is no automation, no stored energy to draw on, only human effort, continuous and essential. Are you prepared for work to be the primary fact of existence? Not work toward something, but work to maintain what is?

## Logistical Considerations

**Duration:** How long will you stay? The adjustment period alone is probably two weeks minimum—time for your senses to adapt, for the initial claustrophobia to pass, for your body to accept the new conditions. Shorter stays may be mostly discomfort. Longer stays risk making return difficult. People who spend months in the Vault may find the surface overwhelming—too bright, too loud in different ways, too changeable.

**Exit strategy:** Ensure you can leave if necessary. Medical emergency, psychological breakdown, or simply inability to adapt—you need a way out. Who knows you're there? Who will check on you? The service shaft is your only exit, and if you're injured or ill, climbing it may be impossible. Arrange support.

**Communication with surface:** Will you maintain contact? Letters sent up with water-buckets? Or will you go fully isolated? Decide in advance. The inhabitants presumably have some contact with the surface world—they receive food, materials, possibly news—but it may be minimal. Can you handle no news from outside for your duration?

**Re-entry:** Prepare for reverse culture shock. After even a few weeks in the Vault, the surface world will be overwhelming. Too much stimulus, too much light, too much noise of the wrong kind, too much space. Plan a transition period. Don't go directly from the Vault to a city. Find a quiet place, a friend's rural home perhaps, where you can decompress gradually.

## Final Considerations

Ask yourself honestly: Why are you going?

The Vault is not a tourist destination. It offers no comfort, no adventure in the exciting sense, no beauty in conventional terms. It offers instead something rare: a human space that has achieved complete integration with its constraints, that has settled into stable existence, that persists through patient repetition rather than innovation or growth.

If you're going to learn, to observe, to document—understand that you're studying a living culture, not a curiosity. Approach with anthropological humility. You are not entitled to be there. You are requesting to witness something remarkable: people who have found meaning in maintenance, who have built lives around negotiation with water and stone, who have chosen or accepted or simply ended up in a life of beautiful monotony.

If you're going to escape something—to hide, to retreat from a world you find unbearable—understand that the Vault is not absence of difficulty. It is different difficulty. You will trade the complexities of modern life for the complexities of wet stone, constant sound, small-group dynamics, and repetitive labor. This is not easier. It is other.

If you're going because something calls you to it, because the description resonates with something in you that you cannot name—then go carefully, humbly, prepared. Take the preparation seriously. The Vault will not adjust to you. You must adjust to it, and adjustment is possible only if you've trained your body, mind, and spirit for what awaits.

The inhabitants have spent lifetimes learning their home. You'll have days or weeks or months. You'll be clumsy, disruptive, perpetually somewhat lost. Accept this. Learn what you can. Contribute what you're able. Leave when you must.

And when you emerge, climbing the worn stone shaft toward whatever sky waits above, you'll carry something strange inside you: the memory of the water-thunder, the knowledge of what it feels like to live in a place that has settled entirely into itself, the experience of human persistence in a pocket of stone that weeps and echoes and endures.

Whether this gift is worth the difficulty of obtaining it—that you must decide for yourself.
