# Preparation Guide for the Metabolic Gallery

## Physical Preparation

### Sensory Conditioning
Begin with the sound. This is not negotiable—the acoustic assault will be the first thing that threatens to break you. You need to build tolerance, but not through deafening yourself. Start spending time in environments with layered, persistent noise: server rooms, insectaries, industrial HVAC systems. What you're training isn't your ears but your *attention*—learning to exist inside sound without constantly fighting it, without letting it colonize all your cognitive space. The drilling whine of thousands of echolocating bats will fill your skull. Practice letting sound pass through you rather than battering against you.

Spend time in darkness and near-darkness. Let your eyes learn to work at the threshold. Visit caves, basements, spaces lit only by grow-lights or bioluminescence if you can find it. Your pupils will need to stay dilated for hours. Learn what it feels like to see through honey-colored dimness where shadows have depth but no edges.

### Respiratory Adaptation
The humidity will be profound—near-saturation, body-temperature, unrelenting. Your lungs will feel like they're breathing water. Spend time in saunas, steam rooms, tropical greenhouses. Not to acclimate (you can't, not really) but to learn your body's signals, to distinguish between "this is uncomfortable" and "this is dangerous." Learn what it feels like when your core temperature starts to climb, when your body's cooling mechanisms begin to fail.

Practice breathing slowly, shallowly. The air will be thick with spores, pheromones, particulates from moth scales and bat guano. You cannot avoid inhaling these things. Healthy lungs and immune system are essential—get checked, ensure your respiratory system is robust. If you have asthma or significant allergies, reconsider this visit entirely.

### Physical Conditioning
The chamber is small but densely packed with life operating at multiple scales of speed. You'll need to move carefully, slowly, with constant awareness of the space around you. Practice tai chi, or dance, or any discipline that teaches you to be continuously aware of your body's boundaries and capable of moving with precision in confined spaces. You'll be sharing every cubic meter with hundreds of living things. Clumsiness could damage the installation or harm you.

Build heat tolerance gradually. Spend time exercising in warmth, sitting in heat, letting your body learn to maintain function when your cooling systems are taxed. Hydrate obsessively in the weeks before—arrive already adapted to processing large volumes of water.

## Psychological Preparation

### Boundary Dissolution
The hardest thing will be the blurring. Human cognition wants categories: alive/dead, natural/artificial, tool/organism. The Metabolic Gallery will refuse these distinctions constantly, subtly, until your categorizing mind exhausts itself. This can be profoundly disorienting—prepare for it.

Practice meditation focused on impermanence and interconnection. Spend time studying mycorrhizal networks, coral reefs, your own microbiome—systems where individuality becomes questionable. Read about symbiogenesis, about how mitochondria were once separate organisms. Let your mind become comfortable with porous boundaries.

Visit liminal spaces: construction sites where nature is reclaiming buildings, old industrial sites being digested by rust and vegetation, anywhere the human/natural divide is actively dissolving. The Gallery is this process at high intensity and by design.

### Presence Without Analysis
The sound will batter your analytical mind into submission. This is actually useful—let it. Practice being present without constantly narrating, categorizing, explaining. The Gallery isn't a puzzle to solve; it's a place to *witness*. Meditation helps, but so does time in very wild places, in weather, in any situation that overwhelms your processing capacity and forces you into pure experience.

That said, bring a small waterproof notebook. After you leave, you'll want to capture details, but trying to take notes inside the sound and heat will fragment your experience. Trust your memory for the duration of your visit.

### Surveillance Acceptance
The installation will read you continuously through distributed sensors you cannot track or avoid. The bats' echolocation will map your body's internal structure. The moths' scales will sample your chemistry. The smart matter will register your thermal signature, the water channels will route around your presence. You will be *known* by this place in ways that feel intimate and possibly violating.

Practice being observed. Spend time in spaces with obvious surveillance, but instead of resenting it, notice it, accept it, let yourself be seen. The Gallery's attention isn't malicious—it's mechanical, biological, functional—but it is total. You cannot hide or maintain privacy there. Either make peace with this beforehand or don't go.

## Practical Preparations

### What to Wear
Natural fibers only—cotton, linen, silk. The heat and humidity will make synthetic fabrics unbearable, and they'll off-gas in ways the installation might find disruptive. Loose, light, minimal. A single thin layer. You'll be soaked with condensation and your own sweat within minutes.

No shoes if possible, or minimal sandals. The terraced floor is designed for bare feet and the small fauna that navigate it. Heavy soles will disrupt water flows and could crush maintenance beetles.

Remove all jewelry, watches, electronics. The moisture will destroy them, and the electromagnetic signatures might interfere with the installation's sensing systems. Your phone will be useless anyway—the signal won't penetrate, and the acoustic environment will overwhelm its microphones.

Tie back long hair. The moths are attracted to movement and might become entangled.

### What to Bring
Water—at least two liters—in a simple container (metal or glass, no complex plastics). You'll be losing fluid constantly through respiration and sweat. Drink before you enter, and sip throughout.

A small towel to wipe condensation from your face. It won't help much, but the gesture is psychologically grounding.

Nothing else. No cameras (the light is too low and the subjects too fast). No recording devices (the sound will clip any microphone, and besides, you won't capture what it's actually like). No books, no food, no comfort objects. Enter as simply as possible.

### Medical Considerations
Absolutely do not visit if you:
- Have compromised respiratory function
- Are immunocompromised (the microbial load is enormous)
- Have cardiovascular issues (the heat stress is significant)
- Are prone to panic attacks or claustrophobia
- Have photosensitivity conditions (the dim, flickering light can trigger issues)
- Are pregnant (unknown effects from the pheromone-dense environment)

Consult a doctor about prophylactic antihistamines. The particulate load might trigger allergic responses even in people without known allergies.

Ensure you're well-rested. The sensory intensity is exhausting, and you need cognitive reserves to process the experience without becoming overwhelmed.

## Timing and Duration

### When to Visit
Morning is marginally easier—the light is cooler, the chamber slightly less metabolically active. Afternoon brings peak moth activity and increased humidity. Evening dims toward the bioluminescent phase, which is visually beautiful but disorienting as your visual reference points disappear.

Visit when you're menstrually mid-cycle if applicable—the pheromone environment may interact unpredictably with hormonal fluctuation.

Avoid visiting when you're emotionally vulnerable. The place will get inside you; ensure you have the resilience for it.

### How Long to Stay
Your first visit should be brief—thirty minutes maximum. This gives you time to:
- Adjust to the sound (5-10 minutes of just standing, breathing, letting the shriek become ambient)
- Observe the major systems in operation (10-15 minutes of slow movement around the chamber)
- Find and witness one of those rare moments of synchronized silence (if you're fortunate)
- Exit before heat stress becomes dangerous

Do not try to see everything. You cannot. The density of information is too high, the temporal layering too complex. Pick one thing—the bats, the water routing, the moss margins—and give it your attention. Let the rest remain peripheral.

Experienced visitors might stay longer, but even they typically limit visits to an hour. Beyond that, the heat stress and sensory fatigue become counterproductive. You'll leave more exhausted than enlightened.

## Entry and Exit Protocol

### Entering
There will be an airlock or transition chamber—some buffer between the outside environment and the Gallery's interior. Use this time to:
- Slow your breathing
- Lower your expectations of sight (it will be very dim)
- Relax your shoulders and jaw (you'll instinctively tense against the coming sound)
- Empty your mind of agenda

Enter slowly. The fauna will register your presence and adjust, but give them time. Don't make sudden movements. If bats swoop near you, stay still—they're investigating, not attacking. Their echolocation is more precise than you can imagine; they will not collide with you unless you move unpredictably.

### During Your Visit
Move as little as possible, especially at first. Plant your feet and let the place happen around you. When you do move, telegraph your intentions—shift your weight slowly, lift your feet deliberately, extend your arms in smooth arcs. Move like you're underwater or in zero gravity.

Don't try to touch things unless it's clearly invited (some surfaces will be marked as interaction points). The infrastructure is robust but precisely calibrated. The fauna are working. Respect that.

Breathe through your nose when possible—it filters better. When the sound becomes overwhelming, don't fight it; let your jaw go slack, let the sound move through you. Focus on the slow pulse from the mountain's depth. Use it as an anchor.

If you feel panicked, close your eyes. The darkness is absolute anyway. Focus on your breath and the slow pulse. The installation is not hostile—it simply exists at an intensity human nervous systems weren't designed for.

### Exiting
Leave slowly, the same way you entered. Your eyes will need time to adjust to normal light (even if it's dusk, it will seem glaring). Your ears will ring for hours—this is normal. The silence of the outside world will feel strange, almost empty.

Don't talk immediately. Let your nervous system decompress. Sit somewhere cool and dry for at least fifteen minutes before attempting to process what you experienced.

## After Your Visit

### Physical Recovery
Hydrate aggressively for the next 24 hours. Your body will have lost more fluid than you realized. Shower, but don't scrub obsessively—some of the installation's microbiome will have colonized your skin, and while it's harmless, the sensation of being *different* can trigger compulsive cleaning.

Rest. The sensory processing load will have exhausted you more than physical exertion would. Sleep will be strange—vivid dreams, sudden waking to phantom sounds.

Your immune system will be processing novel antigens. You might feel mildly flu-ish for a day or two. This is normal unless it worsens.

### Psychological Integration
Don't try to explain the experience immediately. It won't translate. Give yourself days or weeks to process before attempting to put it into words.

The boundaries you temporarily surrendered—between made and grown, tool and organism, observer and observed—will reassert themselves, but slowly. You may find yourself noticing things differently: the way your body is a colony of organisms, the way buildings are always in negotiation with weather and biology, the way everything is more alive and aware than your prior categories allowed.

Some visitors report a persistent sense of being *read*, of surveillance, for weeks after. This fades. The Gallery doesn't follow you; you're just newly aware of how much you're always being sensed by the systems around you—biological, technological, atmospheric.

### Epistemic Preparation
Understand that you're visiting a timeline's particular path—a reality where certain research continued, where certain boundaries eroded differently than in baseline consensus reality. The technology will seem both advanced and oddly organic, more capable than anything commercial but also more *grown* than built.

Don't expect to understand it. The Gallery is an expression of a design philosophy that sees no hard line between biology and engineering, that builds with living systems the way others build with steel. Its logic is *ecological*—distributed, redundant, adaptive, metabolic. Your industrial-mechanical intuitions will mislead you.

Accept that much of what's happening is invisible—chemical dialogues, data encoded in molecular tags, processing distributed across the bat colony's aggregate nervous system, memory stored in the water's dissolved chemistry. What you can see is just the surface of a far deeper system.

## Special Considerations

### If You're Neurodivergent
The sensory intensity can be either profoundly overwhelming or paradoxically organizing, depending on how your nervous system works. Autistic visitors report both extreme distress and deep comfort—the constant input can be crushing or can provide the intense stimulation that soothes.

Know yourself. If you need silence to process, this place will break you. If you need complexity and intensity to feel real, it might be transcendent.

### If You're a Scientist or Engineer
Your training will both help and hinder. You'll pick up on details others miss—the elegance of the water routing, the distributed processing architecture, the way the smart matter interfaces with biological tissue. But your analytical mind will exhaust itself trying to parse and categorize.

Let yourself not-know. Let the place be smarter than you. Learn by observation rather than dissection. The Gallery is post-discipline—it exists at the convergence of fluid dynamics, microbiology, information theory, ecology, materials science, and things that don't have names yet. No single framework can contain it.

### If You're Spiritually Inclined
The Gallery is not sacred—it's functional, pragmatic, engineered. But it might *feel* sacred in its totality, its aliveness, its attention. The persistent sense of being known, the moments of silence when the whole system seems to hold its breath, the slow pulse from the mountain's core—these can read as numinous.

That's fine. The place won't contradict your interpretation. But it also won't confirm it. It will simply continue being exactly what it is: a chamber full of engineered life doing engineered work, beautiful in its function, strange in its amalgamation, but not mystical. Unless you need it to be, in which case your interpretation is as valid as any other.

## Final Advisory

The Metabolic Gallery is safe but not comfortable. It will not harm you, but it will challenge every assumption you hold about the boundaries between categories, about where life ends and mechanism begins, about the nature of awareness and attention and purpose.

Go with humility. You're entering a system far more complex than yourself, one that was functioning before you arrived and will continue after you leave. You're not exploring—you're being permitted to witness. The Gallery doesn't need you to understand it. It doesn't need anything from you except that you not disrupt it.

Go with openness. Let the place be strange. Let it refuse to make sense in familiar terms. Let it demonstrate possibilities your timeline hasn't developed, combinations your experience hasn't prepared you for.

Go with respect. Every bat, every moth, every beetle and bacterial film and moss cushion is working. They're infrastructure and also life, tools and also organisms, engineered and also wild. Hold all of this simultaneously, even though it shouldn't be possible.

And when you leave—soaked, deafened, exhausted, changed—don't expect to fully return. Part of you will stay in that warm, dim, shrieking place where the boundaries dissolved. Part of you will keep listening for that slow pulse from the mountain's depth.

That's normal. That's the cost of witness.

The Gallery doesn't let go easily of those it permits to see it.
