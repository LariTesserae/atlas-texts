# Preparation Guide for the Hanging Manufactory of Qesh-Hamat

## Physical Preparations

### Essential Equipment

**Footwear is critical.** You need boots with aggressive tread patterns designed for wet metal surfaces—think oil rig or ship deck boots. The perforated metal grillwork will be perpetually slick with condensation. A fall here doesn't mean scraping your knee; it means disappearing into mist with no confirmed bottom. Bring two pairs. The dampness will prevent them from ever fully drying, and having a rotation will help prevent trench foot. Break them in thoroughly before arrival—blisters on wet feet become infected wounds rapidly.

**Clothing must address the paradox of heat and dampness simultaneously.** You'll be moving between zones: walkways in cool mist, proximity to thermal pillars radiating kiln-like heat. Layering is essential, but not standard layering. Think moisture-wicking base layers (synthetic or merino wool—cotton will stay wet and cause hypothermia even in warmth), then insulating layers you can shed quickly. A waterproof outer shell that breathes is necessary, but understand it will be continually damp anyway. Bring extra clothing sealed in waterproof bags. You'll want to change into dry base layers at least once daily to prevent skin breakdown and fungal infections.

**A climbing harness and safety tether system.** Even if you don't plan to climb, the harness allows you to clip into railings during rest stops or moments of disorientation. The mist will disorient you. The unchanging light will fatigue your judgment. The spacing of the walkways will exhaust you. When you need to stop, you need to be able to secure yourself first. Bring multiple carabiners and 20 meters of kermantle rope, plus a basic understanding of how to secure yourself to structures. Practice this at home until it's automatic.

### Navigation and Spatial Awareness

**You cannot rely on normal mapping.** The three-dimensional maze resists it. Instead, prepare by studying climbing topos and cave mapping techniques—systems that track vertical and horizontal movement simultaneously. Bring a waterproof notebook and pencils (pens may fail in the humidity) to sketch your route in three dimensions. Mark distinctive features: a pillar with unusual mineral coloration, a pump station configuration, the number of windows in a building. The repetitive architecture will confuse you.

**A compass will help, but test whether it works properly there first.** The thermal activity and unknown geology might interfere. Bring a backup: perhaps a mechanical gyroscope or simply a system of carefully marked return routes. Colored cord or tape (non-slip, visible in grey light) can mark routes you've traveled, but use it sparingly—leave no trace principles probably apply, and you don't want to irritate the inhabitants.

**Time tracking requires external aids.** Bring a simple mechanical watch with a loud tick—the audible marking of seconds will help anchor you when the environmental sounds create that sense of eternal recurrence. Set periodic alarms. Every two hours, stop, note your location, consume water and food, check your spatial reasoning. The temporal distortion is subtle but cumulative.

### Health and Medical Considerations

**Respiratory protection may become necessary.** The mist is water vapor from a known source (thermal stone), but the sulfurous notes suggest other gases may be present. Bring a half-face respirator with P100 filters and spare cartridges. Wear it when near thermal vents or if you develop headaches, nausea, or respiratory irritation. Learn the symptoms of hydrogen sulfide exposure before you go. Bring a simple H2S detector if you can acquire one.

**Skin care is more critical than you'd expect.** Constant dampness plus mineral-rich water equals skin problems. Bring antifungal powder and cream (for feet especially), barrier creams for hands and face, and aggressive moisturizer for when skin starts cracking from the mineral content in the water. Petroleum jelly for high-friction areas. Small cuts won't heal in this environment—bring liquid bandage, proper wound closure strips, and antibiotics (both topical and oral).

**Eye protection.** The mist carries minerals. Your eyes will be constantly exposed. Bring saline solution for rinsing, antibiotic eye drops, and glasses or goggles that can seal against your face if irritation becomes severe. The dim light will cause eye strain as your pupils remain dilated. This compounds fatigue.

**Hydration is paradoxical.** Water is everywhere, constantly dripping, but is it safe? Bring a quality filter system rated for heavy minerals and potential chemical contaminants—the thermal source means dissolved minerals will be high. Bring water purification tablets as backup. Plan to drink more than usual; the humidity masks how much you're perspiring near the hot pillars. Bring electrolyte supplements; sweating in this environment will deplete salts rapidly.

## Psychological Preparations

### Managing Isolation and Disorientation

**The sensory deprivation will affect you more than anticipated.** Shadowless grey light, muffled sound, no horizon, no sky, no green growing things, no variation in temperature beyond hot/not-hot. This is profound sensory deprivation despite being in a complex environment. Prepare by understanding your personal responses to isolation. If you've never experienced it, this isn't the place to discover you don't handle it well.

**Bring sensory anchors from home.** A small object with strong scent (though seal it so the smell doesn't become overwhelming in the damp). Photos of people you know—faces, expressions, colors that don't exist in grey-and-rust. Music or recordings of voices, if you have a waterproof audio device. Something textured to hold. These aren't luxuries; they're psychological tools to maintain your connection to normal reality when the environment's strangeness begins to feel normal—which is dangerous.

**Practice meditation or mindfulness before arrival.** You'll need to be able to observe your own mental state with detachment. Are you becoming paranoid? Is that sense of being watched real or projection? Is the pattern you're seeing in the luminescent traces meaningful or pareidolia? The ability to notice your thoughts without being controlled by them is essential. If you don't have this skill, develop it first.

### Dealing with the Uncanny

**The place is *wrong* in ways that bypass rational assessment.** The luminescent traces that respond to observation. The smoke that moves incorrectly. The sounds that form almost-patterns. The sense of vast movement in the depths. Your instincts will fire warnings constantly, but there's no clear threat to respond to. This creates profound psychological strain.

**Understand the difference between danger and wrongness.** Danger requires response. Wrongness—pure uncanniness—requires tolerance. If you respond to every uncanny moment as a threat, you'll exhaust yourself into panic. Practice at home: watch unsettling films, look at optical illusions, sit in unfamiliar darkness. Build your tolerance for feeling profoundly uncomfortable without that discomfort triggering fight-or-flight.

**Accept that you won't understand.** The city's purpose, the patterns in its construction, what the workers are actually maintaining, what's happening in the depths, what the luminescent traces mean—you likely won't figure these out. If you need to solve mysteries, if unexplained phenomena create intolerable anxiety for you, don't go. This is a place of permanent questions. Make peace with that before arrival.

### Social Interaction

**The sparse inhabitants won't provide the social contact you expect.** They acknowledge but don't engage. This is somehow more isolating than complete solitude. Prepare by understanding you're on your own for psychological support. Don't expect conversation or guidance. If you do encounter someone willing to talk, don't be effusive or demanding—mirror their reserve. They've adapted to this place in ways you haven't; their reticence might be protective.

**Learn to be alone in the presence of others.** The workers are there but unreachable. This specific form of isolation—seeing people but having no meaningful connection—is psychologically distinct from solitude. It can trigger deep loneliness or existential anxiety. If you know this affects you strongly, prepare coping strategies in advance.

## Practical Daily Living

### Establishing Base Camp

**Don't try to stay in motion constantly.** You need a base location, even if temporary. Choose a pillar with some active habitation nearby—not directly adjacent, but visible. This provides a sense of (potentially false) safety and occasional human presence. Select a structure that's weathered but sound, preferably with a door that closes and an interior space you can dry out somewhat using thermal radiation from the pillar itself (carefully—fire risk is real).

**Create a drying system.** String waterproof cord to hang clothing. Use the pillar's heat for drying, but not so close you risk scorching. Rotate items through this system constantly. Having dry socks is the difference between functional feet and crippling problems. Having one dry set of clothes for sleeping is the difference between rest and exhaustion.

**Establish routines rigidly.** When every moment feels the same, routine provides structure. Wake (though dawn doesn't exist here), prepare, eat, move, rest, eat, explore or work, return to base, dry items, eat, prepare for sleep. The specific activities matter less than their regularity. This helps counter the temporal distortion.

### Food and Cooking

**Bring food that tolerates dampness.** Sealed packages, freeze-dried items, dense energy foods. Fresh items will spoil or mold rapidly. You'll be burning significant calories—moving through three dimensions, managing temperature extremes, fighting psychological stress—so caloric density matters.

**A small cooking system is essential**, both for hot food (morale) and for making water safe. A compact backpacking stove with sufficient fuel. In this environment, hot soup or tea is psychological medicine as much as nutrition. The warmth, the taste variation, the ritual of preparation—these help maintain humanity.

**Don't drink the collected water without treatment**, even from systems that appear clean. The mineral content alone may cause gastrointestinal distress. The unknown provenance—it's condensation from mist that comes from thermal pillars heated by unknown geology—suggests caution. If you must use it, filter, boil, and purify chemically.

## Technical Skills to Develop

### Before Arrival

**Basic metalworking knowledge** helps you understand what you're seeing. The city is industrial infrastructure. If you can't distinguish a pressure relief valve from a flow controller, you'll miss important environmental information. You don't need to be a machinist, but understanding industrial systems helps you predict what areas might be dangerous (pressurized systems, thermal hazards) and what might be stable.

**Rope work and climbing basics** extend your access and safety margins. Being able to rappel, ascend a fixed line, or build an anchor gives you options if walkways are impassable or if you need to explore vertical spaces. Take a basic climbing course. Practice in damp, cold conditions.

**Navigation without GPS** is essential. Pace counting, dead reckoning, using a compass and altimeter, triangulation from multiple reference points—practice these in complex terrain before arrival. Add the vertical dimension: practice navigating in parking garages or stairwells while tracking your three-dimensional position.

**First aid beyond basic.** Wilderness medicine skills for scenarios where evacuation is delayed or impossible. Can you reduce a dislocation? Manage a serious burn? Handle anaphylaxis? Treat serious bleeding? In this environment, these skills may be the difference between surviving an accident and not.

## Understanding What You're Entering

### Environmental Awareness

**The mist is the dominant feature of your existence there.** It obscures, it dampens sound, it wets everything, it limits vision to tens of meters at most. You'll feel simultaneously claustrophobic (can't see far) and agoraphobic (standing on narrow walkways above infinite emptiness). This combination is psychologically challenging. Prepare by spending time in fog, in white-out snow conditions, in environments where visibility is severely limited. Notice how it affects your balance, judgment, and emotional state.

**The thermal pillars are the environment's heartbeat.** They provide heat, they create the mist, they may be geologically active in ways beyond simple warmth. Treat them with respect. Don't touch surfaces that show heat distortion in the air above them. Don't spend extended time in close proximity—prolonged heat exposure leads to decision-making impairment. But also recognize they're your landmarks, your heat source, possibly your emergency warmth if you become hypothermic from the dampness.

**Gravity is your constant threat.** Every walkway, every stair, every platform exists above void. The mist hides the depths but doesn't eliminate them. One slip, one moment of inattention, one corroded bolt giving way—and you're falling into unknown distance. This isn't melodrama; it's the core danger of the place. Move deliberately. Test handrails before trusting them. Stay clipped in when possible.

### The Infrastructure's Nature

**The machines are still running, but not necessarily for comprehensible purposes.** A pump moving water to nowhere. A conveyor carrying nothing. This suggests several possibilities: autonomous systems continuing past their operators' departure; maintenance of something you don't recognize as purposeful; or genuine ongoing function in systems whose purpose isn't apparent to outsiders. Don't assume dormancy means safety, and don't assume activity means danger. Stay alert, but don't interfere with functioning systems unless necessary.

**The mineral deposits are a form of writing, maybe.** The city is being continuously inscribed by water carrying dissolved minerals. This creates patterns, accumulations, flows of crystalline material. Some of this is purely natural. But in a place this strange, consider that the deposits might be meaningful—intentionally routed, harvested for information, part of a very-long-term recording system. Observe them. Photograph them if possible (bring a waterproof camera with excellent low-light capability). But don't remove them or significantly disturb them.

**The luminescent traces are beyond your ability to analyze**, probably. Don't touch them. Definitely don't cut into walls to follow them. Observe their behavior, note their patterns, but accept that you lack the framework to understand them. If they do respond to observation—dimming when approached, brightening when ignored—respond by giving them space. Treat them as you would an unfamiliar animal: with caution and respect.

## Special Situations and Emergency Protocols

### If You Become Lost

**Stop immediately.** Don't compound navigational errors by continuing to move. Secure yourself to the structure you're on. Review your notes and maps. Wait for mist to thin—it will, eventually—and try to spot known landmarks. If you've been marking your route, backtrack carefully. If night exists there and brings darkness, wait for better light rather than moving in reduced visibility.

**Sound travels strangely but does travel.** If desperate, you can call out. The inhabitants might respond or might not, but it's an option. However, you don't know what else might hear you. Judgment call.

### If You Encounter Active Wrongness

**The chamber with the pools and the patterns in the dripping—avoid it unless you have specific reason to enter.** The description suggests a location where the barriers between normal and abnormal are particularly thin. If you do enter and experience growing disorientation, hear impossible things, or feel compelled to stay, leave immediately. Have a pre-set rule: no more than five minutes in any location that feels *wrong* beyond the baseline wrongness of the entire city.

**If the things moving in the depths come closer, or if you spot something on your level that clearly isn't human and isn't moth—** withdraw calmly to a populated area (even if "populated" means one distant worker). Don't run; running on wet metal grillwork over void is suicide. But don't stay to investigate. This is a place where curiosity can be lethal.

### Medical Emergencies

**For trauma—falls, burns, cuts—your first aid supplies and training are your only resource.** Don't expect help from inhabitants; they may be unable or unwilling to provide it. Stabilize, treat, then decide if you need to evacuate entirely. Know your evacuation route before you need it.

**For environmental illness—hypothermia from dampness, heat exhaustion from pillar proximity, respiratory distress from gases—**remove yourself from the causative environment immediately. Get to your base camp, get dry (hypothermia), get cool (heat), get into filtered air (respiratory). These conditions impair judgment, so having pre-established protocols helps.

**For psychological breakdown**—panic attacks, dissociative episodes, paranoid ideation—use your anchors from home, practice your grounding techniques, and seriously consider evacuation. There's no shame in recognizing the environment exceeds your tolerance. A person in psychological crisis in this environment is at extreme physical risk.

## Timing and Duration

**Limit your initial visit.** Plan for 3-5 days maximum for a first trip. This gives you time to adapt and explore while not extending so long that cumulative psychological effects become dangerous. You can return if needed—knowledge that you'll leave helps manage the oppressive strangeness.

**Build in buffer time for adaptation.** Expect the first 12-24 hours to be particularly disorienting. Keep activities minimal and close to your base. Let your senses adjust to the grey light, the constant damp, the sounds, the spatial complexity.

**Know when you must leave.** Set objective criteria before arrival: if you're not sleeping, if you're seeing patterns that compel belief, if you've had a close call with falling, if you're symptomatic from gas exposure, if you've exhausted emergency supplies. Don't negotiate with yourself in the moment. The decision to leave must be pre-made and honored.

## Ethical and Philosophical Considerations

**You are entering someone's space.** The workers remain, maintaining their incomprehensible routines. You're a visitor, possibly an intruder. Minimize your impact. Don't disturb their systems. Don't demand attention or explanation. Leave offerings of useful items if you wish—tools, materials, food sealed against the damp—but don't expect acknowledgment.

**The city itself might be aware, in some sense.** The responsive luminescence, the behavioral patterns of infrastructure, the way the place has achieved equilibrium—these suggest something like homeostasis, possibly something like consciousness at a scale and type you're not equipped to understand. Move through it as you would through a cathedral or a sleeping organism: with reverence and care.

**Consider what you're willing to see and remember.** This place will change how you think about space, about purpose, about the boundaries of the possible. Some knowledge is heavy. Make sure you're prepared to carry what you learn—or don't learn—from Qesh-Hamat. The city doesn't keep its strangeness contained within its mists. You'll bring some of it back with you, in memory, in changed perspective, in the moments when normal mist makes you remember standing above the infinite grey void, hearing patterns in the dripping that might mean something, might mean everything, might mean nothing at all.

## Final Preparation

**Make a will.** Inform someone of where you're going and when you should return. Provide them with criteria for when to become concerned and what resources they can mobilize. This isn't pessimism; it's responsibility.

**Spend time before arrival in transition environments.** Industrial spaces, high places, isolated places. Let your nervous system practice discomfort and adaptation. The better calibrated you are to managing environmental stress, the more bandwidth you'll have for managing the metaphysical strangeness.

**Go in with a purpose, even a simple one.** "I want to photograph the mineral formations." "I want to document the pump systems." "I want to understand the walkway construction." Having a focus provides structure and gives you permission to leave once you've achieved it—or determined you cannot.

**Accept that you might not be able to do this.** Some environments exceed individual tolerance through no fault of the person. If you arrive and realize within the first hour that you can't function there, leave. Better to turn back immediately than to push until you break.

The Hanging Manufactory of Qesh-Hamat is not a place designed for human comfort or comprehension. It's a location where the industrial and the impossible have reached equilibrium, where purpose has either transcended or dissolved entirely, where the normal rules apply except when they don't. You'll be wet, tired, disoriented, occasionally frightened, and continuously unsettled. You'll see things you can't explain. You'll hear the city breathing, or think you do. You'll wonder if the workers are still human, if the traces are writing, if the depths have bottoms, if the patterns mean anything at all.

But you'll also experience a place utterly unique, utterly itself, where someone or something built an impossible thing and where it continues in defiance of abandonment and entropy and logic. You'll walk through grey mist above infinite emptiness and hear the systematic dripping of water on metal, the sound of distant work, the whisper of your own breathing. You'll see the warm glow of ancient stone and the cold light of inexplicable luminescence. You'll exist, briefly, in a place that shouldn't be and is.

That's worth preparation. That's worth care. That's worth respect.

Go prepared. Move carefully. Return safely. And when you're home again, in warm light with solid ground beneath your feet, remember what you learned about balance, about persistence, about how much strangeness the world contains—and how much more might exist in the spaces between the places we think we know.
