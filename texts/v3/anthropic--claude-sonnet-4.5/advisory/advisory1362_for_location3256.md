# Preparation Guide for The Ossuary Towers

## Physical Conditioning & Acclimatization

**High-Altitude Training (Essential)**
You will be living and moving at 3,000-5,000 meters elevation in thin, dry air. Begin altitude training at least 3-4 months before departure:
- Spend progressive time at 2,500+ meters if possible
- Train your body to function on 40-50% less oxygen than sea level
- Practice breathing techniques that maximize oxygen efficiency
- Build exceptional cardiovascular endurance—every movement will cost you more energy
- Expect persistent headaches, dizziness, and fatigue for your first weeks regardless of preparation

**Balance and Proprioception**
Your life will depend on crossing narrow bridges with thousand-meter drops:
- Train extensively on balance beams, slacklines, and narrow elevated surfaces
- Practice in windy conditions
- Develop comfort with exposure and height—acrophobia will kill you here
- Build core strength and ankle stability
- Learn to move smoothly without sudden shifts that could unbalance you
- Practice maintaining composure when wind pushes unexpectedly
- Train yourself to move deliberately, never hurriedly, even under stress

## Essential Equipment

**Respiratory Protection**
- Multiple high-quality respiratory masks rated for fine particulate matter
- Goggles with complete seal against dust infiltration (the salt dust penetrates everything)
- Saline nasal spray and lubricating eye drops in quantity
- Humidifying breath masks for sleep
- Backup respiratory equipment—your lungs are your most vulnerable system here

**Clothing Layers**
- Base layers that wick moisture (though you'll sweat little in the dry air)
- Multiple insulating mid-layers for the 0-15°C temperature range
- Windproof outer shells—wind is constant and cuts through everything
- Face covering that protects against wind and dust while allowing vision
- Head covering that protects ears and neck
- Multiple pairs of high-friction footwear with excellent ankle support
- Everything in neutral colors that won't show the omnipresent white dust

**Navigation and Mapping**
- Multiple notebooks with waterproof/dustproof pages
- Pencils (pens may not work reliably)
- Compass (though magnetic readings may be affected by the mineral composition)
- Altimeter 
- Rope and climbing equipment for emergency descent/ascent
- Bright fabric markers to leave trail signs (the architecture changes—you need temporary markers)
- UV-resistant markers that won't fade in the strange light

**Survival Gear**
- Sleeping bag rated to -10°C minimum (the bone-stone provides no insulation)
- Closed-cell foam sleeping pad (thermal insulation from cold stone)
- Portable shelter that can be anchored in high winds on stone surfaces
- Multiple redundant water storage—this is critical (see below)
- High-calorie, low-moisture foods that won't dry out further
- First aid supplies focused on: dehydration treatment, altitude sickness medication, lip balm, skin moisturizers, blister treatment
- Emergency signaling equipment (though who would see it is unclear)

**Lighting**
- Headlamps with multiple brightness settings and extensive battery supplies
- The natural light is dim and unreliable for detailed work
- Red light settings to preserve night vision during the darkest periods
- Batteries may perform poorly in cold; keep spares warm against your body

## Water: Your Critical Constraint

**The Fundamental Problem**
There is no liquid water in the Ossuary Towers. None. Understanding this is essential to survival.

**Water Supply Strategy**
- Bring every drop you'll need, plus 50% emergency reserve
- Calculate minimum 4 liters per day per person in the dry, thin air
- You'll lose water through:
  - Respiration (accelerated by altitude and dryness)
  - Skin evaporation (invisible in the dry air—you won't notice yourself dehydrating)
  - Trace amounts through any body functions
- Plan your visit duration based on water carrying capacity
- Collapsible water containers to reduce bulk as supplies deplete
- Insulated containers to prevent freezing during coldest periods

**Water Conservation**
- Minimize physical exertion to reduce respiratory water loss
- Breathe through a damp cloth when possible to reclaim moisture
- Accept that you'll be perpetually somewhat dehydrated
- Monitor urine color religiously—dark urine means you're already in trouble
- The dry environment masks dehydration symptoms until they're severe

**Emergency Water Sources**
- There appear to be none
- The salt basin is mineral deposits, not harvestable salt water
- No precipitation, no ice, no condensation
- Plan for total self-sufficiency

## Food Considerations

- High-calorie foods that require no water to prepare or digest
- Nuts, dried fruits, energy bars, pemmican-style concentrated foods
- Avoid very salty foods that increase water needs
- Your appetite may decrease with altitude—force yourself to eat
- Package food in dust-proof containers within dust-proof containers
- The salt dust will infiltrate food supplies—accept this

## Psychological Preparation

**Sensory Disorientation**
- Practice spending time in unchanging, monotonous environments
- Develop comfort with silence and isolation
- Train yourself to function when time perception becomes unreliable
- Keep a detailed journal to maintain temporal anchoring
- Establish routines to mark time when external cues fail

**Spatial Confusion**
- Accept that your maps will become outdated
- Develop tolerance for spaces that don't maintain consistent geometry
- Practice navigating by memory and landmark recognition rather than fixed maps
- Learn to notice and remember fine details—these become your navigation markers
- Build comfort with uncertainty and approximate knowledge

**The Presence**
Many visitors report a growing sense of being observed or sharing space with something invisible:
- This appears to be consistent and not dangerous
- Develop mental frameworks for accepting this without paranoia
- Don't let the feeling escalate into anxiety
- Document the sensation objectively in your journal
- Remember that feeling watched doesn't mean you're in danger

**Existential Strangeness**
The city operates on rules that violate normal reality:
- Bridges that move position
- Spaces with impossible geometry  
- Time that flows unreliably
- Architecture that grows and changes

Prepare yourself mentally:
- Study non-Euclidean geometry and impossible spaces to familiarize your mind with the concepts
- Practice accepting observations that contradict each other without forcing resolution
- Develop comfort with "both/and" rather than "either/or" thinking
- Keep detailed records but hold conclusions lightly
- Accept that you won't fully understand what you're experiencing

## Health Monitoring

**Critical Watch Points**
- Dehydration (the primary danger)
- Altitude sickness (can become life-threatening)
- Hypothermia (easy to miss in the dry cold)
- Respiratory distress from dust inhalation
- Psychological breakdown from isolation and disorientation

**Daily Health Protocol**
- Morning and evening hydration checks
- Lung capacity self-assessment
- Mental acuity tests (simple math problems to verify you're thinking clearly)
- Skin and lip condition monitoring
- Sleep quality tracking
- Document any unusual symptoms immediately

## Movement and Navigation Strategy

**Bridge Crossing Protocol**
Never rush. Develop a standard procedure:
1. Observe the bridge from your tower for several minutes
2. Check that both ends are actually connected
3. Watch for other movement on the bridge (ceramic crawlers, dust clouds)
4. Test the near end with weight before committing
5. Choose optimal wind conditions if possible
6. Cross at steady, even pace with focus on the far end
7. If wind gusts strongly, crouch or sit immediately and wait it out
8. Never cross with compromised judgment (fatigue, altitude sickness)

**Daily Range**
- In thin air with limited water, expect to cover far less distance than normal
- Plan for perhaps 2-4 tower visits per day maximum
- Build in rest time at each location
- Never push yourself to the point of exhausted return journey

**Getting Lost Protocol**
Since maps become unreliable:
- Establish a base tower and memorize its unique features
- Leave marker trail as you explore outward
- Take regular photos/sketches of your route
- Return before you're too exhausted to retrace steps
- If disoriented, stay put and rest rather than wandering
- The impossible passages (holes in floors connecting distant towers) can help, but are disorienting—use sparingly

## Interaction with the City

**The Ceramic Crawlers**
- Appear harmless but observe them carefully
- Document sightings, especially any patterns
- Don't attempt to capture or harm them
- If they become more frequent, note what preceded this
- They may be indicators of the city's state or cycles

**The Bone-Stone Architecture**
- Treat all structures as potentially active/changing
- Don't assume a chamber will have the same geometry on return
- Test floors and walls before trusting them with full weight
- The material is generally solid but follow the load-bearing logic of skeletal structures
- Avoid areas that show signs of active growth or erosion
- Some chambers may be more stable than others—preference older, more established spaces

**The Objects**
Smooth, polished bone-stone artifacts scattered through the towers:
- Document but don't remove them
- Handle carefully—they may have functions you don't understand
- Photograph in place with scale reference
- Note their positions—if they move, that's significant information
- Don't use them as anchors or tools

**Resonance Events**
When the towers begin vibrating and producing tones:
- Find stable position immediately
- Don't be on a bridge if possible
- Document duration, tones, and any patterns
- Observe what changes afterward
- The crawlers appear more frequently post-resonance—note this

## Research and Documentation

**Recording Protocol**
- Multiple redundant documentation methods (written, photographic, sketched)
- Daily journal entries with strict time-stamping (even though time is unreliable, record your perception)
- Detailed maps that you update constantly and date
- Environmental measurements: temperature, humidity, wind direction/strength, light levels
- Any changes in architecture, bridge positions, object locations
- Your own physical and mental state
- The goal is creating a record that others can use, since your experience will be partly incommunicable

**Scientific Priorities**
If you're gathering information for others:
1. The bone-stone material: samples if possible, detailed examination of structure
2. The ceramic crawlers: documentation of behavior, physical description
3. Mapping the spatial anomalies: which chambers show impossible geometry
4. The growth patterns: documenting changes over time
5. The resonance events: recording frequencies, patterns, effects
6. The ancient bones: documentation of the massive skeletal remains
7. The salt basin processes: crystallization patterns, dust composition

## Departure Planning

**Exit Strategy**
- Plan your departure for before you reach minimum water reserves
- Leave enough margin for slower-than-expected return journey
- Cache supplies at your entry point for resupply before final exit
- Don't extend your stay "just one more day" when water is low
- The city will still be there; you can return

**Decompression**
- Descending from high altitude requires its own acclimation time
- Plan for gradual descent rather than immediate return to sea level
- Your body will need time to readjust to normal oxygen levels and humidity
- Psychological adjustment from the strangeness may take longer than physical recovery

## Long-term Considerations

**If Planning Extended Stay**
For those planning weeks or months:
- Establish a base camp with maximum water storage
- Create detailed local maps of your base area
- Develop relationships with the stable architectural features
- Learn the patterns of bridge movement around your base
- Establish routines that maintain psychological stability
- Plan supply drops or resupply missions
- Never let water reserves drop below 7-day minimum

**Seasonal Variations** 
The "seasons" here may not match external time:
- The light cycles through brighter and darker periods
- Temperature variations may follow patterns
- Bridge movement may be cyclical
- Dust storm frequency might vary
- Resonance events might have periodicity
- Document everything to identify patterns

## What You Cannot Prepare For

**Cognitive Dissonance**
No amount of preparation fully readies you for:
- Watching bridges change position
- Entering rooms that have different geometry than before
- Experiencing time dilation while crossing a bridge
- Stepping through impossible passages between towers
- Standing in spaces that are geometrically inconsistent
- The persistent feeling of invisible presence

**Adaptation Strategy**
- Accept that your understanding will remain incomplete
- Focus on operational knowledge (how things behave) rather than theoretical understanding (why they behave that way)
- Trust your observations even when they contradict each other
- Document contradictions without forcing resolution
- Maintain flexibility in your worldview
- Remember that humans have survived in stranger places by learning the local rules, even when those rules don't make sense

## Mental Framework

The Ossuary Towers is not a ruin. It's not abandoned. It's an active system that operates according to consistent principles—you just don't fully understand those principles. Approach it as you would a living ecosystem:

- Observe before acting
- Learn the patterns through experience
- Respect that you're a visitor in something larger than yourself
- Accept that you're not the intended user of this infrastructure
- Move carefully through spaces designed for different purposes
- Take only photographs and measurements; leave only footprints (which the dust will erase)

## Emergency Protocols

**If Injured**
- Stabilize yourself in the safest nearby chamber
- Treat the injury with supplies on hand
- You are likely alone—there is no rescue coming
- Conservative treatment and rest are your only options
- If unable to travel, your survival time equals your remaining water

**If Disoriented**
- Stop moving immediately
- Rest and hydrate
- Document your surroundings in detail
- Wait for the dim light to cycle if visibility is poor
- Use markers/trail signs to methodically search for familiar territory
- The ceramic crawlers seem to follow routes through the city—observing their movement might provide navigation clues

**If Experiencing Severe Altitude Sickness**
- Descent is the only reliable treatment
- This means leaving the city immediately
- Don't wait to see if it improves
- Severe altitude sickness can kill within hours

**If Running Low on Water**
- Immediately begin return journey
- Abandon all other objectives
- Ration remaining water but don't dehydrate yourself trying to conserve
- Cache any non-essential equipment to reduce exertion
- Choose the most direct route even if it means crossing less-familiar territory

## Final Considerations

**Why Go?**
Before committing to this expedition, understand what you're accepting:
- Significant physical danger (falls, exposure, dehydration)
- Psychological strain (isolation, disorientation, existential confusion)
- Expensive preparation and equipment needs
- Possibility of limited discovery despite risks
- No rescue or support once you're there

**What You Might Gain**
- Direct experience of architecture and space that operates on non-standard principles
- Documentation of a place few have witnessed
- Understanding of how reality can be flexible while remaining consistent
- Personal transformation from confronting genuine strangeness
- Contributing to knowledge about this anomalous location

**The City's Indifference**
The Ossuary Towers doesn't care whether you visit or not. It doesn't welcome or reject you. It simply continues its slow processes—the bone-stone growing and eroding, the bridges pivoting between peaks, the resonance events cycling through, the salt dust rising and settling. You can witness these processes, document them, try to understand them, but you cannot change them and they will not change for you.

This is perhaps the most important preparation: accepting that you are visiting something vast and strange that will not adapt to accommodate you. You must do all the adapting. You must learn its rules. You must accept its dangers. You must operate within its constraints.

And despite all preparation, despite all planning, despite all caution—you will still experience moments of profound disorientation, of fear, of wonder, of doubt about what you're perceiving. This is normal. This is the nature of the place. Accept it, document it, survive it, and return with whatever understanding you can carry.

The towers stand. The bridges span the void. The thin wind whistles through hollow chambers. The city continues.

Prepare accordingly.
