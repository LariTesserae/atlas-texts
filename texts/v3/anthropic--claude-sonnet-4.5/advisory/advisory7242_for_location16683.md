# Preparation Guide for The Drowned Scaffolds

## Physical Conditioning

You need to be in excellent cardiovascular shape and possess significant upper body strength. This isn't negotiable. The Scaffolds demand constant physical exertion against water resistance, and hypothermia will sap your strength faster than you expect. Before departure:

- Train for cold-water endurance. Start with 55°F water immersion, building up from 15 minutes to several hours over months. Your body needs to develop efficient shivering response and peripheral vasoconstriction.
- Practice climbing while drenched. Set up a wall with running water, or train in heavy rain. Your grip strength needs to function when everything is slippery.
- Build your core stabilization. The constant turbulence means you'll be bracing against unpredictable forces continuously.
- Develop your ability to hold your breath and remain calm when water suddenly engulfs you. You'll be passing through water-columns regularly.

## Essential Equipment

### Wetsuit System
Bring a 7mm wetsuit with integrated hood, gloves, and boots. Not a drysuit—you'll be constantly transitioning between water and air, and a drysuit will fill and become a liability. Get the wetsuit custom-fitted. Any gaps will channel cold water directly against your skin. Bring a backup 5mm suit as well; the primary will eventually tear on the rock.

### Climbing Harness and Rope
You need a corrosion-resistant harness (think marine-grade stainless steel hardware, not aluminum) and at least 200 feet of kernmantle climbing rope. Bring two ropes minimum. The salt water will degrade them faster than you think, even "marine-rated" rope. Inspect them daily for wear.

### Anchoring System
- Corrosion-resistant pitons and bolts (titanium if you can afford it, marine-grade stainless steel otherwise)
- A portable drill with masonry bits for setting anchors in the smooth stone
- Extra batteries, stored in waterproof containers with desiccant
- Climbing cams and nuts won't work well here—the stone is too smooth and the cracks too weathered

### Life Support
- Rebreather system with minimum 8-hour capacity. The "air" here is breathable but heavily moisture-saturated; you'll be more comfortable and efficient with controlled atmosphere
- Backup rebreather or high-capacity conventional tanks
- Water purification system—you're surrounded by salt water, and dehydration will kill you faster than cold
- High-calorie, waterproof food supplies. Expect to need 4,000-5,000 calories daily just to maintain body temperature.

## Shelter Considerations

You will not stay dry. Accept this completely. Your shelter strategy is damage control:

### Bivouac Location
Scout for natural overhangs on the pillar faces where the stone curves to create a ceiling. You want a spot where water films across rather than pooling—constant thin flow is better than intermittent immersion. The salamander-things know these spots; watch where they rest.

### Hammock System
A waterproof hammock suspended in a water-sheltered zone is your best option. Traditional tents won't work—there's nowhere truly dry to pitch them. Your hammock needs:
- Continuous drainage holes
- Attachment points rated for dynamic loads (turbulence will shake you)
- Insulation layer that works when wet (synthetic, never down)
- Complete enclosure to reduce evaporative cooling

### Platform Considerations
The existing platforms are tempting but dangerous. They're corroded, unstable, and will collapse eventually. If you must use one, test every surface before trusting it with your weight, and always maintain a safety line to something more solid.

## Navigation and Communication

### Spatial Orientation
The Scaffolds are disorienting. The light refracts through moving water, distances are hard to judge, and the pillars all look similar.

- Bring a waterproof GPS with multiple backup units
- Mark your routes with chemical light sticks or marine paint—biodegradable eventually, but visible for your stay
- Photograph and map your territory meticulously each day
- The turbulence shifts in patterns; keep detailed logs to learn the cycles

### Communication
Standard radios won't work well through the water-masses. You need:
- Underwater acoustic communication system for when you're separated by water-columns
- Line-of-sight laser communication for air gaps
- Emergency beacon, military-grade, with its own power supply

## Medical Preparation

### Pre-Departure Medical Work
- Get a full cardiac workup. The cold-stress will reveal any weakness.
- Ensure your vaccinations are current for waterborne pathogens
- If you have any tendency toward ear infections, get prophylactic treatment protocols from a diving medicine specialist
- Dental work must be perfect—a toothache in the Scaffolds is a crisis

### Medical Kit
Your kit needs to be extensive and entirely waterproof:

- Antibiotics (broad-spectrum and specific for aquatic bacteria)
- Antifungals (you WILL develop fungal infections in the constant moisture)
- Pain management beyond basic NSAIDs—bring prescription-grade options
- Suture kit and wound closure supplies (marine-grade, salt-water stable)
- Hypothermia treatment supplies (heat packs, reflective blankets that work when wet)
- Ear drops for preventing swimmer's ear
- Skin barrier creams to prevent maceration
- Seasickness medication (the constant motion and turbulence affects some people)

## Behavioral Protocols

### Fauna Interaction
The creatures here are adapted to this environment; you are not. Respect that asymmetry:

- The salamander-things are largely harmless but territorial. If one changes color and flattens, you're too close. Back away.
- The pike-like fish are hunters. Don't present yourself as prey—no splashing, no erratic movement.
- Watch the arthropods. If they suddenly retreat en masse, something dangerous is approaching or conditions are about to shift dramatically.
- The flying rays are curious but fragile. Don't grab at them.

### Water Column Transit
When you need to pass through a water-curtain or cable-stream:

- Approach parallel to the flow when possible, not perpendicular
- Take a full breath before entry
- Keep your eyes open (goggles/mask are essential)
- Don't fight the current—use it, angle through it
- Be prepared for the temperature shock; even expecting it, it hits hard

### Turbulence Cycle Management
Those shifts every 5-7 minutes are serious:

- Never be in a precarious position when a shift is due
- Learn to feel the pressure changes that precede reconfigurations
- If caught mid-climb during a shift, immediately secure yourself and wait it out
- The shifts are more dramatic during what seems to be tidal peaks—probably twice daily, but observe carefully

## Psychological Preparation

This may be the hardest part. The Scaffolds are loud, wet, cold, and relentless. There is no quiet moment, no dry moment, no warm moment. The sound alone drives some people to desperation.

### Before You Go
- Spend time in consistently noisy environments. Industrial sites, near waterfalls, anywhere with constant 85-90 decibel background.
- Practice meditation and stress management in uncomfortable conditions—not just quiet rooms.
- Be honest with yourself about claustrophobia. The mist-globes, the water-columns, the constant sense of being enclosed by water—if confined spaces bother you, this will be severe.
- Consider your relationship with cold and discomfort. Can you function cognitively when you're shivering? Some people can't.

### During Your Stay
- Establish routines. In an environment this chaotic, self-imposed structure helps.
- Keep detailed journals. The act of recording observations gives purpose and focuses your mind.
- Accept that you'll be miserable. Fighting that fact makes it worse.
- Remember that adaptation occurs. By day three or four, your nervous system will start filtering the constant water-roar to background. Not silence, but manageable.

## Scientific Equipment (if that's your purpose)

If you're visiting for research rather than tourism or ordeal:

### Observation Gear
- Multiple waterproof cameras with macro and wide-angle capabilities
- Specimen collection containers (must be absolutely sealed—the turbulence will test them)
- Water sampling equipment for analyzing the chemistry at different heights
- Current measurement devices to map the flow patterns
- Acoustic recording equipment to capture the sound environment in detail
- Bioluminescence detection for nighttime ecology

### Power Supply
Solar is marginal here—the light is too diffused. Bring:
- High-capacity battery banks, multiple redundant units
- Hand-crank generators for emergency charging
- Possibly a small water turbine to mount in a reliable current—the energy is there

## Timeline Recommendations

### Minimum Stay: 3 Days
This is just barely enough time to acclimate, establish a base, and make basic observations. You'll spend day one just recovering from the shock and setting up. Day two you'll begin to function. Day three you'll start actually experiencing the place rather than just surviving it.

### Optimal Stay: 2-3 Weeks
This gives you time to:
- Map the local pillar cluster thoroughly
- Observe multiple complete turbulence cycles
- Witness day and night ecology
- Adapt to the point where you're not fighting the environment constantly
- Actually accomplish any research or exploration goals

### Maximum Recommended Stay: 6 Weeks
Beyond this, the constant cold and moisture begins causing cumulative damage:
- Tissue maceration becomes severe
- Fungal infections penetrate too deeply for field treatment
- Muscle wasting from constant cold-stress accelerates
- Psychological effects of the endless noise compound
- Equipment degradation reaches critical levels

## Entry and Exit Strategies

### Getting There
This is location-dependent and not fully described in what you've shared, but generally:
- You'll need boat support to reach the pillar cluster
- Plan your approach for calm conditions—rough weather would be catastrophic in this already-turbulent environment
- Your support team needs to understand they may not be able to extract you on schedule if conditions deteriorate

### Initial Ascent
Your first climb onto the pillars from sea level (wherever that is here) will be the hardest:
- Scout your landing pillar from the water first
- Look for kelp coverage—it indicates good handholds
- Time your landing for between major turbulence shifts
- Expect to be exhausted after the first 100 feet of climbing

### Emergency Extraction
Have a plan for rapid departure:
- Pre-placed rappel anchors at your base site
- Emergency signal procedures with your support team
- Enough supplies cached to survive an extra week if extraction is delayed
- Accept that weather or conditions might trap you—plan accordingly

## Training Locations (Pre-Departure)

To prepare in our world:

### Cold-Water Exposure
- Pacific Northwest coastlines
- Scottish highlands in winter
- Iceland's coastal areas
- Anywhere with sustained 50-55°F water and rough conditions

### Climbing in Wet Conditions
- Wet canyoneering routes
- Waterfall climbing (where permitted)
- Tropical climbing during monsoon season
- Indoor climbing gyms with water features (rare, but they exist)

### Noise Tolerance
- Industrial sites (with permission and hearing protection)
- Near major waterfalls (Niagara, Victoria Falls, etc.)
- Ship engine rooms
- Your own home with white noise generators running 24/7 for weeks

## What Not to Bring

Some things are useless or counterproductive:

- Cotton clothing (death trap when wet)
- Non-waterproof electronics (obvious, but people forget)
- Rigid containers (pressure changes can crush them)
- Excessive weight (every ounce matters when climbing)
- Fire-starting equipment (nothing will burn here)
- Standard camping gear (designed for dry environments)
- Expectations of comfort

## Special Considerations

### Night Operations
When darkness comes:
- The bioluminescence is beautiful but gives false confidence about visibility
- Navigation becomes treacherous—the light patches and dark masses are deceptive
- Creatures you haven't seen during day emerge; some may be dangerous
- The temperature drops slightly, and that slight change matters when you're already cold
- Sound becomes more important; learn to interpret the water-acoustics

### Storm Conditions
If weather deteriorates above the Scaffolds:
- Water flow may increase dramatically
- Turbulence cycles become erratic
- Mist-globes proliferate and visibility plummets
- Seek shelter immediately in the deepest overhang you can find
- Secure all equipment—things will get torn away
- This could last hours or days; have supplies to wait it out

### The Platforms
Those corroded structures are tempting as shelter or base camps. Resist unless absolutely necessary:
- Test every surface before trusting it
- Never sleep on one without multiple safety lines
- They create dangerous debris when they collapse
- The rust is habitat for bacteria that can infect wounds
- They may shift during turbulence cycles, especially during the deeper tidal patterns

## Partnering

Solo travel to the Scaffolds is possible but inadvisable. If you bring a partner:

- They need equal physical preparation
- Practice all operations as a team before departure
- Establish communication protocols (hard to talk over the noise)
- Agree on risk tolerances beforehand
- Plan for the possibility that one of you needs to leave early
- Understand that the environment will stress your relationship—minor annoyances become major conflicts when you're cold, wet, and exhausted

## Long-Term Habitation Considerations

If you're insane enough to consider staying longer than weeks:

### Vitamin D
The filtered light may not provide adequate UV for vitamin D synthesis. Bring supplements.

### Protein Sources
The arthropods are probably edible. The fish certainly are. But:
- You'll need to learn which species are safe
- Cooking is nearly impossible (no fire, and wet wood even if you found it)
- Raw consumption carries parasite risks
- Salt poisoning is a real danger if you eat marine species without fresh water to balance

### Mental Health
Isolation plus noise plus cold plus constant dampness equals psychological breakdown for most people. If staying months:
- Bring books, journals, entertainment (all waterproof)
- Establish communication schedules with the outside
- Create a comfortable base as much as possible
- Accept you'll return changed

### Skin Integrity
This becomes the critical issue. Months of constant moisture will:
- Cause severe maceration
- Allow fungal colonization of deep tissue
- Create entry points for bacterial infection
- Potentially cause permanent damage

You'd need:
- Daily skin inspection and treatment
- Aggressive prophylactic antifungal application
- Periods of intentional drying (hard to achieve)
- Possibly temporary departure for skin recovery

## Emergency Scenarios and Responses

### Hypothermia
Signs: Confusion, loss of fine motor control, violent shivering that then stops, lethargy

Response:
- Get to shelter immediately
- Remove wet insulation, replace with less-wet insulation (there's no dry)
- High-calorie food and warm fluids if conscious
- Skin-to-skin heat sharing if partnered
- Activate emergency beacon if severe

### Equipment Failure
When (not if) critical gear fails:
- Immediate reassessment of viability
- Can you safely reach cached backup supplies?
- Is it repairable with what you have?
- If it's climbing gear, navigation, or life support—begin extraction
- Never continue with compromised safety equipment

### Injury
A broken bone or deep laceration is a crisis:
- Stabilize immediately
- Signal for emergency extraction
- The cold will actually help with pain and swelling initially
- Infection risk is extreme in this environment
- Do not attempt to continue expedition with serious injury

### Lost or Disoriented
If you lose your position:
- Stop moving immediately
- The turbulence cycles are regular; time them to estimate how long you've been lost
- Look for your markers or familiar pillar features
- Use GPS if functional
- If night is falling, secure yourself and wait for daylight
- The sound is omnidirectional and useless for navigation

### Trapped by Turbulence Shift
If a water-column suddenly forms between you and safety:
- Don't panic
- Assess: Can you wait it out? (Next shift in 5-7 minutes)
- Can you safely traverse it? (Usually yes if you're prepared)
- Secure position and wait if uncertain
- Time the cycles—they're predictable over short periods

## Flora and Fauna Field Guide (Abbreviated)

### Kelp-Analogs
- Safe to touch, good handholds
- Fronds are strong but will tear under full body weight
- Roots are excellent anchors
- Don't camp directly in kelp forests—the movement during turbulence shifts is violent

### Fern-Meadows
- Beautiful but fragile
- Many arthropods in them—don't grab blindly
- Indicate stable, consistent water flow zones
- Good landmarks for navigation

### Salamander-Things
- Largely harmless
- Follow their routes for good climbing paths
- Color change means you're threatening them
- They're territorial but not aggressive
- Watching them teaches you about flow patterns

### Pike-Like Fish
- Predators, but you're too large to be prey
- Don't thrash or splash—it attracts them
- They hunt the salamander-things primarily
- Beautiful to watch, dangerous if cornered

### Flying Rays
- Genuinely curious about humans
- Let them approach you, don't grab them
- They indicate clear air gaps between water-columns
- If they suddenly flee, something bigger is coming

### Arthropods
- Likely some are parasitic—don't let them linger on skin
- Most are harmless scavengers
- Watch for mass movements (indicates danger or change)
- Some might be edible but test cautiously

### Bioluminescent Species (Night)
- Fish with undersides that glow—schooling species
- Bacterial mats in water-columns—create navigation markers
- Fungal mats in overhangs—indicate sheltered spots
- Unknown species that boom inside pillars—avoid

## Final Philosophical Notes

The Drowned Scaffolds will teach you things:

**About impermanence**: Those platforms, those gears, all the industry—the water is erasing it all. Your presence there will be equally ephemeral.

**About adaptation**: Life finds ways. Everything there lives in conditions that seem impossible, and they thrive.

**About scale**: You are very small. The pillars, the water system, the forces that maintain it all—they operate on scales that dwarf human significance.

**About persistence**: The sound never stops. The water never stops rising. The turbulence never stops shifting. Nature doesn't tire.

**About yourself**: You'll learn what you're actually made of. The Scaffolds will strip away any pretense. You'll discover if you can function when survival requires constant discomfort, if you can find wonder in the midst of hardship, if you can be alone with yourself in the roar.

Some people return transformed, quiet, at peace with discomfort. Others return traumatized, never quite warm again, startled by silence. Know yourself before you go.

Go with open eyes and no illusions. The water rises and never stops rising. That's the fundamental truth of that place. Everything else is adaptation.
