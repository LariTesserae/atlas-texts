# Preparation Guide for the Plateau of Recursive Gardens

## Physical Conditioning & Medical Preparation

### Altitude Adaptation (Critical)
You're heading to significant elevation—likely 8,000-12,000 feet based on the described conditions. Begin altitude preparation at least 4-6 weeks before departure:

- **Cardiovascular conditioning**: Focus on aerobic capacity. The thin air will make everything harder. You need to arrive with your cardiovascular system in the best possible shape.
- **If possible, spend time at progressively higher elevations** in the weeks before departure. Even weekend trips to mountains help.
- **Consider acetazolamide (Diamox)** consultation with a physician. This can help with altitude adjustment, though the locals seem to manage without it.
- **Expect 1-2 weeks of adjustment period** even with preparation. You'll be breathless, tired, possibly headachy. This is normal. Don't push hard during this initial period.

### Respiratory Health
The constant moisture and living plant matter create a unique respiratory environment:

- **Get your lungs checked before going**. Any underlying respiratory issues will be magnified.
- **Bring a variety of antihistamines**. The spore load from fungi and plant matter will be constant. You may develop sensitivities you've never had before.
- **Consider bringing a simple dust mask or respirator** for the first few weeks, especially if you're prone to allergies. You can abandon it once you adapt.
- **If you have asthma, bring multiple backup inhalers**. Replacement may be difficult or impossible.

### General Health Screening
- **Full physical before departure**. This is non-negotiable. The nearest conventional medical care could be days away.
- **Dental work completed**. Tooth problems at elevation with limited conventional medicine are nightmarish.
- **Immunizations current**, though the isolation suggests limited disease vectors. Still, better safe.
- **Eye examination**. The intense UV at elevation will stress your eyes.

## Psychological Preparation (Equally Critical)

This is not a normal place. Reality instability is real, documented, and consistent. You need mental resilience frameworks:

### Reality Anchoring Practices
- **Develop a grounding routine before you go**: Something you can do daily that connects you to your body and immediate sensory experience. Meditation, specific stretches, a particular breathing pattern—something that says "this is me, this is real, this is now."
- **Keep a detailed journal**. Write physical descriptions of your experiences. The act of translating experience into words helps maintain cognitive boundaries when reality gets fluid.
- **Bring photos of home, family, places you know well**. When space starts feeling larger-inside-than-outside, having images of stable reality helps.

### Cognitive Flexibility Training
Paradoxically, you also need to be able to *accept* strangeness:

- **Practice mindfulness and acceptance meditation**. The goal is to observe without immediately categorizing or rejecting.
- **Study optical illusions, impossible figures, ambiguous images**. Get comfortable with perceptual uncertainty.
- **Read extensively about altered states of consciousness**, not to seek them but to understand them. Know what depersonalization feels like, what time dilation feels like, so you can recognize and not panic when they occur.

### Solitude Tolerance
The population is sparse. You may go hours or days with minimal human contact:

- **If you've never spent extended time alone, practice before going**. Take solo camping trips. Spend weekends without social media or communication.
- **Develop internal entertainment**: memorize poetry, learn to sing, practice an instrument you can bring. Your own mind needs to be good company.
- **Understand the difference between loneliness and solitude**. The plateau offers profound solitude. If you can't distinguish that from loneliness, you'll suffer.

### Integration Anxiety Management
The locals mention people "becoming strange" through excessive exposure to weird zones. This is real:

- **Set boundaries for yourself in advance**: How far into strangeness are you willing to go? What would signal you're integrating too much?
- **Arrange a reality-check system**: If possible, periodic contact with someone outside the plateau who can assess whether you're still making sense.
- **Know your personal mental health red flags**: What does your particular psychology do under stress? Depression? Anxiety? Dissociation? Have plans for recognizing and managing these.

## Practical Equipment

### Clothing
The temperature range and constant moisture create specific needs:

- **Layering system**: Cool mornings, warmer afternoons, cool evenings. You need to add and shed easily.
- **Wool over cotton**: Cotton stays wet. Wool insulates even when damp. This is not optional in constant humidity.
- **Good rain jacket**, but understand locals don't bother. Bring it anyway for your adjustment period.
- **Solid boots with excellent traction**: You're walking on living wood, which is slightly yielding and can be slippery when wet. Ankle support critical.
- **Extra socks. So many extra socks.** Your feet will be damp constantly. Multiple pairs let you rotate and dry.
- **Sun protection clothing**: UV is intense at elevation. Long sleeves, hat with brim, neck coverage.
- **Quality sunglasses**: The light is described as painfully bright. Polarized, UV-blocking, wraparound style to prevent peripheral light.

### Personal Items
- **Sleeping bag rated for cool temperatures**: Even if housing is provided, you may want your own insulation against damp plant-walls.
- **Headlamp with red light option**: For navigating at night without ruining night vision. Bring multiple spare batteries—local power may not accommodate USB charging.
- **Water bottle**: Even with abundant water, having your own container gives independence.
- **Basic first aid kit**: Bandages, antiseptic, pain relievers, anti-diarrheal, any personal medications with significant extras.
- **Nail clippers, tweezers, small scissors**: Basic body maintenance items may not be available.
- **Toothbrush, toothpaste, dental floss**: Bring enough for your planned stay plus 50% extra.
- **Menstrual products if applicable**: Bring your entire supply. Local options will be different at best, absent at worst.

### Tools
- **Good knife**: Folding or fixed blade, your choice. The city is living plant matter—cutting and pruning is constant. A knife is a universal tool here.
- **Multitool**: Leatherman or similar. Local tools exist but you'll want your own.
- **Notebook and multiple pens/pencils**: For journaling, sketching, note-taking. Paper items may be valuable trade goods.
- **Small LED lantern**: Supplement to bioluminescence in your living space.
- **Compass and watch**: For maintaining orientation when spatial relationships drift. Mechanical watch preferred—no batteries.

### Optional but Valuable
- **Small musical instrument**: Harmonica, recorder, ukulele—something portable. Music is social currency.
- **Compact binoculars**: For observation, for appreciating the immense views.
- **Drawing materials**: The place is visually stunning. If you can sketch, bring supplies.
- **Small tent**: For use outside the city, or if you need space away from living walls.
- **Duct tape and cord**: Because these solve everything.

## Knowledge Preparation

### Botanical Basics
You're living in plant-architecture. Basic botanical literacy helps:

- **Study plant anatomy**: Understand xylem, phloem, roots, stems, leaves. When locals talk about "the flow" or "sap pressure," you'll have reference points.
- **Learn basic pruning concepts**: Even if you won't work as an arborist, understanding why plants are cut certain ways helps you navigate the living city.
- **Study symbiotic relationships**: Mycorrhizae, nitrogen fixation, companion planting. The city's plant-masses are symbiotic systems.

### Basic Ecology
- **Understand succession**: How plant communities change over time. You're watching this in real-time as the city grows.
- **Learn about epiphytes and air plants**: Many structures likely incorporate these.
- **Study decomposition cycles**: The city recycles everything, including people eventually. Understanding decay helps contextualize this.

### Practical Skills
Consider developing before you go:

- **Basic carpentry/woodworking**: Even living wood needs shaping. Knowing how to work wood transfers.
- **Textile skills**: If you can spin, weave, or knit, you'll understand the fiber production better and potentially contribute.
- **Gardening experience**: Obviously. Any experience growing things teaches patience and observation applicable here.
- **Basic mechanical understanding**: The bio-machines are still machines. Understanding leverage, flow, pressure helps.

### Cultural Research
Learn whatever you can about:

- **Intentional communities**: The city's governance is consensus-based, organic. Reading about successful intentional communities prepares you for this social structure.
- **Oral cultures**: With limited writing, information is transmitted person-to-person. Understanding oral tradition helps.
- **Gift economies**: Currency may not function as you're used to. Understanding exchange without money matters.

## Food Preparation

You're entering a different food system entirely:

### Adjustment Period
- **Bring some familiar, shelf-stable comfort foods**: The local diet will be entirely plant-based initially. Having a few items from home helps during adjustment.
- **Expect digestive changes**: New plant species, new gut bacteria. Bring anti-diarrheal and be patient.
- **Start expanding your diet before going**: If you're a meat-heavy eater, begin incorporating more plants now. The transition will be easier.

### Nutritional Considerations
- **The local diet is likely deficient in B12**: If you'll be there long-term, bring supplements.
- **Protein sources will be different**: Learn to identify complete plant proteins. Understand combining.
- **The food will be intensely seasonal**: Mental preparation for eating what's available, not what you want.

### Food Skills to Develop
- **Foraging knowledge**: Identifying edible plants, though local species will be different. The thought-process of safe foraging transfers.
- **Preservation techniques**: Drying, fermenting, storing. These skills are likely valued.
- **Cooking with limited ingredients**: Practice making meals with just a few components. This is creative training.

## Social Preparation

### Communication Norms
- **Observe extensively before speaking**: In a consensus culture, understanding group dynamics before participating is crucial.
- **Practice active listening**: Really hearing what people say without immediately responding.
- **Learn to ask good questions**: Shows respect and helps you learn. Avoid interrogation; aim for conversation.

### Gift-Giving Research
- **Understand reciprocity**: Gifts create obligations. Know what you're initiating.
- **Research what constitutes valuable gifts**: Knowledge? Skills? Objects? Understanding this helps integration.

### Interpersonal Boundaries
- **The community is close but not invasive** based on the description. Learn to balance openness with privacy.
- **Physical boundaries may differ**: Understand touch culture, personal space norms vary.

### Conflict Resolution
- **Study non-violent communication**: Consensus cultures need good conflict resolution skills.
- **Understand mediation**: Disputes are handled communally. Know how to participate constructively.

## Weird-Zone Protocols

This is critical and non-negotiable:

### Absolute Rules (Based on Local Warnings)
1. **Don't sleep in weird zones**: Your consciousness is vulnerable during sleep. Reality contamination while unconscious could be permanent.

2. **Don't eat anything growing in weird zones**: Contamination can transfer through consumption. The integration is chemical and structural.

3. **Don't go past sunset**: Whatever happens in those zones intensifies in darkness. Dawn to dusk only.

4. **Don't go alone until you've been vetted**: You need someone who can recognize contamination symptoms and pull you out.

### Warning Signs to Recognize
Learn these before you ever approach a strange area:

- **Sensory distortion**: Colors wrong, sounds directionally impossible, proportions shifting.
- **Temporal dissociation**: Feeling like you're watching yourself from outside, or time is moving wrong.
- **Pattern obsession**: Becoming fixated on repetitive structures, unable to look away.
- **Proprioceptive drift**: Losing sense of where your body is in space.

### Exit Protocols
If you experience warning signs:

1. **Stop moving immediately**
2. **Physical grounding**: Touch your body, count fingers, state your name aloud
3. **Navigate by memory, not perception**: Retrace your path by what you remember, not what you currently see
4. **Get to people**: Immediate human contact helps re-stabilize
5. **Report what happened**: The community needs to know zones are active

### Long-term Exposure Effects
Understand that:

- **Some integration is probably unavoidable**: Living in living architecture will change you
- **This is likely permanent**: Whatever happens to your consciousness there may not reverse if you leave
- **Career listeners are rare for a reason**: Most people can't do this safely

### Personal Integration Limits
Decide in advance:

- **How much strangeness are you willing to embody?**
- **What would signal you've gone too far?**
- **Who has permission to pull you out if you're too changed to self-assess?**

## Mental Models and Frameworks

### Quantum Superposition Model
Think of the city's reality-drift like quantum states: things exist in multiple states simultaneously until observed/interacted with. This mental model helps accept spatial impossibilities:

- Distances are probabilistic until measured by walking
- Interiors contain superpositions of space
- Observation collapses local reality temporarily

This is metaphor, not physics, but it's a useful cognitive framework.

### Ecosystem-as-Entity Model
Stop thinking "city with plants" and start thinking "plant entity that accommodates humans":

- You're not in a building; you're in an organism
- The organism has needs, cycles, moods
- Your relationship is symbiotic, not proprietary
- Communication is possible but non-linguistic

This mental shift prevents constant category errors.

### Deep Time Perspective
Everything here operates on multiple timescales:

- Your personal time (minutes, hours, days)
- Human community time (seasons, years, generations)
- Plant time (decades, centuries)
- Geological time (millions of years of the bedrock)

Learning to hold multiple timescales simultaneously helps with the patience required.

### Liminality Acceptance
The city exists in permanent threshold state:

- Between built and grown
- Between alive and mechanical
- Between real and impossible
- Between present and past

Accepting permanent liminality—not waiting for resolution—is key to psychological survival.

## Risk Assessment and Mitigation

### Physical Risks (Moderate)
- **Falls from elevation**: The root-bridges and platforms. Stay alert, use handrails where present.
- **Plant toxicity**: Not all growth is safe to touch. Learn local dangerous species.
- **Weather exposure**: Hypothermia is possible in cold fog. Lightning at elevation is serious.
- **Infection**: Constant moisture means wound care is critical. Any cut gets careful attention.

**Mitigation**: Attention, learning, caution, good wound care

### Psychological Risks (High)
- **Reality dissociation**: The drift can become permanent if you're not careful
- **Integration dissolution**: Losing your sense of separate self
- **Isolation stress**: Loneliness becoming pathological
- **Meaning erosion**: The strangeness can make baseline reality seem fake when you return

**Mitigation**: Journaling, grounding practices, maintained outside contact, clear personal boundaries

### Social Risks (Low to Moderate)
- **Ostracism**: Violating norms could result in social exclusion
- **Obligation entanglement**: Gift economies create complex webs
- **Consensus exclusion**: Not being able to participate in decision-making

**Mitigation**: Observation before action, asking questions, starting small with social participation

### Existential Risks (Unknown but Real)
- **Permanent contamination**: Becoming something that can't leave
- **Entity absorption**: The city taking you as structure
- **Reality anchor loss**: Being unable to function in normal space afterward

**Mitigation**: Following all weird-zone protocols, having exit plans, regular self-assessment, outside accountability

## Philosophical Preparation

This may be the most important section.

### Accepting Non-Human Intelligence
The city appears to be semi-aware. This is profoundly challenging to materialist worldviews:

- **You may need to revise your ontology**: What counts as conscious? As alive? As having agency?
- **Practice animistic thinking**: Not as superstition but as cognitive tool. What if plants think differently?
- **Study plant neurobiology**: Plants have signaling, memory, learning. Not consciousness as we know it, but not purely mechanical.

### Death as Transformation
The local burial practice is integration:

- **Contemplate your mortality differently**: What if death isn't ending but joining?
- **Consider material continuity**: Your atoms will be plant-matter. Does this change death's meaning?
- **Explore traditions that see death as transformation**: Buddhist, indigenous, mystic Christian. Not for belief but for conceptual frameworks.

### Technology Redefinition
The bio-machines challenge "technology" as category:

- **What is a tool if it's alive?**
- **What is engineering if it's growing?**
- **Can you have industry without metallurgy?**

Expanding your sense of what's possible technologically helps prevent constant cognitive dissonance.

### Time and Change
Living in space that operates on geological time while you operate on human time:

- **Practice long-term thinking**: Consider decisions at decade and century scales
- **Study slow change**: Watch time-lapse of plants growing, ice melting, erosion
- **Meditate on impermanence**: Buddhist anicca meditation specifically relevant

### The Sublime and the Uncanny
The city is both beautiful and wrong:

- **Study aesthetic philosophy**: Burke and Kant on the sublime. Freud on the uncanny.
- **Practice holding contradictory feelings**: Awe and fear, attraction and repulsion, simultaneously
- **Understand this won't resolve**: The city will remain beautiful-and-wrong. The tension is permanent.

## Red Flags: When to Leave

Know your exit criteria in advance:

### Physical Red Flags
- Persistent illness not responding to treatment
- Respiratory problems worsening despite adaptation time
- Chronic exhaustion not explained by altitude

### Psychological Red Flags
- Inability to maintain grounding practices
- Losing time regularly (hours not accounted for)
- Feeling more real in weird zones than normal areas
- Difficulty remembering why you came or who you were before
- Suicidal ideation
- Desire to "become structure"

### Social Red Flags
- Complete isolation despite efforts
- Community rejection or hostility
- Inability to navigate social protocols despite trying

### Reality Red Flags
- Seeing manifestations constantly
- Unable to distinguish present from past
- Experiencing impossible geometries in normal zones
- Other people expressing concern about your perception

**If you hit two or more red flags simultaneously: Leave immediately. Do not wait. Do not negotiate with yourself. The integration may already be too deep.**

## Final Preparation: The Metacognitive Layer

### Self-Awareness Check
Before going, honestly assess:

1. **Why do I want to go?**
   - Adventure? Escape? Curiosity? Running from something?
   - Motivations matter. Running-from is dangerous here.

2. **What am I hoping to find?**
   - Meaning? Belonging? Knowledge? Transformation?
   - Realistic expectations protect against dangerous risk-taking.

3. **What am I afraid of?**
   - Name the fears. Unnamed fears become panic.
   - Distinguish rational caution from limiting anxiety.

4. **What would success look like?**
   - How will you know if this was worthwhile?
   - Having criteria helps prevent indefinite stay when you should leave.

5. **What would failure look like?**
   - What outcomes would mean this was a mistake?
   - Permission to fail helps you recognize when to exit.

### Integration Intention-Setting
Decide in advance:

- **How much am I willing to change?**
- **What parts of my identity are core and must be preserved?**
- **What parts am I open to releasing or transforming?**
- **Where is the boundary between growth and dissolution?**

Write these down. Revisit them monthly. If your answers change dramatically, that's information.

### Support System
Establish before going:

- **One person who knows where you are and checks in regularly**
- **Emergency contact who can extract you if needed**
- **Someone who knows you well enough to recognize if you're not yourself in communications**
- **Clear signals that mean "I need help" vs "I'm fine just changed"**

### Documentation Protocol
Commit to:

- **Daily journal entries**, even brief ones
- **Weekly self-assessment** against your red flags
- **Monthly review** of your original intentions vs current state
- **Regular communication** with outside support person

The act of translating experience into language helps maintain cognitive boundaries when reality becomes fluid.

## The Essential Truth

Here is what all the preparation comes down to:

**You are about to enter a place where reality is negotiable and you might become something other than what you are.**

This is not metaphor. This is not exaggeration. The city integrates things. It may integrate you.

You can do this safely if you:
- Respect boundaries
- Maintain awareness
- Keep connections to baseline reality
- Know when to leave

But "safely" doesn't mean "unchanged." You will be different after. The question is whether you'll be different in ways you can accept, different in ways that enhance rather than diminish, different in ways that leave you still recognizably yourself.

The city is beautiful. The city is functional. The city is strange. The city is dangerous. All of these are simultaneously true.

Go prepared. Go carefully. Go with eyes open.

Go with the understanding that some who go don't come back—not because they die, but because they become something else, something that has no need of return.

If you can accept that possibility while still maintaining the intention to remain yourself, to learn and experience and then leave:

Then go.

The plateau waits, ancient and patient, breathing with its plant-lungs, thinking with its root-networks, dreaming in the spaces where reality thins.

It will teach you things you didn't know you could learn.

Whether you'll want to have learned them is a question only answerable afterward.

But you'll never be the same person wondering "what if."

And perhaps that, more than any other preparation, is what you need to know.
