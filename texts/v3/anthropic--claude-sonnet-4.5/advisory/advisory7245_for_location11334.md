# Preparation Guide for the Sunken Colonies

## Physical Conditioning (Begin 3-6 Months Before Departure)

### Cold Water Tolerance
You need to fundamentally recalibrate your relationship with being wet and cold simultaneously. This isn't about brief immersion—it's about *sustained* dampness at temperatures that make your body want to conserve heat.

Start taking cold showers, but don't stop there. Spend time in wet clothes in cool environments. Sit in a bathtub of cold water for increasing durations. Your body will try to tell you this is dangerous; you need to learn the difference between actual hypothermia risk and mere discomfort. The Colonies exist in that narrow band where it's cold enough to be miserable but not cold enough to kill you quickly—if you know how to manage it.

Pay attention to your extremities. Your hands and feet will be the first to suffer. Practice tasks requiring dexterity while your fingers are cold and slightly numb. Learn what movements are still possible, what becomes difficult, what's actually dangerous to attempt.

### Balance and Proprioception
You'll be walking on wet wood, crossing narrow bridges, stepping from unstable surfaces to more unstable surfaces, all while your depth perception is compromised by mist. Take up slacklining. Practice walking on wet logs. Spend time moving across uneven, slippery terrain with your eyes partially closed.

Learn to fall safely—you will fall, probably often at first. Practice landing on soft, yielding surfaces from various heights and angles. The moss will cushion impacts, but you still need to know how to distribute force, protect your head, and recover quickly in tight spaces.

### Respiratory Adaptation
The air in the Colonies is heavy with moisture. It requires slightly more effort to breathe, and the cold can make your lungs ache. If you have any respiratory issues—asthma, allergies, even mild seasonal problems—address them aggressively before going. Spend time in steam rooms, but cold ones if you can find them. Practice breathing slowly and deeply in humid conditions.

### Cardiovascular Endurance
Not for running—for existing. Your body will burn more calories staying warm in constant dampness. You need reserves. Build endurance through long, slow, cold activities. Winter hiking in damp conditions. Swimming in cold water. Activities that teach your body to maintain core temperature under sustained challenge.

## Psychological Preparation (Ongoing)

### Spatial Density Tolerance
The Colonies are *dense* in a way modern humans rarely experience. Start reducing your personal space bubble now. Spend time in crowded places, not just passing through but actually existing there. Practice being comfortable when you can see/hear/smell other humans constantly.

Visit places where privacy is conceptual rather than physical—certain types of communal housing, crowded boats, busy markets. Learn to create mental privacy when physical privacy doesn't exist. The ability to "be alone" while surrounded by others is essential.

### Accepting Loss of Control
You cannot control when you're wet or dry in the Colonies. You cannot control the light levels. You cannot control the mist. You cannot control the water's movement. Practice surrender in small ways: spend a day where you don't control the temperature of your environment, don't choose when you eat, don't decide your route through a city.

Meditate on acceptance. Not passive resignation, but active acknowledgment that some conditions simply *are*, and your resistance makes them harder, not easier.

### Pattern Recognition Development
The Colonies operate on complex, unwritten rules developed over generations. Start training yourself to see patterns in seemingly chaotic systems. Study complex natural systems—how water flows through landscapes, how plants colonize spaces, how informal settlements organize themselves. Practice navigating without maps or signs.

Visit places where the logic is implicit rather than explicit. Learn to read a space's function from its form, from the wear patterns, from what's present and absent.

### Temporal Adjustment
Time feels different in the mist, in the cold, in the eternal dampness. Practice being without clocks. Spend days where you don't know the time. Learn to judge duration by internal sense rather than external reference. Get comfortable with activities that have no clear beginning or end.

## Skills Acquisition (Priority Order)

### 1. Watercraft Operation (ESSENTIAL)
Take every boat class you can find. Canoe, kayak, rowboat, punt. Focus on small craft in confined spaces. Learn to read water—how depth changes color and texture, how current moves, where obstacles lurk. Practice in cold water, in mist if possible, in complex environments with obstacles.

Most importantly, learn to pole a boat—standing, using a long pole to push off the bottom. It's not common in modern practice but it's the primary method in the Colonies. Practice in shallow, winding waterways. Get comfortable shifting your weight, making micro-adjustments, moving through tight spaces.

### 2. Fiber Craft and Weaving
Learn to work with plant fibers. Basket weaving, basic thatching, rope making. Not because you'll necessarily practice these trades, but because understanding the materials helps you understand the built environment. You need to see how a wattle-and-daub wall is constructed, how thatch is layered, how rope is twisted.

Work with willow specifically if possible—it's flexible, strong, and ubiquitous in the Colonies. Learn what it can do, how it breaks, how to join it.

### 3. Moisture Management
This is less a skill than a discipline. Learn to keep things dry in wet environments. Study sailor's techniques, mountaineer's approaches, traditional methods from wet climates. Practice wrapping, storing, protecting. Understand that "waterproof" is temporary—learn to dry things partially, rotationally, strategically.

Learn to assess and prevent rot, mold, mildew. Understand what materials resist moisture, which succumb, which need what kind of maintenance. This knowledge will inform everything you touch.

### 4. Navigation Without Clear Sightlines
Practice wayfinding in fog, in forests, in complex urban environments. Learn to build mental maps from fragments. Practice describing routes verbally—in the Colonies, you'll often receive directions as narratives, not maps.

Develop your sense of direction independent of sun position. Learn to use sound, smell, subtle environmental cues. Practice counting steps, estimating distances through mist, recognizing places from different angles.

### 5. Fire in Wet Conditions
Learn to start and maintain fires when everything is damp. Study traditional methods from wet climates. Practice with wet wood—it can be done, but it requires technique, patience, and understanding. This skill might not be daily-useful, but it's potentially life-saving.

More importantly, develop a deep respect for fire in the Colonies. It's precious, difficult, dangerous in a built environment of organic materials. Learn to minimize your need for it.

## Material Preparation

### Clothing Strategy
Forget waterproof. Think water-*managing*. You need:

**Base layers** of wool—merino if you can afford it, regular wool if not. Multiple sets. Wool insulates when wet, doesn't hold smell, and dries relatively fast. Avoid cotton absolutely. Consider silk for sensitive areas.

**Mid layers** that provide insulation without bulk. Thin wool sweaters, multiple of them. The strategy is layers you can add and remove, not single thick garments.

**Outer layers** of tightly-woven wool or oiled fabric. Not to keep you dry—that's impossible—but to break wind and slow water penetration. Something that sheds the worst of it while letting moisture escape.

**Footwear** is critical and difficult. You need something that works wet, drains easily, dries relatively fast, provides traction on wet wood, and doesn't rot. Traditional moccasins might work better than modern boots. Bring multiple pairs of wool socks—many multiple pairs. Your feet will be your weakest point.

Consider bringing materials to have local footwear made once you understand the conditions better.

**Head covering** that keeps some rain off but doesn't trap moisture. A wide-brimmed wool hat, perhaps, or oiled fabric. Avoid hoods that prevent peripheral vision—you need to see where you're stepping.

### Personal Gear (Minimal, Essential)

**Sleeping system**: Wool blankets, not a sleeping bag. Something you can dry in sections, adjust in layers. A hammock might serve better than ground-sleeping—the ground never dries.

**Bag/pack**: Waxed canvas or leather. Something that ages gracefully with water exposure. Include waxed fabric or oilcloth for wrapping items inside.

**Writing materials**: Pencils (multiple) rather than pens. Paper in a waterproof case, but accept that keeping anything dry long-term is difficult. Consider wax tablets for temporary notes.

**Knife**: Good steel, keep it oiled. A simple, robust design. Bring sharpening stones.

**Cordage**: Bring some modern rope or strong cord. Local materials are available but having some waterproof cordage is useful.

**Light source**: This is difficult. Candles are precious in the Colonies. A small LED headlamp with multiple sets of batteries, used sparingly, might be your luxury item. Ration its use carefully.

**Medical kit**: Focused on moisture-related problems. Antifungal treatments for skin. Blister management supplies. Antibiotics if you can get them (water-borne infections are a risk). Basic wound care. Something for hypothermia—emergency blanket, chemical heat packs.

**Water purification**: Tablets or a filter. The water looks clean but it's been flowing through a settlement's daily life. Don't risk it.

### What NOT to Bring

Electronics beyond a single light source—they'll die from moisture. Books—they'll rot (bring one precious one if you must, accept you'll lose it eventually). Valuables you can't stand to lose to water damage. Complicated gear with parts that might rust, seize, or corrode. Anything you can't repair with simple tools.

## Knowledge to Acquire

### Botanical Basics
Learn to identify willows, sedges, rushes, common wetland plants. Not detailed taxonomy—practical recognition. What's edible, what's useful for fiber, what indicates water depth or quality. Study from guides focused on cold-climate wetlands.

### Basic Ecology
Understand how wetland systems work. Food chains, nutrient cycling, seasonal patterns. Learn to read an ecosystem's health. This helps you understand the Colonies' relationship with their environment.

### Traditional Building Methods
Study vernacular architecture from wet climates. Pile dwellings, stilt houses, floating structures. Look at historical examples: Venice, the Marsh Arabs, Pacific Northwest pit houses, Chinese water towns. Not to copy them—the Colonies have their own solutions—but to understand principles.

### Social Anthropology
Read about high-density traditional communities, cooperative resource management, commons governance. The Colonies will have complex unwritten social structures. Understanding general principles helps you avoid serious mistakes.

### Hypothermia Recognition and Treatment
Study it thoroughly. Know the stages, the symptoms, the treatments. Know what to do for yourself and others. This is your primary physical danger.

### Language Preparation
We don't know what language(s) the Colonies speak. Prepare by:
- Learning basic phonetic transcription
- Practicing active listening and mimicry
- Studying one or two languages with complex case systems or verb aspects (this trains mental flexibility)
- Learning non-verbal communication strategies
- Accepting that you'll be functionally illiterate and probably incomprehensible for weeks or months

## Medical Preparation

### Vaccinations and Preventive Care
Consult a travel medicine specialist, but emphasize cold-climate water exposure. Consider:
- Hepatitis A and B (water/food-borne)
- Typhoid
- Tetanus update
- Consider rabies if mammals are common
- Full STI screening (close quarters make transmission easier)

### Dental Work
Get everything fixed beforehand. Dental problems in the Colonies will be... problematic. Consider having wisdom teeth removed if they might cause issues.

### Vision
If you need glasses, bring multiple pairs. If you can wear contacts, don't—too hard to keep clean. If you need vision correction, glasses are essential. Consider having prescription sunglasses for the rare bright days.

### Skin Conditioning
Start moisturizing religiously now. Your skin will be under constant assault from water. Build up its resilience. Pay special attention to hands and feet. Consider using barrier creams.

Deal with any existing fungal infections completely. They'll flourish in the Colonies if you bring them.

### Physical Issues to Resolve
- Any joint problems that might worsen with cold and damp
- Circulation issues in extremities
- Chronic pain that cold exacerbates
- Anything requiring regular medication (consider how you'll keep it dry and supplied)

## Cultural Preparation

### Gift Strategy
Research appropriate gifts for communities with subsistence economies. Avoid anything that seems condescending. Consider:
- Metal tools (if metal is scarce)
- Seeds from your area (new genetic diversity)
- Skills you can teach
- Stories, songs, knowledge you can share

Do not bring:
- Alcohol (you don't know their relationship with it)
- Weapons
- Religious items
- Anything that suggests superiority

### Learning Posture
Adopt the mindset of a student, not a visitor. You're not there to observe quaintly—you're there to learn how to survive in conditions you don't understand. Practice humility now. Practice asking for help. Practice accepting correction.

Spend time in settings where you're the ignorant one, where others know better than you, where you must defer to local knowledge.

### Patience Development
Nothing happens quickly in the Colonies. The cold slows metabolism, activity, decision-making. The water imposes waiting—for boats, for channels to be passable, for conditions to change. Practice waiting without anxiety. Practice boredom without distraction.

### Community Versus Individual
Modern life is individualistic; the Colonies certainly aren't. Practice thinking in terms of group needs. Volunteer for community projects. Practice subordinating individual preference to collective function. Learn to notice what needs doing without being asked.

## Timing Considerations

### Season Strategy
If possible, arrive at the beginning of whatever the "easier" season is. Probably not spring (flooding) or deep winter (if it freezes). Perhaps late summer or early fall, when water is lower, temperatures are relatively mild, but the season gives you time to prepare for harder conditions.

### Initial Duration
Plan to stay at least six months, ideally a full year. Less than that and you'll barely understand the patterns. You need to experience seasonal change to comprehend the system.

### Exit Strategy
Have a way out, but don't let it be easy. You need to commit to being there, not constantly thinking about leaving. Perhaps arrange a check-in point at a specific time—someone will come to the basin edge at an agreed-upon date. If you're not there, they assume you're staying longer.

## Final Preparations (Last Month)

### Physical
- Stop eating processed foods; transition to simpler diet
- Reduce caloric intake slightly, then increase it—your body needs to be efficient but also have reserves
- Spend as much time as possible in cold, damp conditions
- Reduce hot showers to almost nothing
- Practice fasting—you may not eat regularly at first

### Mental
- Reduce digital stimulation drastically
- Practice extended periods without entertainment
- Meditate daily on acceptance and adaptation
- Visualize yourself comfortable in cold, wet, crowded conditions
- Write letters to yourself about why you're going—you'll need reminders when it's hard

### Social
- Explain to loved ones that contact will be minimal or impossible
- Settle affairs that need settling
- Say proper goodbyes
- But don't make it dramatic—you're planning to return

## The Journey to the Basin Edge

Research how to reach the surrounding plain. The Colonies themselves are isolated by choice and geography. The last part of the journey will likely be on foot, possibly difficult, possibly intentionally obscure.

Bring more supplies than you think you'll need for this journey—weather on the plain might be harsh, and you don't know exactly where the basin is until you find it.

As you approach, you'll probably see the basin before you're seen. Use this time to observe: watch traffic patterns, note where people move, try to understand the system before entering it. Don't sneak—be visible, move slowly, appear non-threatening.

## Initial Contact Protocol

When you first encounter inhabitants:
- Make yourself visible from a distance
- Keep hands visible
- Move slowly
- Wait to be approached rather than approaching
- Show respect through posture—slight bow, calm demeanor
- Don't touch anyone or anything without permission
- Try to communicate that you want to learn, to stay, to help
- Accept that you might be turned away
- Accept that you might be treated as a child (functionally, you are one)

Bring one gift, something simple but valuable. Offer it without expectation.

## First Days Survival

You will be cold, wet, disoriented, and probably miserable. This is normal. Your preparation helps, but nothing fully prepares you.

### Priority Actions:
1. Find shelter (probably offered, possibly earned)
2. Learn bathroom protocols immediately (this is crucial in a water-based community)
3. Observe meal times and food customs
4. Learn basic yes/no/thank you in the local language
5. Watch children—they show what's safe, what's normal
6. Make yourself useful in simple ways—carrying things, holding things, basic labor
7. Stay out of boats until you're invited or taught

### What to Expect:
- Constant discomfort from cold and wet
- Difficulty sleeping initially
- Mild hypothermia symptoms (shivering, confusion) until you adapt
- Gastrointestinal issues from new food and water
- Profound disorientation from the mist and repetitive landscape
- Social isolation from language barrier
- Moments of serious doubt about your decision

### What Helps:
- Focus on immediate tasks, not long-term adjustment
- Move regularly to stay warm
- Eat whenever food is offered
- Sleep whenever possible
- Don't judge your experience—just exist in it
- Notice small moments of beauty or connection
- Remember that adaptation is gradual

## Long-term Sustainability

After initial adjustment:

### Find Your Function
The Colonies don't support passengers. Identify what you can contribute:
- Labor for tasks that need doing
- Skills from your world that translate
- Learning to do something they need done
- Teaching if they're interested in your knowledge

### Deep Learning
- Master the local language completely
- Learn the water system's logic
- Understand the social structures
- Memorize routes through the settlement
- Learn the annual cycle
- Study the craft and construction methods
- Understand the ecology thoroughly

### Physical Adaptation
- Your body will adjust—less shivering, better circulation, higher cold tolerance
- Your skin will toughen
- Your balance will improve dramatically
- You'll learn to sleep in dampness
- You'll stop noticing the mist

### Cultural Integration
- Learn the unwritten rules through observation and gentle testing
- Build relationships slowly
- Accept your outsider status while working to reduce it
- Participate in communal activities
- Learn the stories, songs, humor
- Understand the values and priorities

### Maintaining Perspective
- Keep notes on what you learn
- Notice changes in yourself
- Remember your purpose for being there
- Stay curious rather than judgmental
- Accept that you're changed by the experience

## Special Challenges

### Disease
Water-borne illness is a constant risk. Be scrupulous about water treatment. Watch for symptoms: diarrhea, fever, unusual fatigue. Know that medical care will be traditional, plant-based, limited. Your modern medical kit is precious.

### Injury
You will be injured—cuts, bruises, sprains. Keep wounds clean obsessively. The wet environment makes infection likely. Change dressings frequently. Watch for red lines indicating systemic infection.

### Mental Health
Isolation, cold, dampness, and darkness can affect mood profoundly. Stay active. Maintain social connection even when it's hard. Notice if you're withdrawing or becoming depressed. Have strategies: movement, breathing exercises, purposeful activity.

### Conflict
You will violate norms accidentally. Apologize clearly. Learn from mistakes. Don't argue about why you did something—accept that you were wrong. Building trust after social errors takes time.

## What You'll Learn

If you prepare well and adapt successfully, the Colonies will teach you:

- How to be comfortable in conditions you thought were intolerable
- How to navigate complexity through pattern recognition rather than rules
- How to live at high density without losing yourself
- How to work with water rather than against it
- How to find privacy in proximity
- How to cooperate at levels modern societies rarely achieve
- How to live with less certainty, less control, less clarity
- How to exist in liminal space—between wet and dry, between wild and built, between individual and community
- How to find beauty in muted colors, soft sounds, gentle movements
- How to judge wealth by resilience rather than accumulation

## The Return

When you eventually leave:

- The brightness of the dry world will be overwhelming
- You'll miss the mist's softness
- Privacy will feel isolating
- You'll notice how much space modern life wastes
- You'll be more comfortable in cold and wet than seems normal
- You'll see water differently—not as obstacle or resource, but as presence
- You'll understand cooperation at a level that's hard to explain
- You'll carry the Colonies' rhythm—slow, patient, persistent

## A Final Note

The Sunken Colonies are not a tourist destination. They're not a retreat or an adventure. They're a functioning community that has solved problems you've probably never had to consider, using knowledge developed through necessity over what might be only a few generations.

You're not going to discover something. You're going to be taught, if they choose to teach you. You're not going to bring them anything they desperately need. You might learn things you desperately need.

Prepare your body, your mind, your skills, and your attitude. Then go with humility, openness, and realistic expectations of difficulty.

The cold will reach your bones. The wet will become your baseline. The mist will obscure and reveal. The density will teach you about presence. The quietness will teach you about attention.

And slowly, if you're patient and respectful and genuinely interested in learning, the Colonies will begin to make sense. The chaos will reveal its order. The cold will feel less hostile. The wet will feel normal. The bridges and boats and hammocks and willows will become familiar landmarks in a mental map you carry with precision.

You'll learn to read the light through mist. To know water depth by color. To cross wet wood without thinking. To find your way home through channels that all look the same.

And you'll understand that you're experiencing something rare: a completely adapted human community, young enough to still be forming, old enough to have found solutions, living in balance with conditions that seem impossible until you learn to see them as simply what is.

Go prepared. Go humble. Go ready to be cold, wet, confused, and occasionally miserable.

Go ready to learn.
