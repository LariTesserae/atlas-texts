# Preparation Guide for the Constructed Canopy

## Physical Conditioning (Begin 8-12 Weeks Before Departure)

**Altitude Acclimatization is Non-Negotiable**

You cannot cheat altitude. Your body needs time to produce more red blood cells, adjust its breathing patterns, and recalibrate dozens of systems you've never consciously thought about. If possible, spend at least a week at intermediate elevations (2,000-2,500 meters) before attempting the Canopy.

Start a cardiovascular training program immediately. Focus on:
- Sustained aerobic exercise at conversation pace (you'll need efficient oxygen use, not sprint capacity)
- Stair climbing with progressive weight in a backpack
- Breathing exercises that emphasize deep, complete exhalations (at altitude, the problem is often expelling CO2, not getting oxygen in)

Practice moving slowly and deliberately. This sounds trivial but becomes critical. At the Canopy, rushing means gasping, gasping means sitting down for ten minutes, sitting down means you've lost your morning. Learn the "altitude rest step" - pause with your weight fully on your downhill leg before taking the next step upward.

**Expect the First Three Days to Be Difficult**

Even with preparation, you will likely experience:
- Headaches (usually behind the eyes, often worse when lying flat)
- Disrupted sleep (your breathing pattern changes at night, sometimes waking you)
- Mild nausea, especially in mornings
- Exhaustion from minimal exertion
- Strange dreams (hypoxia affects your brain in odd ways)

This is normal. It will pass. What's not normal and requires immediate descent: confusion, loss of coordination, severe headache unresponsive to medication, crackling sounds when breathing.

## Medical Preparations

**Consult a Doctor Familiar with Altitude Medicine**

Be honest about your health history. Certain conditions make high altitude genuinely dangerous:
- Sickle cell trait or disease
- Severe hypertension
- Certain heart conditions
- Pulmonary hypertension
- Sleep apnea (which becomes significantly worse at altitude)

Get prescriptions for:
- **Acetazolamide (Diamox)**: Helps acclimatization by acidifying your blood slightly, which stimulates breathing. Start taking it 24 hours before ascent. Side effects include tingling fingers and toes, altered taste (carbonated drinks taste flat), and increased urination.
- **Ibuprofen or aspirin**: For altitude headaches
- **Ondansetron**: For nausea if needed
- **Dexamethasone**: Emergency medication for severe altitude sickness (learn how and when to use it)
- **Broad-spectrum antibiotic**: For respiratory infections, which develop faster and more seriously at altitude

Bring:
- Pulse oximeter: Track your blood oxygen saturation. Below 85% for extended periods is concerning. Below 75% requires immediate action.
- Extra eyeglasses if you wear them (the UV exposure can damage contact lenses, and your eyes may be drier)
- Comprehensive first aid kit, weighted toward respiratory and altitude issues
- Any regular medications in triple quantity (resupply may be unreliable)

**Dental Work**

Get a thorough dental exam. Cavities or infections that are asymptomatic at sea level can become agonizing at altitude due to pressure changes in air pockets. Get everything addressed before you go.

## Gear and Clothing

**The Layering System You'll Actually Use**

Forget fashion. Forget single-use garments. At the Canopy, you'll be putting on and taking off layers constantly as you move between sun and shade, exertion and rest, clear air and cloud.

**Base layer**: Merino wool or high-quality synthetic. Bring 3-4 shirts, 3-4 underwear, 2-3 long underwear bottoms. Merino resists odor (you'll appreciate this in communal living), wicks moisture, and regulates temperature. It's expensive but worth every penny here.

**Mid-layer**: Fleece or lightweight down. Two options - one lighter, one heavier. Must be easy to stuff into a bag without damage.

**Outer layer**: 
- Rain shell that's actually waterproof (not water-resistant). The clouds here deposit serious moisture. Get pit zips for ventilation.
- Wind shell as a separate layer (sometimes you need wind protection without rain protection)

**Sun protection**:
- Wide-brimmed hat that won't blow off (chin strap essential)
- Neck gaiter or bandana
- Long sleeves even in warm weather
- UV-protective lip balm (bring multiple - you'll lose them)

**Footwear** - This Deserves Its Own Section:

You'll be walking on metal grating, wet concrete, uneven wooden boards, and slick stone, often while carrying things, often in dampness, sometimes with steep drops nearby.

Primary boots:
- Ankle support (the uneven surfaces and metal grating will turn your ankle eventually)
- Aggressive tread that drains water (wet metal is ice-like)
- Not waterproof (controversial, but hear me out: waterproof boots fill with condensation in the humidity and then you're walking in wet boots anyway; water-resistant boots that drain and dry work better here)
- Already broken in (break them in at home, walking on stairs and uneven ground)

Secondary footwear:
- Sandals or slip-ons for inside and around your dwelling
- Bring multiple insoles (they compress and wear faster at altitude for reasons I don't understand but have definitely experienced)

Socks: Merino wool, various weights. Bring more than you think you need. Wet socks are miserable, and things dry slowly in the humidity.

**Other Essential Gear**:

- **Headlamp** with red light option and backup batteries (you'll need hands free on those walkways at night)
- **Sunglasses** with UV 400 protection minimum, plus a backup pair (the UV at this altitude will damage your eyes faster than you realize)
- **Backpack** for daily carry (20-30L) with rain cover, for moving supplies between levels
- **Water bottles** (2L minimum daily capacity) - hydration is critical and you'll be drinking constantly
- **Notebook and pencil** (not pen - pens behave oddly at altitude) for notes, sketching maps, recording observations
- **Watch** - track time for acclimatization rest periods, medication schedules
- **Earplugs** - the place is loud and you'll need sleep
- **Sleeping bag** rated 10°F/-12°C colder than you expect to need (you sleep colder at altitude)
- **Sleeping pad** with high R-value if you're bringing your own bedding

## What NOT to Bring

- Heavy books (bring a e-reader if you must have reading material; every gram counts when you're hauling things up stairs at altitude)
- Cotton anything (it absorbs water, dries slowly, and will make you cold and miserable)
- Expensive jewelry or items you'd be devastated to lose
- More than minimal toiletries (you can acquire basics there; save the weight)
- Rigid expectations about privacy, quiet, or personal space

## Food and Nutrition Preparation

**Start Adjusting Your Diet Now**

At altitude, your digestive system becomes sluggish. You'll crave simple carbohydrates because your body wants easy energy. But you'll also need protein and fat.

Practice eating:
- Frequent small meals rather than large ones
- High-carb foods that you'll want at altitude: oatmeal, rice, pasta, bread, potatoes
- More than you think (you'll burn 200-300 extra calories per day just existing at altitude)

**Bring Supplements**:
- Iron (altitude can cause anemia)
- Multivitamin with extra B vitamins
- Electrolyte powder (you'll be urinating frequently, especially if taking Diamox)
- Ginger tablets for nausea
- Your comfort foods in small quantities (a taste of home matters when adaptation is hard)

**Expect Food to Taste Different**

Altitude suppresses your sense of taste and smell. Things need more seasoning. Bring:
- Hot sauce or preferred condiments in small containers
- Salt tablets
- Tea or coffee if you're particular (caffeine helps some people with altitude headaches but worsens them for others - know which you are)

## Mental and Psychological Preparation

**This Will Test You**

The combination of altitude, density, loudness, and constant dampness creates a specific type of stress. Prepare for:

**Sensory Overwhelm**: The place is constantly stimulating. The wind never stops. The light is intense. The sounds layer endlessly. You can't escape to silence or stillness easily. Practice meditation or mindfulness techniques now. Learn to find calm within stimulus rather than requiring external quiet.

**Social Intensity**: You'll be living in close quarters with a community that's been here far longer than you. They have established rhythms, relationships, and unspoken rules. Be prepared to be:
- An outsider initially (friendly but not presumptuous)
- Wrong about things you thought you understood
- Helped, possibly more than your pride wants
- Observed (you're the novelty; accept this with grace)

**Slower Cognition**: Altitude makes your brain work less efficiently. You'll:
- Forget words mid-sentence
- Make simple arithmetic errors
- Take longer to process information
- Need to write things down more
- Misjudge distances and time

Accept this as temporary and normal. Don't make important decisions in your first week.

**Mood Shifts**: Altitude can affect emotional regulation. You might:
- Cry more easily
- Feel irritable without clear cause
- Experience anxiety spikes
- Have periods of euphoria (also altitude-related, not spiritual awakening)

**Useful Mental Frameworks**:

Think of yourself as an apprentice, regardless of your expertise elsewhere. You're learning a specific place's specific requirements.

Practice gratitude exercises now. At altitude, when you're tired and hypoxic and frustrated, being able to genuinely appreciate small positives (hot tea, a moment of sun, a cat appearing) makes the difference between misery and contentment.

Develop patience with your body's limitations. "I can't" means "not right now" not "I've failed." Rest, hydrate, try again in an hour.

## Skills to Develop Before Arrival

**Practical Skills That Will Serve You**:

- Basic gardening (you may be expected to contribute; know the difference between a weed and a seedling)
- Knot tying (things blow away; you'll need to secure them)
- Basic tool use and repair (self-sufficiency is valued)
- Water system understanding (how gravity feed works, basic plumbing concepts)
- Food preservation (if arriving during harvest season)
- Basic first aid beyond the kit

**Navigation Skills**:

Study the concept of three-dimensional navigation. The Canopy requires tracking not just north/south/east/west but up/down through levels. Practice:
- Mentally mapping multi-story buildings
- Following complex directions with elevation changes
- Marking routes so you can retrace them
- Asking for help when lost (pride kills in confusing terrain)

## Language and Communication

Learn key practical phrases if there's a dominant language you don't speak:
- "Where is [water/toilet/medical help]?"
- "I don't understand"
- "Please speak slowly"
- "Thank you"
- "I need help"
- "Which level is this?"

Also learn to communicate clearly when you're hypoxic and your eloquence has fled. Practice saying essential things in simple, direct sentences.

## Cultural Preparation

**Research What You Can, But Accept What You Can't**

The Canopy has presumably developed unique cultural elements. You won't learn them all beforehand. But general principles:

**Respect established systems**: If water is collected certain ways, gardens tended on schedules, pathways used in patterns - there are reasons, often learned through experience. Observe before innovating.

**Understand communal vs. private space**: In density, these boundaries matter intensely and may be marked in non-obvious ways. Watch where people do and don't go.

**Contribution expectation**: You'll likely be expected to contribute to maintenance, whether through work, skill-sharing, or resources. Come prepared to participate, not just observe.

**Resource consciousness**: Water, energy, growing space - all managed carefully. Waste is probably noticed and remembered.

## Arrival Strategy

**Plan Your Ascent Carefully**

If there's a choice of routes/times:
- Ascend in early morning (better weather, cooler temperatures)
- Carry minimal load initially (you can bring more later or have it sent up)
- Plan to do nothing the first day except rest, hydrate, and orient
- Have more money/resources than you think you need (emergencies, unexpected needs)

**First 48 Hours**:

Hour 1-8: Find your dwelling. Unpack minimals. Drink water. Rest.

Hour 8-16: Short walk to orient. Locate: water sources, toilets, medical help, food, key exits. Return to rest.

Hour 16-24: More water. Light food. More rest. Accept the headache.

Day 2: Slowly expand your range. Meet immediate neighbors. Learn one new section. Drink. Rest. Repeat.

## Ongoing Maintenance (Daily Practices for Thriving)

**Hydration Protocol**:
- Drink before you're thirsty
- Monitor urine color (should be light yellow)
- If taking Diamox, increase intake further
- Keep water by your bed (nighttime dehydration is common)

**Sun Protection**:
- Reapply sunscreen every 2 hours in direct sun
- UV exposure is cumulative and severe
- Your scalp can burn through thin hair

**Foot Care**:
- Check feet daily for hot spots or blisters
- Address problems immediately with moleskin/bandages
- Keep toenails trimmed short
- Change socks if they get wet

**Energy Management**:
- Plan one major activity per day maximum initially
- Rest between exertions
- Horizontal rest is better than sitting
- Naps are strategic, not lazy

**Monitor Yourself**:
- Daily pulse oximeter check
- Track sleep quality
- Note mood and cognition
- Be honest about symptoms

## Emergency Preparation

**Know Your Exit Strategy**

Before you need it, understand:
- How to rapidly descend if altitude sickness becomes severe
- Who to contact for emergency help
- What communications systems exist
- Where the nearest lower-altitude medical facility is

**Descent Indicators** (any of these means go down NOW):
- Confusion or altered consciousness
- Loss of coordination (can't walk a straight line)
- Severe breathlessness at rest
- Crackling/gurgling in lungs
- Severe, unrelenting headache

Don't wait. Don't negotiate. Don't pack carefully. DESCEND.

**Emergency Cash**:
Bring enough money to bribe, hire, or buy your way out of a bad situation. Keep it separate from daily funds, in multiple forms (local currency, universally accepted currency, maybe precious metal if appropriate to setting).

## What No Guide Can Fully Prepare You For

**The Strangeness of Living Vertically**

Your spatial sense is built for horizontal living. It will be disorienting to have neighbors above, below, and beside you, to have gardens overhead and dripping water as background reality, to track location by level as much as by direction.

**The Intimacy of Density**

You'll hear your neighbors' conversations, smell their cooking, see their laundry. They'll hear, smell, and see yours. The boundaries exist but are permeable. This is neither good nor bad but profoundly different from privacy-oriented living.

**The Constant Low-Level Challenge**

Every day, your body works harder just to maintain normal function. This accumulates. You'll be more tired than expected. Accept this. Rest more. Expect less of yourself initially.

**The Beauty That Comes Later**

Probably you'll arrive and think "this is difficult, strange, loud, overwhelming." That's accurate. But give it time. The place reveals itself slowly. The morning light on wet metal. The orchestration of the dripping water. The improbable abundance of the gardens. The cats appearing and vanishing. The community's quiet competence. These rewards come to those who stay long enough to see past the challenge.

## Final Preparations

**One Week Before Departure**:
- Recheck all medications and supplies
- Break in any new gear items
- Get excellent sleep
- Hydrate heavily
- Eat nutritiously
- Avoid alcohol
- Avoid illness (be cautious about exposure)

**Day Before Departure**:
- Pack and repack (leave what you touched only once)
- Confirm plans and contacts
- Tell someone trustworthy your itinerary
- Copy important documents
- Start Diamox if prescribed

**Day of Travel**:
- Hydrate continuously
- Avoid alcohol and sedatives (they suppress breathing)
- Sleep if possible but don't force it
- Eat lightly
- Keep emergency contacts and medical info on your person

## A Final Word

The Constructed Canopy is not hostile, but it is demanding. It requires physical adaptation, mental flexibility, cultural humility, and practical competence. Many of these you can prepare. Some you can only develop by being there.

You will probably struggle initially. You might question your decision. You might feel overwhelmed, exhausted, and inadequate. This is normal. This passes.

The place has sustained a community because, for all its challenges, it offers something worth having: a functioning example of humans creating viable life in an unlikely place through knowledge, cooperation, and maintained balance. You're visiting a working answer to questions about density, ecology, altitude, and community.

Prepare your body, pack your gear, humble your expectations, and go with curiosity rather than assumptions. The Canopy will teach you what you need to know, but only if you arrive ready to learn.

And bring extra socks. Seriously. You'll thank me.
