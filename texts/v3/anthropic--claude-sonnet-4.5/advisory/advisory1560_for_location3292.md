# Preparation Guide for the Breathing Plains of Umbrathene

## I. Fundamental Understanding: What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, accept this: you are about to become functionally blind for an extended period. Not temporarily sightless in a dark room where you might wait for your eyes to adjust—but in a place where light does not exist. No stars, no moon, no bioluminescence visible to unprepared human eyes, no dawn coming to save you. Your visual cortex will protest, creating phantom images, hallucinations of light. This is normal. It will pass, but it takes weeks, and those weeks will be difficult.

You are going to a continent-sized darkness where you must learn to exist as a different kind of creature than you have been.

## II. Physical Preparation (6-12 Months Minimum)

### Sensory Training

**Auditory Development:**
- Begin working with an audiologist and acoustic specialist immediately
- Practice echolocation daily: click with your tongue, snap your fingers, learn to interpret the returning sounds in various environments
- Spend hours blindfolded in increasingly complex spaces, building mental maps purely from sound
- Learn to distinguish between dozens of water sounds: dripping vs. flowing vs. standing vs. falling
- Train yourself to detect direction and distance of sounds with precision
- Study acoustic behavior: how sound reflects off different materials, how weather affects propagation, how to read acoustic shadows
- Practice with subsonic and ultrasonic frequencies using specialized equipment—you won't hear them naturally, but understanding them intellectually helps interpret what you feel

**Tactile Sensitivity:**
- Walk barefoot extensively to toughen feet while maintaining sensitivity
- Practice texture discrimination with your hands, feet, and skin
- Learn to read ground conditions through your feet: wet vs. dry, stable vs. undermined, stone vs. soil vs. moss
- Develop awareness of air movement on your skin—wind direction and intensity become navigation tools
- Train to detect temperature gradients: warm zones vs. cool, the breath of underground chambers, the approach of weather

**Olfactory Training:**
- Work with a sommelier or perfumer to develop scent vocabulary and memory
- Practice identifying and tracking scents in outdoor environments
- Learn to distinguish: plant decay vs. animal presence vs. mineral signatures vs. water types
- Understand how scent moves with air currents and temperature layers
- Build a library of scent memories—you'll need to recognize markers you've never encountered before based on similar categories

**Proprioception and Balance:**
- Practice extensive movement in complete darkness
- Develop absolute confidence in your body's position in space without visual reference
- Train on uneven terrain while blindfolded
- Build stamina for long-distance walking with heavy packs in challenging conditions
- Learn to move quietly—sound discipline becomes critical

### Physical Conditioning

You need to be capable of:
- Walking 15-20 miles daily in wet, uneven terrain while carrying 40-60 pounds
- Maintaining core temperature in constant moisture
- Functioning in high humidity that makes breathing feel like drinking
- Recovering from stumbles and falls without visual warning
- Operating in temperature ranges from warm (think subtropical) to cool (temperate), with high variability

Conditioning should emphasize:
- Cardiovascular endurance over raw strength
- Joint stability and flexibility
- Core strength for balance on uncertain ground
- Foot and ankle strength to prevent injury on hidden obstacles
- Respiratory capacity for humid air

### Medical Preparation

**Required vaccinations and prophylactics:**
- Consult with a tropical medicine specialist about the ecosystem
- Assume exposure to waterborne parasites, fungal infections, unusual bacteria
- Bring extended courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics (if you can access them)
- Antifungal medications, both topical and systemic
- Antimalarial prophylaxis if analogous organisms exist there

**Health considerations:**
- Dental work completed—no tooth problems that could develop into emergencies
- Any necessary surgeries done well in advance
- Chronic conditions must be stable and manageable without frequent medical intervention
- Mental health assessment: can you handle sensory deprivation and disorientation?
- Consider bringing a medical person if traveling in a group

**Skin preparation:**
- Toughen skin on feet and hands gradually
- Treat any existing fungal infections aggressively—they'll explode in that humidity
- Learn to manage constant moisture without skin breakdown
- Pack extensive foot care supplies: antifungals, barrier creams, moisture-wicking materials

## III. Psychological Preparation (Ongoing, Intensive)

### Confronting the Darkness

The psychological challenge cannot be overstated. Humans are visual creatures. Losing sight triggers primal anxiety.

**Before departure:**
- Spend increasing periods in complete darkness—start with hours, build to days
- Document your psychological responses: fear, frustration, disorientation, acceptance
- Work with a therapist experienced in sensory deprivation
- Practice meditation and mindfulness to manage panic responses
- Learn your breaking point in darkness, then learn to push it further

**Understanding adjustment:**
- Expect 2-4 weeks of severe disorientation
- Visual hallucinations are normal—phosphenes, phantom lights, constructed images
- Spatial confusion is normal—your brain will struggle to build consistent mental maps
- Emotional volatility is normal—the stress is profound
- Sleep disruption is normal—without day/night cycles, your circadian rhythm collapses

**Coping strategies:**
- Establish artificial routines to maintain psychological stability
- Keep detailed audio journals to process experience
- Practice accepting uncertainty—you will not know what's around you
- Develop trust in your non-visual senses despite your brain's protests
- Learn to find comfort in the soundscape rather than silence

### Cultural Humility

The indigenous inhabitants have adapted to this environment over generations. You are an infant here.

- Study whatever is known about their cultures, but expect to be wrong
- Learn basic protocols if any are documented: greetings, respect markers, taboos
- Understand you will be completely dependent on local guidance initially
- Prepare to be patient with your own incompetence
- Accept that you may never fully adapt—and that's acceptable
- Bring appropriate gifts or trade goods (research this carefully)
- Learn whatever language you can, even if poorly

## IV. Equipment and Supplies

### Clothing System

**Base principles:**
- Everything will be wet, always
- Temperature regulation matters more than waterproofing
- Durability over weight savings
- No cotton—it stays wet and becomes hypothermia risk
- Neutral colors are meaningless here

**Layer system:**
1. Base layer: Merino wool or advanced synthetics that manage moisture, resist odor, dry relatively quickly
2. Mid layer: Lightweight fleece or wool for warmth even when wet
3. Outer layer: Not for waterproofing (impossible) but for wind protection and abrasion resistance
4. Spare layers: At least one complete change, sealed in waterproof storage

**Footwear:**
- Boots that drain and dry quickly, with aggressive tread
- Closed-toe design to protect from unseen obstacles
- Multiple pairs—your feet will be wet constantly, rotating helps
- Consider amphibious designs used for canyoneering
- Extensive sock supply: wool or synthetic, never cotton
- Gaiters to keep vegetation and debris out

**Head and hands:**
- Wide-brimmed hat to manage constant dripping
- Gloves that work wet for handling rope, tools, vegetation
- Bandana or cloth for wiping face, filtering water, multiple uses

**Specialized items:**
- Tactile markers on all clothing to identify pieces in darkness
- Textured zippers and closures you can operate by touch
- Everything secured or tethered—dropped items are lost forever
- Bright colors actually—if you do need rescue or return to lit worlds, visibility matters

### Navigation and Orientation Tools

**Sound-based:**
- Quality compass—but learn to verify it against the sonic environment
- Whistle (multiple frequencies) for signaling and echolocation
- Small bells or chimes to mark direction of camp or to identify yourself
- Recording device to document sonic landmarks
- If available: portable sonar or echolocation devices designed for blind navigation

**Physical:**
- Walking stick/pole: essential for probing ahead, detecting obstacles, stream depth
- Rope (plenty): for safety lines, marking routes, crossing obstacles
- Paracord for multiple purposes
- Tactile markers: textured ribbons, rough stones, anything to mark routes by touch

**Recording and mapping:**
- Audio recording equipment (waterproofed extensively)
- Waterproof notebooks with raised-line paper for tactile writing
- Stylus for making marks you can feel
- System for documenting routes: knots in cord, specific marker sequences

### Camp Equipment

**Shelter:**
- Tent that's 100% waterproof with excellent ventilation
- Ground sheet essential—the ground is saturated
- Hammock system might be better: gets you off wet ground, good drainage
- Extensive waterproof stuff sacks for everything
- Mosquito netting (assume biting insects)

**Sleep system:**
- Sleeping bag rated for humid conditions, synthetic fill (down becomes useless when wet)
- Sleeping pad that insulates from ground moisture
- Pillow improvised from clothes in waterproof bag

**Cooking:**
- Stove that works in high humidity—test extensively beforehand
- Fuel calculations assuming wet conditions affect efficiency
- Waterproof matches, multiple fire-starting methods
- Cooking pot, cup, utensil with tactile markers
- All food in waterproof containers

**Water management:**
- Filtration system (the water will need treatment despite constant flow)
- Chemical treatment backup
- Collection system for the constant condensation
- Containers with tactile differentiation: clean vs. dirty water

### Tools and Repair

- Multi-tool with each implement identifiable by touch
- Repair kit: needle, thread, patch material, adhesives
- Duct tape (the universal solution)
- Cordage of various types
- Small saw or hatchet if you're allowed
- Extensive first aid kit (see medical section)

### Communication

- If traveling in a group: whistles, bells, or drums for maintaining contact
- System of acoustic signals: agreed-upon meanings for different sounds
- Personal locator beacons if they work there (test!)
- Drum or similar instrument if you're staying long-term

### Personal Items

**Hygiene:**
- Biodegradable soap
- Toothbrush and paste with tactile markers
- Toilet paper (though it will be wet)
- Trowel for catholes
- Menstrual supplies if applicable (bring extra—resupply may be impossible)
- Towel (synthetic, fast-drying)
- Antifungal powder for feet, body

**Moisture management:**
- Silica gel packets (though they'll saturate—can you dry them?)
- Waterproof bags in many sizes
- Stuff sacks with different textures for identification
- Dry bags that actually stay dry (test them in bathtub for hours)

**Mental health:**
- Journal (audio or tactile)
- Musical instrument if you play (becomes navigation tool and comfort)
- Comfort object from home
- Photos won't help, but textured items with emotional significance
- Whatever grounds you psychologically

## V. Skills to Master Before Arrival

### Practical Skills

**Navigation without sight:**
- Dead reckoning using pace counting
- Using sound to build spatial maps
- Tracking time without visual cues (mechanical watch, counting heartbeats)
- Route-finding using tactile and sonic landmarks
- Understanding topography through sound propagation and air flow

**Water skills:**
- Crossing streams safely without seeing them
- Distinguishing safe from dangerous water by sound
- Swimming in complete darkness
- Finding and evaluating water sources by sound and scent

**Weather prediction:**
- Reading atmospheric pressure changes in your body
- Interpreting sound propagation changes that precede weather shifts
- Recognizing temperature and humidity patterns
- Understanding wind patterns through sound and feel

**Foraging and food:**
- If edible plants exist there, you must learn by touch and smell exclusively
- Safety protocols: never eat unidentified vegetation
- Probably you'll carry all food—but know principles of preservation in humidity
- Food storage in environments where smell attracts predators

**Acoustic discipline:**
- Moving quietly when necessary
- Understanding how your sounds travel in that environment
- Reading others' sounds to understand their status and intentions
- Using sound deliberately for communication vs. remaining undetectable

### Safety Skills

**Predator awareness:**
- The predators hunt by sound—understand what sounds attract attention
- They coordinate through acoustic communication—learn to recognize it
- Your scent will be novel—understand scent discipline
- Group movement patterns that maintain safety

**Environmental hazards:**
- Detecting unstable ground (sinkholes, undermined areas)
- Identifying dangerous water (deep channels, currents)
- Recognizing the acoustic signatures of cliffs, drops, obstacles
- Understanding when weather turns dangerous

**Medical skills:**
- Treating injuries by touch alone
- Identifying infections, sprains, fractures without seeing
- Improvised first aid in complete darkness
- When to abort and seek help vs. treating in field

**Psychological crisis management:**
- Recognizing panic attacks vs. legitimate danger
- Grounding techniques when disoriented
- Helping others through sensory adjustment
- Maintaining morale in extended darkness

## VI. First Days: What to Expect

### Arrival

You will likely arrive at one of the established entry points where lit-world travelers are received. The transition is managed, not abrupt.

**Immediate experience:**
- Profound disorientation as visual input cuts off
- Nausea and vertigo are common—your brain will struggle
- Fear response: this is normal, work through it
- Overwhelming soundscape: it will seem like chaos initially

**Initial adjustment (first 72 hours):**
- Remain in a controlled environment (settlement, station)
- Practice basic movement in familiar space
- Begin building acoustic map of immediate area
- Eat and sleep on artificial schedule to maintain stability
- Accept help from guides—do not attempt independence yet

**First week:**
- Short, supervised excursions outside settlements
- Practice basic skills: walking on varied terrain, water crossing, route-finding
- Build confidence in non-visual senses
- Learn basic acoustic landmarks of the area
- Begin to distinguish normal sounds from significant ones
- Your visual cortex will create hallucinations—acknowledge, ignore, proceed

### Working with Guides

The indigenous inhabitants who guide outsiders are specialists. They're doing you an extraordinary service.

**Protocol:**
- Listen more than you speak (literally—you're learning to hear)
- Follow instructions exactly—your intuitions are wrong here
- Ask questions, but accept that some things must be experienced
- Pay agreed-upon rates fairly—their time and knowledge are valuable
- Understand that some places may be off-limits to outsiders
- Never separate from your guide without explicit permission
- Learn their acoustic signals for different situations

### Building Independence

This takes months minimum, possibly years.

**Progression:**
- First: navigate familiar routes with guidance
- Then: same routes with guide following
- Then: short solo excursions with defined boundaries
- Then: longer independent travel with scheduled check-ins
- Finally: true independence (which most outsiders never achieve)

**Markers of competence:**
- You can build accurate mental map of several square miles
- You can navigate between landmarks reliably
- You can identify and respond to weather changes
- You can assess and manage common dangers
- You can maintain yourself physically and psychologically
- You can communicate effectively with inhabitants
- You stop experiencing visual hallucinations
- You start to perceive the faint bioluminescence

## VII. Long-term Survival Strategies

### Mental Frameworks

**Accept that you are always a visitor:**
- Even with years of experience, you lack generational adaptation
- The born inhabitants process information faster and more accurately
- Your role is observer, student, guest—not master of this environment
- Find purpose in documentation, trade, specific skills you bring

**Develop new relationships with time:**
- Without day/night, create artificial structures
- Align with the seven weather cycles instead
- Mark time by herd movements, seasons, processes
- Keep track of how long you've been there—it's easy to lose count

**Find beauty in the soundscape:**
- The place is not hostile, just different
- The acoustic complexity is magnificent once you learn to hear it
- The feel of the world—constant moisture, texture, temperature—becomes rich
- Many visitors report profound peace after adjustment

**Maintain connection to lit world:**
- If you plan to return, maintain visual memory through description
- Keep audio journal documenting experience
- Stay in contact with other travelers if possible
- Remember that readjusting to light will also be difficult

### Physical Maintenance

**Hygiene in constant moisture:**
- Daily attention to feet: clean, dry as possible, treat hotspots immediately
- Skin folds need regular cleaning to prevent fungal infections
- Teeth cleaning is critical—dental problems become emergencies
- Hair management: short or secured, becomes uncomfortable when constantly wet
- Regular equipment checks: mold and moisture degrade everything

**Nutrition:**
- High calorie intake: the humidity and constant movement demand fuel
- Salt replacement: you're sweating constantly even if you don't notice
- Variety prevents deficiency: supplement if necessary
- Food storage is challenging: eat fresh when possible, preserve carefully
- Water is everywhere but must be treated—parasites and bacteria are real

**Rest and recovery:**
- You need more rest than you expect—the sensory adjustment is exhausting
- Sleep quality may be poor initially—this improves
- Regular stretching prevents injury from constant walking
- Listen to your body: pain is harder to assess without visual inspection

### Social Integration

**If staying long-term:**
- Learn the languages (there are several)
- Participate in seasonal gatherings
- Offer skills you bring: medical knowledge, different technologies, documentation
- Accept that full integration may not be possible
- Build genuine relationships, not transactional ones
- Understand local politics and avoid missteps
- Respect sacred sites and cultural practices even if unexplained

**Trading and economy:**
- Understand what's valuable: certain metals, technologies, knowledge, medicines
- The economy is partly acoustic—information has value
- Reputation matters enormously in dispersed communities
- Keep commitments absolutely—trust is everything
- Some settlements use physical currencies, some don't

## VIII. Special Considerations

### Traveling with Others

**Advantages:**
- Shared psychological support
- Divided labor
- Safety in numbers
- Different skillsets complement

**Challenges:**
- Maintaining contact in complete darkness
- Coordinating movement without visual signals
- Managing group conflicts when stressed
- Different adjustment rates create tension

**Protocols:**
- Physical tethers for dangerous terrain
- Regular acoustic check-ins
- Agreed-upon signal system
- Clear leadership structure
- Private space even in groups (psychological necessity)

### Traveling Alone

**Advantages:**
- Move at your own pace
- Simpler logistics
- Deeper integration potential
- Profound personal transformation

**Challenges:**
- No backup in crisis
- Psychological isolation can be severe
- All decisions are yours—no one to reality-check
- More dependent on local inhabitants

**Requirements:**
- Extensive experience in darkness before attempting
- Robust psychological stability
- Excellent health
- Clear itinerary left with reliable contacts
- Scheduled check-ins at stations

### Specific Hazards to Understand

**Predators:**
- They know you're there—your sound, scent, and movement patterns are obvious
- They generally avoid humans (you're not normal prey)
- But injured, young, or desperate animals are dangerous
- Never approach herd animals—they panic easily, trampling is a real risk
- Mothers with young are aggressive
- Learn warning sounds and how to respond

**Environmental:**
- Sinkholes open suddenly—probe ahead always
- Flash floods in valleys during certain weather
- Temperature inversions can cause respiratory issues
- Some zones have toxic gas emissions from underground
- Certain weather combinations create dangerous conditions

**Disease:**
- Fungal infections are constant battle
- Waterborne parasites are common
- Insect-borne diseases may exist
- Infections progress quickly in humidity
- Monitor wounds obsessively—you can't see inflammation

**Psychological:**
- Panic attacks in darkness can be lethal—you flee blindly
- Sensory deprivation psychosis is real
- Isolation depression develops insidiously
- Phantom sounds and touches become concerning
- Suicide risk increases—know warning signs in yourself

### Emergency Protocols

**Lost:**
- Stop moving immediately
- Use whistle or drum to signal
- Listen for acoustic landmarks
- If possible, return to last known location
- Prepare to shelter in place and be found
- Inhabited areas have search systems—but you must be patient

**Injured:**
- Assess by touch systematically
- Treat what you can
- If serious: signal, shelter, wait for help
- If alone and mobile: carefully proceed toward known settlement
- If alone and immobile: this is worst case—signal continuously

**Psychological crisis:**
- Recognize it's happening
- Use grounding techniques: tactile, proprioceptive focus
- Control breathing
- Talk yourself through: verbal self-coaching
- If traveling with others: communicate your state
- If alone: survival mode—simple tasks, focus on immediate needs

**Abort triggers:**
- Serious injury or illness
- Psychological breakdown
- Critical equipment failure
- Becoming truly lost with no landmarks
- Weather creating immediate danger
- Any situation where remaining is higher risk than attempting exit

## IX. Preparation Timeline

### 12 Months Before Departure

- Medical evaluation and treatment of any issues
- Begin sensory training program
- Physical conditioning starts
- Research culture and environment
- Language learning begins
- Equipment acquisition and testing
- Psychological evaluation and preparation

### 6 Months Before

- Intensive sensory training: spending days blindfolded
- Advanced physical conditioning
- Equipment shakedown trips in wet environments
- Practice all skills in complete darkness
- Advanced cultural study
- Medical preparations: vaccines, prophylaxis planning
- Arrange guides and contacts

### 3 Months Before

- Final medical checks
- Extended darkness immersion
- Final equipment testing and modifications
- Confirm all logistics
- Document baseline psychological state
- Complete required immunizations
- Final physical conditioning push

### 1 Month Before

- Taper physical training to prevent injury
- Pack and repack, testing all systems
- Mental preparation intensifies
- Final briefings with anyone who's been there
- Arrange affairs in lit world
- Say proper goodbyes
- Final psychological preparation

### Final Week

- Light physical activity only
- Equipment final checks
- Mental preparation, meditation, acceptance work
- Logistical arrangements finalized
- Last contact with loved ones
- Begin adjusting sleep cycle if possible
- Rest, hydrate, prepare psychologically

## X. What Cannot Be Prepared For

Some things you will only learn by being there:

- How completely disorienting total darkness actually is
- How loud the world becomes when you can only hear it
- How your identity shifts when you cannot see yourself or be seen
- How touch and scent become intimate and immediate in new ways
- How time feels different without visual marking
- How the body knows things the mind doesn't understand
- How beautiful sound can be as primary sense
- How the darkness becomes not enemy but environment
- How you are both more fragile and more capable than you believed
- How coming home to the lit world will be its own difficult transition

## XI. The Real Question

Before all this preparation, before the training and equipment and planning, ask yourself:

**Why are you going?**

Because curiosity alone won't sustain you through the adjustment. Neither will adventure-seeking or prestige. The darkness will strip away superficial motivations and leave you with whatever is underneath.

Good reasons:
- Genuine calling to understand this place
- Scientific or documentary purpose that serves the inhabitants
- Spiritual seeking that requires this specific challenge
- Contributing skills that are needed there
- Deep fascination that has persisted through honest research

Insufficient reasons:
- Proving something to yourself or others
- Escaping problems in lit world
- Thrill-seeking
- Casual curiosity
- Assumption it will be easier than it is

The Breathing Plains will teach you about yourself—truths that are easier to hide from in the lit world. Be certain you're ready for that education.

## XII. After You Return

If you return.

**Readjustment to light:**
- Painful initially—literally, light hurts
- Visual processing feels overwhelming, chaotic
- You may miss the simplicity of the darkness
- Depression is common—the lit world seems less rich
- Relationships are different—you've changed profoundly

**Integration:**
- Most people can't understand the experience
- You may feel isolated even among people
- Useful work: documentation, helping prepare future travelers
- Some people return to the plains—it calls them
- Therapy helps process the transformation

**What you bring back:**
- New ways of sensing and knowing
- Different relationship with sight—less dependent
- Appreciation for acoustic information
- Patience with uncertainty
- Understanding of how adaptation works
- Stories no one will quite believe
- Possibly, a sense of homesickness for darkness

---

The Breathing Plains of Umbrathene are not hostile, but they are utterly uncompromising. They will not adjust to you; you must adjust to them. The darkness does not care about your fear or your limits. The weather cycles will not wait for your readiness. The herds will not slow for your confusion.

But if you prepare thoroughly, approach humbly, learn patiently, and accept transformation—the plains will teach you. They will show you a world that functions magnificently without light, that is rich and complex and beautiful in ways the lit world cannot imagine.

You will learn that darkness is not absence but presence. That blindness is not deficit but difference. That human adaptability is more profound than you knew.

And you will learn what you are made of when every superficial thing is stripped away and you stand in the ancient darkness, listening to the breathing of the world, becoming part of its vast cycles.

Prepare carefully. Travel safely. Return changed.
