# Preparation Guide for the Breathing Pavilion

## Physical Preparation

### Your Body's Adaptation Needs

You must understand: your body will be colonized. This is not invasion—it is the nature of the place. Begin preparing your immune system months before departure.

**Microbiome conditioning**: Start taking broad-spectrum probiotics, but more importantly, expose yourself to living systems. Spend time in greenhouses, conservatories, anywhere warm and wet where things grow densely. Let your skin touch moss, lichen, wet bark. Your body needs to remember that it can host other life without panicking. The pavilion will introduce hundreds of new microbial relationships in the first hours. Your immune system should meet this as negotiation, not war.

**Respiratory conditioning**: Practice breathing in humid environments. Use a humidifier in your sleep space, gradually increasing moisture until the air feels thick. Visit steam rooms, saunas, tropical conservatories. Your lungs need to adjust to extracting oxygen from air that's nearly liquid. If you have any respiratory issues—asthma, chronic bronchitis, even mild allergies—address them now. The air there will challenge compromised lungs.

**Skin preparation**: Your largest organ will be permanently damp. This means fungal risks, bacterial opportunities, constant moisture exposure. Build resilience by:
- Gradually reducing your use of harsh soaps and antimicrobial products
- Spending time with wet skin—swimming, then air-drying slowly
- Building calluses on your feet; you'll be walking barefoot on textured, slippery surfaces
- If you're prone to skin infections, work with a dermatologist on prophylactic strategies

**Temperature regulation**: The pavilion maintains blood warmth. Your body needs to handle constant heat without the relief of coolness. Heat acclimate beforehand—spend time in warm, humid spaces, let your sweat response optimize. If you're from a cold climate, this is especially critical.

### Physical Fitness Considerations

You don't need athlete-level fitness, but certain capabilities will transform your experience:

**Balance and proprioception**: The surfaces are uneven, slippery, often unstable. Practice:
- Walking on irregular surfaces barefoot
- Balance exercises on soft, yielding materials
- Moving through spaces where you must duck, weave, adjust constantly
- If possible, spend time in shallow water with unclear footing

**Flexibility**: You'll be climbing through organic architecture, squeezing through passages, sitting on surfaces that aren't quite furniture. Yoga or similar practices help. Focus on hip flexibility, spinal suppleness, shoulder mobility.

**Climbing ability**: Not technical climbing, but comfortable movement in three dimensions. The root-strut architecture invites vertical exploration. Practice climbing trees, bouldering, navigating jungle gyms. Build confidence with height and with trusting organic structures to hold your weight.

**Stamina over strength**: You won't need to lift heavy objects, but you'll be moving constantly in heat and humidity. Build your aerobic base. Long walks in difficult conditions are ideal preparation.

## Medical and Health Considerations

### Essential Health Interventions

**Vaccinations and prophylaxis**: This is complex because the pavilion's microbiome is unique. Consult with a physician familiar with expedition medicine, but consider:
- Broad-spectrum fungal prophylaxis (discuss antifungals with your doctor)
- Updated tetanus (organic architecture means organic injuries)
- Tropical disease panels even though this isn't quite Earth-tropical
- Consider antibiotic options for skin infections, though you may not use them

**Dental work**: Complete all dental care before going. The humidity and unique microbiome create unpredictable oral environments. Cavities will progress faster. Gum disease becomes complicated. Go with perfect dental health.

**Wounds and scars**: Any open wounds must heal completely first. The pavilion's microbiome will colonize open tissue aggressively. Old scars are fine; fresh wounds are invitations to infections you can't predict.

**Chronic conditions**: 
- Diabetes: Extremely challenging; the humidity affects insulin, and the symbiotic colonization may alter metabolism unpredictably
- Autoimmune conditions: Problematic; your body's confusion about self/other will be amplified
- Allergies: Assess carefully; you'll be exposed to countless novel proteins and spores
- Heart conditions: The constant heat-humidity is cardiovascular stress
- Immunocompromised: Likely prohibitive; discuss extensively with specialists

**Medications**: 
- Bring extras; humidity may degrade them faster
- Consider how the pavilion's chemistry might interact with your medications
- Antimicrobials are ethical questions there—using them affects the ecosystem
- Waterproof all medication storage multiple times over

### Mental Health Preparation

This is as important as physical prep, perhaps more so.

**Boundary dissolution tolerance**: If you have strong needs for bodily autonomy, for clear self/other distinction, this place will be profoundly difficult. The pavilion will grow on you, in you, with you. Small creatures will tend your body. Plants will begin to root in your clothes, your hair. You must be psychologically capable of accepting this merger.

**Work with a therapist** on:
- Body image flexibility
- Acceptance of uncontrollable change
- Comfort with ambiguity and impermanence
- Processing feelings about being "colonized" even though it's symbiosis
- Strategies for maintaining core identity while external markers shift

**Isolation tolerance**: Despite the inhabitants, you can be alone for long periods. The sparseness of people means solitude is common. Yet you're never truly alone—the ecosystem presses in constantly. This paradox (isolated yet surrounded) troubles some minds. Practice meditation in crowded natural spaces. Learn to be alone among life.

**Change navigation**: The turbulence, the constant flux, the inability to map consistently—this destabilizes some psychological types. If you need predictability, routine, controllable environments, either build flexibility first or reconsider going. Practice exercises where your environment changes without your input and you must adapt with grace.

**Claustrophobia and agoraphobia**: The enclosed-yet-open spaces create unique triggers. You're always bounded by growth, but the scale varies dramatically. If you have either spatial anxiety, explore whether the pavilion's specific geometry (living walls, generous spaces, soft boundaries) will soothe or aggravate you.

## Practical Preparations

### What to Bring (Very Little)

The pavilion will consume or colonize most objects. Bring minimal, simple, surrenderable items:

**Clothing**: 
- Minimal, natural fibers only
- Nothing you love; it will be transformed
- Lightweight, quick-drying in theory (though nothing dries there)
- Simple shorts, simple shirts, perhaps a sarong-like wrap
- Absolutely no waterproof synthetics—they'll be colonized externally and become rigid with growth
- Most people transition to near-nudity quickly; perhaps bring clothing as a psychological transition object only

**Footwear**:
- Water sandals for the transition period
- Accept that you'll be barefoot soon
- Bring foot care supplies: clippers, files for calluses, antifungal powder (though its use is an ethical question)

**Practical items**:
- Water bottle (metal, simple, will be colonized externally)
- A knife (stainless steel, keep it oiled obsessively)
- Notebook and pencils (not pens—ink runs) in waterproof case (will fail eventually)
- Basic first aid supplies in multiple layers of waterproofing
- Microfiber towel (will become a biology project but useful initially)
- Headlamp (corrosion-resistant, with many spare batteries in waterproof case)
- Menstrual supplies if relevant (silicone cups work better than disposables)

**What not to bring**:
- Electronics (they will die, the humidity is absolute)
- Books (they will rot, literally become compost)
- Photographs or sentimental items (they will be destroyed or transformed)
- Jewelry (will colonize, may cause skin issues)
- Makeup, skincare, haircare products (pointless and will upset your microbiome transition)
- Sleeping bag or tent (unnecessary and will be consumed)

### Skills to Develop

**Botanical knowledge**: Study:
- How to identify edible vs. toxic fungi
- Recognition of symbiotic vs. parasitic relationships
- Basic plant physiology—you'll be living inside it
- Moss and lichen ecology
- How to read plant health and what different growths indicate

**Water safety**: 
- Learn to assess water quality by observation
- Understand biofilm formation
- Know signs of water-borne illness
- Practice moving safely through shallow water with unclear footing

**Minimal living**: Practice at home:
- Living with very few possessions
- Finding comfort without familiar objects
- Creating routine without external structure
- Being entertained by observation rather than devices or media

**Sensory development**: 
- Practice identifying plants by smell, touch, sound
- Develop your sensitivity to humidity changes, air pressure, temperature subtleties
- Learn to navigate by sound in low-visibility conditions
- Practice walking silently and attentively

**Body-reading**: The inhabitants communicate significantly through posture, gesture, and subtle body language. Study:
- Non-verbal communication across cultures
- Animal body language
- Mindfulness of your own physical presence and signals
- How to be still in a way that communicates receptivity

## Cultural and Social Preparation

### Understanding the Inhabitants

The people there have chosen integration with the pavilion. They are:
- Not primitive—deeply technologically sophisticated at scales you can't see
- Not mystical—pragmatic about the realities of symbiotic living
- Not seeking converts—indifferent to whether you join or leave
- Not a community in conventional sense—loosely coordinated individuals who sometimes cluster, often separate

**Learn to:**
- Respect space and distance; the sparseness is intentional
- Communicate minimally and meaningfully
- Offer help indirectly (work on shared tasks rather than "helping" someone)
- Accept help when offered without excessive gratitude or reciprocity pressure
- Touch the growth rather than the people—it's often more appropriate
- Understand that you're joining an ecosystem, not a society

### Ethical Framework

Develop clear personal ethics about:

**Intervention**: When is it appropriate to guide growth vs. let it proceed? The inhabitants have deep knowledge; you don't. Default to observation.

**Taking**: Everything there is alive or recently alive. If you eat fungi from a platform, you're eating someone's furniture. This isn't forbidden but requires awareness. Watch what the inhabitants harvest, how, when, how much.

**Giving**: Your body will give whether you choose or not—dead skin, shed hair, waste products, the microbes you arrived with. This is your contribution. Consider what else you might offer. Your presence changes things; is the change beneficial?

**Leaving**: If you take anything (knowledge, samples, even memories externalized in notes), what's the impact? The pavilion is self-contained. It doesn't need the outside world. You're the one seeking something. What's the exchange?

**Antimicrobials**: Using antifungals or antibiotics there is like using herbicides in someone's garden while living in it. You may need them for your health, but understand the implications. Discuss with the inhabitants if possible.

## Psychological Preparation for Specific Experiences

### The Initial Colonization

In your first hours, your body will be discovered by the ecosystem. Expect:
- Tickling sensations as small creatures explore you
- Moisture accumulating on your skin and never evaporating
- Unfamiliar smells from your own body as your microbiome shifts
- Mild itching as symbiotic fungi establish on your skin
- Changed taste in your mouth as oral flora adapt
- Possible mild nausea as gut bacteria adjust
- Emotional responses: vulnerability, violation, wonder, surrender

**Prepare by**:
- Practicing radical acceptance meditation
- Visualizing these sensations in advance and breathing through them
- Understanding intellectually that this is safe, normal, and not pathological
- Having mantras or grounding phrases ready: "I am becoming part of it; it is becoming part of me"

### The Boundary Dissolution

At some point, you'll realize you can't tell where you end and the pavilion begins. This might be:
- Terrifying (loss of self)
- Ecstatic (union with larger being)
- Disorienting (confusion about identity)
- Peaceful (relief from separation)
- All of these in sequence

**Prepare by**:
- Reading about ego dissolution experiences (psychedelic literature is relevant)
- Meditation practices focused on non-self
- Journaling about your identity markers and which are actually essential
- Discussing with a therapist what your core identity is vs. external attributes
- Having anchor practices (breathing patterns, mantras, physical gestures) that remain "yours"

### The Temporal Disorientation

The constant change makes time feel strange. Days blur. The light is always diffuse. Growth happens on multiple timescales simultaneously. You may experience:
- Loss of date/time awareness
- Feeling like you've been there forever after just days
- Feeling like weeks have been minutes
- Difficulty planning or anticipating
- Living in eternal present (can be liberating or destabilizing)

**Prepare by**:
- Practicing being without clocks/calendars before going
- Developing comfort with not knowing what time it is
- Creating personal rhythms based on internal states rather than external schedules
- Accepting that you may not be able to report accurately how long you stayed

### The Communication Shift

Language becomes less useful. The communication is chemical, vibrational, postural, temporal. You may:
- Forget words temporarily
- Find speech effortful or unnecessary
- Begin to "know" things without knowing how you know them
- Sense intentions from the growth itself
- Feel frustrated by the inability to articulate experiences

**Prepare by**:
- Practicing silence retreats
- Learning to be comfortable with incomplete understanding
- Developing trust in non-rational knowing
- Keeping journal entries simple (sketches, single words, fragments)
- Accepting that some experiences won't translate to language

## Physical Survival Knowledge

### Food

The pavilion provides, but you must learn what's offered:

**Edible fungi**: Many of the shelf growths are nutritious. Learn:
- Which ones the inhabitants harvest
- How to take portions without killing the organism
- How to assess freshness (though in this humidity, "fresh" is relative)
- That some are mildly psychoactive; start with small amounts
- Preparation is usually unnecessary; eat directly from the shelf

**Fruits and flowers**: Some flowering vines produce edible fruits. Watch:
- What the furred creatures eat (generally safe)
- Timing of ripeness
- Which flowers are food vs. which serve other purposes

**Water**: It's everywhere, but:
- Drink from flowing areas, not stagnant
- Watch where the inhabitants drink
- The amber-stained water is safe once your gut adjusts
- You can drink moisture directly from certain leaves
- Your water needs will be different in this humidity; you're absorbing moisture through your skin

**Potential issues**:
- Everything tastes like the pavilion: earthy, slightly sweet, fungal
- Nutritional completeness may be questionable long-term
- Some visitors lose appetite initially; the smells are overwhelming
- Others become ravenous; the constant metabolic activity is contagious

### Shelter

You don't build shelter; you find it within the architecture:

**Sleeping spots**:
- Fungal shelves large enough to lie on
- Hollows within root masses
- Platforms formed by intersecting struts
- Elevated areas less likely to flood during the heavy exhales

**Comfort**:
- Gather moss to create cushioning
- Accept that you'll be damp while sleeping
- Temperature is never uncomfortable; no blankets needed
- The sounds continue all night; they become white noise
- Bioluminescence provides dim night-light

**Privacy**:
- Easily found due to sparseness of people
- The growth itself creates visual barriers
- Psychological privacy is harder; you're always in the ecosystem's awareness

### Hygiene (Reconceptualized)

Traditional hygiene concepts don't apply:

**Bathing**: Immerse in the deeper pools, but:
- You won't feel "clean" by outside standards
- The biofilm on your skin is intentional
- Scrubbing it off triggers rapid recolonization and may cause irritation
- The inhabitants rarely bathe traditionally; they allow creature-tending

**Waste**:
- Defecation: In designated water areas; it's integrated into the nutrient cycle
- Urination: Anywhere in the water; again, it's nutrients
- This feels transgressive initially; it becomes normal
- The ecosystem processes waste faster than you can imagine

**Teeth**:
- Continue cleaning them somehow (frayed stick as brush, clean water as rinse)
- Your oral microbiome will change regardless
- Some visitors report improved gum health; others report issues

**Hair**:
- Will become a biology experiment
- Combing or brushing becomes impossible as growth establishes
- Most visitors eventually ask an inhabitant to help cut or remove colonized hair
- Some embrace it, becoming mobile gardens

## Duration Planning

### Short Visit (Days to Weeks)

If you're visiting briefly:
- Stay in the outer zones; less integration, easier reversal
- Keep some outside clothes in waterproof storage at the edge
- Accept you'll experience the least of what the pavilion offers
- Focus on observation over participation
- Understand you'll be perpetually adjusting, never settled
- Plan for re-entry shock; the dry outside world will feel harsh, hostile, empty

### Medium Stay (Weeks to Months)

This is the integration period:
- You'll become substantially colonized
- Your microbiome shifts significantly
- You begin to sense the chemical signals
- Social bonds with inhabitants may form
- Psychological identity shifts are likely
- Plan for serious medical evaluation upon return
- Consider the psychological support you'll need outside afterward

### Long Stay (Months to Years)

This is commitment:
- You may become permanent
- Reversal becomes progressively difficult
- Your body chemistry changes fundamentally
- Outside world becomes alien
- You'll need exit strategies if you choose to leave: gradual de-colonization, medical support, psychological deprogramming
- Some stay forever; this is neither good nor bad, but should be chosen consciously

## Re-Entry Preparation

Even if you plan to return to your origin, prepare for re-entry:

### Physical Re-Entry

- Gradual drying-out period in transition zone
- Medical evaluation for retained symbiotes
- Possible antifungal treatment (coordinate with specialists beforehand)
- Skin will peel and shed extensively as moisture-adapted layers die
- Respiratory adjustment to dry air (will feel like suffocation initially)
- Temperature regulation recalibration
- Possible nutritional deficiencies requiring supplementation

### Psychological Re-Entry

- Culture shock in reverse; the "normal" world will seem:
  - Rigidly boundaried (everything separate from everything else)
  - Harshly dry and cold
  - Dead (the air doesn't breathe)
  - Loud in wrong ways, silent in wrong ways
  - Deeply lonely despite being surrounded by people
  
- You'll need:
  - Therapist familiar with integration experiences
  - Community of others who've been to similar places
  - Permission to grieve the loss of connection
  - Time to re-establish identity outside the ecosystem
  - Patience with yourself as you remember how to be separate

### Maintain Connection

Some things that help:
- Terrarium or greenhouse time; humid green spaces
- Growing moss in your home
- Recordings of water sounds (though they'll make you ache)
- Keeping a piece of the pavilion with you is generally not possible (it dies outside), but the memory remains
- Writing, art, or other integration of the experience
- Knowing you can return, if the pavilion accepts you again

## Final Considerations

### Who Should Not Go

Be honest about whether you're a candidate:

**Do not go if:**
- You have severe OCD about contamination
- You have trauma around bodily autonomy violations
- You require climate control for medical reasons
- You have severe allergies or respiratory conditions
- You're fleeing something rather than going toward something
- You need control over your environment for psychological stability
- You're unstable in identity and need external structure
- You're seeking escape rather than experience

**Reconsider carefully if:**
- You're claustrophobic or agoraphobic
- You have significant health issues
- You need regular medication
- You have strong attachment to physical possessions or appearance
- You're uncomfortable with insects or fungi
- You need social interaction for well-being (the sparseness may be difficult)
- You're going to prove something rather than learn something

### Who Might Thrive

**Good candidates:**
- Comfortable with ambiguity and change
- Fascinated by biology and symbiosis
- Able to find stillness in activity
- Flexible about boundaries
- Psychologically resilient but not defended
- Curious without agenda
- Comfortable being small part of larger system
- Able to be alone without loneliness
- Respectful of non-human intelligence
- Seeking understanding rather than achievement

### The Essential Question

Before going, sit with this question:

**Can you allow yourself to be changed by a place without losing yourself entirely?**

The pavilion will change you. Your body will be different. Your perception will be altered. Your understanding of where you end and the world begins will be challenged. But you must maintain enough coherent self to make choices, to leave if needed, to integrate the experience.

The pavilion isn't dangerous in the sense of hostile. But it is transformative, profoundly so. Transformation without grounding becomes dissolution. You must be strong enough in your core to bend without breaking.

If you can answer yes to that essential question—if you can change while remaining—then prepare carefully, surrender thoughtfully, and enter the breathing pavilion with open awareness.

It will grow on you, in you, with you. You will become part of its breath, one more element in its ancient, turbulent, perfect collaboration. And when you leave (if you leave), you will carry it with you forever, a small colony of memory and transformation, a piece of living architecture inhabiting the cathedral of your body.

The oldest thing decided to become shelter. In visiting, you too become part of what shelters and what is sheltered, what grows and what enables growth, what breathes and what is breathed.

Go gently. Go aware. Go prepared to be amazed.
