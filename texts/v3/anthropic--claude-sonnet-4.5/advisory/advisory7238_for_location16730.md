# Preparation Guide for the Thundering Gardens of Koreth

## Physical Conditioning

Begin cardiovascular training at least three months before departure. The altitude here is severe—thin air atop mountains so old they've transcended conventional geology. You'll need your body accustomed to functioning with less oxygen. If possible, train at elevation. If not, use an altitude simulation mask or sleep in a hypoxic tent. Your target should be comfortable exertion at 12,000-14,000 feet minimum.

But don't neglect balance and proprioception. This isn't technical climbing, but the wet moss, smooth stone, and hidden depressions demand constant micro-adjustments. Yoga, slacklining, or regular practice on unstable surfaces will serve you well. Your ankles need to be strong and responsive. A turned ankle here, alone, could be catastrophic.

## Auditory Preparation

This is critical and often overlooked: you must prepare for the *sound*. Constant, layered, immense noise will be your reality. The water-rush, the wind-tones, the mountain-song—it never stops. Some people break psychologically within days.

Start now: sleep with soundscapes playing—waterfalls, wind, multiple layers simultaneously. Train yourself to remain calm, to think clearly, to maintain emotional equilibrium in constant noise. Practice meditation with audio chaos. Learn to distinguish sounds within cacophony. Your survival may depend on hearing a warning amid the din: rockfall, a sudden stream surge, the crack of failing stone.

Consider: you will not have true silence for weeks or months. If silence is where you find peace, find peace elsewhere instead. Learn now whether you're someone who can endure this, or if it will slowly unmake you.

## Gear: Footwear

This is where most preparation succeeds or fails. You need boots with aggressive tread that can grip wet stone. But they also need flexibility for the constant balance adjustments. Stiff mountaineering boots will betray you here. Look for approach shoes or light hiking boots with sticky rubber compounds designed for wet rock.

Bring two pairs minimum, and a third if you can carry it. The wet will destroy them faster than normal use. Have a rotation system to allow drying time—though "dry" is relative here. Pack spare insoles. Your feet will be damp constantly; the insoles will compress, lose support, begin to smell biblically. Replace them weekly.

Gaiters are essential. Not for snow, but to keep the endless water from pouring down into your boots with every step through the moss and fern.

## Gear: Clothing

Synthetic or wool only. Cotton is death here—it absorbs the omnipresent moisture and will not dry. Ever. You'll be damp regardless, but the right fabrics will keep you warm while damp.

Layer system: lightweight synthetic base layer, merino wool mid-layer, water-resistant (not waterproof—you need breathability) outer layer. The mist doesn't fall hard enough for waterproof gear, but waterproof also traps sweat, and you'll be soaked from inside. Water-resistant softshells that allow vapor escape are your friend.

Bring more socks than you think reasonable. At least ten pairs. Change them twice daily. The moisture fosters maceration and fungal growth. Trench foot is a real risk despite the comfortable temperature.

A wide-brimmed hat helps keep mist from your face and eyes. In the clear moments, the sun at altitude is fierce. In the mist, the brim provides psychological relief—a small dry zone for your face.

## Gear: Sleep System

A hammock setup may serve you better than a tent. The ground is never dry, and the moss, while soft, is essentially a saturated sponge. A good camping hammock with an integrated bug net and rainfly allows you to sleep above the wet. The angle you'll sleep at takes adjustment—practice at home.

If you prefer ground sleeping, bring a tent with excellent ventilation and a full-coverage waterproof fly. The moisture will condense on every surface. A tent that doesn't breathe will be a swimming pool by morning. Pack a closed-cell foam pad as your bottom insulation layer—inflatable pads will work, but a puncture means you're sleeping on wet ground with no barrier. The foam is insurance.

Your sleeping bag should be synthetic. Down will absorb moisture from the air and lose all insulating value within days. A synthetic bag rated to 30°F (about -1°C) will keep you comfortable. Store it in a waterproof compression sack, but air it out daily when possible. It will still accumulate moisture, but you can slow the process.

## Gear: Shelter and Fire

The ancient buildings offer shelter, but they're scarce and their roofs may not be trustworthy. Don't plan around them; consider them bonuses when found. The wind-shelters and natural overhangs are more reliable, if you can spot them amid the stone curves and moss drapes.

Fire is possible but challenging. Everything is wet. Bring waterproof matches, at least three lighters, a ferrocerium rod as backup. But critically: bring fire starter that works when wet. Trioxane bars, wetfire cubes, or petroleum-jelly-soaked cotton balls in a waterproof container. You'll need significant starter to get local materials burning.

The small twisted trees provide wood, but it requires processing. Bring a good folding saw and a full-tang knife. You'll need to split wood to reach the drier interior. The outer layers will be saturated. Even then, expect long, smoky fires that struggle to establish. A small backpacking stove for cooking is not luxury but necessity. Bring more fuel than your trip length suggests—you'll use fire for morale as much as cooking.

## Navigation

GPS functions if you have a unit, but bring paper maps and a compass. The mist can reduce visibility to arm's length for hours or days. You need to navigate by terrain features, by dead reckoning, by careful attention.

Before you enter, study the topography obsessively. Memorize the major features—the tall pillar draped in moss, the green-glass lake below, the darker mineral patches. These are your landmarks. In the mist, you'll navigate pool to pool, outcrop to outcrop, counting paces, noting the direction of moss growth (though this shifts), feeling for the subtle grade changes that indicate you're crossing from one drainage to another.

Bring a notebook with waterproof paper. Map your route as you go. Sketch the landmarks. Note the cairns and ancient walls—they mark something, even if you don't know what. They may guide you out.

## Water and Food

Water is everywhere, but treat it all. The pools are stagnant, full of algae and tiny ecologies. The streams are temporary and flow across moss and through stone that may contain minerals you don't want concentrated in your tissues. Bring a filter good for at least two liters per day, plus chemical backup (iodine or chlorine dioxide tablets). The water will taste of stone and green growth even after treatment. Accept this.

For food, dense calories in waterproof packaging. The moisture will find its way into everything eventually. Zip-loc bags inside dry bags inside pack. Nuts, dried fruit, energy bars, summer sausage, hard cheese, instant rice or pasta sides, oatmeal, olive oil for calorie density. You'll be working hard on the constant slopes and balance adjustments. Eat more than you think necessary.

Avoid foods that require complex preparation. Your cooking will be minimal—boiling water, rehydrating meals. In persistent mist and wind, even simple cooking is an ordeal. Plan for it.

## Medical Considerations

Foot care is primary. Bring multiple treatment options for blisters: moleskin, Leukotape, hydrocolloid bandages. Address hot spots immediately, before they become blisters. Bring antifungal powder and cream. Apply the powder daily. Watch for any signs of trench foot: maceration, numbness, discoloration. If your feet start going numb, you need to dry them and keep them dry for at least 12 hours, even if it means losing travel time.

Bring more ibuprofen than you think you'll need. The constant balance work and altitude will leave you sore in unexpected places. Ibuprofen also helps with altitude headaches.

Pack treatment for cuts and scrapes—the wet environment promotes infection. Antibiotic ointment, adhesive bandages that are actually waterproof (most aren't), gauze, medical tape. Clean and treat any wound immediately.

Altitude sickness is a concern. Know the symptoms: headache, nausea, dizziness, fatigue. If they develop, descend. Don't try to push through. Bring acetaminophen or ginkgo biloba as preventatives if you're prone to altitude issues.

Consider bringing earplugs, but the good kind for musicians that reduce volume while preserving sound fidelity. You still need to hear warnings, but reducing the constant noise by 15-20 decibels might preserve your sanity. Test them beforehand—some people find earplugs in this environment more disturbing than the noise itself, creating a muffled disorientation worse than the din.

## Psychological Preparation

Understand that you will be *alone* in a way modern humans rarely experience. The structures suggest people lived here once, but you will likely see no one. The animals are sparse. You will be alone with stone, water, green, and sound for the duration.

Some people find this transcendent. Others find it annihilating. Know yourself. If you've never spent significant time in true solitude, this is not the place to discover you can't handle it.

The sound-and-mist combination creates a sensory deprivation/overload paradox. You're bombarded with noise but can't see. This can produce hallucinations—auditory first, sometimes visual. You might hear voices in the wind-tones, see figures in the mist. These are normal. They're not real. Practice grounding techniques now: focus on breath, touch something textured, name five things you can see even if it's just variations in the mist density.

Bring something for morale. A small book, waterproofed. A musical instrument if you play—a harmonica, a wooden flute. Something to create beauty that's yours, not the mountain's. You'll need to assert your own humanity against the overwhelming inhuman patience of the place.

## Duration and Exit Strategy

Don't plan a trip longer than you've trained for. This place is exhausting in ways that aren't obvious from the description. The constant damp, noise, and vigilance drain you psychologically as much as the altitude and terrain drain you physically.

For a first visit, one to two weeks maximum. You need to test your tolerance. Some people could live here for months and find it restorative. Others will be desperate to leave after three days. There's no shame in the latter—it's data about who you are.

Plan your exit before you enter. Know your egress routes. The place is vast, the mist conceals distance, and in extremis you need to be able to navigate out by compass bearing and dead reckoning. Mark your maps with bail-out routes that don't depend on good weather or visibility.

## Special Considerations: The Strangeness

The description notes subtle wrongness: moss that orients to unknown logic, pools whose levels don't match precipitation, sounds that approach meaning, flowers that shouldn't exist. Treat these as weather—interesting, potentially dangerous, not to be directly engaged.

Don't drink from pools that seem wrong. Don't sleep near the geometric flowers. If the mountain-song starts sounding like language, recognize that you're pattern-matching, that human brains find patterns in randomness, that this is psychology not communication. Write it down in your notebook if you want, but don't follow it, don't try to decode it, don't let it become important.

The place has been what it is for longer than human civilization has existed. It will not harm you through malice because it doesn't notice you enough for malice. But it's not a garden in the human sense, not tended for human safety. Respect the strangeness. Don't poke it. Move through it like you move through the weather—aware, responsive, but not assuming it has plans for you.

## What to Leave Behind

Your phone is useless and the moisture will kill it. Leave it. Bring a camera if you want images, but a simple waterproof one. This isn't an Instagram location—the beauty is too vast and too subtle to capture, and trying will only frustrate you.

Leave your expectations of comfort. You will be damp. You will be tired. You will be sore. You will smell. Your sleep will be broken. Accept these as the price of admission.

Leave your need for productivity. There is nothing to achieve here except presence, except survival, except witnessing. If you need goals and measurable outcomes, this place will feel like failure. It offers only itself, which is everything, but can feel like nothing to minds trained in accomplishment.

## Final Thoughts

The Thundering Gardens of Koreth are not hostile, but they are profoundly indifferent. They will kill you through that indifference if you're unprepared or unlucky. A fall on wet stone, an injury that prevents movement, a psychological break that leads to poor decisions—the place won't save you. It has been singing its song since before your species stood upright. It will sing after your bones have become stone.

But if you can meet the place on its terms—prepared, physically capable, psychologically resilient, respectful—it offers something increasingly rare: a landscape that is fully itself, untamed not because it's been preserved but because it was never tameable, because human presence here was always negotiation never conquest. You'll return changed, if you return. The sound will stay in your ears for weeks. You'll startle at silence. The memory of mist will hover at the edge of vision.

For some people, this is a pilgrimage. For others, an ordeal survived. Know which you're seeking before you go. Then prepare as if your life depends on it, because it does.

The mountains are patient. They've forgotten the shape they were born to, but they remember how to wait. They'll be there when you're ready, singing, dripping, growing, wearing themselves away grain by grain, the same today as ten thousand years ago, the same ten thousand years hence. Approach with humility. Move with care. Listen to the song. Come back.
