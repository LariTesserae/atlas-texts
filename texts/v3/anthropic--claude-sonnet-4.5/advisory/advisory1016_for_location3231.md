# Preparation Guide for the Sinking Plain

## Understanding What You're Visiting

Before practical preparations, understand this: you are visiting a place that will challenge your relationship with stimulation, change, and purpose. The Sinking Plain is not dangerous in conventional ways—there are no predators hunting humans, no extreme temperatures, no acute hazards—but it is profoundly, relentlessly itself. The psychological preparation is as important as the physical.

## Physical Preparations

### Clothing Strategy

The constant warmth (28-30°C) combined with persistent moisture creates a unique challenge. You need clothing that:

- **Breathes exceptionally well** - Synthetic moisture-wicking fabrics or very lightweight merino wool. Cotton will stay damp and become uncomfortable within hours.
- **Dries quickly** - Everything will become damp from the humidity, your sweat, sitting on wet stone, and the occasional drizzle.
- **Covers your skin** - The sun is consistent and clear. Prolonged exposure without protection will burn you, and there's no shade anywhere. Long, loose sleeves and full-length pants in light colors.
- **Can be layered minimally** - You'll want the option to strip down, but complete nakedness is impractical (sun exposure, insects landing on skin, rough stone).

Bring multiple sets of the same outfit. The dampness means nothing fully dries; you'll want to rotate. Plan on 4-5 complete changes for a week-long visit.

### Footwear: Your Most Critical Decision

Your feet will determine your experience. The terrain alternates between:
- Smooth, damp bedrock (slick, especially in morning condensation)
- Damp sediment (yielding, cool, but requires more effort to walk through)
- Shallow pools (body-temperature water, sometimes unavoidable)
- Rough stone exposures (will abrade soft soles)

You need:
- **Amphibious hiking shoes or sport sandals with excellent grip** - Something like Chacos, Tevas, or Keens with aggressive tread. They must be comfortable when wet and secure enough for extended walking.
- **Backup pair** - Foot problems will end your visit. Redundancy is essential.
- **Thin merino wool socks if you prefer covered feet** - They'll stay damp but won't chafe like wet cotton. Many visitors prefer sandals without socks once acclimated.

Break in your footwear extensively beforehand. Walk in them wet. Walk in them for 8-hour days. Any hot spots at home will become incapacitating injuries on the plain.

### Head Protection

The sun is relentless and there is no escape from it during daylight hours.

- **Wide-brimmed hat with chin strap** - The wind is constant. Baseball caps will blow off. The brim should shade your neck, ears, and face.
- **Lightweight buff or neck gaiter** - For additional neck protection and to cover your face when the light is most intense.

### Eye Protection

- **High-quality polarized sunglasses with retention strap** - The reflection off wet stone and still pools is considerable. Losing your sunglasses to wind would be miserable.
- **Backup pair** - Non-negotiable.

## Sun Protection

The clear atmosphere and reflective surfaces create intense UV exposure.

- **Reef-safe, water-resistant SPF 50+ sunscreen** - The water here is ancient and pristine. Use products that won't damage it. Reapply every 2-3 hours despite the water-resistance claims.
- **Zinc-based face stick** - For nose, ears, lips, anywhere that gets perpendicular sun exposure.
- **Lip balm with SPF** - Your lips will dry from the constant wind despite the humidity.

Apply sunscreen before you think you need it and more than you think necessary. The consistent moderate warmth disguises how much UV you're receiving.

## Hydration System

This is complex because while water is everywhere, drinking from the pools requires consideration.

### What to Bring:

- **3-liter hydration reservoir** - Bladder-style is preferable to bottles; you want to drink continuously in the warmth without thinking about it.
- **Backup 2-liter bottle or bladder** - Redundancy for water is non-negotiable.
- **High-quality portable water filter** - Something like a Sawyer Squeeze, LifeStraw, or Katadyn BeFree. The pool water is warm, alkaline, and mineral-rich but safe once filtered.
- **Electrolyte supplements** - The constant mild sweating depletes salts. Bring packets or tablets for at least 4 liters per day.
- **Water testing strips** - To check pH and general quality if you're concerned about specific pools.

### Water Strategy:

You'll sweat constantly in the warmth, though not dramatically. Count on drinking 4-6 liters per day. The pools are your water sources—choose ones that appear to have circulation (where water seeps in/out) rather than completely isolated pools. The water tastes mineral and slightly alkaline; this is normal. Filter everything. The warm temperature means bacteria can proliferate, and while local species are adapted, your gut flora are not.

Let filtered water cool in your reservoir before drinking if possible—body-temperature water is psychologically unsatisfying even though it hydrates the same.

## Nutrition

You'll need to carry all food. There's nothing to forage unless you're an expert on the specific sedge species (and you're not).

### Food Characteristics Needed:

- **High calorie-to-weight ratio** - You're carrying everything.
- **Tolerant of constant warmth** - Nothing will stay cool. Avoid chocolate, anything that melts, anything requiring refrigeration.
- **Not moisture-sensitive** - Everything will absorb humidity. Avoid crackers, chips, anything that goes soggy.
- **Low cooking requirements** - Covered below, but assume limited or no cooking.

### Suggested Foods:

- **Nuts and nut butters** - Stable, calorie-dense, don't mind warmth.
- **Dried fruits** - Will get slightly tacky in humidity but remain edible.
- **Foil-packed fish or chicken** - Stable at room temperature, good protein.
- **Vacuum-sealed hard cheeses** - Aged cheeses survive warmth better than fresh.
- **Energy bars designed for heat** - Check specifications; some are specifically formulated not to melt.
- **Instant rice or couscous** - If you can heat water, these cook in minutes.
- **Honey packets** - Stable, energy-dense, never spoils.
- **Jerky** - Traditional and reliable.
- **Electrolyte drink mixes** - Both for hydration and some calories.

Pack food in waterproof bags within your pack. The humidity will infiltrate everything given time.

## Cooking System (Optional but Recommended)

While you can eat entirely cold food, having hot meals improves morale significantly in an environment that offers so little sensory variation.

- **Small canister stove** - MSR PocketRocket or similar. Compact, reliable, quick.
- **Fuel canisters** - More than you think you need; the constant warmth means you won't need much fuel for cooking but you'll want hot drinks.
- **Single pot with lid** - 700-1000ml capacity, titanium or aluminum.
- **Insulated mug** - Hot coffee or tea becomes a meditative ritual here.
- **Long-handled spoon/spork** - Doubles as stirrer and eating utensil.
- **Lighter and backup matches** - The humidity makes fire-starting tricky.

The wind is constant—bring or improvise a windscreen for your stove. Cooking on warm stone surrounded by warm wind requires protecting the flame.

## Shelter

This requires deep consideration of your goals.

### Minimalist Approach (Recommended for Immersion):

- **Sleeping pad** - Closed-cell foam is bullet-proof but less comfortable; inflatable is more comfortable but can puncture on rough stone. Bring patch kit if inflatable.
- **Lightweight bivy sack or emergency shelter** - For the rare rain, not for warmth. You don't need insulation.
- **Groundsheet** - To protect your sleeping pad from abrasion and damp.

Sleep directly under the stars on the warm stone. This is uncomfortable for the first night but profoundly connects you to the place. The stone releases heat through the night. You'll hear every sound across the plain. You'll see the stars without impediment.

### Comfort Approach:

- **Ultralight tarp or single-wall tent** - Minimal structure. You don't need bug protection (the midges don't bite) or warmth. Rain protection only.
- **Sleeping pad** - As above.
- **Sleeping bag or quilt rated to 15°C** - More for psychological comfort than necessity. Many visitors end up sleeping on top of their bags.

The tent/tarp reduces your sensory engagement with the environment. Consider whether that's what you want.

## Pack

- **40-50 liter capacity** - Adequate for a week if you pack efficiently.
- **High-quality hipbelt and suspension** - You'll be walking constantly on the smooth stone and yielding sediment. Poor weight distribution causes pain quickly.
- **Rain cover** - For the occasional rain and to keep dust/moisture from infiltrating.
- **Pack should be light-colored** - Dark packs absorb more heat, making the contents warmer.

## Navigation

This is psychologically important even though getting lost is nearly impossible.

- **Detailed map of the plain** - If available. Mark pool locations, any slight rises, distances.
- **Compass** - The flatness is disorienting. The similarity of the landscape in all directions makes it easy to lose directional sense.
- **GPS device or phone with offline maps** - Redundancy. The horizon offers few landmarks.
- **Notebook and pencil** - For noting positions, sketching, recording observations. The pencil won't fail in humidity like pens.

The difficulty isn't getting lost—it's knowing where you are in a landscape that repeats without variation. You'll want to navigate deliberately to pools, to areas with different vegetation, to the slight rises.

## Personal Items

### Hygiene:

- **Biodegradable soap** - Small amount. You'll wash in the pools; use products that won't contaminate them.
- **Toothbrush and toothpaste** - Spit well away from pools.
- **Toilet paper and trowel** - Cat-hole burial is standard. Walk far from pools and water seepage areas.
- **Menstrual products if needed** - Plan for disposal; pack out all waste.
- **Quick-dry towel** - Small, for drying after pool bathing.

The warmth and constant movement mean you'll sweat steadily. You'll feel dirty. Embrace bathing in the warm pools—it becomes a daily ritual.

### Medical:

- **Comprehensive first aid kit** including:
  - **Blister treatment** - Most common injury. Moleskin, Leukotape, antibiotic ointment.
  - **Wound care supplies** - Abrasions from rough stone are common.
  - **Antibiotic ointment** - The warmth and moisture encourage infection.
  - **Anti-diarrheal medication** - In case the water disagrees with you despite filtering.
  - **Pain relievers** - Ibuprofen for inflammation from constant walking.
  - **Antihistamines** - For any allergic reactions to unfamiliar plants/insects.
  - **Tweezers and needle** - For splinters, thorns from succulents.
  - **Medical tape and gauze** - Secure bandages that will stay on in humidity.
- **Personal medications** - Bring extras. Plan for being delayed.
- **Foot care supplies specifically** - This deserves its own category. Extra blister treatment, foot powder, antifungal cream.

### Protection:

- **Sunburn treatment** - Aloe gel, despite your best prevention efforts.
- **Insect repellent** - The midges don't bite, but it's wise to have this for unidentified species. DEET or picaridin.

### Documentation:

- **Camera** - In waterproof case or bag. The constant humidity and occasional rain will destroy unprotected electronics.
- **Waterproof notebook** - Rite in the Rain or similar. Regular paper becomes pulp.
- **Pencils, not pens** - Multiple pencils. Pens fail in humidity.
- **Binoculars** - For watching distant birds, examining details across the flat expanse. 8x or 10x magnification.

The plain is visually subtle. Binoculars reveal details—a bird at a pool edge two miles away, the texture of vegetation, the slight rises that break the horizon.

## Communication and Emergency

- **Satellite communication device** - inReach, SPOT, or satellite phone. Cell service is unlikely.
- **Emergency beacon (PLB)** - Separate from communication device. Redundancy for catastrophic situations.
- **Whistle** - Sound carries extraordinarily well across the flat plain.
- **Signal mirror** - For aerial rescue if needed.

Understand that rescue will take time. The flat openness makes you visible but the remoteness means help is distant. You must be self-sufficient.

## Psychological Preparations

This is as important as physical gear.

### What to Expect Mentally:

**The Monotony**: The plain is beautiful but it is relentlessly similar to itself. Every hour looks like the previous hour. Every direction looks like every other direction. This is not a place of dramatic reveals or changing vistas. Some people find this meditative; others find it oppressive.

**The Slowness**: Nothing happens quickly. Birds move slowly. Plants don't visibly move at all. The light changes at a glacial pace from dawn to dusk. Your own walking is slow—the yielding sediment, the careful foot placement on damp stone, the heat. If you need constant stimulation, this place will break you.

**The Sounds**: The rustling grass, the bird calls, the insect hum—they never stop. This constant audio layer is either soothing or maddening. You can't escape it. Consider whether you're someone who tolerates repetitive sounds or someone driven to distraction by them.

**The Aloneness**: Even if you visit with companions, the vast flatness creates isolation. You can see someone a mile away and they seem close but shouting won't reach them. The scale collapses and expands simultaneously. This does things to your sense of social connection.

**The Purpose Problem**: In mountains, you climb peaks. In forests, you explore trails. On the plain, there are no goals the landscape provides. You must bring your own purpose—observation, contemplation, documentation, simply experiencing. If you need external structure, create it beforehand.

### Mental Preparation Strategies:

**Meditation practice** - If you don't meditate, learn before visiting. The plain is essentially a meditation environment. Fighting that is exhausting.

**Journaling** - Bring prompts or questions to explore. "What did I notice today that I didn't notice yesterday?" The changes are so subtle that deliberate observation is required.

**Photography or sketching projects** - Give yourself assignments. Document every bird species. Photograph the same pool at different times of day. Draw the grass clumps. Having creative structure helps.

**Accept boredom** - Practice being bored before you visit. Sit in an empty room for an hour. Walk the same path repeatedly. The plain will bore you at times. That's part of what it offers.

**Prepare for profound relaxation** - The flip side of boredom is that your nervous system will downregulate in ways it may never have before. No threats, no urgency, no stimulation—just warmth, rustling, bird calls. For urban people, this can be intensely therapeutic or intensely unsettling.

## Duration Considerations

**Minimum visit: 3-4 days** - The first day you're adjusting. The second day you're still processing. The third day you start actually experiencing the place.

**Optimal visit: 7-10 days** - Long enough to settle into the rhythm, to stop expecting change, to notice the subtle variations.

**Extended visit: 2+ weeks** - You'll start perceiving changes invisible to shorter visits. A pool shrinking by an inch. A grass clump's different response to morning versus evening wind. This is when the plain reveals itself fully.

Be honest about your tolerance. A miserable week is worse than a satisfied four days.

## Physical Conditioning

**Walking endurance** - You should be comfortable walking 8-12 miles per day on varied terrain before arriving. The distances feel longer here because of the yielding sediment and the psychological effect of unchanging landscape.

**Heat tolerance** - If possible, spend time in 28-30°C environments before visiting. Sauna time, hot yoga, anything that acclimates you to sustained warmth.

**Foot conditioning** - Walk barefoot or in minimal sandals to toughen feet. The constant damp warmth softens skin, making blisters more likely.

## What Not to Bring

- **Electronics for entertainment** - Kindle, tablet, phone games. These violate the spirit of the place and their batteries will die quickly anyway. If you need these, reconsider visiting.
- **Heavy camera equipment** - A light system only. Carrying a DSLR with multiple lenses across the plain is misery.
- **Excessive clothing** - You don't need fashion options. Functional duplicates only.
- **Cosmetics** - Will melt, will feel gross in humidity, serve no purpose.
- **Cotton anything** - Stays damp, chafes, dries slowly, becomes heavy.
- **Books** - The humidity destroys them. If you must have reading material, digital in waterproof case or waterproof paper only.
- **Jewelry** - Will tarnish from the mineral water, gets in the way, serves no purpose.

## Pre-Visit Research

**Bird identification** - Study wading birds, pipits, ibis-like species. Knowing what you're seeing enriches the experience immensely.

**Geology basics** - Understanding metamorphic rock, erosion processes, water tables. The plain makes more sense with context.

**Meditation techniques** - Particularly open awareness practices. You'll use them.

**Photography in flat light** - The even illumination is challenging. Learn techniques beforehand.

## Entry and Exit Logistics

Understand the practical realities:

**Access** - How do you reach the plain? Is there vehicle access to the edge? A drop-off point? This determines how much gear is practical.

**Waste management** - Plan to pack out ALL waste. Every food wrapper, every piece of toilet paper, everything. The plain is pristine; maintain that.

**Emergency extraction points** - Know where help would come from. How long would rescue take? What's the nearest medical facility?

## Weather Timing

While the plain is remarkably consistent, there are subtleties:

**Dry season** - Pools are slightly lower, vegetation is at its minimum, temperatures may be fractionally higher. Easier walking on the sediment (less wet), but water sources require more attention.

**Wet season** - More frequent drizzle, pools are fuller and more connected, sediment is more yielding (harder walking), slight temperature decrease. More bird activity.

**Shoulder periods** - Transitions between wet and dry can offer the best balance.

Research the specific timing if possible, but know that unlike temperate zones, the seasonal differences are subtle.

## Cultural and Ethical Considerations

**Leave No Trace principles** - Sacred here. The plain has arrived at equilibrium over incomprehensible time. Your visit should leave it unchanged.

**Sound discipline** - Your voice carries for miles. Shouting disturbs the birds and ruptures the sonic environment. Speak quietly or not at all.

**Pool etiquette** - If bathing in pools (which you will), do it thoughtfully. No soap directly in water (soap up away, rinse in pool). Don't disturb sediment unnecessarily. The aquatic life is delicate.

**Vegetation respect** - Don't step on the moss/lichen mats—they're a century old. Walk around grass clumps when possible. The succulents are ancient; touching them is touching a 500-year-old organism.

**Bird behavior** - Don't approach nesting areas. Don't flush birds from pools repeatedly. Observe from distance.

## The First 24 Hours

**Expect disorientation** - The flatness, the constant sound, the unchanging warmth—your system won't know what to do with it initially.

**Expect physical discomfort** - Your feet will hurt from the different terrain. You'll sweat in unfamiliar patterns. The damp warmth will feel strange.

**Expect emotional response** - Some people feel immediate peace. Some feel claustrophobic despite the openness. Some feel profound loneliness. All are normal.

**Move slowly** - Don't try to cover distance the first day. Set up your sleeping area, explore nearby, acclimate. Speed is irrelevant here.

**Observe systematically** - Count bird species. Examine a pool closely. Study a grass clump. Active observation gives your mind something to do while adjusting.

## Daily Rhythm

Develop a routine quickly:

**Dawn** - The stone sweating, the morning light, the temperature at its lowest (barely). This is magical and worth being awake for.

**Midday** - The light at its brightest, the warmth at peak, the bird activity often lower. Time for slow walking, sitting by pools, long observations.

**Dusk** - Different bird species emerge, the larger waders arrive, the light softens fractionally. Another peak experience time.

**Night** - The stars, the sounds intensified, the darkness complete. Lie on warm stone and listen.

**Routine is psychologically anchoring** - When the environment provides no variation, your internal structure matters more.

## Re-Entry Preparation

After days or weeks on the plain, returning to normal environments is jarring:

**Sensory overwhelming** - The visual complexity, the noise variation, the temperature changes—all will feel intense.

**Speed discomfort** - Everything moves too fast. Cars, people, conversations, expectations.

**Grief** - Many people experience genuine grief leaving the plain. Its simplicity and completeness are profound. Expect to miss it.

**Integration time** - Give yourself days after returning to readjust. Don't schedule important commitments immediately.

## Final Considerations

**This place is not for everyone** - And that's okay. Some personalities thrive in stimulation-rich environments. Some need dramatic landscapes. Some can't tolerate boredom. If you're uncertain whether the plain is for you, that uncertainty is data.

**This place changes people** - Those who resonate with it find it profoundly affecting. The deep time, the completeness, the slowed pace—these reframe perspectives on change, on achievement, on the pace of modern life. If you're not ready for that reframing, consider whether now is the right time to visit.

**Trust your preparation** - With proper gear, physical conditioning, and psychological readiness, the plain is safe. It's not dangerous—it's challenging in subtle ways. The challenges are manageable with preparation.

**The plain requires surrender** - You can't conquer it, summit it, complete it. You can only experience it on its terms. Fighting those terms is exhausting and pointless. Accepting them is the entire point of visiting.

---

Pack carefully. Train your body. Prepare your mind. Bring water filtration and sun protection and good footwear. Bring less than you think you need. Bring a willingness to be bored. Bring a capacity for observation. Bring a tolerance for warmth and dampness and endless rustling.

Leave behind your need for stimulation, for accomplishment, for variety, for comfort.

The plain is waiting. It has been waiting longer than mountains have existed. It will continue waiting after you leave.

Go slowly. Notice everything. Let the warmth seep into you. Listen to the rustling and the calls. Watch the birds at the pool edges. Drink the warm, mineral water. Sleep on ancient stone.

You will return different, even if you can't articulate how.

That's what the plain offers: a completeness that makes you aware of your own incompleteness, and the time to begin addressing it.
