# Preparation Guide for the Incipient Plateau

## Physical Preparation

### Altitude Acclimation
You're going to a place where breath comes thin. Start preparing at least 6-8 weeks before departure:

- If possible, spend time at progressively higher elevations (2,000m, then 3,000m, then 3,500m+)
- Your body needs to increase red blood cell production; this cannot be rushed
- Expect headaches, fatigue, and nausea for your first week regardless of preparation
- Consider consulting a doctor about acetazolamide (Diamox) for altitude sickness prevention
- Learn to recognize HAPE (High Altitude Pulmonary Edema) and HACE (High Altitude Cerebral Edema) symptoms—these can kill

The air there isn't just thin; it's *wet*. Your lungs will be working harder while also processing constant humidity. Practice breathing exercises. Learn to take deliberate, slower breaths. Your body will want to hyperventilate; train it not to.

### Physical Conditioning
Build endurance rather than strength:
- Walking stamina—you'll be on your feet constantly on uneven, slippery surfaces
- Core stability for navigating uncertain terrain where distances and paths change
- Balance exercises; the stone is perpetually damp
- Cold tolerance—practice being productively uncomfortable in cool, humid conditions

Understand that you'll be weaker there. Everything takes more energy at altitude. Accept this.

## Equipment and Clothing

### Footwear (Most Critical)
Invest heavily here:
- High-quality waterproof boots with aggressive tread—not hiking boots, more like mountaineering boots
- Consider cleated soles or bring microspikes
- Break them in completely before arrival; blisters at altitude heal slowly
- Bring backup boots; you'll be walking through water channels
- Wool socks, multiple pairs, and sock liners to prevent blisters

### Layering System
The temperature "hovers just above cold" in constant dampness:

**Base layer:** Merino wool or synthetic moisture-wicking—never cotton, which will stay wet and leach your warmth

**Mid layer:** Wool sweaters, fleece, or down that can function while damp

**Outer layer:** Waterproof but breathable—you need something that handles the horizontal fog without becoming a sauna

**Specific items:**
- Oilskin or waxed canvas coat (traditional materials work better there than modern technical fabrics—the place has an industrial logic)
- Wool cap that covers ears
- Gloves that function when wet—neoprene or treated leather
- Neck gaiter or scarf
- Multiple pairs of wool pants or heavy canvas work pants

### Practical Accessories
- Headlamp with extra batteries (nights are dim, not dark, but you'll need light for interior spaces)
- Waterproof notebook and pencils (not pens—ink runs in the damp)
- Small personal chronometer/watch, but understand it will become unreliable
- Compass—though navigation is more about landmarks than direction
- Water bottle (the water is drinkable but mineral-heavy; you may want to carry your own)
- Basic first aid kit with blister treatment, pain relievers, altitude sickness medication
- Sunglasses and sunscreen (the dim light is deceptive; UV at altitude is intense)
- Lip balm—your lips will crack in the thin, damp air

## Mental and Philosophical Preparation

### Uncertainty Tolerance
This is the hardest part, and you cannot fake it:

The Plateau will violate your expectations of how reality works. Not constantly, not dramatically, but persistently. A hallway will be longer than yesterday. Time will feel elastic. You'll hear things that have no source. The question is not whether you can tolerate this intellectually (most people can), but whether you can *function* while it's happening.

Practice exercises:
- Deliberately break your routines; take different routes, eat at random times, rearrange your living space frequently
- Work on tasks where the rules change mid-process
- Meditate on impermanence—not as philosophy but as lived experience
- Spend time in liminal spaces: airports, train stations, parking garages at odd hours
- Learn to complete tasks without knowing if you're making progress

If you need predictability to feel safe, don't go. If uncertainty makes you angry rather than curious, don't go. This place will break people who need the world to make consistent sense.

### Observational Skills
Everything there matters, but you won't know what matters until later:
- Practice detailed observation and documentation
- Learn to sketch or photograph systematically
- Develop memory techniques for recall of spatial relationships
- Study pattern recognition, but also learn to recognize when patterns are illusions

The residents maintain extensive archives because the anomalies only make sense in aggregate, sometimes years later. Train yourself to notice and record without needing immediate understanding.

### Time Perception
Begin loosening your relationship with clock time now:
- Use timers less; estimate durations
- Practice judging time by body rhythms, light levels, hunger
- Let go of rigid scheduling
- Understand that "being on time" becomes meaningless when time itself is negotiable

But don't abandon temporal awareness entirely—you'll need to mesh with the industrial schedules and shift work.

## Practical Skills to Develop

### Basic Geology and Hydrology
You don't need a degree, but understanding helps:
- How travertine and tufa form
- Basic water flow dynamics
- Recognition of different rock types
- Understanding of pH and mineral content in water

This knowledge lets you read the environment, predict which areas are actively growing, understand why the engineers make certain decisions.

### Basic Industrial Literacy
The place runs on hybrid technology:
- Steam engine basics (how they work, their sounds, their dangers)
- Elementary electrical knowledge
- Basic plumbing and water management
- Understanding of geothermal systems

You'll be living inside a functioning industrial process. Knowing how not to disrupt it (or kill yourself) is essential.

### Salamander Protocol
Learn this before arrival:
- Never disturb their constructions (cairns, stick patterns, stone arrangements)
- Don't block their access to channels
- If one approaches you, stay still and let it investigate
- Their slime is harmless but will harden—scrape it off before it sets
- Watch where they congregate; these are significant hydrological points
- If you see unusual behavior (massing, vocalizing—they're usually silent), alert an engineer

Think of them as sacred animals, even if you don't believe in the sacred. The residents do, in their pragmatic way.

## Health and Safety

### Medical Preparation
Consult a physician about:
- Altitude sickness prophylaxis
- Respiratory health (the damp, mineral-laden air can irritate lungs)
- Immunizations (standard, but be current)
- Any chronic conditions and how they'll respond to altitude and humidity

Bring:
- Any prescription medications, doubled, in waterproof containers
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics (wounds heal slowly at altitude)
- Antifungal treatments (the constant damp encourages fungal growth)
- Blister treatment supplies
- Pain relievers
- Anti-nausea medication

### Water and Food
The water builds the city—it's safe to drink but extremely mineral-rich:
- Your body will need time to adjust; expect digestive oddness initially
- The taste is chalky, metallic
- Consider bringing water flavoring
- Tea is the primary beverage there; the tannins help

Food is simple, hearty, high-calorie:
- Primarily mutton, barley, root vegetables, preserved goods
- Fresh produce is rare (short growing season, high altitude)
- If you have dietary restrictions, bring supplemental foods
- Vitamin supplements might be wise

### Psychological Hygiene
Isolation and weirdness compound:
- Have a routine you can maintain even when the environment changes
- Find anchors—objects, habits, relationships
- Keep a journal (this also serves the documentary function)
- Allow yourself to feel unsettled without acting on it
- Build relationships slowly; the community is small and needs to trust you

Some people experience depression at altitude. Some people experience euphoria at the anomalies, then crash when the novelty fades. Monitor your mental state.

## Cultural Preparation

### Social Dynamics
The community is small, self-selected, and functional under strange conditions:
- Competence is valued over credentials
- Contribution matters more than status
- Privacy is respected but not emphasized—living quarters are often shared
- Decision-making is largely consensus-based among the engineers and long-term residents
- Newcomers are watched (not suspiciously, but carefully—will you adapt or become a problem?)

### Work Expectations
You'll be expected to contribute:
- Most newcomers start with basic maintenance: channel clearing, moss harvesting, material moving
- Observe shift schedules (marked by steam whistles)
- Learn from the experienced workers—the knowledge is practical and specific
- Don't innovate until you understand why things are done the way they are (usually good reasons)

### Documentation Culture
Everyone maintains records:
- Your observations have value to the community
- Anomalies should be reported to the archives
- Sketches, measurements, descriptions—all matter
- This isn't bureaucracy; it's survival and understanding

### Relationship with the Place
The residents have a specific attitude toward the Plateau:
- Respect without anthropomorphizing
- Curiosity without entitlement
- Adaptation without surrender
- They work with the place, not against it or for it

Adopt this stance. The Plateau isn't hostile, but it's not accommodating either. It's doing something, and you're living inside that process.

## Travel Logistics

### Getting There
The description mentions "impossible roads from the forests two thousand meters below":
- Expect the journey to be difficult and possibly dangerous
- It will likely take days
- Vehicle transport may only go partway
- Pack animals or human porters for the final approach
- Weather-dependent—you may need to wait for clear conditions
- The path itself may be one of the uncertain spaces

### Arrival Protocol
When you first arrive:
- Report to whoever seems to be coordinating new arrivals (likely an engineer or administrator)
- You'll probably be assigned temporary quarters
- Expect an orientation, possibly informal
- Don't wander alone initially—the spatial inconsistencies are dangerous if you don't know the safe routes
- Ask questions, but understand that many answers will be "it depends" or "usually but not always"

### Initial Adjustment Period
Plan for your first two weeks to be rough:
- Altitude sickness peaks around days 2-3
- Spatial disorientation takes time to process
- The temporal inconsistencies are deeply unsettling at first
- You'll be exhausted
- Your thinking will be foggy
- This is normal

Don't make major decisions during this period. Don't quit. Don't commit to long-term plans. Just acclimate.

## Long-term Considerations

### If You're Staying Extended Period
Consider:
- Shipping additional supplies if possible
- Building relationships deliberately—this is your community now
- Learning a specialized skill (the place values expertise)
- Finding your role in the documentary/archive work
- Developing a personal project or study
- Understanding that you'll change there; plan for who you'll be, not who you are now

### Exit Strategy
Have a plan for leaving:
- Maintain connection with the outside world (if communication exists—this isn't specified)
- Keep enough resources for departure
- Understand that the journey out may be as difficult as the journey in
- Recognize when you need to leave (usually before you're desperate to)

Some people stay forever. Some stay too long. Know yourself.

## Specific Warnings

### Don't Do These Things
- Don't touch the salamander constructions
- Don't redirect water flow without consulting an engineer
- Don't enter buildings marked as unstable or active growth zones
- Don't trust that a path will be the same twice
- Don't panic when anomalies occur (panic makes bad decisions)
- Don't try to "fix" the weirdness
- Don't assume causality works in straightforward ways
- Don't go into the deep channels (the ones large enough to enter—you could become embedded)

### Potentially Dangerous Situations
- Rapidly growing architecture can trap you
- Water chemistry changes can be toxic
- Spatial inconsistencies can cause falls or disorientation
- Temporal anomalies might separate you from your group
- The thin air makes all injuries more serious
- Hypothermia from the constant damp
- Altitude sickness progressing to HAPE/HACE
- Getting lost when paths change

### Emergency Responses
- If trapped by growing stone: stay calm, call for help, mark your location, keep moving body parts to prevent embedding
- If spatially lost: stop moving, mark your location, wait for consistency to return or for search party
- If temporally displaced: document your experience, look for landmarks, wait for realignment
- For medical emergencies: seek the engineers (they have practical first aid experience) and get to lower altitude if possible

## Philosophical Grounding

### Why This Matters
Understand what you're really preparing for:

The Incipient Plateau is not a tourist destination. It's not an adventure or an experience to collect. It's a place where reality is actively negotiating with impossibility, where a community maintains human life and industry inside that negotiation.

You're preparing to be part of something that doesn't make sense, that may never make sense, but that continues anyway. The preparation isn't about mastering the environment—you can't. It's about becoming someone who can function, contribute, and remain human while certainty dissolves around you.

### The Core Question
Before you go, answer this honestly:

Can you work steadily, live cooperatively, and maintain your humanity in a place where the rules change, where time is elastic, where salamanders might be building something you'll never understand, where your hallway might be longer tomorrow, where the stones themselves are alive with slow intention?

If the answer is "I think so" or "I want to find out," then prepare thoroughly and go carefully.

If the answer is "I need to know what's happening and why," don't go. The Plateau doesn't offer those answers.

## Final Thoughts

The Incipient Plateau is becoming something. The city is growing. The water flows with purpose. The salamanders build their incomprehensible cairns. The residents work and document and wonder.

You're preparing to enter a place that is, in the most literal sense, under construction—not just physically but ontologically. Reality itself is still being precipitated there, one mineral-rich layer at a time.

Prepare your body for altitude and cold and constant damp.

Prepare your skills for industrial work and careful observation.

Prepare your mind for uncertainty and strangeness and wonder.

But most of all, prepare your spirit for patience. Everything there moves at geological pace—except when it doesn't. You'll need to match that rhythm: steady work, slow adaptation, persistent curiosity, and the willingness to be part of something whose completion you'll never see.

The residents there have chosen to live inside a question that may never be answered. If you go, you're making the same choice.

Pack accordingly.
