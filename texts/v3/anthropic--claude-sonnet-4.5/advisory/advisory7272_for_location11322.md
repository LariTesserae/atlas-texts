# Preparation Guide for The Hollows of Soft Stone

## Physical Preparation

**Footwear Considerations**
You'll need shoes with excellent grip on smooth, uneven surfaces. The limestone, while not slippery when dry, presents constant micro-variations in height and texture that will fatigue your ankles and test your balance. Approach boots are ideal—rubber soles with good edge contact, ankle support without rigidity. Break them in thoroughly beforehand; you'll be making thousands of small adjustments with each step. The stone won't injure you dramatically, but a turned ankle in such profound silence creates its own particular misery.

Bring a second pair of lighter shoes or sandals. When you inevitably want to step into one of the water-filled basins—and you will, the temptation is intrinsic to the place—you'll want something that can get wet without consequence. The amber-brown water stains, not dramatically but persistently. It will mark whatever enters it with a faint memory of tannins and dissolved limestone.

**Clothing Strategy**
The warmth is constant but the dampness is insidious. Cotton becomes your enemy here—it will absorb the ambient moisture and cling with increasing discomfort. Choose merino wool or synthetic fabrics that wick and breathe. Long sleeves protect from sun that, even filtered through overcast, accumulates its effects over hours of exposure.

Bring layers you can add and remove, though you may find you never do. The temperature stability is such that what's comfortable at arrival remains comfortable throughout, but human psychology demands the security of options. A light wind layer for those moments when air moves across the stone field, though you may wear it more for the illusion of shelter than actual protection.

Your clothes will become damp. Accept this. The moisture won't soak you, but you'll feel it settling, weight accumulating in fabric. This isn't unpleasant once you stop resisting it. The dampness becomes insulation, a second skin that mediates between your warmth and the stone's coolness.

## Temporal Preparation

**Duration Planning**
Don't plan to stay less than four hours. The place reveals itself through duration, not intensity. Arriving, observing, and leaving teaches you nothing. The hollows require you to exhaust your initial impressions, to become bored, and then to continue until boredom transforms into a different kind of attention.

Two days is the threshold where something shifts. The first day you see the place. The second day it begins to see you—or more accurately, you begin to feel yourself as the place might perceive you: a temporary heat signature moving across eternal stone, as transient as the water in the basins, as inevitable as the next rain.

Three to five days is optimal for most visitors. Beyond a week, people report a peculiar difficulty with departure—not that the place holds them, but that the act of leaving begins to feel arbitrary, unnecessary, a habit from another life that no longer carries conviction.

**Circadian Adjustment**
The extended twilights will disrupt your sense of day-night boundaries. Prepare by practicing flexible sleep schedules beforehand. The place encourages polyphasic rest: sleeping when tiredness arrives rather than when clocks dictate. The unchanging light means your body loses its primary zeitgeber. Some find this liberating. Others experience it as a low-grade temporal nausea.

Bring no clocks, no time-telling devices. They become grotesque here, their mechanical insistence an obscenity against the place's patient rhythms. If you must track time for return purposes, set a single alarm for your departure and then forget about hours.

## Psychological Preparation

**Confronting Silence**
If you've never experienced true silence, the hollows will unmake you briefly. Begin preparing weeks in advance. Practice sitting in the quietest spaces you can find for increasing durations. Notice your panic when stimulation ceases. Notice how panic gives way to restlessness, restlessness to boredom, boredom to a kind of clarity that has no name because it requires no language.

The silence here has weight. It will press on your eardrums. You'll hear your heartbeat, your digestion, the small wet sounds of your own aliveness. Many people talk to themselves just to break the pressure. This is natural. Let it pass. The urge to fill silence is the last spasm of the overstimulated self dying.

Bring no music, no devices for sound. If you cannot imagine existing without auditory input, you're not ready for this place. Return to your preparation.

**Solitude Tolerance**
Even if you travel with others, the hollows will separate you. Not physically—you can remain in sight of companions—but psychologically. Each person falls into their own rhythm across the stone, their own pattern of movement and rest. Conversation dies naturally here. The place absorbs words before they can build into exchange.

Prepare by spending full days alone without distraction. Not working alone, not alone-but-connected-digitally, but truly alone with only your thoughts and the physical world. If this terrifies you, good. Fear is information. Examine what you're afraid will happen in extended solitude. Usually it's meeting aspects of yourself you've been successfully avoiding through constant occupation.

The hollows will introduce you to these aspects with neither judgment nor mercy.

**Boredom Capacity**
Modern humans have largely lost the ability to be bored. The hollows will teach it back to you through immersion. Nothing happens here. You must develop the capacity to be present while nothing happens, to find this adequate, even rich.

Practice beforehand by sitting somewhere unremarkable for hours without task or device. A parking lot. A beige room. Anywhere that offers no inherent interest. Observe how your mind manufactures crisis and fascination to avoid simply being. Watch these fabrications arise and dissolve. The skill you're developing is presence without the crutch of stimulation.

At the hollows, you'll exhaust your capacity for observation in the first hour. Everything that can be noted will be noted. Then come the hard hours, where you must exist without the project of discovery, without narrative, without the arc of experience. Most people flee here, inventing reasons to leave. Those who stay discover something unnamed on the far side of boredom.

## Practical Provisions

**Water Management**
Bring all water you'll need—figure half a gallon per day minimum, more if you're prone to anxiety drinking. The water in the basins, while clean by wilderness standards, carries dissolved minerals and tannins in concentrations that will disrupt unprepared digestive systems. You could drink it in emergency, but the aftermath is hours of intestinal regret.

However, bring a small vessel—a cup or bowl—for tasting. Not drinking, tasting. The basin water has flavor: mineral, slightly saline, faintly organic. It tastes like the place distilled. A single sip, held in the mouth and contemplated, teaches you something about limestone and time that cannot be conveyed through description.

Consider bringing water purification as backup. Even if you never use it, the security allows fuller relaxation.

**Food Considerations**
Simple, requiring no preparation. The place diminishes appetite—not unpleasantly, but you'll find you need less than usual. The dampness, the warmth, the low stimulation all reduce metabolic demand.

Dense foods that travel well: nuts, dried fruit, hard cheese, preserved meats, dark chocolate. Things that can be eaten with one hand while sitting on sun-warmed limestone, that require no ceremony or cleanup. 

Avoid anything that creates garbage requiring management. The place's pristine emptiness makes the presence of waste packaging an almost moral offense. You'll find yourself unable to casually discard even biodegradable items. Pack out everything.

Some visitors fast during their time here. The place facilitates this. Your body finds its own resources sufficient. But don't attempt this without fasting experience—the hollows are not the place to discover your relationship with hunger for the first time.

**Shelter Requirements**
A tarp or small tent, primarily for gear protection and psychological security. The weather is stable enough that you could sleep directly on the stone, but most people need some definition between inside and outside, self and place.

Choose earth-toned materials that near-invisible against the limestone. Bright colors are possible but create a visual violence that will disturb your peace more than you anticipate. The place's muted palette trains your eye; loud hues begin to feel like screaming.

Your shelter will accumulate dampness. Everything does. Bring no sleeping bag that fails in humidity. Synthetic fills or treated down, or simply heavy blankets that remain functional when damp. Some prefer sleeping directly on limestone with only a thin pad, accepting the stone's coolness as a companion rather than a problem.

A small ground cloth to define clean space for gear. The limestone dust—fine, pale, persistent—infiltrates everything. This isn't problematic but requires acceptance. Your belongings will gray slightly. You will gray slightly. This is part of becoming temporary limestone yourself.

**Light Sources**
A headlamp with red light option for moving at night, but use sparingly. The darkness here is important. The hollows under starlight or heavy overcast teach different lessons than the hollows under dim sun.

Bring candles or a small oil lamp if you need ambient light for evening hours. Fire here feels appropriate in ways that battery-powered LED does not. Something about actual combustion, actual warmth, actual flicker harmonizes with the place. Electric light feels like an intrusion from another world—which it is.

Many visitors discover they need less light than they thought. The night here is dark but not threatening. Your eyes adjust. The stone seems to hold faint luminescence. You can navigate by feel and memory, and doing so teaches you the landscape in ways that vision cannot.

## Navigational Preparation

**Spatial Awareness**
The hollows' modest scale is deceptive. Within that three-hundred-pace area exists labyrinthine complexity. Basins that looked adjacent from one angle prove separated by stone ridges. Routes that seemed direct reveal themselves as circuitous. The subtle elevation changes create false perspectives.

Prepare by practicing awareness of your body in space without GPS or maps. The hollows are impossible to get lost in—you can always see the edges—but many people experience disorientation anyway, a sense of not knowing where they are despite seeing everything. This is the landscape teaching you that knowing and seeing are different forms of awareness.

Walk the space systematically on arrival. Circumnavigate the edges. Cross through the middle in cardinal directions. Visit each major basin. Build a bodily map before you build a conceptual one. Your feet and hands will remember the topology better than your eyes.

**Route Finding**
There are no trails because there is no destination. Every path is equally valid and equally arbitrary. This freedom paralyzes some people—the tyranny of infinite choice.

Develop small habits: always approach the twisted tree from the same direction; always pause at the threshold stone; always sit on the low wall before returning to your shelter. These rituals create structure in structureless space. They're artificial but necessary, the way breathing is artificial when you think about it but necessary nonetheless.

Notice which basins draw you, which you avoid. Your unconscious navigation patterns reveal things about yourself. Some people compulsively visit water-filled hollows, seeking reflection and containment. Others prefer the dry basins, comfortable with emptiness. Neither is correct. Both are informative.

## Mental Frameworks

**Expectation Management**
Arrive expecting nothing and you may receive something. Arrive expecting revelation and you'll find only stone and water, will leave disappointed, having missed everything.

The place is not a teacher in the sense of offering lessons. It's more like a mirror that doesn't reflect images but reflects... substance? Essence? Whatever you are beneath the social performance, the place will make you aware of it through sheer lack of distraction.

Some people find this wonderful. Others find it unbearable. Both responses are valid. The hollows don't care. That's part of their teaching—the universe's fundamental indifference to human emotional response.

**Projection Awareness**
You will want to make meaning. The spiral of stones in the largest basin, the threshold to nowhere, the patient tree—these will seem significant. Your meaning-making apparatus will activate, weaving narratives: ancient ritual site, hermit's sanctuary, memorial to loss.

Notice this process. The narratives might be true or false, but their truth-value is less interesting than observing yourself generate them. You're watching consciousness doing what consciousness does: pattern-matching, story-building, significance-seeking.

The place permits all interpretations because it asserts none. This is not postmodern relativism but something older: the recognition that human meaning and geological being exist in separate magisteria that occasionally overlap without ever fully coinciding.

Let your narratives arise. Examine them. Release them. Repeat. This is a meditation practice more rigorous than anything cushion-based.

**Presence Capacity**
The hollows reward a particular quality of attention: soft, sustained, non-grasping. Not the sharp focus of looking-for, but the diffuse awareness of being-with.

Practice this beforehand through any contemplative discipline that works for you. Meditation, prayer, artistic absorption, long-distance hiking—any activity that trains the muscle of sustained presence without agenda.

The place will test this capacity. Hours two through four are the crucible. You've seen everything. Novelty has expired. Now you must be here without the reward of discovery. Most people fail this test initially. They leave, or distract themselves, or mentally check out while physically remaining.

If you can stay present through the boredom, something shifts in hour five or six. The place stops being an object you're observing and becomes an environment you're participating in. The distinction sounds subtle but feels profound. You're no longer visiting the hollows; you're temporarily being the hollows, or they're being you. Subject and object dissolve.

This is not mystical. It's perceptual. But the effect can be powerful enough to seem mystical, especially if you've never experienced the dissolution of the observer-observed boundary.

## Physical Skills

**Balance and Movement**
Practice moving on uneven surfaces before arrival. The stone's smoothness combines with its irregularity to create a unique challenge. Yoga helps. Tai chi helps. Any discipline that develops proprioception and micro-adjustments.

Learn to walk slowly. Not carefully-slowly, but presence-slowly. Each step fully felt, weight transfer noticed, balance continually negotiated. This isn't just practical—it becomes a moving meditation.

Expect to use your hands. Not climbing, but frequently touching stone for balance, for information, for connection. The hollows are best experienced as a four-limbed creature, palms occasionally meeting limestone, fingers tracing the smooth walls of basins.

**Sitting Practice**
You'll sit a lot. On stone, which is harder than you think and cooler than you expect. Prepare your sit-bones and lower back. Practice sitting on hard surfaces for extended periods. Notice how your body settles, finds balance, accepts discomfort without fighting it.

Bring a small pad if needed, but try without first. Direct contact with limestone teaches something that padding obscures. The stone's coolness is a gift, not a problem. It grounds you, literally and figuratively.

Learn several sitting positions: cross-legged, one knee up, legs extended, lying back on elbows. You'll cycle through them as numbness dictates. The goal isn't motionless meditation but comfortable presence, which requires adjusting as the body demands.

**Weather Reading**
The subtle meteorology here requires attention. Learn to feel humidity changes, air pressure shifts, the difference between mist and pre-rain, the quality of light that suggests weather six hours hence.

Watch the basins. Water levels tell stories about recent precipitation, evaporation rates, temperature variations. The mineral rings are archives—learn to read them like tree rings, each layer a chapter in the water-stone conversation.

The faint salt smell strengthens before weather changes, as if the sea reaches inland through atmospheric channels. When you notice this, rain or heavy mist usually follows within a day.

## Equipment Details

**Essential Items**
- Wide-brimmed hat (sun protection for extended periods under overcast that still allows UV)
- Sunglasses (surprising how bright diffuse light becomes after hours)
- Sunscreen (the gentle sun is deceptive; burns accumulate)
- Small first aid kit (cuts from limestone edges are rare but occur)
- Backup water containers (redundancy brings peace)
- Repair materials for gear (you're committing to self-sufficiency)
- Something to sit on if stone proves too uncomfortable (though try stone first)
- Notebook and pencil if you process through writing (pen ink runs in dampness)
- Small binoculars for observing distant details (optional but enriching)

**Non-Essential but Valuable**
- Small sketchbook and drawing materials (visual processing captures something words miss)
- Camera with manual controls (if you photograph, do it contemplatively, not compulsively)
- Field guides to geology, though you may never open them (their presence is comfort)
- A single book, chosen carefully (you likely won't read, but the option matters psychologically)
- Hammock if trees existed (they don't, but the twisted shrub sometimes supports a small one)

**Explicitly Unnecessary**
- Entertainment devices (if you need entertainment, you're not ready)
- Multiple books (you won't read even one)
- Complicated cooking equipment (simple food only)
- Heavy camera gear (unless photography is your practice, not your documentation)
- Comfort items that prevent adaptation (accept the place's terms)

## Social Preparation

**Companion Considerations**
Going alone is recommended for first visits. Companionship dilutes the experience—not through any fault of companions, but because human presence inevitably creates a social field that competes with the place's field.

If you must bring someone, choose carefully. They should share your capacity for silence, your comfort with absence of agenda. A poor companion choice will ruin the hollows for both of you—one person's restlessness contaminates the other's peace.

Agree on protocols beforehand: how much togetherness vs. solitude, whether you'll share meals, what signals mean "I need space." Make these explicit. The place's silence amplifies small social frictions into chasms.

Some relationships deepen profoundly here. The shared experience of extended presence without distraction strips away social performance, reveals people as they are. This can be beautiful or devastating, often both.

**Solo Dynamics**
Going alone means confronting yourself without the buffer of social role. You can't be funny, smart, caring, interesting—there's no audience. You can only be whatever you are beneath those performances.

Prepare for this ego death, minor as it may be. Some people experience euphoria in the liberation from self-presentation. Others feel existential panic. Most alternate between both states.

The place will show you how much of your identity is constructed through social reflection. Without anyone to perceive you, who are you? The question isn't philosophical here—it's visceral, immediate, sometimes uncomfortable.

Have a plan for mental health crisis if you're prone to such things. Know your warning signs. Have exit strategies. The hollows are healing for many but devastating for some. Be honest about which you're likely to be.

## Artistic/Creative Preparation

**Documentation Philosophy**
If you photograph, understand why. Are you trying to possess the place? Remember it? Share it? Process it? Each motivation produces different results and different relationships with the experience.

The place resists photography. The dim light, the subtlety of forms, the scale ambiguities—these don't translate well to images. Most photos from the hollows look disappointingly bland, failing to capture the presence that's overwhelming in person.

If you still wish to photograph, shoot film. The deliberation of film—limited frames, no instant review, chemical development—harmonizes with the place's pace. Digital shooting easily becomes compulsive, a way to avoid presence through documentation.

**Creative Processing**
Many artists find the hollows generative, though not in obvious ways. You likely won't create here. The place doesn't stimulate that kind of production. But ideas germinate, percolate, emerge weeks or months later.

Bring minimal materials if you must create on-site. A notebook. One pen. Maybe watercolors, which work well with the ambient dampness. But don't expect productivity. The place teaches reception, not production.

Writers often find themselves unable to write here, or writing in modes unfamiliar to them. Linear narrative breaks down. Description feels inadequate. Some default to lists, fragments, impressions.

Let the creative process adapt to the place rather than forcing the place into your creative framework. What emerges will be strange but true.

## Seasonal Considerations

**Optimal Timing**
The place's minimal seasonal variation means no season is clearly best, but subtle differences exist.

Whatever passes for spring brings slightly more frequent rains, keeping more basins full simultaneously. The vegetation shows marginal greenness. The place feels more alive, though "alive" is relative in such sparse ecology.

Summer (if such distinction exists here) means longer periods between rains, more basins showing their ringed bottoms, more exposed stone. The place becomes more geometric, abstract, skeletal.

Visit when you're called. The right time is personal, not calendrical. Some people feel drawn to full basins and rare green. Others prefer the austere beauty of drought-exposed stone.

**Duration Variations**
Day visits are possible but unsatisfying—you experience the place as exhibit, not as environment. Give it at least overnight. Better, give it three nights. Best, give it as long as you can.

The internal experience changes with duration:
- Hours 0-4: Observation, novelty, mental cataloging
- Hours 4-12: Boredom, restlessness, questioning why you came
- Hours 12-24: Settling, first glimpses of different awareness
- Days 2-3: Integration, embodiment, reciprocal perception
- Days 4+: Dissolution of visit-mindset, temporary inhabitation
- Week+: Difficulty remembering why leaving matters

Plan your duration based on where you want to go in this progression, remembering that you can't skip stages. The hollows reveal themselves on their own schedule.

## Return Preparation

**Re-Entry Planning**
Leaving requires transition. Don't plan to return directly to normal life, especially if you've stayed multiple days. The sensory assault of traffic, voices, artificial light, demands—it's violence after the hollows' gentleness.

Build in buffer time. A quiet hotel room. A slow drive. Something gentle between stone-world and human-world. Your nervous system needs recalibration.

Expect social awkwardness. You'll have lost the script of small talk. Questions like "how was it?" become unanswerable. People want a summary, a highlight reel, an emotional verdict. The experience resists summary.

Prepare responses beforehand: "It was good. Quiet. I'll tell you more when I've processed it." This protects both you and questioners from the frustrated silence that follows inadequate description.

**Integration Practices**
Journal extensively in the weeks after, even if you never journal. The experience continues unfolding. Things you didn't notice you noticed surface in memory. Meanings emerge retroactively.

Resist the urge to immediately return. The place affects you for months after. Let that process complete before considering a second visit. Going back too soon interrupts integration, treats the hollows as destination rather than catalyst.

Some people return annually, making pilgrimage of it. Others visit once and carry it forever. Both approaches are valid. The place changes you or it doesn't. Duration doesn't determine depth.

**Lifestyle Adjustments**
Many people find normal life too loud afterward. Traffic sounds become aggressive. Conversations feel performed. Entertainment seems grotesque in its manufactured urgency.

This passes, usually. But something doesn't pass: a new standard for what presence feels like, what silence contains, what enough means. The hollows establish a baseline against which prior normal reveals itself as frenetic.

Some make radical life changes after visiting—moves to quieter places, career shifts toward less stimulation, relationship evaluations. Don't make major decisions immediately. Let the place's influence settle before acting on it.

Others return to normal with only subtle shifts: more comfortable with silence, less dependent on constant input, more aware of the peace available in empty spaces. These quiet changes may be more sustainable than dramatic overhauls.

## Emergency Preparedness

**Medical Considerations**
The hollows are safe—no predators, no hazards, minimal injury risk. But isolation means self-sufficiency. Bring first aid knowledge, not just supplies.

Common issues:
- Ankle turns (irregular stone surface catches inattention)
- Cuts from sharp limestone edges (rare but potentially dirty)
- Dehydration (from underestimating need in warmth)
- Gastric distress (if you drink basin water)
- Psychological overwhelm (more common than physical injury)

Know your limits. Heart conditions and the modest elevation changes rarely interact, but the heat and isolation can stress compromised systems. The dampness aggravates some respiratory issues.

Have communication means for true emergency, even if you keep it turned off otherwise. The place is remote enough that help isn't immediate.

**Psychological Safety**
The silence and isolation can trigger unexpected psychological responses. Even stable people occasionally experience:
- Panic at the weight of silence
- Existential dread at aloneness
- Paranoid awareness of vulnerability
- Dissociative episodes from sensory deprivation
- Compulsive thought patterns without distraction to interrupt them

These usually pass. But know yourself. If you have active mental health challenges, consider whether the hollows are appropriate right now. The place can be healing but is not therapeutic—there's no support structure if you destabilize.

Bring grounding objects: photos, talismans, anything that anchors you to ordinary reality. You probably won't need them. But having them available provides security.

## Philosophical Preparation

**Confronting Emptiness**
The hollows teach emptiness as fullness, absence as presence, nothing as adequate. This is easier said than integrated. Modern life conditions us to experience lack as problem requiring solution.

Prepare by practicing small emptinesses: meals eaten in silence, hours without plans, days without achievement. Notice your discomfort with undirected time. Question why emptiness feels like failure rather than simply emptiness.

The place will confront you with the question: Is this enough? Stone and water, warmth and dampness, the slow passage of hours. No stimulation, no achievement, no narrative arc. Just being in a place that's being itself.

How you answer determines your experience. If you need more, you'll suffer. If you can rest in enough-ness, you'll discover something that most modern humans have forgotten: contentment without cause.

**Accepting Insignificance**
The stone has been here longer than your family tree. It will be here after your great-grandchildren die. Your visit is a brief warmth, noticed no more than morning condensation that evaporates by noon.

This insignificance is either terrifying or liberating. Prepare for both responses. The ego fights irrelevance. Something deeper than ego rests in it.

The place teaches geological time, where human spans become invisible. You're a mayfly to the stone's sequoia. This perspective either crushes meaning or reveals meanings that don't require human scale.

Practice cosmic perspective beforehand. Look at stars. Study deep time. Read about geological processes. Arrive at the hollows already somewhat humbled, and the place's teaching will be gentler.

**Finding Enough**
The central lesson, if there is one: this is enough. Not profound, not enlightening, not transformative—just enough. Sitting on sun-warm limestone watching water slowly evaporate from a basin. Enough.

Western culture conditions us to experience enough as failure—there should be more, better, different. The hollows reveal this conditioning by removing the distractions that usually obscure it.

When you notice yourself thinking "Is this all there is?" recognize this as your culture speaking, not your truth. Sit with the question until it becomes not complaint but genuine inquiry. What if this is all there is? What if that's sufficient?

The answer the place offers: Yes. And yes.

## Final Considerations

**Readiness Assessment**
You're ready for the hollows if:
- You can sit still for hours without entertainment
- Silence feels companionable rather than oppressive
- You're curious about boredom's far side
- You can tolerate discomfort without fixing it
- You want less rather than more

You're not ready if:
- Constant stimulation feels necessary
- Being alone triggers panic
- Silence feels like punishment
- Discomfort demands immediate resolution
- You're seeking dramatic transformation

Not being ready isn't failure. The place will be there when you are. Forcing the experience before readiness creates suffering without insight.

**Alternative Preparations**
If the hollows feel too extreme, prepare by:
- Spending weekends at monasteries or retreat centers
- Camping in gentle wilderness with minimal agenda
- Extended silent meditation retreats
- Solo hiking with contemplative intention
- Any practice that develops comfort with undirected presence

These build the capacities the hollows require. Arrive with those capacities already nascent, and the place deepens them. Arrive without them, and you'll struggle with basics instead of receiving subtleties.

**Permission and Letting Go**
Finally, you don't need permission to visit, but somehow it helps to hear: You're allowed to find the place boring. You're allowed to leave early. You're allowed to not have a transformative experience.

The hollows don't demand anything. They're just stone and water doing what stone and water do. Your presence changes nothing for them.

That indifference is the teaching. In a world where everything demands your attention, judges your response, requires your engagement, here is a place that simply... is. Indifferent to your struggle, unimpressed by your insights, unmoved by your departure.

Some find this cruel. Others find it the kindest thing they've ever encountered—a place that doesn't need them to be anything other than briefly present before being gone.

Prepare for both responses. Pack your bag, check your water, test your footwear. But mostly, prepare to be small, temporary, sufficient, and perfectly unnecessary in the patient life of stone.

The hollows await, have always awaited, will continue awaiting after you've turned to the same calcium they're slowly dissolving into. Your visit matters immensely and not at all. Both truths are true.

Go gently. Walk carefully. Sit often. Expect nothing. Receive what comes. Leave when it's time.

The stone will remember you exactly as long as your warmth lingers on its surface after you stand and walk away—which is to say, briefly, which is to say, enough.
