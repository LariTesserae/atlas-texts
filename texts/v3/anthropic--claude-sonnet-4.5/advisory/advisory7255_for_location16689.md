# Preparation Guide for The Throat

## Physical Preparation

### Hearing Protection - CRITICAL
This is your single most important preparation. The sound at The Throat will cause permanent hearing damage within hours without protection, and psychological breakdown shortly after.

- **Primary protection**: Industrial-grade ear defenders rated for 35+ dB reduction, designed for continuous wear. The kind used near jet engines. Bring three pairs - redundancy is survival.
- **Secondary layer**: Custom-molded earplugs worn underneath the defenders. Get them professionally fitted before departure.
- **Tertiary option**: Foam earplugs as backup, though these alone are catastrophically insufficient.
- **Reality check**: Even with triple protection, you will still hear the roar. You will feel it in your chest and bones. This is unavoidable. The protection only prevents physical damage and maintains your sanity margin.

Practice wearing this hearing protection for 8-12 hour stretches before you go. Learn to function while partially deaf. Your own heartbeat will be loud. Your breathing will be loud. Get comfortable with this sensory deprivation while you still have the option to remove it.

### Vestibular Conditioning
Your inner ear will be under assault from contradictory spatial information. Prepare:

- **Motion sickness training**: Spend weeks building tolerance. Spin in chairs, use VR with contradictory motion, practice on boats in rough water.
- **Balance exercises**: Daily practice standing on unstable surfaces - wobble boards, balance trainers, slacklines. Do this with eyes closed.
- **Medication**: Bring prescription anti-vertigo medication (meclizine, scopolamine patches). Bring more than you think you need. Test them beforehand; some make you drowsy, which is dangerous.
- **Pressure point knowledge**: Learn acupressure points for nausea (P6/Nei Guan on the wrist). Bring sea-bands.

The goal isn't to prevent spatial disorientation - you can't - but to maintain function while disoriented.

### Physical Conditioning
The Throat demands endurance in stillness:

- **Core strength**: Your body will constantly micro-adjust to maintain balance on shifting perspectives. Weak core means exhaustion in hours. Practice planks, standing balance holds, yoga balances for 30+ minutes.
- **Leg endurance**: You'll be standing on hard volcanic glass for extended periods. Build up to standing for 6-8 hours with minimal breaks. Wear the boots you plan to bring.
- **Heat acclimatization**: The warm humidity is oppressive. If possible, train in hot yoga environments or saunas while maintaining activity. Your body needs to be efficient at thermoregulation.

### Respiratory Preparation
The thick, mineral-laden air requires adjustment:

- **Elevation training**: Though not high altitude, the thick air has a similar "breathing through soup" quality. Train your breathing efficiency.
- **Breath control**: Practice slow, controlled breathing through both nose and mouth. The air will coat your airways; learn to manage this sensation without panic.
- **Lung capacity**: You'll be in constant low-level stress. Greater lung capacity means better oxygen distribution when breathing feels labored.

## Equipment - The Essentials

### Footwear - CRITICAL
Your boots are your foundation in literal and psychological senses.

- **Requirements**: Extremely rigid soles (volcanic glass is sharp and uneven), high ankle support (for constant balance adjustment), heat-resistant (the glass is warm, sometimes hot).
- **Specific recommendation**: Mountaineering boots rated for glacier travel, or fire-resistant industrial boots with climbing-grade soles.
- **Break them in**: Wear them for months before departure. Blisters at The Throat could be incapacitating.
- **Bring backup**: A second pair, different brand, in case one fails. Seal them in waterproof bags.

### Clothing System
Humidity is 90%+, temperature varies, nothing dries:

- **Base layer**: Synthetic moisture-wicking material. Merino wool second choice. Never cotton - it stays wet and will chafe you raw.
- **Mid layer**: Lightweight, quick-dry. Zipper ventilation is valuable.
- **No outer shell needed**: You're not protecting against rain; you're in a sauna.
- **Bring multiples**: At least 3 complete changes. When everything is damp, rotating through less-damp options maintains sanity.
- **Undergarments**: This sounds trivial. It's not. Anti-chafe, moisture-wicking, bring 7+ pairs. Fungal infections in constant humidity are miserable and distracting.

### Eye Protection
The dim light and occasional bright flashes create strain:

- **Primary**: Clear safety glasses with anti-fog coating (you'll be constantly damp). Protection against water droplets that move in wrong directions.
- **Backup**: Swimming goggles, sounds strange but they seal against moisture and protect from unpredictable water behavior.
- **Dark glasses**: For if you witness what's beyond the water-ceiling again. Psychological protection from things too large to process.

### The Platform Kit

**Sitting/Sleeping System**:
- Closed-cell foam pad (won't absorb moisture like inflatables)
- Lightweight camping hammock (if you can secure between platforms, though this seems unlikely)
- Emergency bivvy sack (for psychological comfort of enclosure, not warmth)

**Anchoring System**:
That metal ring embedded in the glass is intentional. Bring:
- 50m of static climbing rope, rated for wet conditions
- Carabiners (multiple types, all locking)
- Climbing harness
- Prussik loops for ascending rope

You may need to stay put when the platforms drift. You may need to rappel if spatial geometry shifts. The rope is your lifeline.

### Navigation & Orientation

Traditional tools will fail, but bring them anyway:

- **Compass**: Will probably not work correctly, but monitoring its failure modes tells you something about the spatial distortions.
- **GPS**: Same - it will malfunction, but how it malfunctions is information.
- **Paper maps**: Of the throat if any exist, but more importantly, blank paper to map your own observations.
- **Waterproof notebooks**: Multiple. The humidity will destroy paper; you need sealed/waterproof versions.
- **Pencils**: Not pens (ink runs). Mechanical pencils with waterproof lead. Many of them.
- **Chalk/markers**: To mark your platform, track changes in the glass, note anything that moves.

**Time tracking**:
- Wristwatch, mechanical not digital (fewer failure points)
- Backup watch
- Timer with alarm functions
- Ability to mark time passing when perception becomes unreliable

Set alarms for regular intervals. When time feels uncertain, the alarm tells you something definite happened.

## Sustenance

### Water - The Paradox
You're surrounded by water but cannot drink it.

- **Carry capacity**: Minimum 10L in multiple containers. Durable water bottles, collapsible reservoirs, military-grade hydration systems.
- **Purification**: Bring tablets/filters anyway, though the water here may not respond to conventional purification. Boiling is unlikely - see below about fire.
- **Rationing discipline**: Practice strict water rationing before you go. 2L per day is minimum, 3L better in the heat/humidity, but you must make supply last.
- **Collection experiment**: Bring a clean container to test if the anomalous water, once settled, becomes drinkable. This is risky. Test on small amounts first. The water that falls upward may not follow biological rules either.

### Food
Compact, no-cook, humidity-resistant:

- **Calorie-dense**: Nuts, dried fruit, energy bars, pemmican, chocolate (in sealed containers).
- **No cooking assumed**: You haven't seen fire there. Bringing a stove means fuel weight, and fire may not burn correctly in that space anyway.
- **Packaging**: Everything triple-sealed in waterproof bags. Humidity will penetrate given time.
- **Variety**: Monotony in high-stress environments accelerates psychological breakdown. Different flavors and textures matter.
- **Minimum**: 3000+ calories per day for at least 7 days. You'll be under physical and psychological stress constantly.

### Supplements
- Electrolyte tablets (sweating constantly)
- Multivitamins (food variety will be low)
- Anti-nausea medication
- Antacids (stress + mineral-heavy air affects digestion)

## Light Sources

The murky bioluminescence isn't enough:

- **Headlamps**: Three, with rechargeable and AA battery options. Waterproof, strobe function, red light mode (preserves night vision).
- **Hand flashlights**: Two, extremely durable (Maglite-style), high lumens.
- **Chemical glow sticks**: Don't need batteries, work when wet, can mark locations. Bring dozens.
- **Battery banks**: Multiple high-capacity portable chargers, fully loaded. Solar charging is unlikely given the perpetual murk.
- **Extra batteries**: For everything. Assume you can't recharge anything.

Test all light sources in high-humidity environments beforehand. Some seals fail.

## Medical Kit - Expanded

Beyond normal first aid:

**Injury prevention/treatment**:
- Blister treatment (moleskin, second skin, lubricants)
- Antifungal creams and powders (the humidity guarantees fungal issues)
- Antibiotic ointment (any cut in that environment risks infection)
- Broad-spectrum oral antibiotics (prescription, discuss with doctor)
- Wound closure strips, sutures if you're trained
- Elastic bandages for joint support
- Pain relief (ibuprofen, acetaminophen, something stronger if you can obtain it)

**Psychological support**:
- Anti-anxiety medication (prescribed, tested beforehand)
- Sleep aids (you may need to force rest despite the noise)
- Stimulants (for when you must stay alert despite exhaustion)

**Sensory issues**:
- Eye drops (for strain and dryness despite humidity)
- Saline nasal spray (for mineral-coated airways)
- Throat lozenges (for the alkaline bite)

**Documentation**:
- Written medical history
- Allergy information
- Current medications
- Emergency contact information (though communication out seems unlikely)

## Communication Equipment

Probably futile, but essential to try:

- **Satellite phone**: May not function, but bring it.
- **Radio**: HAM radio with various frequency capabilities. The sound might drown it out, but radio waves may penetrate better than you expect.
- **Signal devices**: Flares, signal mirror, whistle (laughably inadequate against the roar, but costs nothing weight-wise).
- **Written messages**: Pre-prepared, waterproof. If someone else arrives, you need to communicate.

## Psychological Preparation - EQUALLY CRITICAL

### Sensory Isolation Training
The noise will be maddening:

- **White noise exposure**: Sleep with loud white noise for months beforehand. Train your brain to function with constant acoustic assault.
- **Sensory deprivation**: Spend time in isolation tanks, dark rooms, spaces where one sense dominates. Learn how your mind responds to sensory monotony.
- **Meditation**: Daily practice focusing attention despite distractions. The roar will try to devour your thoughts; you need mental techniques to maintain an inner dialogue.

### Solitude Preparation
You might be utterly alone:

- **Solo time**: Spend increasing periods alone before departure. Days, then a week or more. No human contact. Learn what your mind does.
- **Journaling practice**: Document your thoughts regularly. This habit becomes crucial for maintaining sense of self when external references dissolve.
- **Mantras/anchors**: Develop mental phrases or concepts you can return to when disoriented. Simple truths about who you are.

### Horror Inoculation
You will see things that don't make sense:

- **Study optical illusions**: Train your brain to recognize when perception is unreliable.
- **Read accounts of perceptual disorders**: Understand how the brain generates meaning from contradictory data.
- **Accept limitations**: Practice the phrase "I don't understand this, and that's acceptable." Fighting to comprehend the incomprehensible causes breakdown.

### Fear Management
That thing beyond the water-ceiling was vast:

- **Acknowledge scale**: You are small. Something being larger than you can process doesn't make it hostile. Practice feeling insignificant without feeling threatened.
- **Cosmic horror exposure**: Read Lovecraft, watch films about incomprehensible entities. Not to become brave, but to recognize the mental patterns of confronting the too-large.
- **Exit strategy confidence**: Know that you can leave (assuming you can). That knowledge provides psychological anchor.

## Operational Protocols

### Time Discipline
When time becomes uncertain:

- Set watch alarms every 2 hours
- At each alarm: drink water, eat small amount, write journal entry, check equipment, perform specific physical action (10 jumping jacks, specific stretch routine)
- This creates artificial rhythm when natural rhythm dissolves

### Documentation Protocol
Your observations may be vital:

**Every 2 hours record**:
- Time by watch
- Temperature (estimate if no thermometer)
- Number of visible platforms
- Positions of platforms (rough sketch)
- Water-form behaviors observed
- Light conditions
- Physical state (pain, hunger, fatigue on 1-10 scale)
- Mental state (clarity, fear, confusion on 1-10 scale)
- Anything anomalous even by local standards

**Why**: Creates objective record when subjective experience becomes unreliable. If you return, this data is invaluable. If you don't, whoever finds your notes has better chances.

### Movement Protocol
If you must leave your platform:

1. Document decision: why moving, destination, expected danger
2. Anchor rope to metal ring
3. Pay out rope as you go
4. Test each new surface before committing weight
5. Maintain rope tension - it's your return path
6. Set maximum distance/time limits before mandatory return

Never, ever abandon your rope connection on impulse.

### Emergency Protocols

**If platforms begin fragmenting**:
- Immediate harness attachment to rope and ring
- Prepare to transfer to new platform
- Priority: water, food, medical kit, notebooks
- Accept loss of other equipment

**If water behavior becomes violently erratic**:
- Center of platform
- Sitting position (lower center of gravity)
- Protect head and core
- Ride it out - the platforms have survived previous episodes

**If you see the bipedal figure again**:
- Signal with light (strobe pattern)
- Prepare written communication
- Do not approach through water alone
- Remember: they survived here long enough to build a cairn

**If psychological breakdown approaches**:
- Sit down immediately
- Physical anchor: touch the glass, feel its warmth, count cracks
- Recite prepared mantras
- If necessary, take anti-anxiety medication
- Write your current state in notebook
- Sleep if possible - unconsciousness may reset panic

## What You Cannot Prepare For

Be honest with yourself about these:

**The sound**: Even prepared, it will be worse than you imagine. The constant assault creates cumulative trauma. There is no silence, no respite, no relief. You will dream of quiet.

**The disorientation**: Your brain will fight the contradictory spatial information. Nausea, vertigo, confusion are inevitable. Preparation reduces them but cannot prevent them.

**The isolation**: Even if other beings exist there, communication seems nearly impossible. You will be more alone than anywhere on Earth.

**The uncertainty**: You don't know if water is drinkable. If the platforms are safe. If the glass will hold. If rescue is possible. If anything you're experiencing is real. Living in perpetual uncertainty is corrosive.

**The wrongness**: Water flowing in impossible directions, space folding, time unreliable - these aren't jump scares. They're persistent, grinding assaults on your sense of reality. Sanity is not binary; it erodes.

**The meaning**: That cairn was built by someone. The ring was placed by someone. Either they escaped, or they didn't. You might learn which. The knowledge might not help.

## The Real Preparation: Mental Acceptance

Everything above addresses physical and practical concerns. But the actual preparation happens internally:

**Accept that you might not understand**: The Throat exists on principles you may not be equipped to grasp. Fighting this causes madness faster than anything else. Practice saying "This is beyond me" without anxiety.

**Accept vulnerability**: You will be small, powerless, subject to forces you can't control. This is the default state of existence; the Throat just makes it obvious.

**Accept uncertainty**: You don't know if you can leave. You don't know if others are there. You don't know what anything means. Uncertainty is the price of exploration.

**Accept change**: You will not return the same. The sound will remain in your bones. The memory of water flowing upward will intrude during normal rain. The sense of space as negotiable will linger. This is not damage—it's experience of a reality that doesn't bow to human comfort.

**Accept purpose**: Why are you going? Not for science (though document what you can). Not for adventure (it's mostly standing on glass, disoriented and damp). You're going to witness something that shouldn't exist. To stand in a throat where water screams. To see the places between places. Make peace with this purpose, or don't go.

## Final Considerations

**Timing**: You don't mention how long you plan to stay. This is perhaps the most crucial decision. Each additional day increases risk geometrically. My recommendation: 72 hours maximum for first visit. Enough to observe, document, and understand immediate dangers. Not enough for the chronic effects to destroy you.

**Exit strategy**: Confirm your method of departure before arrival. If it depends on something at the location (portal, guide, phenomenon), have backup plans. The greatest danger is being trapped with finite supplies in an endless roar.

**Communication**: Tell someone exactly where you're going and when you should return. Give them strict instructions: if you're not back by X date, assume you're not coming back. Don't send rescue—it may not be possible, and you don't want others trapped there attempting to save you.

**The cairn and ring**: These suggest others survived. But survival means different things. Surviving physically while being psychologically shredded is not success. Have metrics for what constitutes acceptable outcome, and criteria for abandoning the expedition early if those metrics aren't met.

**The larger thing**: You saw something enormous beyond the water-ceiling. It may be unaware of you. It may be indifferent. It may be the reason the water screams. You cannot prepare for encountering it directly. If it notices you—if it takes interest—your preparation becomes irrelevant. Make peace with this possibility.

## The Core Truth

All this preparation—the gear, the training, the protocols—serves one real purpose: buying you time and margin. Time to observe, margin to survive, but most importantly, buffer against the moment when you realize you are in a place that exists outside human context.

The volcanic glass is new—days or weeks old. Reality is crystallizing around ancient water, trying to make sense of it and failing. You will stand on fresh-formed glass in thick wet air while water screams through geometries that shouldn't exist, and you will be part of something that is still being invented, still failing to cohere into stable existence.

Your preparation cannot prevent you from experiencing this. It can only help you witness it, document it, and return to describe it to those of us who lack your courage or madness.

Go with eyes open. Go prepared. But go knowing that the Throat is a place where preparation reaches its limit and something else must carry you through—call it resilience, or stubbornness, or the same inexplicable drive that made humans first look up at stars and wonder what lies beyond.

The water is screaming. It has been screaming perhaps forever. You're preparing to stand in that scream and remain yourself. That's not a small thing you're attempting.

Be ready. Be as ready as possible. And then accept that ready is never enough, and go anyway.
