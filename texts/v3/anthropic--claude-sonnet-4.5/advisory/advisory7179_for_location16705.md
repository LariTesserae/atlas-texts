# Preparation Guide for the Ascending Foundries

## Physical Conditioning: The Altitude Challenge

Your most critical preparation must address the altitude. The Foundries exist at elevations where human lungs burn and vision swims—likely 15,000 feet or higher, based on the descriptions of thin air, extreme solar radiation, and sharp temperature swings. An ordinary human arriving unprepared will suffer immediate, debilitating altitude sickness.

**Begin altitude conditioning at least three months before departure.** If you live near mountains, spend weekends progressively higher—12,000 feet, then 13,000, building toward 14,000 if possible. Sleep at these elevations; your body adapts most during rest. If you live at sea level, consider an altitude training mask or hypoxic chamber sessions, though these are poor substitutes for actual elevation.

**Cardiovascular fitness is non-negotiable.** The thin air means every action—climbing stairs, carrying objects, even sustained conversation—demands more from your heart and lungs. Build endurance through long, slow distance work. Running, cycling, swimming—the specific activity matters less than developing a robust aerobic base. Include interval training to prepare for moments of exertion.

**Expect to feel terrible for the first week regardless.** Headaches, nausea, fatigue, disrupted sleep, loss of appetite—these are normal. The residents are adapted; you are not. Plan to accomplish almost nothing during your initial days. Rest. Hydrate obsessively. Let your body manufacture more red blood cells. Push through this adjustment period and you risk pulmonary or cerebral edema, both potentially fatal.

## Medical Preparations

**Consult a physician specializing in altitude medicine.** Request a prescription for acetazolamide (Diamox), which accelerates acclimatization. Begin taking it two days before arrival. Some travelers respond better to dexamethasone; know your options. Pack both if possible.

Assemble a comprehensive medical kit:
- **For altitude**: acetazolamide, dexamethasone, ibuprofen (for headaches), ondansetron (for nausea)
- **For the environment**: broad-spectrum antibiotics, antifungal cream, burn treatment supplies, eye drops for dryness
- **For injuries**: comprehensive first aid supplies, splinting materials, hemostatic gauze—remember that help, if it exists, is far away
- **Diagnostics**: pulse oximeter to monitor blood oxygen levels (anything below 90% requires immediate rest), thermometer

**Dental work must be completed before departure.** The pressure changes and lack of medical infrastructure make a dental emergency catastrophic. Similarly, address any chronic conditions. Your asthma, that occasionally troublesome knee, your digestive issues—everything becomes worse at altitude.

**Consider your blood.** If you have sickle cell trait, even mild anemia, or clotting disorders, this location may be impossible for you. The combination of altitude, heat, and dehydration creates dangerous conditions for anyone with blood-related vulnerabilities.

## Equipment: Surviving the Extreme Environment

The Foundries present a brutal combination: intense solar radiation, extreme heat, thin dry air, and nighttime cooling. Your equipment must address all of these.

### Sun Protection

The sun at this elevation is almost white, hammering down through minimal atmosphere. You will sunburn in minutes without protection.

**Clothing must cover everything.** Long sleeves, long pants, high collars. Choose lightweight, light-colored fabrics that reflect rather than absorb heat—but not the pure white that will blind you with glare. Natural fibers like linen breathe well; modern synthetics with UV protection ratings (UPF 50+) work excellently. Bring multiple sets; you'll sweat through them.

**Head covering is critical.** A wide-brimmed hat with a neck cape (legionnaire style) protects your face, ears, and neck. Add a thin scarf that can wrap around your lower face—the reflected glare from stone and metal can burn you from below.

**Eye protection**: Glacier glasses or high-altitude mountaineering sunglasses with side shields. The light here will damage your retinas. Bring two pairs in case one breaks. Consider ski goggles for the worst glare conditions.

**Sunscreen**: SPF 50+ minimum, zinc-based physical blockers rather than chemical ones. Apply obsessively, especially to often-forgotten areas: ears, back of neck, backs of hands, any exposed skin. Reapply every two hours. Bring more than you think you need—at least 8 ounces per week.

### Heat Management

Despite the thin air, the heat pools and concentrates. You're surrounded by dark stone and metal structures that absorb and radiate heat.

**Hydration system**: Not just water bottles—a proper hydration system. Plan for consuming 6-8 liters of water daily, possibly more during active periods. A hydration bladder with drinking tube allows constant sipping without stopping work. Bring electrolyte replacement—at this altitude with this heat, you'll deplete salts rapidly. Bring more electrolyte powder than seems reasonable.

**Cooling aids**: Lightweight cooling towels that activate when wetted. A spray bottle for misting yourself during peak heat. These seem minor but will significantly improve your functioning during the worst afternoon hours.

### Respiratory Support

The air is thin and dry. Your respiratory system will suffer.

**Breathing aids**: A buff or lightweight balaclava that can cover your nose and mouth, humidifying inhaled air slightly. This prevents the searing sensation in your lungs during exertion.

**Lip protection**: Chapstick with SPF, multiple tubes. Your lips will crack and bleed otherwise. Similarly, nasal moisturizer—your nasal passages will dry out painfully.

### Foot Care

The terrain is steep, made of hard stone and metal, involving constant climbing and descending through narrow passages.

**Boots**: Hiking boots with excellent ankle support, already broken in. The thin air means your feet swell more than usual; size up a half-size. Bring two pairs if possible—if one fails, you're immobilized.

**Socks**: Multiple pairs of moisture-wicking socks with good cushioning. Change them frequently. Foot care is critical—blisters or injuries in this terrain become disabling quickly.

**Trekking poles**: Collapsible, lightweight. The steep terrain and thin air mean you'll need the stability and the reduction in knee strain.

### Sleeping Arrangements

The dormitory-style buildings suggest basic accommodations. Bring what you need for comfort and warmth.

**Sleeping bag**: Temperature-rated for warm conditions but remember the nighttime cooling. A 40-50°F rated bag should suffice. Compression stuff sack to minimize space.

**Sleeping pad**: Self-inflating or foam. The stone conducts heat away from your body. Insulation from the ground improves sleep quality enormously.

**Earplugs**: The industrial sounds never fully stop. Eye mask for the arc lights that burn through the night.

## Nutrition Strategy

Appetite suppression is a standard altitude effect. You must eat anyway—your body is working much harder than usual just to exist here.

**Bring calorie-dense foods you can eat even when nauseated**: nut butters, energy bars, dried fruits, nuts, honey, hard candies. The communal meals exist but may be insufficient for your needs during adjustment, or the food may not agree with you.

**Avoid alcohol entirely** for at least the first two weeks. It exacerbates altitude sickness and dehydration. 

**Small, frequent meals** work better than large ones. Your digestive system struggles at altitude. Eat before you're hungry; by the time you feel hunger, you're already behind on calories.

**Salt your food liberally.** You're losing electrolytes constantly through sweat and the dry air.

## Navigation and Orientation Tools

The city's layout is deliberately described as confusing—dense, vertical, winding. Spaces suddenly open onto thousand-foot drops.

**Mapping materials**: Waterproof notebook, pencils (pens may fail in the heat). Map your living space, work areas, and the routes between them. The topology seems slightly unstable—whether this is perceptual or actual, you'll need records to establish patterns.

**Headlamp**: LED headlamp with extra batteries. Despite the arc lights, there will be dark passages. Hand-crank backup flashlight that doesn't rely on batteries.

**Compass and altimeter watch**: Even if the topology is strange, these provide data points. Document readings, look for patterns. A watch with barometric pressure readings helps you track the minimal weather changes.

**Rope and climbing gear**: Even if you're not a climber, basic equipment—50 feet of static rope, carabiners, webbing—might save your life on these cliffs and verticality. At minimum, learn basic knots and anchor-building.

## Communication and Documentation

There's no mention of communication with "below" or of any outside contact. Plan for complete isolation.

**Documentation supplies**: Waterproof notebooks (multiple), pencils, pens, drawing materials. You'll want to record what you observe. The strangeness reveals itself gradually—you need records to track the accumulating oddities.

**Camera**: If you bring electronics, keep them simple and heat-resistant. A rugged point-and-shoot rather than a complex DSLR. The heat and dust may kill electronics regardless. Bring multiple memory cards.

**Watch**: Mechanical, not electronic. Something that works in heat and altitude without batteries. You need to track time—the description suggests time feels different there, and days blend together. An external timekeeper becomes crucial.

**No expectation of communication out**: Bring no devices that require cellular signal or internet. They won't work, and they'll just be dead weight. Write letters you may never send if you need the psychological comfort.

## Psychological Preparation

The Foundries will challenge your mind as much as your body.

**Prepare for isolation and purpose-without-clear-objective.** Everyone there is focused on work whose ultimate goal remains unclear. This creates a strange psychological space—neither meaningless (the work clearly matters to those doing it) nor clearly meaningful (you won't understand the end point). Practice being comfortable with ambiguity. Study existentialist philosophy if it helps. Develop meditation or mindfulness practices.

**The silence between sounds can be disturbing.** If you're accustomed to urban environments, the acoustic environment—isolated sounds in silence—may be deeply unsettling. Practice spending time in quiet environments. Learn to be comfortable without constant auditory input.

**Prepare for the visceral strangeness to accumulate.** The warm stone, the geometric fractures, the shifting distances, the cast objects that suggest pattern without revealing it—these will compound over time. Keep your documentation detailed. Write down what seems odd each day. This serves two purposes: it helps you track whether you're experiencing perceptual changes, and it gives you an anchor to your own perspective.

**You may see things in the deep tunnels.** Pale things in peripheral vision. Workers report them; reports are filed but not dismissed or validated. Decide now how you'll respond if you experience this. Will you investigate? Flee? Report it? Having a pre-established framework helps prevent panic responses.

**The altitude affects cognition.** Decision-making slows, memory suffers, concentration wavers. Important decisions should be delayed until after acclimatization if possible. Use your documentation—write down your reasoning for decisions so you can review it later when you're thinking more clearly.

## Skills to Develop

**Basic metallurgy and industrial processes**: Even elementary knowledge will help you understand what you're observing. Read about casting, forging, foundry operations, heat treatment. You don't need to be an expert, but recognizing what's normal versus unusual requires baseline knowledge.

**Mechanical aptitude**: The power here is mechanical—engines, shafts, gears, hydraulics, cable systems. Understanding how force is transmitted mechanically will help you navigate the city more safely and understand its operations better.

**Basic geology**: Study volcanic stone, basalt, obsidian, mineral formation. When you encounter the stone that "hasn't fully decided to be solid," you'll need familiar geology as a reference point to articulate how it's different.

**Technical drawing reading**: The workers consult drawings constantly. Being able to interpret technical illustrations, assembly diagrams, and engineering drawings will help you understand the work being done.

**Patience**: Practice slow, methodical work. Practice focusing on process rather than outcome. The rhythm here is patient, measured. Arriving with an urgent mindset will exhaust you psychologically.

## Social and Cultural Preparation

**Learn to communicate through gesture.** The workers speak little while working. Practice non-verbal communication. This also serves as a backup if the altitude affects your breathing to the point where talking is difficult.

**Embrace functional minimalism.** There are no markets, no entertainment, no obvious leisure. Your few possessions should be functional and meaningful. Bring one or two items of pure comfort—a small object from home, a beloved book—but understand that the culture here won't support materialism or leisure activities.

**Prepare to be alone in a crowd.** There are thousands of people, but everyone is focused on their work. The social fabric exists but is woven around shared purpose rather than socializing. If you need frequent social interaction for psychological stability, this will be very difficult.

**Respect the work.** Whatever you think about the unclear objectives, the people here are dedicated to precise, careful work. Dismissiveness or mockery of efforts you don't understand will mark you as an outsider and likely get you ostracized. Ask questions about how, not why. The technical specifics are freely shared; broader purpose questions make people uncomfortable.

## Timing Your Visit

**Plan for a minimum stay of one month**, ideally longer. The first week will be consumed by altitude adjustment. The second week you'll begin functioning minimally. Only in the third and fourth weeks will you start to perceive the deeper patterns, the accumulating strangeness, the way the place reveals itself gradually.

**Understand you may not want to leave.** Or may not be able to—the description is vague about how people arrive ("from below") and makes no mention of anyone departing. Have an exit strategy, but be prepared for it to be irrelevant.

**If possible, time your arrival for after a rain.** The moisture lingers briefly, the cisterns fill, and the mineral scent might be slightly less overwhelming than during the peak dry heat. This is a minor consideration, but every small advantage helps during acclimatization.

## Financial and Legal Preparation

The description makes no mention of currency, trade, or economic systems. This is concerning. How do you obtain food if communal meals are insufficient? How do you acquire additional supplies if something breaks?

**Bring items for trade**: Quality tools, spare rope, extra batteries, medical supplies beyond your own needs, tobacco or other preserved trade goods if you know they're valued (though there's no indication of what's valued here). Small, durable, useful items that could be exchanged.

**Assume money is useless.** Bring no more than minimal cash—it's just weight. Credit cards will obviously be irrelevant.

**Legal documentation**: Copies of identification, passport if relevant, medical information, emergency contacts. Store these in waterproof containers. In a place this strange, proving who you are might matter, or might not matter at all.

**Will and emergency instructions**: Leave information with someone you trust about where you're going (as much as you can describe it) and what should happen if you don't return. Provide a date after which they should assume you're not returning.

## Final Mental Framework

You are preparing to visit a place that exists in the narrow space between the real and the impossible. It follows physics—mostly. It serves a purpose—unclear. It has people, industry, construction—but the end point remains obscure.

**Approach it as an anthropologist, not a tourist.** Observe, document, try to understand without imposing familiar frameworks. The strangeness reveals itself gradually. Patience and attention will teach you more than aggressive questioning.

**Respect the unknown.** The pale things in the tunnels, the topology that seems to shift, the stone that hasn't decided to be solid—these suggest the place is negotiating its own existence. You are visiting something being born. Approach with appropriate caution and wonder.

**Accept that understanding may be incomplete.** You may leave (if you leave) without ever fully grasping the purpose of the cast objects, the nature of the engines, the ultimate goal of the stabilization. This doesn't mean the trip was wasted. Partial understanding of something profound beats complete understanding of something mundane.

**Trust your documentation over your memory.** The altitude affects cognition. The time feels different. Days blend together. Write everything down. Trust what you wrote when you can't trust what you remember.

**Prepare for transformation.** Places like this change people. The altitude, the heat, the silence, the focused work, the accumulating strangeness—these aren't experiences you'll return from unchanged. Consider whether you're ready for that transformation before you go.

## The Journey Itself

No information exists about how to reach the Ascending Foundries. The workers came "from below," but specifics are absent. This presents an enormous problem—you're preparing for a destination without knowing the journey.

**Physical approach**: If the Foundries are accessed by climbing from lower elevations, the approach itself will be arduous and will begin your altitude acclimatization. Pack accordingly for a multi-day climb with camping gear. If access is by some other means (and given the multiverse traveler framing, it might be), the approach conditioning is still valuable—you don't want to arrive already exhausted.

**Staged acclimatization**: If at all possible, spend time at intermediate elevations before arriving at the Foundries. Even a few days at 10,000 feet, then 12,000 feet, will significantly reduce your suffering upon arrival.

**Arrival protocol**: When you arrive, immediately identify your sleeping quarters, water sources, and toilet facilities. Establish these basics before trying to explore or understand. Then rest. Resist the temptation to immediately investigate everything—you need to stabilize physiologically first.

## What Success Looks Like

Success in visiting the Ascending Foundries doesn't mean "seeing everything" or "understanding fully." Success means:

- Surviving the altitude and environment without serious medical crisis
- Maintaining detailed documentation of your observations
- Engaging respectfully with the work and workers
- Remaining psychologically stable amid the strangeness
- Recognizing patterns in the cast objects, the architecture, the stone
- Returning with some understanding, even if incomplete
- Being changed by the experience in ways you can integrate

This is not a tourist destination with a checklist of sights. This is an immersion in an emerging place with its own logic, its own rhythm, its own slowly revealing purpose. You're preparing to be a witness to something that hasn't finished becoming.

Go carefully. Go humbly. Go prepared. And may the altitude be kind to your lungs, the sun to your skin, and the strange forces rising beneath the stone to whatever part of you is seeking this journey.
