# Preparation Guide for the Plateau City of Gathered Water

## Physical Conditioning (Begin 3-6 Months Prior)

**Altitude Acclimatization**
You are preparing to live at high elevation where the air is genuinely thin. If you live at sea level, this is non-negotiable preparation. Spend progressive time at altitude if possible—mountain hiking, visits to elevated regions. Your body needs to increase red blood cell production. Without this, the first weeks will be marked by constant headache, dizziness, nausea, and dangerous fatigue that clouds judgment.

If pre-visit altitude exposure is impossible, plan your arrival carefully. Enter the plateau with the understanding that the first 72 hours will be partially incapacitating. Your breathing will be labored from simple walking. Your sleep will be poor—expect to wake gasping. Mild tasks will exhaust you. Build this degraded capacity into your initial plans. Do not attempt long walks or complex navigation until acclimatization begins.

**Cardiovascular Endurance**
The distances here will break you if you arrive unprepared. "Walking all day" in normal conditions is different from walking all day at altitude where each step requires more oxygen than your blood can efficiently deliver. Train for long-distance walking—not hiking with elevation gain, but sustained horizontal distance. Work up to 15-20 mile days. Your legs need to know how to continue when everything feels repetitive and your mind grows numb to the motion.

**Cold Tolerance**
Even summer brings temperatures that drop sharply after sunset. Your body will lose heat rapidly in the thin air. Practice extended exposure to cold—not extreme cold, but the persistent chill that never quite lifts. Your metabolism will work harder here. You'll burn more calories staying warm than you expect.

## Medical Preparation

**Altitude Sickness Prophylaxis**
Consult a physician familiar with high-altitude medicine. Consider acetazolamide (Diamox) for the first days. Understand the progression of altitude sickness: mild (headache, nausea, fatigue), moderate (severe headache, vomiting, weakness), severe (confusion, inability to walk, fluid in lungs or brain). Know that severe altitude sickness can kill you, and the only cure is immediate descent.

Carry dexamethasone for emergency treatment of severe symptoms. Understand when to use it—this is your "something has gone seriously wrong" medication.

**Respiratory Health**
The dry, thin air will damage your respiratory system if it's compromised. Any chronic bronchial conditions, asthma, or reduced lung capacity becomes significantly more serious here. If you smoke, stop—months before arrival. Your lungs need to be operating at full capacity.

**Dental Work**
Atmospheric pressure changes can cause problems with compromised teeth. Get a thorough dental examination. Have any questionable fillings or root canals addressed. A tooth abscess at this elevation, far from any medical care, in extreme cold and dryness, could become a crisis.

**Dermatological Concerns**
The intense UV radiation at altitude will damage your skin in ways you haven't experienced. Any tendency toward cold sores will manifest here—the combination of UV exposure, physical stress, and temperature extremes triggers outbreaks. Carry appropriate antivirals. Bring serious sun protection—SPF 50+ for any exposed skin, lip balm with high SPF, physical sun barriers.

**Comprehensive First Aid Knowledge**
You need more than a first aid kit—you need deep practical knowledge. Take a wilderness first responder course, minimum. Learn to treat: frostbite and frostnip, various stages of hypothermia, severe dehydration, wound care and infection management in cold dry conditions, sprains and fractures with improvised materials, burns (from both cold and your cooking fire).

The silence and isolation mean no help is coming. You are your own medical system.

## Practical Skills to Develop

**Stone-Fire Craft**
There is virtually no wood here. The sparse vegetation cannot be sustainably harvested for fuel. You must bring fuel or learn to create heat with minimal combustible material. Practice with twig fires, with dried dung (if fauna exist, their droppings will be your primary fuel source), with conserving fire once established. Learn to cook and warm yourself with the absolute minimum.

Better: develop cold-food protocols. Learn to eat and find satisfaction in meals that require no heating. Your body will adjust, though it takes psychological preparation.

**Water Assessment**
Not all basin water will be safe. Learn to evaluate water quality by observation—clarity, smell, the presence of algae or insect larvae (actually a good sign—it means the water supports life). Understand that cold water can still harbor parasites and bacteria, though less than warm water.

Bring multiple water purification methods: filter, chemical treatment, UV light if you can power it. Redundancy is essential. A broken filter or lost iodine tablets could end your visit.

**Minimalist Navigation**
GPS may work here, but batteries fail in extreme cold, and the vast similarity of the landscape defeats electronic waypoints. Learn deep navigation: reading landscape at scale, maintaining direction by sun and star positions, pacing distance accurately, creating memorable mental maps using subtle terrain features.

Practice in large, relatively featureless terrain. Learn to navigate when everything looks the same. Understand that getting lost here doesn't mean danger in the next hour—it means you might walk for days in wrong directions, depleting resources, unable to find your exit point.

**Weather Reading**
Your survival depends on reading subtle atmospheric signs. Learn to predict weather from: cloud formations at different altitudes, changes in wind direction and strength, pressure changes (felt in your sinuses, seen in how sound carries), the particular quality of light at different times of day, temperature shifts and what they indicate.

The plateau has its own weather patterns. Plan to spend your first weeks observing, learning the local signs before trusting your predictions.

**Psychological Endurance**
The silence and scale will affect your mind in ways that surprise you. Practice extended solitude before arriving. Spend days alone without digital distraction, without conversation, without the ambient noise of human civilization. See how your mind responds.

Some people experience profound peace. Others experience creeping anxiety or dissociation. Know which you are. If isolation destabilizes you, this place could be dangerous—not physically, but psychologically, which can lead to poor decisions that become physical dangers.

## Equipment Considerations

**Clothing System**
Bring layers designed for: extreme UV protection, wind that never fully stops, persistent dampness from mist, temperature swings from near-freezing nights to merely cold days, physical activity that generates sweat you cannot afford to soak your insulation.

Wool and synthetic materials. Nothing cotton. A waterproof/breathable outer layer that actually breathes—cheap rain gear will trap moisture and leave you cold and wet from your own condensation. A warm hat that covers your ears. Face protection from wind and sun. Multiple pairs of socks and the discipline to change them, keeping one pair dry for sleeping.

Good boots—waterproof, insulated enough for cold but not so heavily insulated that your feet sweat, broken in until they're an extension of your body. Bring boot repair supplies. A sole separation or broken lacing system becomes a serious problem.

**Shelter System**
A tent designed for: sustained wind, possible snow load, extreme UV degradation of fabric, the psychological toll of spending night after night in confined space. A four-season tent minimum. Practice setting it up in wind, in darkness, in cold that makes your fingers clumsy.

A sleeping system rated for colder than you expect. A quality sleeping pad with high R-value—you lose more heat to the cold stone beneath you than to the air above. Consider bringing two pads for additional insulation and comfort across weeks or months.

**Food Strategy**
High-altitude metabolism burns more calories. Cold burns more calories. Long-distance walking burns more calories. Plan for 4,000-5,000 calories per day, possibly more. You cannot carry enough food for an extended stay—plan resupply caches if you're doing serious exploration, or plan a shorter visit than you romantically imagine.

High-fat, high-calorie foods. Nuts, dried fruit, chocolate, cheese that keeps, dense crackers, oil. Your appetite may be suppressed by altitude initially—force yourself to eat. Dehydration mimics hunger; constant sipping of water is essential.

The sparse plants are barely edible and cannot sustain you. The rare fauna cannot be reliably hunted and should not be—you're a visitor in their already-marginal ecosystem. Bring your calories.

**Hygiene and Waste Management**
Cold and dryness mean you'll sweat less, but hygiene still matters. Bring: biodegradable soap for minimal washing (you'll mostly be cleaning hands and face—full bathing is impractical), a system for managing human waste that leaves no trace (burial is difficult in stone terrain; pack-it-out systems are more reliable), dental care supplies, feminine hygiene products if applicable (cold and altitude can affect menstrual cycles unpredictably).

Your waste management must be impeccable. The place is pristine. Leave it that way.

**Documentation Tools**
If you're inclined to record your experience: a notebook that functions in cold and damp, pencils rather than pens (ink freezes or fails), a camera that handles temperature extremes (keep batteries warm against your body), possibly audio recording equipment for capturing the subtle sounds.

But consider going without. The place may offer something that documentation interrupts. You'll know when you're there.

## Mental Preparation

**Expectation Management**
You will not "conquer" this place. You will not fully "understand" it. Let go of achievement orientation. The plateau offers participation, not completion. There is no summit to reach, no challenge to overcome, no goal to accomplish. Westerners especially struggle with this—we want the place to test us so we can prove ourselves. The plateau doesn't care about your proving.

Prepare to simply be present. To walk. To observe. To participate in something operating at scales that make your presence essentially irrelevant—and to find peace rather than diminishment in that irrelevance.

**Time Reorientation**
Your watch will become meaningless, though you should still track time for practical reasons (navigation, rationing). But psychological time will slow and stretch. Days will blur together. The repetitive landscape and repetitive activities create a trance state. This is not a problem to fix—it's a state to allow.

Practice beforehand: spend days without checking time, without scheduling, without the compartmentalized structure of modern life. See if you can tolerate unstructured time, or if it makes you anxious.

**Confronting Insignificance**
The scale here reduces you. You are a small mammal on an enormous plateau, surrounded by construction that has existed for potentially thousands of years and will exist for thousands more regardless of your presence. Some people find this liberating—the pressure of self-importance falls away. Others find it destabilizing or depressing.

Consider which you are. If your sense of meaning depends on feeling significant, central, impactful, this place will challenge you. Come prepared to find meaning in participation rather than importance, in witnessing rather than acting, in being part of something vast rather than being the center of your own story.

**Solitude vs. Loneliness**
These are different states. Solitude is chosen, peaceful, restorative. Loneliness is unchosen, painful, depleting. You will experience profound solitude here—possibly the most complete solitude available in the multiverse. Whether it transmutes into loneliness depends partly on your preparation.

Bring internal resources: memory of loved ones (some bring photos, though this is personal), internal dialogues that are nourishing rather than critical, creative or intellectual projects that exist entirely in your mind (composing music, solving mathematical problems, building elaborate memory palaces), practices like meditation or prayer if those are part of your framework.

The silence will amplify whatever internal voices you bring. Make sure they're voices you can live with.

## Specific Scenario Preparations

**If You Get Lost**
And you will, probably, at least briefly. The protocol: stop walking immediately. Sit down. Drink water. Eat something sweet. Let the panic pass—give it fifteen minutes. Assess: When did you last know your location? What direction were you traveling? What landmarks do you remember? Can you backtrack?

If backtracking isn't possible, establish a shelter in place. The construction is everywhere—find walls to block wind, use your tent. Wait for clear weather and navigate by sun/stars. The plateau is vast but not infinite. Walking consistently in one direction will eventually reach an edge, though it might take days.

Most importantly: do not panic-walk. Panicked walking covers ground rapidly in unknown directions, depleting energy and resources. Slow, considered movement or strategic waiting almost always ends better.

**If Weather Traps You**
Severe storms can make navigation impossible and dangerous. If you see weather coming (and you should learn to see it coming), establish shelter before it arrives. A full day sitting in your tent waiting out weather is boring but safe. Attempting to "push through" risks hypothermia, disorientation, and depleted resources.

Bring entertainment for enforced shelter time: books, journal writing, mental exercises. A storm day is a rest day. Accept it.

**If You're Injured**
Minor injuries you treat and continue. Moderate injuries might end your exploration—a sprained ankle means slow, careful progress back to wherever you entered. Severe injuries... are very serious.

This is the fundamental risk. A broken leg, a severe illness, a crisis that leaves you immobile—you will not be found quickly. There is no cell service, no satellite rescue, no helpful passersby. Consider bringing a personal locator beacon if your home reality has satellite infrastructure that reaches this location. Otherwise, understand that self-rescue is your only rescue.

This isn't meant to discourage—only to ensure you understand what "remote" means.

**If You Encounter Other Visitors**
The place seems empty, but you might not be alone. If you see other human figures: approach slowly, non-threateningly, with visible empty hands. They may be as startled as you. Or they might be regular visitors who know the plateau well—potential sources of knowledge and guidance.

If you see non-human figures, or figures that might be human but behave strangely: observe without approaching. The mention of "other figures moving between cairns" at dawn suggests you might encounter something. Keep distance. Show respect. Don't assume communication is possible or desired. Don't assume intentions are comprehensible.

If something feels wrong, trust that feeling. Leave the area calmly but decisively.

## Philosophical Preparation

**Why Are You Going?**
This matters. If you're seeking adventure, challenge, achievement—the plateau will frustrate those seeks. It offers no adrenaline, no conquerable obstacles, no bragging rights. If you're fleeing something, understand that you bring yourself with you. The silence will not erase your problems; it will give them space to echo.

Good reasons for going: curiosity about construction at this scale, desire to understand water-gathering systems, need for deep solitude, interest in sparse ecosystems, attraction to minimalist beauty, longing to exist at a different pace, readiness to learn what the place teaches rather than imposing what you want to learn.

Be honest with yourself about motivation. The plateau will reveal whether your reasons can sustain you.

**What Will You Do There?**
Have some answers, but hold them loosely. Daily activities might include: long slow walks between structures, sketching or photographing cairns and basins, sitting in one location for hours observing weather, tracing water flow through the construction, studying plant and animal life, navigating to distant features visible on the horizon, simply existing in silence.

But plans will change. The place will suggest its own activities. Be ready to follow its suggestions rather than imposing your itinerary.

**How Will You Know When to Leave?**
Some visitors stay days, some weeks, some longer. Know your limits—of supplies, of psychological endurance, of whatever commitments wait in your home reality. But also stay open to the possibility that the right duration reveals itself. You might arrive planning two weeks and know after four days that you're complete. Or you might plan a week and find yourself extending, extending, reluctant to return.

Set a hard limit based on supplies and obligations, but within that limit, let the experience guide its own duration.

## Final Preparations

**Test Everything**
Take a shakedown trip to cold, high, remote conditions. Test your entire system: gear, skills, psychological readiness. Find the failures in controlled conditions. Adjust. Test again. Do not let the plateau be your first experience with your equipment, your navigation skills, your solo camping system.

**Physical Examination**
Get a comprehensive physical shortly before departure. Ensure you're healthy enough for the stress. Bring copies of important medical information, blood type, allergies, medications. If you have chronic conditions requiring medication, bring triple the supply you think you need.

**Notification Protocol**
Someone in your home reality must know where you're going and when to expect you back. Set a clear deadline: "If you haven't heard from me by [specific date], I am overdue." Give them information about the plateau's location and characteristics. Make arrangements for rescue or recovery if you don't return—though understand that rescue might be impossible and recovery might be... what it is.

This is morbid but necessary. You're going somewhere genuinely remote and potentially dangerous.

**Financial and Legal Arrangements**
If you're planning an extended stay, ensure your affairs are in order. Bills paid, responsibilities covered, relationships and obligations addressed. The plateau exists outside your normal life's demands, but those demands will resume on your return—or need to be managed if you don't return.

**Gradual Withdrawal**
In the weeks before departure, begin pulling back from digital stimulus, from constant connectivity, from the noise of normal life. Let your nervous system start adjusting to quieter input. Read about silence, about vastness, about construction and water and stone. Let your mind begin orienting toward what awaits.

But don't over-romanticize. The plateau is not a spiritual retreat center with comfortable accommodations and guided meditations. It's a harsh, cold, thin-aired place that will tax your body and potentially challenge your mind. Respect it as a genuine environment with genuine dangers, not as a metaphor or a backdrop for your personal journey.

## What You're Actually Preparing For

Ultimately, you're preparing to be small and cold and tired, walking through silent stone fields, drinking from ancient basins, watching weather move across distances your home reality might not contain, participating briefly in a system that operated long before you arrived and will operate long after you leave.

You're preparing to be uncomfortable, probably often. To be uncertain, to be lost and then found, to be insignificant, to be present without purpose, to witness without understanding.

You're preparing to let something that works at geological timescales and continental distances teach you whatever it teaches—which might be profound or might be simple or might be nothing that translates into words.

You're preparing to walk all day and cover less distance than seems possible. To hear silence that contains sound. To see distance that defeats intuition. To drink water that fell as mist and was caught by stones placed by something, sometime, for reasons that remain mysterious.

The plateau doesn't care if you're prepared. It will receive you regardless, accommodate you into its cycles, and release you when you leave. But preparation determines whether that reception is survivable, whether that accommodation is sustainable, whether that release comes as planned or in ways you didn't anticipate.

Prepare thoroughly. Prepare practically. Prepare for the place as it is, not as you imagine it to be. Then go, if you're called to go, and let the vast field of gathered water show you what it shows those who come prepared to receive it.
