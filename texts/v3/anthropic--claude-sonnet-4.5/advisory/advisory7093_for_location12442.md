# Preparation Guide for the Drowned Switchyard

## Essential Understanding

You are preparing to enter a liminal industrial space that has achieved a kind of perfection through abandonment. This is not a ruin in distress—it is a ruin in equilibrium. Your preparation must respect this balance while acknowledging that you are a warm-blooded mammal entering an environment calibrated for cold-blooded patience.

## Physical Preparations

### Thermal Management (Critical)

The 52-53°F constant temperature is your primary adversary. This is the temperature that extracts heat through patience rather than aggression. You won't feel dangerously cold immediately, which is precisely the danger.

**Wetsuit or dry suit** (3mm minimum, 5mm preferred): This is non-negotiable. You will be wading through water, potentially for hours. The water will wick heat from your legs relentlessly. A wetsuit rated for 50-60°F water is essential. If you choose a drysuit, ensure you have proper thermal underlayers—merino wool or synthetic, never cotton.

**Neoprene boots** with good traction: The submerged floor is uneven, covered in rails, broken concrete, and machinery. You need ankle support, thermal protection, and soles that grip both wet metal and algae-covered stone. Consider boots rated for canyoneering or water rescue work.

**Thermal layers for your upper body**: Even if you're not submerged above the waist, the air is saturated with moisture. Wear merino wool or synthetic base layers, a mid-layer fleece, and a water-resistant outer shell. Bring a spare dry layer in a waterproof bag—if you stay more than two hours, you'll want to change.

**Neoprene gloves**: Your hands will be touching wet metal, cold stone, and will be constantly exposed to the damp air. Thin neoprene gloves (2mm) allow dexterity while preventing the creeping numbness that will otherwise develop in your fingers.

**Thermal hat or hood**: You lose significant heat through your head, and in this environment, that loss is constant. A wool or synthetic hat that can get wet without losing insulating properties is essential.

**Chemical hand warmers**: Keep several in waterproof bags. If you begin to lose core temperature, these can be critical. Place them against major blood vessels (armpits, groin, neck) if needed.

### Navigation and Safety Equipment

**Lighting system (triple redundancy)**:
- **Primary headlamp**: LED, minimum 400 lumens, with red light mode to preserve night vision. Waterproof rating of IPX7 minimum. Fresh batteries, plus complete spare set.
- **Secondary headlamp**: Around your neck or in an immediately accessible pocket.
- **Backup handheld flashlight**: Waterproof, floating preferred.
- **Chemical light sticks**: As absolute backup. They don't fail, don't need batteries, and provide gentle light for hours.

Test all lights before entering. In this environment, losing your light source means being in nearly complete darkness in water with an uneven floor and countless obstacles.

**Rope or paracord** (50+ feet): For maintaining connection to known reference points, for measuring depth, for emergency use. Bright color—you need to see it in dim light.

**Waterproof navigation tools**:
- Compass (analog, doesn't need batteries)
- Waterproof notebook and pencil for mapping
- Dive slate if you're familiar with them
- Consider a simple ball of bright surveyor's tape to mark your route in—one strip every 30 feet means you can follow it back out

**Whistle**: Waterproof, loud. For emergency signaling if you're with others or if there's any chance of surface contact.

**Personal flotation device** (PFD/life vest): If you cannot confirm water depth throughout the space. The center sump might be 10 feet deep. An inflatable PFD worn as a belt is minimal bulk with maximum insurance.

**First aid kit** (waterproof): 
- Treatment for hypothermia (emergency blanket, chemical warmers)
- Supplies for cuts and punctures (rust and bacteria-rich environment)
- Waterproof bandages
- Antibiotic ointment
- Pain medication
- Any personal medications

**Communication device**: Waterproof radio or phone in a waterproof case. Cell service is unlikely but not impossible. If with a group, waterproof radios are essential.

### Contamination Protection

**Water quality concerns**: The water is described as mineral-rich, ancient, chemically balanced—but you are not part of that balance. The water contains:
- Dissolved metals (rust, possibly heavy metals from industrial use)
- Bacteria adapted to this environment
- Possible chemical contamination from decades of industrial use
- Fungi and organic matter in decomposition

**Protective measures**:
- Consider hip waders over your wetsuit if you want an extra barrier
- Avoid putting your face near the water
- Do not drink the water under any circumstances
- If you have any open wounds, cover them with waterproof dressings
- Bring water purification tablets—if you're trapped and desperate, at least give yourself a fighting chance

**Post-visit decontamination**: Plan to shower thoroughly with antibacterial soap. Consider bringing a spray bottle of diluted bleach solution to disinfect your boots and equipment before transporting them. Wash all clothing separately in hot water. Monitor any cuts or scrapes for signs of infection over the following week.

**Tetanus status**: Ensure your tetanus vaccination is current. If it's been more than 5 years, get a booster before visiting. This environment is essentially an industrial rust garden.

## Physiological Preparations

### Thermal Acclimation

If possible, spend time in cold water (swimming pools, cold showers) in the weeks before. Learn what your body's warning signs for hypothermia feel like:
- Shivering (early sign—if you're shivering, you need to warm up soon)
- Confusion or difficulty thinking clearly
- Numbness in extremities
- Fatigue or desire to sleep

**Establish a hard time limit before you enter**: Based on your cold tolerance, the water temperature, and your thermal protection, decide how long you'll stay. Two hours maximum for a first visit, even with good gear. Set a watch alarm.

### Sensory Preparation

**Vision**: Your eyes will need to adapt to extremely low light. Consider:
- Avoiding bright screens for an hour before entering
- Allowing 20-30 minutes after entering for full dark adaptation
- Being aware that the space will seem darker when you first enter than after your eyes adjust

**Proprioception**: The uneven submerged floor, the disorienting reflections, and the dim light will challenge your sense of balance and spatial orientation. If you have balance issues, this location is dangerous for you. Consider:
- Practicing walking on uneven surfaces with your eyes closed or in dim light
- Using a walking stick or pole to probe ahead (collapsible trekking poles work well)
- Moving slowly and deliberately—speed is not your friend here

**Time distortion**: The description makes clear that time sense becomes unreliable. Set multiple alarms on your waterproof watch:
- One at 30 minutes (checkpoint: how are you feeling?)
- One at 60 minutes (serious evaluation: core temp okay? Exit strategy clear?)
- One at 90 minutes (time to begin exiting)
- One at 2 hours (hard exit deadline)

### Psychological Preparation

This is not a place of terror, but it is profoundly otherworldly. Prepare yourself for:

**Extended isolation in an alien environment**: Even if you go with others, the acoustic properties and low light will isolate you psychologically. If you have claustrophobia, agoraphobia, or severe discomfort in strange spaces, this location may trigger distress.

**The uncanny**: Axolotls making sounds they shouldn't make. Industrial hermit crabs. Spaces that should be empty but are full of strange life. Your rational mind knows it's not supernatural, but your limbic system may not care. Practice rational observation:
- Name what you see: "That's an axolotl, a salamander, adapted to this environment"
- Breathe deliberately: 4 counts in, 4 counts hold, 4 counts out
- Remember: this ecosystem has existed in balance for decades without you

**The temptation to stay longer than you should**: The place is described as having a strange pull, a sense of being outside time. Your time limits are physiological necessities, not suggestions. Commit to someone outside that you'll return by a specific time, and commit internally to honoring that.

## Logistical Preparations

### Entry and Exit Strategy

**Never enter alone** on your first visit. Have at least one person, preferably two:
- One to accompany you
- One to remain near the entrance with timing awareness and emergency readiness

**Surface support person**: If your visit is longer than 30 minutes, consider leaving someone on the surface with:
- Knowledge of when you entered
- Instruction to alert emergency services if you're overdue by more than one hour
- Description of the space and entry point
- Cell phone numbers, though signal is unlikely below

**Scout the entrance in daylight** if possible:
- Identify the access point
- Note any hazards in the approach
- Establish where daylight ends
- Mark the entrance clearly so you can find it on exit

**Exit plan before entry**:
- Know where you came in
- Know your route back
- If you explore laterally, maintain awareness of your entry direction
- Consider the rope/tape trail system for complex navigation

### Timing Your Visit

**Time of day considerations**:
The description mentions light shafts moving with the sun, and fluorescent tubes on unpredictable schedules. Your visit timing affects:

- **Midday**: Maximum natural light through the ventilation shafts, but still quite dim
- **Dawn/dusk**: Less natural light, but potentially beautiful when shafts are angular
- **Night**: Artificial lighting only, but the space may feel more authentically itself

**Seasonal considerations**:
- Water temperature likely constant year-round (groundwater)
- Air temperature might vary slightly—winter visits might mean colder air above the water
- Surface conditions (ice, snow) might affect access or emergency extraction
- Spring might mean more water flow through drainage channels

**Weather considerations**:
- Heavy rain could affect water levels—avoid visiting during or immediately after significant rainfall
- Flooding risk: research the area's hydrology
- Thunder/lightning: if there's any metal infrastructure connecting to the surface, the space could be dangerous

## Skills to Develop Beforehand

### Water Navigation
Practice walking in water with uneven footing—streams, flooded areas, shallow pools with rocks. Learn to:
- Test footing before committing weight
- Maintain balance in unstable conditions
- Move efficiently without splashing excessively (respecting the space)

### Low-Light Operation
Practice using your lighting setup in dark conditions:
- Can you change batteries in near-darkness?
- Can you find items in your dry bag without full light?
- Can you navigate by headlamp while keeping hands free?

### Cold Water Exposure
If possible, practice in similar conditions:
- Wear your thermal gear in cold water
- Learn how long you stay comfortable
- Recognize your early warning signs of cold stress

### Navigation Fundamentals
- Basic compass work
- Maintaining sense of direction in featureless environments
- Pacing (counting steps to measure distance)
- Mapping as you go

## Medical Considerations

### Pre-existing Conditions That Increase Risk
Consult a doctor if you have:
- Cardiovascular issues (cold stress increases heart workload)
- Respiratory conditions (damp, mineral-rich air may irritate)
- Immune system compromise (infection risk from water)
- Balance or mobility issues (fall risk)
- Diabetes (cold affects blood sugar, and you need steady energy)
- Raynaud's syndrome or poor circulation (cold exposure dangerous)

### Medications and Supplements
- Bring any regular medications in waterproof container
- Consider anti-inflammatory (ibuprofen) for muscle strain
- Consider anti-diarrheal in case of accidental water ingestion
- Antihistamine if you're sensitive to mold or fungi

### Post-Visit Monitoring
Watch for 7-10 days after:
- Respiratory issues (inhaled fungi or bacteria)
- Skin infections (especially around any cuts or scrapes)
- Gastrointestinal distress (if any water was swallowed)
- Fever or flu-like symptoms
- Unusual rashes

Seek medical care if anything develops and mention the environment you were in.

## Behavioral Guidelines

### Respecting the Ecosystem

This place has achieved balance. You are a disruption. Minimize impact:

**Movement**: 
- Move slowly, deliberately
- Avoid splashing unnecessarily
- Don't touch the axolotls (they have delicate skin and you have contaminants on your hands)
- Don't disturb the crabs in their mechanical shells
- Stay clear of the willow and other vegetation
- Don't touch the moss colonies—they've taken years to grow

**Waste**:
- Pack out everything you bring in
- No food waste, no wrappers, nothing left behind
- If you must relieve yourself (on a longer visit), do so at the entrance/exit area before fully entering

**Noise**:
- Speak quietly if with others
- No music or artificial sounds
- Be conscious that you are the loudest thing present
- Honor the acoustic space

**Light**:
- Use minimal light necessary
- Avoid shining lights directly at roosting bats
- Be aware that you're disrupting the darkness the fauna has adapted to

### Scientific Observation

Bring a waterproof camera if you wish to document, but:
- No flash photography (respects wildlife and the character of the space)
- Consider whether you need to share the location publicly—some places are better preserved by obscurity
- Take only photographs, leave only footprints (and minimize even those)

Consider taking notes on:
- Water temperature (bring a thermometer)
- Air temperature and humidity
- Species observed and behaviors
- Changes in light over time
- Acoustic phenomena
- Emotional/psychological responses

## Emergency Protocols

### If Someone Becomes Hypothermic
- Get them out of the water immediately
- Remove wet outer layers if possible
- Wrap in emergency blanket
- Apply chemical warmers to core (armpits, groin, neck—not directly on skin)
- Give warm, sweet drinks if they're conscious and coherent
- Begin moving toward exit
- Monitor consciousness and coherence
- Call emergency services if condition doesn't improve rapidly

### If Someone Is Injured
- Assess injury severity
- Apply first aid as trained
- Waterproof bandaging in this environment is critical
- Consider whether they can self-evacuate or need assistance
- If serious: send one person out to call emergency services while one stays with injured
- Keep injured person warm (hypothermia risk increases when immobile)

### If You Become Lost or Disoriented
- Stop moving immediately
- Sit down on the most stable surface available (platform if possible)
- Turn off all lights and let eyes dark-adapt fully (10-15 minutes)
- Listen for:
  - Direction of flowing water (leads to drainage channels, which lead to exit)
  - Any surface sounds (traffic, weather) from ventilation shafts
  - Sounds of your companion if separated
- Shine light on water surface and look for reflections—might show passage routes
- Use whistle to signal if with others
- If you have rope, tie one end to current location and explore in careful radius, then return to center
- If truly lost, stay put and wait—your surface support will eventually send help

### If Water Level Rises
- Move immediately to highest platform accessible
- Monitor rate of rise
- Begin moving toward exit if rise is rapid
- If trapped on platform, stay put and signal with whistle/light
- Water level rise should be slow (groundwater, not flash flood) but remain vigilant

### If Infrastructure Fails
- That groaning sound is normal; sudden cracking or metal screaming is not
- If you hear/feel structural failure beginning:
  - Move away from the sound source
  - Seek cover under most solid-looking overhead structure (reinforced concrete better than steel beams)
  - Protect head
  - Once immediate danger passes, evacuate directly—no exploration
  - Report structural failure to local authorities (risk to others)

## What to Expect Psychologically

Based on the description, you should prepare for:

**Temporal distortion**: You will lose track of time. Your alarms are not paranoia—they're essential anchors to the surface world and its demands (like not freezing to death).

**Scale confusion**: The space is large but enclosed. The darkness obscures boundaries. You may feel simultaneously trapped and lost in vastness. This is disorienting but normal.

**Hyper-awareness**: The quiet means every sound is noticeable. You'll hear your heartbeat, your breath, the water displaced by your movement. This can be meditative or unsettling depending on your relationship with silence.

**Presence of the other**: The axolotls especially, with their alien sounds and their abundance, create a sense that you're a visitor in someone else's home. This is accurate. Move as a respectful guest.

**Attraction**: The space may pull at you, make you want to stay, make you want to return. This is the allure of liminal spaces, of places between. Respect this feeling but don't be controlled by it—your body's thermal limits are not negotiable.

**Insignificance**: A space this old, this balanced, this indifferent to human presence can make you feel very small. For some this is terrifying; for others it's liberating. Know which you are.

## Advanced Considerations for Extended Visits

If you're planning a visit longer than 2 hours (not recommended for first visit):

### Thermal Management Escalation
- Chemical warming packs are not sufficient for longer visits
- Consider a wetsuit with battery-powered heating elements (expensive but effective)
- Plan mandatory warm-up breaks: exit to entrance area, dry off, consume hot fluids, rewarm, then re-enter
- Have complete dry clothing changes available

### Nutrition and Hydration
- Cold stress burns calories rapidly
- Bring energy-dense foods (nuts, chocolate, energy bars)
- Maintain hydration even though you don't feel thirsty (cold suppresses thirst)
- Warm water in a thermos helps maintain core temperature

### Documentation
- Waterproof audio recorder for notes
- Underwater camera (even though you're not diving, the water is everywhere)
- Waterproof sketchbook if you're an artist

### Research Preparation
- Study axolotl behavior and vocalizations (are they documented making sounds?)
- Research cave-adapted fish of your region
- Learn about hermit crab behavior and adaptation
- Review industrial archaeology—understanding what the machinery was for enriches the visit

## Special Note on Axolotl Interactions

The description emphasizes their abundance and strangeness. Important facts:

- Axolotls have extremely sensitive skin—any contact transfers oils, contaminants, and can damage their protective mucus layer
- They're neotenic (permanently juvenile) salamanders, which may contribute to their adaptability to this environment
- Their sounds, if real, would be scientifically significant—axolotls are not documented as vocal
- If you observe them systematically, you might be documenting something new
- Do not attempt to catch, touch, or disturb them
- They seem comfortable with human presence if you're quiet and still—you can observe them closely if you're patient

## The Intangible Preparation

Finally, and perhaps most importantly: prepare yourself to be changed.

This is not a tourist destination. It's not a theme park or a museum. It's a liminal space, a place that has transcended its original purpose and become something other. It's beautiful in a way that has nothing to do with prettiness. It's alive in a way that has nothing to do with human life.

You will wade through cold water in darkness, surrounded by strange creatures making impossible sounds, under tons of rusted metal and weeping concrete, in a place that was built for purposes that no longer exist, and you will either find that terrifying or transcendent or both.

Prepare your body with gear and knowledge. Prepare your mind with meditation and research. But also prepare your spirit—whatever that means to you—to encounter something genuinely other, genuinely strange, genuinely outside the normal flow of human experience.

And then, when you're warm and dry again in the sunlight above, when you're back in the world of deadlines and traffic and bright screens, some part of you will still be down there, standing on a platform, watching purple axolotls drift through black water, listening to crickets sing to the darkness, existing in that cold, patient, alien equilibrium.

That's what you're preparing for.

That's what you can't really prepare for.

Go carefully. Go respectfully. Come back safely.

And if the space allows it, let yourself be astonished.
