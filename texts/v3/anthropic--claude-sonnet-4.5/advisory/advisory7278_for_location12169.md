# Preparation Guide for Visiting the Woven Hollows

## Before You Go: Psychological Preparation

**Understand what you're surrendering.** The Woven Hollows will take from you, gently but completely, your reliance on causality, linear time, and spatial consistency. Begin practicing now: sit with cognitive dissonance. When you see something impossible in your daily life—a strange coincidence, a déjà vu, a moment when time seems to skip—don't rationalize it away. Let it be strange. Get comfortable with strangeness.

**Practice non-attachment to narrative.** Your mind will desperately try to create stories: who built this place? Where are the builders? What is the thing beneath the platforms? These questions have no answers there, or they have answers that contradict each other, or the answers change each time you ask. Start now: spend time in experiences without clear beginning, middle, or end. Sit in waiting rooms. Watch clouds. Let events happen without needing them to *mean* something.

**Prepare for loneliness that isn't loneliness.** You'll be alone but surrounded. The isolation is absolute; the presence is constant. These aren't contradictory there. Meditate on this koan. Practice being with yourself while aware you're being observed—not by anyone in particular, just observed by the act of existing in a space.

**Accept that you cannot prepare.** This is the first and last lesson. Everything that follows is technical detail, physical preparation, mental exercises—but none of it will truly ready you. The Woven Hollows is a place that defeats preparation by being precisely what it is, which cannot be anticipated. Go anyway. The unreadiness is part of what you bring.

## Physical Conditioning

**Heat acclimation is essential.** Spend weeks, ideally months, in hot humid environments. Not dry heat—you need the oppressive weight of moisture-saturated air. Go to greenhouses. Visit tropical botanical gardens. Sit in steam rooms until the heat becomes boring rather than challenging. Your body needs to learn that blood-warm, moisture-thick air is simply air, not an emergency.

But understand: the heat there doesn't change. Your body's circadian rhythms expect temperature variation. Without it, your sleep cycle will collapse, your appetite will become confused, your sense of time passing will degrade. There's no full preparation for this, but heat acclimation helps. At minimum, you'll arrive already understanding that sweat-soaked clothing is just clothing, that dripping constantly is just existing.

**Foot conditioning matters more than general fitness.** You'll walk on woven surfaces—not solid ground. The lattice gives slightly, unevenly. It requires constant micro-adjustments in balance and gait. Practice walking on uneven surfaces: fallen logs, rope bridges, suspension walkways, cargo nets laid horizontally. Go barefoot when possible. Your feet need to become intelligent, sensitive, capable of reading texture and tension through wood and fiber.

The pools will be unavoidable. You'll wade through ankle-deep water, knee-deep water, across submerged sections of lattice slippery with moss. Practice walking through water while maintaining balance. Practice not minding wet feet. Western footwear will be worse than useless—the drainage ports will clog with plant matter, the materials will rot, they'll provide false confidence on slippery surfaces. Go barefoot, or use minimalist sandals that can be kicked off instantly.

**Build stillness endurance.** The Woven Hollows is a place of profound inactivity. You'll stand and look at things. You'll sit on platforms for hours. Motion happens, but slowly, at the pace of consideration rather than action. If you're accustomed to constant movement, constant stimulation, the stillness will feel like suffocation.

Practice: sit without entertainment for increasing durations. No phone, no book, no music. Just sitting, in a warm place, while nothing happens. Work up to hours. Notice when your mind starts generating false emergencies to create action. Notice the panic of having nothing to do. Breathe through it. The stillness there is not empty—it's full of observation, of small perception, of the slow accumulation of understanding through witnessing. But your nervous system needs to learn that stillness isn't death.

**Strengthen your eyes for brightness.** The light there is relentless and thick. Sunglasses won't help—they'll separate you from the essential experience of how light behaves in that place. Instead, practice being in bright environments without eye protection. Not staring at the sun—don't damage yourself—but getting comfortable with glare, with reflected brightness off water, with the slight ache of too much light. Your pupils need to learn to stay constricted for long periods. Your visual cortex needs to adapt to processing high-brightness environments without washing out all detail.

Paradoxically, also practice seeing in constrained light—twilight, single-candle darkness. The underneath spaces between platforms exist in dim half-light where normal vision doesn't quite work. You need eyes that can adjust to both extremes.

## What to Bring (and What Not to Bring)

**Clothing should be minimal, natural, and sacrificial.** Bring nothing you care about. The constant wetness, the heat, the mysterious absence of decay for everything *except* what you bring—your possessions will rot with supernatural speed while the ancient platforms remain perfect. Expect anything you bring to be destroyed.

Light cotton or linen. A single layer. Loose enough to allow air movement and water drainage. Natural dyes only—synthetics seem to offend that place somehow, degrading even faster. Light colors that won't absorb heat. But even this will become uncomfortable. You'll probably end up wearing nearly nothing, just enough for sun protection and personal modesty if that concerns you.

A wide-brimmed hat, woven, natural fiber. The sun-that-isn't-quite-sun will beat down without variation. You need shade you can carry. But nothing with metal grommets, plastic adjusters, or synthetic cords. The hat will probably dissolve eventually anyway. Bring two.

No shoes, as discussed, or simple sandals—leather or woven fiber, easily replaceable. They'll rot. Accept this.

**Water is unnecessary but bring it anyway.** You won't die of thirst there—the normal urgencies of survival seem suspended, or slowed to match the place's time. Hunger and thirst become distant, manageable, theoretical. But the *ritual* of drinking matters. Your mind needs familiar anchors. Bring a simple container—bamboo, hollowed gourd, ceramic if you can carry it. Not metal, not plastic. Fill it from the pools when you arrive. The water is safe, or safety doesn't apply, or you won't be able to resist drinking it eventually anyway.

**No food.** Don't bring any. Food from outside seems to vanish—not rot, not get eaten, just *vanish*. It disappears from packs, from pockets. The fruits that grow there look edible but you'll probably never eat them. You won't need to. Hunger becomes a distant signal, like a bell heard from far away. Some travelers report never eating, never feeling diminished. Some report plucking fruits that taste like memory, like copper, like colors. Bring nothing. Trust the place or don't, but bringing food is useless.

**A knife, simple, single-blade, fixed.** Not for defense—there's nothing to defend against. Not for tool use—you probably won't make anything. But for the psychological comfort of having capability. Choose one that's beautiful to you. You'll look at it often. It might be the only thing you brought that remains unchanged. Or it might develop a patina you don't understand. Or it might become something else—rounder, softer, less knife-like—and you'll only notice after days (weeks?) when you realize it's been transforming while you weren't looking.

**No electronics. None.** They stop working immediately or slowly, but they all stop. Cameras produce only whiteness—too much light, wrong kind of light, light that exposes the film or sensor to something other than photons. Phones find no signal, obviously, but also their clocks go strange. Hours pass in seconds. Seconds stretch to hours. The batteries drain and recharge according to no pattern. Eventually screens crack for no reason, circuits corrode in the humidity, chips develop faults.

GPS is worse than useless—it will show your position but the position will be wrong, or will be right but also show you in three other locations, or will show you somewhere that doesn't exist on any map, coordinates that place you underground or in mid-air.

Bring nothing electric. Nothing digital. Nothing that relies on the consistency of physical law.

**Writing materials—maybe.** Paper survives poorly. The humidity curls it, browns it, makes ink run. But some travelers report that paper degrades slowly enough to keep a journal, if you're diligent about keeping it wrapped. Use pencil, not ink. Graphite seems more stable there.

But consider: what will you write? "Walked to platform. Saw bird. Pool showed wrong reflection." The events don't narrate well. Description fails. You'll end up with fragments, observations that don't cohere. Which might be exactly the record you need—proof later, when you're home (if you return home, when you return home), that the place resisted even being written about.

Or bring nothing to write with and simply remember. Memory from that place is strange—crystalline in some details, absent in others. You'll recall with perfect clarity the exact pattern of lashing on a specific joint, but not how many days you were there. You'll remember the feel of warm water through the lattice under your feet but forget which direction you walked. The memories will themselves be woven hollows—present, detailed, but not quite coherent.

**Bring almost nothing.** Truly. A hat, a knife, a water container, minimal cloth. That's all. You're visiting a place of essential making, of stripped-down construction, of reality at its most basic assembly. Arriving with a backpack full of gear is both futile and disrespectful—futile because it will fail, disrespectful because it suggests you don't trust the place to provide what it provides, which is itself, completely, with nothing else needed.

## Mental Preparation: Specific Practices

**Learn to see peripherally.** Direct vision lies there. What you see straight-on is probably wrong, or incomplete. But peripheral vision—the corner of your eye, the edge of attention—that's where movement happens, where the presences appear, where the changes occur. Practice now: look at something, anything, and pay attention to everything *around* what you're looking at. Notice the periphery. Trust it more than the center.

This is trainable. Walk through familiar spaces while focusing on peripheral information. You'll be amazed what you've been missing. In the Woven Hollows, this skill becomes essential. The place reveals itself slantwise, in glimpses, in the almost-seen.

**Develop comfort with cognitive dissonance.** Your mind will insist that platform is twenty meters away. Your feet will tell you it took forty meters to reach it. Both are true. Practice holding contradictions without resolving them. Study Zen koans—not to understand them but to get comfortable with questions that have no answer, statements that are true and false simultaneously.

Read about quantum mechanics, about superposition, about particles that are waves and waves that are particles. Not to understand (you won't, not really) but to marinate your worldview in the knowledge that reality is fundamentally paradoxical at its base level. The Woven Hollows is that paradox made manifest, made inhabitable. If paradox breaks your mind, don't go.

**Practice trance states.** Not deep meditation necessarily, but the light trance of highway driving, of repetitive activity, of being absorbed in something while simultaneously aware of your surroundings. The Woven Hollows induces this state naturally—the heat, the stillness, the unchanging light, the subtle wrongness. You'll drift into a consciousness that's both heightened and dreamlike. If you've never experienced this deliberately, it will be disturbing. Practice entering and exiting trance states now, with control, so you recognize the state when the place imposes it on you.

**Contemplate impermanence and permanence simultaneously.** Everything there is ancient and new, rotting and perfect, temporary and eternal. This is not metaphor—it's literal paradox. Spend time with old things that are well-maintained: ancient temples, restored furniture, living traditions. Notice how something can be hundreds of years old and also fresh. Then spend time with new things that seem ancient: certain rocks, certain trees, certain ideas. Get comfortable with temporal ambiguity.

**Prepare for isolation while being watched.** You'll be alone. You'll be absolutely certain you're being observed. Both conditions are true and total. Practice this: be in a private space, doing something private, while imagining (knowing?) that you're being witnessed by something that isn't judging, just attending. The gaze of the universe itself, if you can conceptualize that. Not hostile, not friendly—just aware. If being watched makes you perform, makes you change behavior, work on that now. You need to be yourself while witnessed, because you'll be witnessed constantly there.

## Medical Considerations

**Get fully healthy before you go.** Any existing condition will be complicated by that place's relationship to time and change. Medications need to be taken on schedule—but schedule becomes meaningless. Injuries need to heal—but healing requires time's passage, which is ambiguous there.

If you have chronic conditions requiring regular treatment, seriously reconsider going. Several travelers report that conditions requiring daily medication became unmanageable not because the medication failed but because they couldn't track daily. Days blurred. They'd take medicine twice, or not at all, or lose count. Time-sensitive treatments don't work in a place where time doesn't work properly.

**Dental work, wounds, infections—resolve everything.** Go to the Woven Hollows healthy. A toothache there will be eternal—not worsening, not improving, just present, now and always now, unchanging in its ache. A cut will neither heal nor fester. It will be cut, perpetually, until you leave.

**Pregnant women absolutely should not go.** Pregnancy is a time-sensitive process. Gestation requires linear time, requires change, requires the future arriving on schedule. In a place where time is negotiable, pregnancy becomes conceptually dangerous in ways I can't fully articulate. Just don't. Not until long after birth, until you're entirely separate from time-dependent biology.

**Mental health requires special consideration.** If you have conditions that require chemical stability—depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, anything involving altered perception or mood regulation—understand that the Woven Hollows will stress every coping mechanism you have. The wrongness there isn't psychosis but it rhymes with psychosis. The presences aren't hallucinations but they echo hallucinations. If your grip on consensus reality is already challenged, that place might dissolve it entirely.

This isn't judgment. Some people with these conditions might be *better* prepared—already familiar with reality being flexible, with perception being untrustworthy. But go with clear understanding of risk, with support plans in place, knowing you might not be able to leave quickly if things go wrong.

**Prepare for returning.** Assume you'll be there for an undetermined duration. When you return—if you return, when you return—you'll need re-acclimation. The normal world will seem wrong at first. Solid ground will feel unstable because it doesn't give. Shadows that move will startle you—they're *changing*, something that didn't happen for however long you were there. Clocks will be disturbing—the mechanistic certainty of time's passage will seem like violence.

Plan for a buffer period. Don't return and immediately go back to work, to obligations, to schedule. Give yourself time in a quiet place to let consensus reality re-establish itself. Some travelers report needing weeks. Some report never fully readjusting—they're permanently slightly elsewhere, marked by having been in a place where the rules slipped.

## Navigational Concepts

**There is no navigation, but there are principles.** You can't map the Woven Hollows—maps require consistent spatial relationships. But you can learn its language, its tendencies.

Structures attract. If you want to go to a platform, look at it then stop looking at it. Walk in a direction that feels right while thinking about something else. You'll probably arrive, though not by the path you expected, not in the time you anticipated.

Pools are anchors. They stay relatively consistent—that pool is there today and tomorrow (though today and tomorrow are questionable terms). Use pools as landmarks. "The pool with three palms at its north edge" is more reliable than "the platform with the thick roof" because platforms change while pools persist, mostly, usually.

Follow the birds. Not because they're going anywhere useful, but because following *something* helps your mind accept the walk. The birds move with purpose even if they have no purpose. Walking behind them gives you permission to move without destination, which is the only way to navigate there.

Don't retrace steps. Walking backward through a route doesn't take you back. The landscape doesn't work that way. Each journey is unique. If you walk from Platform A to Platform B, walking from B to A is not the reverse journey—it's a completely new walk. Accept this. Never expect to "go back." Only ever go forward, which might arrive at previous locations or might not.

**Learn to recognize when you're looping.** Sometimes you'll pass the same place multiple times without realizing. Then suddenly you'll notice: that platform with the specific weaving pattern, you've been here before. Maybe three times. Maybe you've been walking in a circle, or a spiral, or a pattern that isn't a geometric shape. When you notice looping, stop. Sit. Wait. The landscape might be asking you to stop moving, to simply be in that location. After resting, after observing, walk in a deliberately different direction using a different principle. Sometimes this works.

**There may be guides.** The thing beneath might be one. Some travelers report that following the underwater movements leads somewhere—not anywhere specific, but somewhere that feels intended, feels right. Others report that the birds, particularly the white wading birds, sometimes arrange themselves in patterns that suggest direction. A line of birds, all facing the same way, might be an invitation or instruction.

Or these might be projections, pattern-seeking from desperate minds. Impossible to say. Try following. Try not following. Notice if either produces different results. Probably neither does.

## Cultural/Interpersonal Preparation

**You will probably be alone, but prepare for not being alone.** Most travelers report solitude. But some encounter others—other visitors, other wanderers, other witnesses. These encounters are brief, often silent, sometimes impossible to verify as real.

If you meet someone there, be courteous but not attached. They might be real. They might be the place wearing a human shape. They might be you, from a different moment, seen across folded time. Conversations rarely cohere. You'll talk but the words will be slightly wrong, or right but arriving in the wrong order, or perfectly clear in the moment and nonsensical when remembered.

Don't expect companionship. Don't expect shared experience even if you're experiencing the same things simultaneously. The Woven Hollows is profoundly individual—each visitor seems to be in their own version of the place, versions that overlap but don't align. You might stand next to another person, both looking at a platform, and you're seeing five support columns while they're seeing three, and both of you are right.

**Learn the ethics of making.** Everything there is made with care—simple care, human care, hand care. If you touch things, touch them carefully. If you move things, move them deliberately. Don't break. Don't disturb. Don't leave trash—though trash probably won't stay anyway, it will vanish or integrate or transform, but don't create that burden.

If you feel compelled to make something, to contribute to the construction, consider carefully. Your making might persist. It might become part of the permanent landscape. Or it might offend some principle you don't understand and cause problems you can't predict. Probably safest not to make anything. Just witness the making, the being-made, the continuous gentle construction of reality through attention and craft.

**Prepare to be changed.** You won't return as who you were. The changes might be subtle—a slightly different relationship to time, to certainty, to causation. Or they might be profound—a complete re-understanding of what reality is, what consciousness is, what being-present means.

Some changes are reported consistently: returning travelers have difficulty with clocks, with schedules, with the assumption that future events will arrive in order. They're better at peripheral vision. They're more comfortable with paradox. They're less attached to explanations. They notice when things are made by hand. They prefer natural materials. They're comfortable with silence.

Some travelers report persistent symptoms: seeing reflections that don't match, feeling watched in empty rooms, occasional moments when distance becomes unreliable. These aren't necessarily problems—they're more like souvenirs, pieces of the Woven Hollows that followed you home.

Don't fight the changes. You went to a place where reality works differently. You can't un-experience that. Integration is healthier than resistance.

## Practical Logistics

**Getting there is unclear.** The Woven Hollows doesn't have coordinates, doesn't have a map location, doesn't have a physical place you can travel to conventionally. Reports vary on how travelers arrived:

Some walked into fog in a mangrove forest and emerged onto the woven ground. Some went swimming in warm shallow seas, dove down, and surfaced in a pool there. Some fell asleep in greenhouses and woke there. Some walked through particular doorways at particular times. Some simply decided to go and found themselves going, without clear memory of the transition.

The commonality: all transitions involved wetness, warmth, green growing things, and some form of threshold—fog, water surface, sleep, doorway. Intention matters. You can't stumble into the Woven Hollows accidentally. You have to want to go, or need to go, or be ready to go. The place seems to respond to readiness.

**Leaving is similarly unclear.** Most travelers simply find themselves leaving—they're walking and then they're not-there-anymore, they're in the fog that becomes a forest, in the water that becomes an ocean, awake in the greenhouse. The transition isn't dramatic. The place releases you when it's done with you, or when you're done with it, according to logic that isn't quite negotiable.

Some travelers report being able to choose to leave by walking toward the horizon with clear intention of departing. Others report this doesn't work—the horizon recedes, they remain. Possibly leaving requires permission. Possibly it requires completion of something you came to do, even if you don't know what that is. Possibly it's random.

**Time outside while you're inside.** Inconsistent reports. Some travelers are gone for what feels like days and return minutes later. Others are gone for what feels like hours and weeks have passed. There's no correlation between perceived internal duration and external elapsed time. 

Plan for temporal uncertainty. Don't go if you have time-sensitive obligations waiting. Don't go if people depend on you being back by a specific time. You can't control when you'll return. Leave affairs in order. Write letters explaining you're going somewhere and you'll return when you return. Make peace with people. Not because it's dangerous—it doesn't seem to be dangerous, not in ways that kill—but because you might be gone longer than you expect, or return different than you left, or have trouble explaining where you were.

## What to Expect: Stages of Experience

**First arrival: orientation collapse.** When you arrive (however you arrive), the initial minutes or hours will be the hardest. Every sense will be telling you things are wrong. Distance is wrong. Light is wrong. Sound is wrong. Your instinct will be to flee, to find the exit, to return to normal.

Resist this instinct. Sit down. On the woven ground, on a platform, in the moss. Sit and breathe and wait for your nervous system to stop screaming. It will. Eventually you'll notice the wrongness hasn't killed you. You're still breathing. Your heart is still beating. The wrongness is just... different rules, not broken rules. Let your mind adjust.

This phase might last minutes or hours. You'll know it's ending when the brightness stops feeling aggressive and starts feeling normal. When the silence stops feeling like absence and starts feeling like presence. When you can look at a pool and appreciate the beauty of the impossible reflection instead of being disturbed by it.

**Second phase: exploration.** Once acclimated, you'll want to see things. Walk. Look at structures. Examine joints. Watch birds. Wade through pools. This is good. The place rewards careful observation. Every platform is slightly different. Every section of woven ground has its own pattern. The vegetation, while repetitive in type, is unique in every instance.

You'll notice things: how the lashings are tied (always clockwise? or sometimes counterclockwise? does it matter?). How the flowers emit no scent but their lack of scent is itself a quality, a presence of absence. How the lizards are never quite where you expect, but always where they should be once you see them.

Take your time in this phase. It might last days (if there are days). Let yourself be a student of the place. It has vocabulary, grammar, syntax. Learn to read it.

**Third phase: integration.** At some point you'll stop being a visitor and start being present. You'll stop comparing things to normal reality and accept this reality on its terms. The pools reflect wrong because that's how pools are here. The distances change because space is like that here. The time doesn't pass because time is different here.

This is the deepest phase. In it, you'll understand things without words. You'll feel the making that's always happening. You'll sense the presences without needing them to be definable. You'll be comfortable with paradox because paradox is just how things are.

You might spend most of your visit in this phase. It's peaceful, despite the wrongness. Or peaceful because of the wrongness—the normal world's insistence on consistency is actually quite stressful once you've experienced an alternative.

**Final phase: completion.** Eventually you'll feel done. Not bored, not tired, but complete. You've seen what you came to see. You've learned what was available to learn. You've been sufficiently present. This feeling is subtle but unmistakable.

When it arrives, walk toward any horizon with the clear intention of leaving. Or lie down and sleep with the intention of waking elsewhere. Or wade into a pool and submerge completely. Usually one of these works. Usually the place lets you go when you're ready.

## Special Warnings

**Don't try to take things.** Materials from there—a piece of bamboo, a woven section, a flower—they don't survive transit. They vanish during the transition, or crumble immediately upon return, or transform into something else (ash, water, nothing). The place isn't souvenir-able. What you take is memory, understanding, change. Nothing physical.

**Don't try to document extensively.** The place resists documentation. Photographs fail, as mentioned. Detailed written descriptions degrade. What survives is impressions, fragments, poetry. If you must record, record feelings rather than facts. "The light was thick" will remain true. "The platform was 3 meters wide" will become questionable.

**Don't go with someone you need to stay with.** If you travel there with a companion, you'll likely lose each other immediately. You might be in the same place but unable to find each other. Or you might be in different versions of the place that don't intersect. Going with someone means accepting you'll be separated, possibly for the duration. If separation would be traumatic, don't go together.

**Don't resist the wrongness.** Fighting against the slipped rules, trying to force the place to make sense, attempting to logic your way through paradox—these approaches cause suffering without changing anything. The place is what it is. Your acceptance or rejection doesn't modify it. Accept, and you'll be uncomfortable but functional. Resist, and you'll be uncomfortable and additionally exhausted from futile resistance.

**Don't assume you can return at will.** Some travelers report being able to find their way back multiple times. Others try desperately and never manage it again. The place chooses its visitors according to principles that aren't clear. Don't assume that going once means you can go again. If you're there and there are things you want to witness, witness them fully, because you might not get another opportunity.

## Why Go At All?

This is the essential question. The Woven Hollows is difficult, strange, disorienting. It will stress your reality-management systems. It offers no obvious rewards—no treasures, no secrets, no powers. You can't bring anything back. You might not even be able to explain where you went.

So why?

Because it's *real*. Whatever else it is, it's authentically itself, completely and without compromise. In a world of simulation, mediation, representation, the Woven Hollows is direct experience of a reality that operates on different principles. It proves that reality is more flexible than consensus suggests, more strange than materialism allows, more multiple than singularity assumes.

Because it teaches presence. The continuous now, the attention to small things, the acceptance of paradox—these are valuable skills, valuable understandings. They make returning to normal life richer, more textured, less anxiously attached to certainty.

Because it's beautiful. Despite everything, it's beautiful. The woven ground is beautiful. The scattered platforms are beautiful. The bright air, the warm pools, the perfect plants, the silent birds—all beautiful in ways that regular beauty isn't, in ways that include wrongness as part of the aesthetic.

Because it changes you, and some changes are worth having even if they're difficult. Coming back with a different relationship to time, to space, to causation—these changes can feel like damage at first but integrate into wisdom eventually. You become someone who knows, bodily and undeniably, that reality is stranger than you were taught. This knowledge is uncomfortable and precious.

Because you feel called. Some people do. They hear about the Woven Hollows and something in them responds with recognition, with yearning, with certainty that they need to go. If you feel that, trust it. The place might be calling you for reasons you don't understand but will understand only by going.

Or don't go. That's legitimate too. Not everyone needs to visit places where rules slip. Some people are well-served by staying in consensus reality, trusting the ground to be solid, trusting time to pass linearly, trusting that reflections show what's actually there. There's no shame in choosing the known world.

But if you go—when you go—go with as much preparation as possible while accepting that preparation is insufficient. Go humble. Go open. Go ready to be confused, to be changed, to be stripped of certainty and rebuilt with something else.

Go carefully.

Go bravely.

Go knowing you might not come back the same, and that this might be exactly what you need.

The woven ground awaits. The bright air hangs thick. The pools reflect things that aren't there. The silence listens.

And in that listening, in that brightness, in that made place that makes itself continuously—you'll find whatever you went seeking, or you'll find something else entirely, or you'll find that seeking and finding are the same continuous action, never completed, always present, woven into the lattice of existing alongside everything else that exists.

Prepare as best you can.

Then go.

Be there.

Return.

Remember.
