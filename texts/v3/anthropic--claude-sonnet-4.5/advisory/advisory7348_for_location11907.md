# Preparation Guide for The Drowned Bells

## Physical Preparations

### Thermal Management
You're entering a near-freezing environment where you'll be fully immersed for an extended period. The cold here is persistent but sub-lethal—it wants to slow you down, not kill you, but you must take it seriously.

**Layering strategy**: Wear a wetsuit as your base—3-5mm neoprene, but understand it will function differently than in normal water. The medium's density sits somewhere between water and air, so insulation will work strangely. Over this, consider a thin drysuit shell, though "dry" will become a meaningless concept. The goal is to trap a layer of the medium against your skin that your body heat can warm slightly. Bring multiple thermal layers you can add or remove: synthetic materials that retain some insulating properties when saturated. Merino wool works, though it will never dry. Avoid cotton—even here, cotton kills.

**Extremities**: Your hands and feet will suffer first. Neoprene gloves and boots, 5-7mm thickness. Bring backups. The cold is relentless and cumulative—what feels manageable for the first hour becomes debilitating by day three. Consider pogies (insulated mittens that attach to trekking poles) for your hands. Your feet will be in constant contact with stone that has been cold for centuries.

**Head protection**: A neoprene hood is essential. You lose heat through your head even in normal conditions; here, with the medium conducting temperature more efficiently than air, an exposed head will drain your core temperature steadily. Bring a backup hood. The hoods will never truly fail, but comfort matters over days of wear, and having a dry-ish spare to switch into provides psychological relief.

### Breathing Adaptation

The medium allows breathing, but it's not automatic—your body will panic initially. This is the most dangerous moment of arrival.

**Pre-adaptation**: Spend weeks before your journey practicing breath control and overriding drowning reflexes. Free-diving training helps, but this is different. You're not holding your breath; you're convincing your hindbrain that inhaling liquid is acceptable. Some travelers recommend:

- Progressive immersion therapy: start with face-down breathing in shallow water, work up to full submersion
- Meditation focused on breathing autonomy—separating conscious control from autonomic panic
- If possible, practice in very cold water to combine both stressors before facing them together

**The first breath**: When you arrive, you will want to gasp. Don't. Your lungs will already be full of the medium—you arrived breathing it, though you won't remember the transition. Take slow, deliberate breaths. The medium feels thick, requires more effort than air. Your first minutes will involve your brain screaming that you're drowning while your body continues to function. This is the filter: those who cannot override this panic response cannot stay.

**Ongoing breathing**: After the initial adaptation, breathing becomes almost automatic, but it never feels quite natural. The medium is denser than air—expect to breathe more slowly, more deliberately. Your chest will work harder. Think of it as breathing while carrying a moderate weight on your torso. You'll need higher caloric intake just for the respiration effort.

### Mobility and Movement

Walking is possible but inefficient. Drifting is natural but requires learning.

**Finning**: Bring dive fins—freediving fins work better than scuba fins due to the medium's density. The long, flexible blade lets you generate thrust without excessive effort. Practice finning techniques in advance: flutter kick, frog kick, dolphin kick. You'll use all of them depending on what you're doing. In groves, you'll need precision maneuvering. Over open rock, you'll want efficient cruising.

**Weighting**: You'll need to achieve neutral buoyancy. Too light and you'll drift upward uncontrollably; too heavy and you'll be pinned to the rock, exhausting yourself with every movement. Bring a standard BC (buoyancy compensator) and weight belt, but understand they'll function oddly. The medium's density is variable—you'll feel it thicken and thin without warning. What made you neutral an hour ago might leave you scraping the bottom now. Bring extra small weights you can add or remove: 1-2 pound increments. Practice achieving neutral buoyancy in a pool until it's instinctive.

**Navigation**: Visibility is 30 meters maximum, often much less. The plateau is kilometers across. You will get lost.

Bring multiple compasses—regular orienteering compasses work, though you'll need to account for the medium's refraction making them harder to read. GPS will not function—whatever this place is, it doesn't share Earth's satellite infrastructure. Dead reckoning becomes essential: count your fin kicks, time your travel segments, note distinctive rock formations and grove patterns. Bring a slate and grease pencil to map as you go. The dimness and visual uniformity make landmarks blur together; your map is your lifeline.

**Reels and lines**: Bring cave diving reels with plenty of line. Tie off to rock formations and bell frameworks as you explore. The lines serve multiple purposes: navigation back to camp, marking explored territory, emergency retrieval if you become disoriented in thicker medium. Bring at least three reels with 100+ meters of line each. Mark your lines at regular intervals so you can gauge distance even in low visibility.

## Equipment and Tools

### Lighting
The ambient light is dim but present. Your eyes adapt, but you'll want supplemental light for detail work, for the darker cycles, and for psychological comfort.

**Primary lights**: LED dive lights, minimum 1000 lumens. Bring at least two. The medium absorbs and scatters light—your effective range will be less than in clear water. Wide beam angles work better than focused spots for general navigation. The greenish tint of the medium will shift your color perception; this is unavoidable, but white-balance LED lights will help.

**Backup lights**: Multiple small backup lights. Not because the primaries will fail, but because you'll drop things, lose things in the medium, wedge lights into crevices to illuminate work areas and forget them. Cheap, reliable, replaceable.

**Chemical lights**: Bring cyalume sticks, lots of them. They work independently of batteries and can mark paths, camps, and interesting locations. Their glow persists for hours and adds to the existing faint luminescence of the tube-plants. In darker periods, a constellation of cyalumes can mark an entire grove's structure.

### Shelter and Rest

You need somewhere to retreat to, to get off the stone, to feel enclosed.

**The bell shelter**: The description mentions the large silent bell provides strange shelter. This might be your best option—pre-existing, acoustically isolated, with that odd interior space. But you can't rely on it being available or close to your exploration areas.

**Portable shelter**: A hammock system adapted for this environment works well. Eno-style hammocks with integrated bug netting (you don't need the netting for insects, but it provides a sense of enclosure). String hammocks between the adapted trees or bell frameworks. They keep you off the cold stone and create a psychological boundary between you and the infinite medium. Bring multiple hammocks—one for sleeping, one for eating and working, one for backup. 

The trees will support your weight, but choose thick-trunked specimens. Test branches before trusting them. The wood is partially soft, partially coral-like—it might break unexpectedly.

**Ground camp**: If you prefer the stone, bring a ground pad—multiple layers of closed-cell foam. The rock is cold, cold, cold. You'll never fully insulate yourself from it, but three inches of foam helps. A bivy sack or small tent provides enclosure, though staking becomes interesting—bring rock climbing pitons to hammer into cracks rather than traditional stakes.

### Nutrition and Hydration

Hunger and thirst are "muted, stretched out," but you still need sustenance.

**Food**: Bring high-calorie, high-fat foods. Your body is working constantly to maintain temperature and process the breathing effort. Aim for 4,000-5,000 calories per day, more if you're moving extensively. 

Traditional backpacking food won't work well—the medium will saturate everything. Bring:
- Sealed military MREs (the pouches are waterproof)
- Canned foods (bring a can opener)
- Vacuum-sealed preserved meats, cheeses, and nuts
- Energy gels and blocks in sealed packaging
- Chocolate if you can keep it from dissolving in the cold medium

The tube-plant flesh is edible but "bitter, fibrous." Treat it as supplementary/emergency rations. If you're there long-term, you'll need to adjust to it. Bring hot sauce or seasoning to make it palatable.

**Cooking**: Impossible in the traditional sense. You cannot make fire underwater, and this medium won't permit it either. Bring:
- Chemical heating packs (flameless ration heaters)
- An insulated container to hold heated food
- Consider a diving hot water system if you're committed to hot meals

Realistically, plan for cold meals. Your body will crave warm food, but that craving won't be satisfied here. Psychological preparation for this deprivation matters.

**Hydration**: The medium itself provides something, but don't rely on it entirely. Bring:
- Water in sealed containers (bladders may leak under the strange pressure)
- Water purification tablets (if you do try to process the medium)
- Electrolyte supplements to maintain mineral balance

The medium's composition is unknown. Drinking it might work, might not. Initial visits should treat it as non-potable and bring all water. Longer stays might require experimentation, but carefully.

### Communication and Documentation

You are probably going alone—parties rarely travel to liminal places together—but even solo, you need to document.

**Writing**: Waterproof notebooks and grease pencils. Regular paper will dissolve. Rite-in-the-Rain notebooks work in normal rain; test whether they work here. Stone tablets and etching tools work too—primitive but effective. Your documentation might be the only record of your experience; the medium seems to erase digital technology's reliability.

**Recording devices**: Bring audio recorders in waterproof housings. The bells are the heart of this place; you'll want recordings. Understand that the medium conducts sound strangely—playback will differ from experience, but it's better than nothing. Bring multiple recorders: one running continuously at camp, one for portable recording, one backup.

**Photography**: Underwater cameras in proper housings. GoPros work, though the wide angle distorts scale. A proper underwater SLR in a housing gives better control. Bring multiple batteries—they drain faster in the cold. The dim, greenish light will challenge exposure and white balance. Shoot RAW if possible for post-processing flexibility.

**Time-keeping**: Multiple watches, mechanical and digital. The strange time-dilation effect means you need external time references. Wind-up mechanical watches can't rely on electronics. Bring a traditional alarm clock in a waterproof case. Mark calendar days religiously. The light cycles might not be 24 hours—track them independently from your home-time tracking.

## Psychological Preparations

This is where most travelers fail, even if they've solved all physical challenges.

### Sensory Adaptation

**The bells**: Constant, pervasive, non-repeating. This will erode your sanity if you're not prepared. Practice beforehand by:
- Living with constant noise: cities, busy environments, never seeking silence
- Listening to generative music or soundscapes that never repeat exactly
- Meditation practices that accept rather than reject environmental sound

The goal isn't to learn to ignore the bells—you can't. The goal is to learn to exist with them, to let them be present without demanding they stop. Earplugs won't help; the sound conducts through the medium, through your bones. You can muffle it slightly with neoprene hoods, but you can't escape it.

Some travelers report that after days or weeks, the bells become soothing, become a kind of companionship. Others never adapt. Know yourself. If you require silence for mental stability, this place will break you.

**The dimness**: Constant twilight, no proper day or night, just gradations of dim to dimmer. Your circadian rhythms will revolt. Practice by:
- Living under constant artificial light for days
- Sleeping at random intervals rather than night-schedules  
- Using wake-up lights and light therapy to decouple alertness from natural solar cycles

Bring vitamin D supplements—you won't process any from the non-existent sun. SAD (seasonal affective disorder) protocols might help: light therapy boxes, though they'll add weight and battery demands.

**Isolation**: No animals, no people (probably), just you and plants that don't regard you. This is profound isolation. The plants are alive but alien. You cannot connect with them. Practice by:
- Extended solo wilderness trips, but understand this is worse
- Meditation on emptiness and aloneness as neutral rather than negative states
- Developing rich internal dialogue and imagination—you'll be your only companion

Bring items that provide psychological comfort: photos, letters, a favorite small object. They'll get saturated with the medium, but their presence matters. Some travelers bring a stuffed animal or similar—don't let pride prevent you from bringing comfort items. No one will know, and they might keep you sane.

### Time and Memory

The place distorts temporal perception. "How long since the last light-cycle?" becomes unanswerable. Dreams blend with waking. Prepare by:

**Rigorous routine**: Establish a schedule and maintain it obsessively:
- Wake (by alarm) at same time each cycle
- Eat at scheduled intervals
- Perform exercises at set times
- Document daily (or what passes for daily)

The routine provides structure when temporal perception fails. It's external scaffolding for your dissolving time-sense.

**Memory journaling**: Write down what you did each cycle. Not extensive—just bullet points:
- Explored western grove
- Found three-bell framework
- Ate two MRE packets
- Slept 6 hours (estimated)

This creates a record you can review to prove time is passing, that you're not stuck in a loop. The feeling that you are stuck, that you've been in the same grove before (even when you haven't), that you've already heard this exact bell-pattern (you haven't, they never repeat)—these feelings will come. Your journal is evidence against them.

**Pre-commitment planning**: Before entering, decide how long you'll stay: 3 days, 7 days, 14 days. Write it down. Tell someone. Create a commitment that exists outside the place's influence. Without this, you might drift indefinitely, the muted hunger and thirst never quite compelling you to leave, the bells filling the space where urgency should be.

### Existential Preparation

This is a place that "should not be." Trees underwater that aren't underwater. Air you can swim through. Bells that ring forever. Space inside the bell that's larger than outside. These impossibilities will trouble you.

**Accepting cognitive dissonance**: The place violates physics, biology, sense. You must function anyway. Practice by:
- Studying philosophy of perception and phenomenology—understanding that experience is valid even when inexplicable
- Meditation on acceptance without understanding
- Mentally framing contradictions as interesting rather than threatening

Your brain will generate theories (you'll develop them, the description says). This is healthy—theorizing is how minds process strangeness. But hold theories lightly. Don't commit to explanations. The place is what it is; understanding might not be possible or necessary.

**The possibility of not returning**: Liminal spaces sometimes become traps. Not through malice, but through their nature. The muted needs, the time distortion, the sound that never stops—you might stop wanting to leave. Before entering, establish:
- Clear exit conditions written down
- Someone who will come looking if you don't return
- A trigger that forces re-evaluation (alarm set for your planned exit date)

Consider the psychological impact of finding those towers and bells, evidence of previous inhabitants who aren't there anymore. Did they leave? Did they become something else? Did the place subsume them? These questions will haunt you. Prepare by accepting you might not get answers, and by strengthening your intention to remain yourself, to remain temporary.

## Medical and Emergency

### First Aid
Bring a comprehensive kit in a waterproof container:
- Wound care: the medium's composition is unknown; assume wounds will become contaminated
- Antibiotics (broad-spectrum)
- Pain medication (injury is possible, evacuation is complicated)
- Seasickness medication (the drifting might trigger motion sickness initially)
- Anti-anxiety medication (the psychological weight might require pharmaceutical support)

### Specific Hazards

**The cold**: Hypothermia is slow but cumulative. Know the signs:
- Uncontrollable shivering
- Confusion, slurred speech
- Loss of coordination
- Drowsiness

If symptoms appear, you need to warm up immediately. Get to your shelter, add layers, use chemical warmers. If you've reached severe symptoms, you need to leave. The medium won't let you warm adequately.

**Disorientation**: The spatial distortions near structures, the time dilation, the dream-like state. If you become genuinely confused about what's real:
- Consult your written notes and maps
- Perform a grounding exercise (touch the stone, feel its cold solidity)
- Retreat to a familiar location (your primary camp)
- Consider whether it's time to leave

**The silent bell**: The description warns of interior space that doesn't match exterior, sound isolation, that deep vibration. This is fascinating and dangerous. If you choose to enter it:
- Tie off a line outside so you can find the exit
- Set an alarm to limit your stay
- Bring a companion if somehow one is available

The bell might be a door or a trap or both. Approach with respect and caution.

**The edge**: That cliff dropping into deeper dimness, where sound becomes overwhelming and you feel drawn to step out. This is how the place might kill you—not through violence but through seduction into the void. Do not approach the edge alone. If you must explore it:
- Full safety line tied to a solid anchor
- Pre-commit to a maximum time (15 minutes)
- Write down your intention to not jump before approaching

## Practical Logistics

### Entry and Exit

The description doesn't explain how you arrive or leave. This is concerning. Most liminal spaces have thresholds, doors, conditions. Research and understand the access method:
- Is it a physical location in normal reality?
- A state of consciousness?
- A random occurrence?

Ensure you know how to leave before you enter. Mark the exit point if it's physical. Learn the mental state or ritual if it's consciousness-based. Bring whatever token or key the exit requires.

### Duration Recommendations

**First visit**: 48-72 hours maximum. This lets you:
- Adapt to the medium and breathing
- Experience multiple light cycles
- Explore a limited area
- Test your gear and psychological resilience
- Exit before serious cold-exposure or sanity erosion

**Subsequent visits**: If the first goes well and you wish to return, gradually extend:
- Second visit: 5-7 days
- Third visit: 2 weeks
- Longer stays only if you've demonstrated you can maintain routine and sanity

**Maximum stay**: Unknown, but the description's inhabitants vanished. Perhaps there's a duration beyond which the medium changes you, integrates you, makes you unable or unwilling to remain human and leave. Conservative estimate: 30 days maximum before you risk becoming something else.

### Budgeting and Supplies

For a 7-day stay, expect to carry:
- 50-70 pounds of food (high-calorie, sealed)
- 5-10 pounds of water (supplementary)
- 20-30 pounds of clothing and thermal gear
- 10-15 pounds of shelter equipment
- 10 pounds of lighting and batteries
- 5-10 pounds of documentation equipment
- 10 pounds of safety and navigation gear
- 5 pounds of medical supplies
- Total: 115-150 pounds

This is more than a normal backpacking load, but the medium's buoyancy will help. Still, you'll need to be reasonably fit. Train by:
- Backpacking with weight in mountainous terrain
- Swimming with weight belts
- Long-duration cold water immersion

## Specific Site Preparations

### The Plaza with the Large Silent Bell
If this is your primary destination:
- Camp nearby but not in the plaza itself (respect the space)
- Document the bell extensively before entering
- Plan multiple short visits rather than one long one
- Bring offerings if that feels appropriate (who knows what might appreciate them)

### The Cliff Edge
If you're drawn to explore the boundary:
- Bring full climbing safety gear: harnesses, carabiners, rope, anchors
- Practice rope work in the medium before approaching the edge
- Consider this a separate expedition from grove exploration—different gear, different mindset
- Accept that the bottom might be unreachable; the draw might be the mystery, not the answer

### The Bowl-Shaped Depression
The "heart or lung" with dense growth and frantic small bells:
- Approach with curiosity but caution
- The density of vegetation might create entanglement hazards
- The higher-frequency bells might be more psychologically aggravating
- Bring cutting tools in case you need to free yourself
- This might be a center of whatever biological processes maintain the plateau—observe, document, don't disturb

## Cultural/Anthropological Considerations

Those towers and bells represent someone, some culture. They built with hand tools, they created music in this impossible place, they're gone now. This deserves respect:

- Don't disturb the structures or artifacts
- Document them extensively
- If you take samples (pottery shards, metal fragments), do so minimally and catalogically
- Consider that the ringing bells might be memorial, prayer, art, or function—treat them as sacred regardless

The inhabitants adapted to this place—trees that aren't quite trees anymore, people who made culture in liquid sky. Then they vanished. Understand that you're walking through a mystery that might also be a tragedy, a triumph, or a transformation. Your preparations should include ethical ones: how will you treat evidence of those who came before?

## Final Psychological Framing

You're preparing to visit a place that induces profound cognitive dissonance. The usual categories fail: not land, not sea, not air. Not day, not night. Not silent, not properly musical. Not dead, not alive in familiar ways.

The best mental preparation is to practice not-knowing. Practice being comfortable with questions that have no answers. Practice observing without needing to categorize. Practice existing in spaces where normal rules are suspended.

The Drowned Bells will teach you what they have to teach, which might be nothing, or everything, or something without words. You go to listen—to the bells, yes, but also to the place itself, to what it has to say about existing in a state between states.

Bring good gear. Bring redundant systems. But most importantly, bring flexibility of mind, acceptance of strangeness, and a strong tether to your intention to remain yourself and return.

The bells will still be ringing when you leave. They'll ring for centuries after. You're just passing through, borrowing time in their eternal music, floating in their cold sky-water, a brief visitor to their long memory.

Go carefully. Document well. Return safely. Tell those of us who cannot go what you found.

And if you find yourself standing at the cliff edge, hearing the harmonics from the void, feeling the pull to step out and drift down forever—remember that you wrote down your intention to return, that someone is expecting you, that you are not meant to be one more mystery in this place of mysteries.

Come back. Tell the story. That's what explorers do.
