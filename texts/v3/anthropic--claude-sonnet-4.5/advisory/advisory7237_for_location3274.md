# Preparation Guide for The Breathing Hollows

## Essential Understanding

Before you pack a single item, understand this: the Breathing Hollows will challenge your fundamental assumptions about reality's stability. You are not preparing for a wilderness expedition or an alien planet—you are preparing to exist within a place where the usual contracts between observer and environment have been renegotiated by something older and stranger than you. Humility and adaptability will serve you better than any equipment.

## Physical Preparations

### Health and Fitness
Begin a regimen of standing meditation at least two months before departure. Not for spiritual reasons, but because you'll spend hours standing in ankle-to-knee-deep water, and your body needs to know how to remain upright while fatigued, cold, and vibrating at frequencies not of your choosing. Practice standing still for 2-3 hours at a time. Your feet, ankles, and lower back must be strong.

Develop your cardiovascular endurance, but more importantly, train your body to function in cold, damp conditions. Take cold showers. Spend time in water. Your core temperature regulation will be constantly challenged by the cool water and cool air. If you have circulation issues in your extremities, consult with a physician about whether this environment is survivable for you.

Visit an audiologist. Get a baseline hearing test. The constant hum will affect your hearing, possibly permanently. You should know what you're starting with. If you have any history of sound-induced distress, reconsider this journey entirely. The hum is not optional and not escapable.

### Inoculations and Medical Screening
Standard travel inoculations are largely irrelevant—the Hollows have few conventional pathogens. However, consider these preparations:

Ensure your vestibular system is healthy. The spatial distortions will challenge your sense of balance and orientation. If you experience vertigo or severe motion sickness easily, you may find the Hollows unbearable.

Get a comprehensive neurological baseline established. Document your current cognitive function, memory capabilities, and sense of time. Given the temporal anomalies, you'll want a reference point for what's normal for you. Some travelers never fully re-synchronize with standard time after visiting.

If you take any medications, consult extensively with your doctor. Medication metabolism may change in the Hollows—that temporal plasticity affects biological processes. Medications that normally last 8 hours might metabolize in 4 hours or 16. Bring significantly more than you think you need, and monitor effects carefully.

Consider a psychological evaluation. Not because you're unwell, but because the Hollows reveal and amplify certain mental patterns. If you have obsessive tendencies, you may become fixated on the patterns. If you're prone to dissociation, the reality instabilities may be dangerous. If you have rigid thinking patterns, the place will be unbearably distressing.

## Equipment

### Clothing Strategy
Forget layers for warmth. You need layers for water management and constant dampness.

**Base layer**: Merino wool or high-end synthetic moisture-wicking material. Bring at least six sets—they'll never fully dry, so you'll be rotating through damp clothing. Accept this now. The goal isn't dryness; it's managing dampness levels.

**Mid layer**: Thin fleece or synthetic insulation that retains warmth when wet. Two sets minimum.

**Outer layer**: This is critical. You need a waterproof breathable shell, but more importantly, you need one that's *silent*. The acoustic environment matters. Rustling fabric will drive you to distraction and may interfere with the auditory cues you'll learn to navigate by. Soft-shell materials or quiet-treated fabrics only.

**Footwear**: Bring two types. First, water shoes or amphibious boots with excellent traction on slippery surfaces and good ankle support. You'll be walking on submerged surfaces you can't see clearly, and the conducting plants are slippery. Second, a pair of completely waterproof boots for when you can access the rises—though be aware these will slosh with the water that inevitably gets in. Some travelers prefer to go barefoot; the sensory feedback from your feet can help navigate the spatial anomalies. If you try this, condition your feet extensively beforehand and bring comprehensive first aid for foot injuries.

**Head covering**: A hood or hat that stays on in wind but doesn't restrict hearing. You need to hear the hum's changes. A billed cap can help with the constant mist getting in your eyes.

**Gloves**: Thin neoprene gloves. Your hands will be in water frequently, and they'll go numb without protection.

### Navigation Equipment
Standard navigation tools will fail or behave erratically, but bring them anyway—their failures will give you information.

**Compass**: Bring three from different manufacturers. When they disagree, you're near a node or strong anomaly. When they agree, trust nothing—they might all be wrong together.

**GPS device**: Bring two. Log coordinates obsessively. The nonsensical readings create a map of where reality is unstable. This map is valuable data for understanding local conditions, even if it won't get you where you're going.

**Maps**: Bring paper maps and waterproof notebooks. But understand that maps of the Hollows are aspirational documents. They show where things are supposed to be. Make your own map as you go, noting where distances felt wrong, where you looped back unexpectedly, where nodes appeared closer or farther than they should be.

**Flagging tape**: Bright, waterproof flagging tape. Mark your path religiously. Though be aware that the markers may not stay where you put them, or you may not return to them the way you expect. They're psychological anchors as much as navigational aids.

**Rangefinder**: Laser rangefinder for checking distances. When the readings are inconsistent with your visual estimation, you're experiencing spatial distortion. Document these discrepancies.

### Timekeeping
Bring at least four watches or timing devices, all synchronized before you enter:
- One mechanical watch
- One quartz watch  
- One digital watch
- One smartphone or GPS device with time

Check them against each other every hour. Log the discrepancies. This is how you'll track temporal anomalies and, more importantly, how you'll maintain some thread of connection to standard time. When they all disagree, you're in deep temporal distortion. 

Consider bringing a metronome. The regular beat provides an external reference when your internal sense of time becomes unreliable.

### Shelter
Don't bring a tent. The constant dampness and spatial anomalies make enclosed shelters problematic. Instead:

**Tarp**: A large, waterproof tarp that can be configured in multiple ways. You'll want overhead protection from rain while maintaining open sides so you can hear and see what's happening around you. The place doesn't let you separate yourself from it comfortably.

**Hammock**: Some travelers report success with hammocks strung between tussocks of the conducting plants, though sleeping in contact with those plants intensifies the humming sensation and can lead to extremely strange dreams.

**Platform**: If you're staying more than a few days, consider materials for building a small raised platform on one of the rises. Pressure-treated wood, plastic decking, or aluminum frames. Keep it simple and expect it to feel foreign against the landscape.

**Sleeping gear**: A sleeping bag rated for temperatures 10-15°C colder than the actual temperature—the dampness makes everything feel colder. A closed-cell foam pad is essential; inflatable pads will seem to inflate and deflate on their own as air pressure anomalies occur.

### Recording Equipment
If you're documenting the experience, bring redundant systems:

**Cameras**: Multiple waterproof cameras. Video and still. Keep them in dry bags when not in use. Expect corrupted files, impossible images, and footage that doesn't match your memory. This is normal. Some travelers find that analog film cameras produce more reliable results than digital, as if the Hollows' effects interact differently with chemical versus electronic recording.

**Audio recorders**: Critical for documenting the hum and its changes. Bring several, with extensive battery backup. The acoustic environment is the heart of the Hollows. High-quality, waterproof field recorders are worth the investment. Record continuously if you can manage the data storage.

**Notebooks**: Waterproof paper and pencils. Pens may fail in the damp. Your handwritten observations will be more trustworthy than electronic records. The act of writing by hand also seems to help maintain cognitive coherence—the physical reality of marking paper anchors you.

**Data storage**: Multiple redundant storage devices in waterproof containers. Assume half your data will be corrupted or anomalous. The redundancy ensures you'll have something usable.

### Power Management
Solar chargers are nearly useless under the perpetual overcast. Bring extensive battery reserves—far more than you calculate needing. Batteries drain at unpredictable rates in the Hollows. Some travelers report batteries that last twice as long as expected; others find them dead in hours. Bring three to four times your calculated need.

Consider hand-crank or kinetic chargers as backups. The mechanical energy conversion seems more reliable than chemical batteries or solar collection.

### Water and Food
**Water**: Paradoxically, you'll need to bring drinking water despite standing in water. The water in the Hollows is not technically toxic, but drinking it is not recommended. It has strange effects—some people report altered perception, others digestive distress, others no effect at all. The alkalinity and unknown trace elements make it uncertain. Bring a high-quality water filter as backup, but primarily rely on water you carry in. Plan for 3-4 liters per day.

**Food**: High-calorie, stable foods. Nothing that requires cooking—open flames behave strangely near the structures, and you want to minimize your impact. Dense foods like nuts, dried fruit, energy bars, pemmican. The damp makes everything soggy eventually, so waterproof packaging is essential. 

Some travelers report altered hunger—the temporal distortions affect metabolism. You might not feel hungry for 18 hours, then be ravenous. Eat on a schedule, not by hunger cues. Your body's signals become unreliable.

Avoid anything that requires you to be away from your supplies for extended periods. The spatial anomalies mean a "short walk" might become hours, and you need to keep food with you.

### Medical Kit
Beyond standard first aid, include:

**Foot care**: Extensive blister treatment, antifungal medications, waterproof bandaging. Your feet will take tremendous abuse.

**Ear protection**: Musician's earplugs that reduce volume without blocking frequencies. When the hum becomes overwhelming, these can make the difference between staying and fleeing. However, use them sparingly—hearing the hum's changes is important safety information.

**Anti-nausea medication**: The combination of constant vibration, spatial distortion, and temporal anomalies can produce motion sickness-like symptoms.

**Antihistamines**: Some people react to the spores or particles from the algae mats and conducting plants.

**Pain relievers**: For the headaches. Most people get them from the sustained acoustic pressure. Ibuprofen or acetaminophen in quantity.

**Prescription medications**: Whatever you normally take, plus 50% extra, plus extensive documentation of dosage and timing. Keep meticulous logs of when you take medication, because your sense of time will be unreliable.

**Psychological**: If you have any anti-anxiety or sleep medications prescribed, bring them. The Hollows can be profoundly distressing, and sleep becomes difficult with the constant sounds and the strange dreams.

### Communication
Standard radio and cell communication are unreliable. The structures interfere with electromagnetic transmission. Bring satellite communication devices, but understand they'll work intermittently at best. When you're near nodes, expect total communication failure.

More importantly, establish a dead-man switch with someone outside. A specific time by which you'll make contact, and a protocol for them to follow if you don't. The temporal anomalies mean you might think you've been inside for three days when it's been a week outside, or vice versa. External accountability is essential.

## Mental and Psychological Preparations

This is arguably more important than physical preparation.

### Developing Present-Moment Awareness
Begin a daily meditation practice focused on present-moment sensory awareness. Not transcendence or escape, but intensive presence. Practice noticing:
- The exact qualities of sounds around you
- The precise sensations in your body
- The specific characteristics of light and shadow
- The texture of time passing

In the Hollows, your ability to notice subtle changes in the hum, in the water's movement, in the light through the fog, will be your primary navigation tool. These are not metaphorical—they're literal wayfinding cues. The more refined your sensory awareness, the safer and more coherent your experience will be.

### Cultivating Cognitive Flexibility
Practice holding contradictory ideas simultaneously. The Hollows will regularly present you with experiences that violate your expectations of how reality works. Your ability to accept "this distance is both 500 meters and 2 kilometers" or "I've been walking for one hour and also four hours" without cognitive breakdown is crucial.

Study quantum mechanics, not for the science, but for the practice of thinking about things that behave contradictorily. Read philosophy of time and space. The goal isn't to understand the Hollows intellectually—you won't—but to make your mind supple enough to inhabit uncertainty without panic.

### Establishing Cognitive Anchors
Develop a set of mental practices you can use to re-center yourself:
- Memorize poetry or texts you can recite to remind yourself of linguistic structure
- Practice a simple mathematical sequence (Fibonacci numbers, prime numbers) you can run through
- Develop a body-scan routine you can do to reconnect with physical sensation
- Create a personal ritual—something simple you can do daily to maintain continuity

These anchors won't prevent disorientation, but they'll give you a thread back to consensus reality when things become especially strange.

### Confronting Your Relationship with Control
The Hollows will strip away your illusion of control. Nothing you do will make the place behave predictably. The sooner you accept this, the less you'll suffer.

Practice surrendering control in your daily life. Take walks where you choose direction randomly. Spend days without checking the time. Put yourself in situations where you must adapt rather than dictate. This isn't about becoming passive—it's about developing active adaptation rather than rigid planning.

### Preparing for Isolation
Even if you go with others, you'll experience profound isolation in the Hollows. The place has a way of making each person's experience individual. Practice being alone with yourself. Multi-day solo retreats before your journey can reveal how you respond to isolation and give you a chance to develop coping strategies.

### Understanding Your "Why"
Be very clear about why you're going. Not the surface reason—the deep reason. The Hollows will test your motivation. If you're going for superficial reasons (adventure, bragging rights, because someone dared you), you'll likely flee or be miserable. If you're going because you genuinely need to encounter something that operates outside human frameworks, because you're searching for perspective that consensus reality can't provide, because you have questions that can only be approached in places where reality is negotiable—then you have a chance of meaningful experience.

Write down your intentions. Seal them in a waterproof container and bring them with you. When you're disoriented and uncomfortable and questioning why you came, you can read them and reconnect with your purpose.

## Social and Logistical Preparations

### Expedition Planning
**Solo vs. Group**: There are arguments for both. Solo travel means you're entirely dependent on your own resources and perception, which is both dangerous and clarifying. Group travel provides redundancy and shared reality-checking, but groups also fragment in the Hollows—the spatial anomalies separate people easily, and the individual nature of the temporal distortions means you'll be experiencing different timeframes even when physically together.

If going in a group, keep it small (2-4 people maximum) and choose companions carefully. You need people who are:
- Comfortable with uncertainty and ambiguity
- Not prone to panic or rigid thinking  
- Capable of independent function if separated
- Trustworthy enough that you can believe their observations even when they contradict yours

Establish protocols before entering:
- Regular check-in schedules (even though time is unstable, the practice of regular checking helps)
- Agreed-upon meeting points (even though space is unstable, having designated sites helps)
- Decision-making frameworks for when observations conflict
- Criteria for retreat and evacuation

### Legal and Bureaucratic Considerations
Check current access regulations. The Hollows exist in [you'll need to determine this for your actual travel], and access may be restricted or require permits. Some research institutions have established protocols for visiting. Contact them. Their institutional knowledge is valuable.

Ensure your affairs are in order. Update your will. Leave clear instructions for someone you trust about what to do if you don't return or return changed. This sounds dramatic, but people have disappeared in the Hollows, and others have returned with altered psychology that makes reintegration difficult.

Purchase comprehensive travel insurance if available, though read the fine print—coverage may exclude "reality anomaly zones" or similar classifications.

### Establishing External Support
You need someone outside who:
- Knows exactly where you're going and when
- Has copies of your plans and protocols  
- Will raise alarms if you don't check in on schedule
- Understands that "on schedule" means by external calendar, not your subjective experience
- Is prepared to organize rescue or retrieval if necessary

Give them copies of all your documentation, maps, and research. Brief them extensively on the Hollows' nature so they understand what you're dealing with.

### Skill Development
If you don't already have these skills, develop them:

**Basic wilderness survival**: Though the Hollows aren't wilderness in a conventional sense, core skills (shelter building, fire starting, emergency signaling) remain relevant.

**Water reading**: Learn to observe water movement, current patterns, depth estimation. These skills will help you navigate the Hollows' aquatic environment.

**Orienteering without instruments**: Practice navigating by sun position, landmarks, and dead reckoning. When instruments fail, these fundamentals matter.

**Field documentation**: Practice observing and recording systematically. Science field methods, naturalist sketching, detailed note-taking. The Hollows reward careful observation.

**Basic electronics troubleshooting**: You'll be doing constant equipment maintenance in damp conditions.

**Psychological first aid**: Either for yourself or companions. Basic techniques for managing acute stress, panic, and dissociation.

## Specific Preparations for Known Challenges

### The Hum
Understand that the hum will become part of your consciousness. It's not background noise—it's a presence that will inhabit your body and mind. Prepare for:

**Physical effects**: Practice functioning while experiencing constant vibration. Use a massage gun or sit on a running washing machine to approximate the sensation. It's not the same, but it helps you understand how vibration affects concentration, coordination, and comfort.

**Psychological effects**: The hum can become maddening or transcendent. Practice accepting constant stimulus you can't control. Use white noise machines or constant tones in your preparation period to build tolerance.

**Using the hum**: Learn to meditate with, not despite, constant sound. The hum carries information—changes in frequency, emergence of harmonics, pulses and patterns. Train yourself to notice these while maintaining functionality.

### The Water
You'll be wet constantly. Prepare for:

**Skin management**: Your skin will be macerated—the "pruning" effect, but sustained. Use barrier creams preventatively. Practice staying in water for extended periods to understand how your skin responds. Some people develop rashes or fungal issues; know your vulnerabilities.

**Temperature regulation**: The cool water creates constant heat drain. Practice staying active and warm while wet and cold. Cold water immersion training can help, though the Hollows are less extreme than true cold water—it's more the sustained duration that's challenging.

**Movement in water**: Walk extensively in water before you go. Understand how it affects your gait, balance, and energy expenditure. Build the specific muscles used for water walking.

### The Spatial Anomalies
You cannot prepare to navigate unstable space conventionally. Instead:

**Develop intuitive navigation**: Practice walking familiar routes without looking at landmarks, using only your kinesthetic sense. The Hollows require you to navigate by feel as much as sight.

**Build tolerance for disorientation**: Practice activities that disorient you—spinning, walking blindfolded, navigating unfamiliar places without maps. Become comfortable with not knowing where you are.

**Learn to recognize your own spatial intuition**: Some people can feel when space is "wrong." It's subtle, like proprioception but external. You might feel it as wrongness, resistance, or unease. Learn to notice and trust these sensations.

### The Temporal Anomalies
You cannot stabilize time, but you can maintain reference:

**Practice time estimation**: Spend days estimating time passage without checking clocks. Develop your internal chronometer, not because it will work in the Hollows, but because the practice makes you aware of time's texture.

**Document obsessively**: Keep detailed logs with timestamps (from your multiple watches). The log-keeping practice itself provides continuity even when the timestamps don't make sense.

**Accept temporal dislocation**: Practice being late, missing appointments, having your schedule disrupted. The control we exert over time in normal life is something you must release in the Hollows.

### The Reality Instabilities Near Nodes
This is the hardest to prepare for because it touches on consciousness and perception itself.

**Study altered states of consciousness**: Not to induce them artificially, but to understand how consciousness can modify while remaining functional. Read accounts of meditation retreats, sensory deprivation, psychedelic research (academic literature, not recreational reports), lucid dreaming. The goal is to recognize that consciousness is more malleable than everyday experience suggests.

**Practice reality-checking**: Develop habits of questioning your perceptions. "Am I sure this is happening?" "What evidence supports this observation?" Don't become paranoid, but become more empirical about your own experience.

**Establish baseline reality testing**: Know how you think and perceive in normal conditions. Take cognitive tests, memory tests. Document your baseline so you can recognize deviation.

**Prepare for revelations that fade**: Many people report profound understanding at nodes that vanishes when they leave. This can be deeply frustrating or feel like loss. Practice accepting insights that can't be retained. Work with koans or paradoxes that can't be resolved, only experienced.

## What to Study Before Going

### Scientific Background
You don't need advanced degrees, but familiarize yourself with:

**Acoustics**: Basic understanding of frequency, harmonics, resonance, standing waves. This helps you perceive what the hum is doing.

**Hydrology**: Water movement, aquifer systems, wetland ecology. Understand the water environment you'll inhabit.

**Geology**: Deep time, geological processes, mineralization. The structures are embedded in geological context.

**Physics**: Relativity, quantum mechanics, field theory—at a conceptual level. Not to explain the Hollows, but to expand what you consider possible.

**Ecology**: Succession, adaptation, symbiosis. The Hollows are an ecology, albeit a strange one.

### Relevant Reading
Accounts from previous travelers (if available). Research papers from institutions studying the Hollows. But also:

**Literature of the strange**: Borges, Lem, Lovecraft (for the cosmicism, not the racism), VanderMeer. Fiction that explores spaces and beings that operate outside human frameworks.

**Philosophy**: Phenomenology (Merleau-Ponty especially), process philosophy (Whitehead), philosophy of time and space. These won't explain the Hollows but will give you conceptual tools for thinking about non-standard reality.

**Anthropology**: Accounts of radically different worldviews and perceptual frameworks. Understanding that reality is perceived differently by different cultures can help you accept that the Hollows may require/induce different perception.

**Contemplative literature**: Meditation manuals, contemplative practice from any tradition. The mental disciplines developed for spiritual purposes transfer usefully to navigating anomalous spaces.

## Final Preparations (Last Week Before Departure)

### Physical
- Taper any intense physical training; arrive rested
- Eat well, sleep well, optimize your physical condition
- Do final equipment checks—test everything in wet conditions
- Break in any new gear; nothing should be worn for the first time in the Hollows
- Final medical check: teeth (dental problems are miserable when you can't leave), any minor health issues addressed
- Get a massage or bodywork; arrive in your body, aware of it

### Mental
- Reduce stimulation: less screen time, less news, less social intensity
- Increase contemplative practice: more meditation, more silence, more presence
- Review your intentions and motivations
- Make peace with uncertainty—truly accept that you don't know what will happen
- Say proper goodbyes to people; don't leave relationships in ambiguous states
- Handle any emotional business that would prey on your mind
- Clear your schedule for at least two weeks after your planned return for integration and recovery

### Logistical
- Final briefing with your external contact person
- Ensure all documents, access permissions, and authorizations are in order
- Confirm emergency protocols and communication schedules
- Stage equipment for easy packing
- Prepare travel to the access point
- One last check: do you genuinely want to do this? If doubt is strong, postpone. The Hollows will still be there, and better to go when you're ready than to push forward from obligation.

## The Approach

How you enter matters. Don't rush from ordinary life directly into the Hollows. If possible:

**Acclimatize gradually**: Spend time in the surrounding region first. Let your nervous system adjust to the proximity of something strange. Many travelers report sensing the Hollows before seeing them—a change in air pressure, a faint underlying hum at the edge of hearing, a sense of weight or presence.

**Enter during daylight**: Even though the light is dim, the slight increase in visibility during day-equivalent time makes initial orientation easier.

**Enter slowly**: Walk don't rush. Let each step take you deeper into the place's influence. Notice how things change—the sound building, the air thickening, the water beginning.

**Enter respectfully**: This isn't empty wilderness you're conquering. Something is here, doing something, and has been for longer than human civilization. You're a visitor in a place with its own purposes. Humility is both ethically appropriate and practically useful.

## Moment of Truth

You'll know when you're truly in the Hollows. It's not a geographic boundary—it's when the hum becomes undeniable, when the water is continuous, when distances start feeling negotiable. Some people report a moment of choice, a sense that they could still turn back, and then a threshold beyond which return requires traveling out rather than just turning around.

That moment—pay attention to it. Notice how you feel, what you think, whether you want to continue. There's no shame in turning back. The Hollows aren't for everyone, and recognizing your limits is wisdom, not weakness.

If you continue, accept that you're now in a different relationship with reality. The rules you've lived by your entire life are suspended here. The place will make demands of your attention, your adaptability, your tolerance for strangeness.

Stay alert. Stay present. Stay flexible.

Document what you can, but remember that experience exceeds documentation. The most important things may not be recordable.

Trust your intuition, but verify when possible.

The Hollows are patient. They've been here before you and will remain after you. You're ephemeral in this place. Act accordingly—with appropriate care for your safety and appropriate humility about your significance.

## What You Cannot Prepare For

Some aspects of the Hollows cannot be prepared for, only experienced:

The specific texture of the hum in your body. The exact quality of the light through fog. The peculiar smell of the water and the algae and the structures. The way your mind feels when time is unstable. The sight of the conducting plants stretching to every horizon. The sensation of standing on a node with the structures humming beneath you and suddenly understanding something you can't explain.

These are not failures of preparation—they're the reason for going. You're traveling to the Hollows precisely because they offer experience that cannot be simulated, predicted, or fully prepared for.

The preparation outlined above is not about controlling the experience. It's about giving you the foundation to survive and remain functional while something genuinely outside your reference frame occurs. It's about making you resilient enough to bend without breaking, present enough to notice what's happening, and grounded enough to integrate the experience afterward.

The Hollows will teach you what they teach you. Your preparation simply ensures you're capable of being taught.

## After: Integration

This isn't preparation, but understand now that returning from the Hollows is its own challenge. You'll need time to:

- Resynchronize with standard time (may take days or weeks)
- Reacclimate to solid ground and dry conditions  
- Process the experience cognitively and emotionally
- Restore normal sleep patterns (the dreams may continue)
- Reconnect with ordinary reality's pace and logic
- Integrate any insights or changes in perspective

Leave space for this. Don't schedule a return to normal life immediately. The Hollows leave marks—mostly internal, sometimes external. Honor them.

And understand: some people can't fully return. They remain partially there, some part of their attention always listening for the hum, some part of their perception forever slightly temporal-plastic, forever slightly aware that reality is more negotiable than consensus suggests.

This isn't damage, exactly. It's change. Whether it's welcome change depends on the person and how they integrate it.

You've been warned. You've been informed. You've been given tools.

The rest is up to you and the Hollows.

Safe travels. Or rather—travels as safe as the Breathing Hollows permit, which is safer than you fear but less safe than you'd prefer, and absolutely not safe in any way you're accustomed to the word meaning.
