# Preparation Guide for The Canopy of New Engines

## Physical Conditioning

Begin your preparation at least three months before departure. This location sits between 8,000-9,000 feet elevation, and you'll be navigating unstable terrain for extended periods. Focus on:

**Cardiovascular adaptation**: If you live at sea level, spend weekends at progressively higher elevations. Your body needs to produce more red blood cells. Altitude sickness here won't just be uncomfortable—it could be fatal given the isolation and difficult evacuation routes.

**Balance and proprioception training**: Practice walking on unstable surfaces. Foam balance pads, slacklines, or simply walking on pillows will help. The moss compresses unpredictably—sometimes three inches, sometimes twelve. Your ankles will be constantly adjusting. Weak ankles have no place here.

**Core and leg strength**: The constant micro-adjustments of unstable terrain will exhaust muscles you didn't know you had. Weighted squats, single-leg deadlifts, and extensive hiking with a loaded pack on uneven ground. Your pack will weigh 40-50 pounds minimum for any multi-day visit.

## Gear: The Critical Considerations

**Footwear will make or break you.** This deserves its own section because it's that important.

You need waterproof boots, but here's the problem: nothing stays waterproof when you're squeezing through saturated moss for eight hours. The water will find its way in—through the laces, down from your pants, eventually through the membrane itself. So you need:

- Primary boots: High-quality waterproof mountaineering boots with excellent ankle support. Full leather, not fabric. They'll get soaked eventually but will protect longer.
- Camp boots: Completely separate dry footwear that never leaves your tent/shelter. Neoprene or rubber boots with good insulation.
- Three pairs of wool socks minimum per day of travel. Merino wool, expedition weight. Accept that you'll be changing socks every few hours.
- Foot powder and prevention supplies for trench foot: This is a real risk. The constant moisture, the temperature just below comfort, the inability to ever be truly dry—your feet will try to rot. Antifungal powder, breathable liner socks, vigilant daily inspection.

**Clothing system:**

The temperature "hovers just below comfort" which means you'll be perpetually managing a narrow margin between too cold when stopped and overheating when moving. You need:

- Base layers: Merino wool or high-end synthetic. Two sets minimum. One to wear, one to keep dry.
- Insulation: A quality down or synthetic jacket that packs small. You'll be putting it on and taking it off constantly.
- Outer layer: Here's where it gets tricky. A traditional waterproof shell will trap moisture from your sweat in this humidity. Consider a highly breathable soft shell for movement, with a proper hardshell for when you're stationary or clouds fully envelope you.
- Rain pants: Essential, but accept they'll be constantly damp inside.
- Gloves: Multiple pairs. Waterproof shells with removable wool liners. Your hands will be touching wet surfaces constantly—climbing over moss-covered boulders, steadying yourself. Cold hands become useless hands quickly.
- Head covering: Wool beanie and a brimmed hat. The sun is "merciless" at this elevation despite the moisture.

**Navigation and communication:**

- GPS with pre-loaded topographic maps: Distances are "uncertain" in the luminous haze. You cannot trust your visual assessment of how far something is.
- Compass and paper maps in waterproof cases: Electronics fail, especially in constant moisture.
- Satellite communication device: There is no cell service. There are no nearby humans. If you're injured, you need to be able to call for extraction, and even then, understand that help is many hours away at minimum.
- Bright-colored surveyor's tape: The paths are "suggestions rather than scars." Mark your route, especially if exploring off the compressed paths. The moss all looks the same, and every dome and terrace resembles every other.

**Shelter:**

Do not rely on the existing structures. They're minimal, infrequent, and potentially serving some purpose you don't understand. Bring:

- A four-season tent with excellent waterproofing and ventilation: The moisture will condense on every surface. You need air circulation or you'll wake up in a puddle.
- Ground pad considerations: You're not camping on ground but on compressed moss. Bring an insulated pad that's generous in thickness. The cold will seep up through that organic matrix.
- Camp site selection will be difficult: You need something relatively flat, which means finding exposed stone or exceptionally compressed moss. Factor in extra time for site preparation. You may need to create a platform.

**Water—the paradox:**

Water is "everywhere and nowhere you can properly see." Hydration is critical at altitude, and you're surrounded by moisture, but:

- Filtration system: Despite the apparent purity, that water is moving through thick organic matter, possibly through machine oil leaks, definitely through fungal mats and decomposition layers. Bring both a pump filter and chemical backup treatment.
- Collection strategy: Wide-mouth containers to squeeze moss into, or soft containers you can position under weeping stone faces.
- Drink constantly: The dry air at elevation plus physical exertion means you need 4-5 liters per day minimum.

**Food:**

High-calorie, no-cook options are your friend. You can cook—bring a reliable camp stove and fuel—but everything will take longer at this elevation, and the moisture makes fire management complex. 

- Calories: 3,500-4,500 per day minimum. The cold, the altitude, the unstable terrain—all burn energy ferociously.
- Packaging: Double-bag everything. The moisture will penetrate.
- Ease: Nuts, dried fruit, energy bars, instant meals, cheese, hard salami. Things that don't require drying out.

## Medical Preparation

**Pre-departure:**

- Physical with focus on cardiovascular health: Altitude plus exertion can reveal heart issues you didn't know you had.
- Dental check: A tooth problem here becomes a crisis.
- Medications pre-stocked: Altitude sickness prevention (acetazolamide/Diamox), strong pain relievers, antibiotics for respiratory infections, anti-diarrheal, antifungal for feet.

**First aid kit specifics for this environment:**

- Blister treatment: Extensive. Moleskin, liquid bandage, various sizes of hydrocolloid dressings.
- Wound care in moisture: Waterproof bandages, antiseptic, consideration for how to keep injuries dry (nearly impossible).
- Respiratory supplies: The constant moisture and altitude can trigger breathing issues. Inhaler even if you don't typically need one.
- Hypothermia treatment: Emergency bivy, chemical hand warmers, knowledge of how to stabilize someone.
- Splints and wraps: Ankle injuries on unstable terrain are likely. Know how to stabilize and self-evacuate if alone.

## Psychological Preparation

This may be the most overlooked and most critical element.

**The sound will affect you.** Read that section again. "Ten thousand trickles, a million drips," plus the machines, plus the wind, plus the subsonic mountain settling. This never stops. Ever. Not for one second of your visit.

Some people find constant white noise soothing. Others find it maddening after hours or days. You need to know which you are. Practice by:

- Spending nights with recordings of rain, streams, and industrial sounds playing continuously
- Notice how you sleep, how you feel after 8 hours, 16 hours, 24 hours
- Pay attention to whether you can think clearly, whether anxiety builds
- Consider bringing earplugs, but know that reducing your auditory awareness also reduces safety awareness

**Isolation preparation:**

The description mentions seeing "a bird... once in an afternoon." You might walk for hours seeing no animals, no humans, nothing but green and stone and machines. Some minds handle this beautifully. Others begin to fracture.

Before committing to an extended visit:
- Spend time alone in nature, progressively longer periods
- Notice your thought patterns when isolated
- Understand your comfort level with being entirely self-reliant
- Consider that evacuation could take 12-24 hours even after calling for help

**The machines will unsettle you.** They function without obvious purpose, "not abandoned" but "not attended." They've integrated into the ecology in ways that blur the line between mechanical and organic. The philosophical strangeness of patient engines tending an infant ecosystem while making sounds you "feel in your sternum"—this can produce profound unease or profound wonder, depending on your disposition.

Think about how you respond to:
- Ambiguity without resolution
- Systems operating outside human control
- The combination of natural and industrial
- Beauty that includes rust and decay

## Timing and Seasons

The description doesn't specify seasonal variation, which suggests this place maintains relatively constant conditions, but:

**Avoid winter if this location has one.** The temperature "rarely cold enough to threaten frost" suggests it approaches freezing. Add altitude, moisture, and wind, and winter could mean dangerous ice layers hidden under moss, machinery frozen into dysfunction, impossible navigation.

**Optimal visiting window:** Likely late spring through early fall, when days are longest and temperatures warmest. But verify this with any existing records or agencies monitoring the area.

**Weather volatility:** High-altitude weather changes rapidly. Clouds can "sock in" the valley completely. Plan for:
- Being weathered in for extra days
- Sudden temperature drops
- Visibility dropping to meters
- Wind that makes certain areas impassable

Bring extra food, extra fuel, extra patience.

## Route Planning

**Pre-visit research:**

- Study satellite imagery if available, though the "moss the size of dining tables" may obscure ground truth
- Contact any agencies that might maintain those machines—someone services them, or did originally
- Research the geology: "three or four million years" means these are indeed young mountains, likely still seismically active. Understand volcanic and earthquake risk.
- Look for trip reports from others, though this place sounds obscure enough that they may not exist

**On-site strategy:**

- Day 1: Short exploratory hike from your entry point, return to base camp, assess how your body handles the terrain
- Never commit to a long cross-valley traverse until you know your pace on the moss
- Machine locations serve as navigation anchors, but don't depend on them being accessible—a pump station on a cliff isn't a useful waypoint
- The compressed paths are your friend: Use them when available, even if they don't go exactly your direction
- Time everything at half your normal pace, then add contingency
- Mark your camps and caches elaborately—everything looks the same

## Technical Skills to Acquire

**Before arrival:**

- Map and compass navigation: Essential
- GPS operation in complex terrain: Practice
- Weather prediction from cloud formations: Critical at altitude
- Basic equipment repair: You can't replace things here
- Rope work and climbing skills: Not for technical climbing, but for safely navigating short steep sections in slippery conditions
- Emergency shelter construction: If your tent fails, can you survive the night?
- Self-rescue techniques: You are your own rescue team

## What to Expect Experientially

**The first hour:** Wonder. The luminous green, the sound, the otherworldly combination of natural and mechanical—it will be unlike anything else.

**Hours 2-6:** Physical adjustment. Your ankles will hurt. Your sense of distance will be confused. You'll learn to trust the moss, mostly.

**First night:** The sound continues in darkness. The moisture continues. You cannot see the machines but you hear them. The mountain mutters below. This will either be meditative or disturbing.

**Days 2-3:** You'll have naturalized somewhat. The constant damp becomes normal. The sound becomes the new silence. You'll start seeing patterns—routes, good camping spots, which machines seem to serve which purposes.

**Extended stays:** The alienness may return. The question of purpose—why the machines, why this place, what is being served or monitored—may become obsessive. The lack of fauna may start feeling actively wrong rather than just unusual. The landscape's youth, its unfinished quality, may make you feel like you're trespassing in a place not yet ready for visitors.

## Ethical Considerations

**Do not interfere with the machines.** You don't know what they're doing. They may be part of an active research project, a monitoring network for volcanic activity, a critical infrastructure element for communities you can't see. Photograph them, note their locations, but don't touch.

**Leave no trace is inadequate here.** Practice leave no trace AT ALL. The ecosystem is "inaugural," still forming its rules. Your tent platform, your waste, your paths—all could disrupt processes that operate on century timescales. 

- Pack out everything, including toilet paper
- Disperse your stepping spots when possible
- Don't camp on untouched moss formations if exposed stone is available
- Consider whether your visit is justified at all

**The compressed paths suggest permitted routes.** Stay on them when they exist. Someone or something has determined these are acceptable travel corridors.

## Emergency Protocols

**If injured:**

1. Immediate self-stabilization and first aid
2. Activate satellite communicator with precise GPS coordinates
3. Move to most visible, accessible location possible for extraction—probably near a machine on relatively flat terrain
4. Prepare for a long wait in deteriorating conditions
5. Make yourself visible: bright colors, signal mirror, even a small fire if you can manage it safely

**If lost:**

1. Stop moving immediately
2. GPS check against maps
3. If electronics failed, use compass and last known position
4. Return to last certain location even if it means backtracking hours
5. Follow compressed paths when found, even if direction seems wrong—they go somewhere

**If a machine fails or behaves differently:**

1. Note the time and changes carefully
2. Move away from it
3. Understand this might indicate broader system changes—seismic activity, infrastructure failure
4. Consider ending your visit earlier than planned

## Communication Plan

Before departure, establish a check-in schedule with someone in the outside world:

- Specific times you'll make satellite contact
- What they should do if you miss check-ins (when to trigger rescue)
- Your planned route and campsites by date
- Expected return date with buffer
- Give them a copy of all your maps and plans

## Philosophical Preparation

This place will raise questions you may not want to answer:

- What is wilderness when machines are integrated into it?
- What is "natural" when human infrastructure and plant ecology co-evolve?
- What is the purpose of beauty no one sees?
- What does it mean that patient engines tend a landscape, possibly after human attention has moved elsewhere?

Consider these before arrival. The isolation and strangeness will amplify existential discomfort if you bring it with you.

## A Final Word

The Canopy of New Engines is described with a particular kind of specificity that suggests real observation, but also with an ambiguity about location and access that should give you pause. A place where "young volcanic peaks" are colonized by moss thick enough to walk on, where scattered machines operate in apparent perpetuity, where the sound alone could drive some visitors to early departure—this is not a casual destination.

If you approach this location with appropriate humility, preparation, and respect for both its youth and its strangeness, it may offer experiences available nowhere else: walking on a living surface stretched across mountains still deciding their shape, hearing the conversation between water and stone and machine in a landscape that hasn't yet written all its rules.

But if you come unprepared—physically, mentally, or logistically—this place will humble you quickly and possibly dangerously. The moss that seems soft will hide crevasses. The machines that seem picturesque will remind you of your irrelevance. The beauty that photographs as luminous green wonder will reveal itself as cold, wet, constant, and indifferent to your comfort.

Prepare accordingly. Go cautiously. Return safely with stories of a threshold world where engines tend an infant ecology under merciless light, where every surface weeps, where sound never ceases, where green conquers all.

And maybe, if you're very fortunate and very attentive, you'll understand what those machines are doing, what purpose was encoded in their placement, what vision saw this valley of new stone and decided it needed patient mechanical witness to its loud, luminous becoming.
