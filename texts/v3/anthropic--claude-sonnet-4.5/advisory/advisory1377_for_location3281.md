# Preparation Guide for Visiting the Counting Vaults

## Physical Conditioning and Acclimatization

You'll need to prepare your body for heat endurance in uniquely challenging conditions. The Counting Vaults don't offer volcanic drama or desert dryness—they offer something worse: sustained body-temperature humidity in the upper levels, escalating to 105-115°F in the depths, with moisture that prevents your sweat from evaporating. This is the heat that kills quietly.

Spend at least six weeks before your journey doing heat acclimatization training. Start with hot yoga or sauna sessions, but progress to exercising in steam rooms if you can access them. The goal is to teach your body to function when its primary cooling mechanism—evaporative sweat—is compromised. Pay attention to early heat exhaustion symptoms: the headache, the disorientation, the confusion that creeps in before you realize you're in trouble. Learn to recognize these signs in yourself when you're safe, so you'll catch them when you're not.

Your cardiovascular system will be stressed constantly. Even light activity in the lower levels will spike your heart rate as your body desperately tries to cool itself. If you have any heart conditions, consult a physician who understands heat stress—this isn't a journey for the cardiovascularly compromised.

Consider that you'll be moving through vertical space repeatedly, climbing narrow brick and metal staircases while carrying supplies and light sources. Your legs need to be strong, but more importantly, your stabilizer muscles need conditioning. Practice stair climbing with a loaded backpack in warm conditions. The staircases are steep, turning at hard angles, sometimes enclosed in walls that radiate retained heat.

## Water Strategy: This Cannot Be Overstated

Water is everything in the Vaults. You'll lose fluid at a terrifying rate in the lower levels—potentially a liter or more per hour—and while the settlement has collection systems, you cannot assume free access to their carefully hoarded supplies.

Bring a hydration system that allows constant sipping without stopping: a bladder-style reservoir with an insulated drinking tube. You'll need at least 6-8 liters of carrying capacity, and even that may not be enough for extended stays in the lower tiers. The water from the cisterns is described as "clean enough," but that's resident immunity talking. Your virgin gut flora will have other opinions.

Pack a robust water filtration system—not a simple camping filter, but something that handles mineral contamination, potential chemical infiltration from the ancient pipes, and whatever biological elements colonize that hyper-clear water in the older tanks. A combined UV sterilizer and ceramic/activated carbon filter system is minimum. Bring water purification tablets as backup to your backup.

Electrolyte replacement is critical. The heat will drain sodium, potassium, and magnesium from your system faster than you can replace them with water alone. Bring powdered electrolyte supplements—enough for twice as long as you plan to stay. Hyponatremia (water intoxication from drinking too much pure water without electrolyte replacement) is a real danger when you're losing salts that rapidly.

Watch your urine. Dark yellow means you're already dehydrated. Clear and copious means your electrolyte balance is off. You're aiming for pale yellow—that's the narrow window of proper hydration in extreme heat.

## Clothing and Textile Considerations

The moisture content varies dramatically by location, and you'll be moving between zones. This creates a unique challenge: you need clothing that handles both dry heat and humid heat, sometimes within minutes of each other.

For the upper and middle tiers: loose, light-colored cotton or linen in layers. Natural fibers breathe and absorb the moisture that will coat your skin. The moisture won't evaporate to cool you, but at least it won't trap against your skin as it would with synthetics. Long sleeves and long pants protect you from the hot brick surfaces you'll inevitably brush against in narrow passages.

For the lower tiers: you might counterintuitively want to strip down to minimal clothing. The heat is so severe that any fabric becomes a liability, trapping the moisture your body produces. Bring a minimal set of moisture-wicking synthetic undergarments for the deep descents, and resign yourself to being soaked regardless.

Multiple changes of clothing are essential. You cannot dry anything in the humid sections—moisture has nowhere to go. In the drier areas, the mineral-laden condensation will leave your clothing stiff with salt deposits after it dries. Plan on needing a complete change of clothing every 12-24 hours.

Footwear must handle multiple surface types: brick (often slick with condensation), metal grating (which can be sharp and unforgiving), wet stone, and standing water in the flooded sections. Bring boots with excellent ankle support for the staircases, aggressive tread for traction on damp brick, and quick-drying materials. Bring backup footwear. Bring supplies to treat blisters, because you will get them—the combination of heat, moisture, and constant stair climbing guarantees it.

A head covering is essential—something light-colored with a brim to shield your eyes from the dim lights and protect your head from low beams. The construction is dense and irregular; you will hit your head. The brick absorbs sound, so you won't hear others approaching on the other side of a partition. Stay alert in narrow passages.

## Light Sources: Redundancy and Diversity

Natural light ends at the third tier. Below that, you're dependent entirely on human-made light—yours and the settlement's unreliable electric bulbs that dim and surge on unknowable schedules.

Primary light source: a high-quality LED headlamp with multiple brightness settings and a red light mode for preserving night vision. Bring at least one complete backup. These are warm-orange environments; your light will need to cut through the existing dimness without washing everything into obscurity.

Secondary sources: at least two handheld LED flashlights with different beam patterns (flood and spot). Keep one accessible on your person at all times—if your headlamp fails in a deep, unlit section, you need immediate backup before you start stumbling into machinery or over partition walls.

For extended stays in the deeper tiers, consider a battery-powered LED lantern that can be hung or set down, freeing your hands for other tasks. The residents use oil lamps—you could adopt this practice if you're staying long-term, but it requires securing oil, maintaining equipment, and dealing with open flame in close quarters. Not recommended unless you're committing to an extended residency.

Bring batteries. Then bring twice that many. Cold batteries lose charge; hot batteries also lose charge. The heat in the lower vaults will degrade battery performance. Store your spares in the coolest area you can access—possibly in a water-cooled container kept in one of the upper-tier cisterns if you can negotiate permission.

Chemical light sticks can serve as emergency backups and as location markers if you're exploring the uninhabited sections at the boundaries. They're not affected by heat the way batteries are, though extreme temperatures may alter their duration.

## Navigation and Spatial Awareness

The Vaults are disorienting by nature: seven tiers compressed into sixty vertical feet, each tier partitioned and subdivided by walls built over unknown timescales. There's no consistent layout, no clear center, no obvious organizational logic.

Bring paper—actual paper—and something to write with that won't fail in the heat. Pencils, not pens; ink can run in the moisture or dry up in the heat. Map as you go. Note landmarks: the fan in the third tier, the pumping station in the fifth, the flooded cane section in the fourth. Record water sources you're permitted to use. Mark which staircases connect which levels.

A compass will be useless without knowing the overall orientation of the Vaults, but bring one anyway—if you can reach one of the upper-tier ventilation shafts and see the sun, you can establish cardinal directions and use that to orient your maps.

The density of the construction means you can be twenty feet from something and not know it's there because three partition walls and a pipe array block the direct route. Think in three dimensions. The ventilation shafts connect levels vertically in offset positions—these can serve as reference points if you can identify which shaft is which from tier to tier.

Learn the settlement's water-clock and light-timing systems if possible. Time becomes unreliable in spaces without natural light, especially when the heat induces mild disorientation. Having external temporal references helps maintain your circadian rhythm and prevents the time-dissolution that occurs in unchanging environments.

## Medical Considerations and First Aid

Beyond the standard first-aid kit, you need specific provisions for the Vaults' environmental hazards:

Heat illness treatment: cooling towels, a spray bottle for misting yourself with water (evaporation helps even when it's slow), knowledge of heat exhaustion progression and treatment protocols. If you go to the seventh tier, have a cooling plan: know where the nearest water source is, have a partner watching for confusion or disorientation, set a maximum time limit before ascending to cooler levels.

Skin care becomes critical in the alternating dry and humid environments. Your skin will crack in the drier areas, become pruned and susceptible to fungal infection in the humid areas. Bring both moisturizing lotion and antifungal powder. Petroleum jelly or similar barrier cream for areas prone to chafing—the constant moisture will make this worse than any long hike you've experienced.

Respiratory protection: while not immediately necessary, a dust mask or respirator might be valuable in sections where salt efflorescence is heavy or where the fungi are actively releasing spores. The air quality is generally described as breathable, but that doesn't mean uncontaminated.

Eye care: the mineral-laden moisture and the dim light will stress your eyes. Bring saline solution for rinsing. If you wear contacts, consider switching to glasses—the moisture content of the air will make contact lens wear uncomfortable at best.

Cuts and scrapes in the humid heat become infection risks quickly. Your ability to keep wounds clean and dry is compromised. Bring antibiotic ointment, waterproof bandages, and consider prophylactic treatment of any injury, no matter how minor.

Psychological first-aid: the Vaults are claustrophobic, unchanging, and disorienting. The narrow vertical compression of space, the limited light, the constant heat—these accumulate psychologically. Bring something that connects you to the outside: photos, a smooth stone from home, anything tactile and meaningful. Set maximum duration limits for your stay. Have a partner on the surface who expects regular check-ins if possible.

## Supplies and Equipment

Bring food that tolerates heat and won't spoil without refrigeration: dried goods, nuts, preserved meats, dense calorie sources. You'll need more calories than usual—your body will burn energy fighting the heat stress. But you may find you're not hungry; heat suppresses appetite even as it increases caloric need. Force yourself to eat on a schedule.

Multi-tool or knife: essential for the thousand small tasks of navigating a densely constructed environment. Opening things, cutting cordage, making repairs to your gear.

Cordage: paracord or similar. You'll need to hang things, secure items, possibly jury-rig repairs to equipment. Bring 50 feet minimum.

A small tarp or emergency blanket: not for warmth, but as a ground cloth for setting up a rest area on the hot, possibly damp brick floors, or as a privacy screen if you need to claim a corner of a communal tier.

Something to sit on that insulates you from the hot brick—a closed-cell foam pad, minimal camping seat, anything that creates a thermal barrier between you and the stored heat in the floors and walls.

Zip-lock bags or waterproof stuff sacks: everything that can't get wet needs protection from the omnipresent moisture. Electronics, paper maps, spare clothing, food—assume moisture will find its way into everything eventually.

Duct tape and repair supplies for your gear: the heat and moisture will test every seam, seal, and joint in your equipment.

## Cultural and Social Preparation

The settlement has protocols, territories, and systems you'll need to understand and respect. These people maintain a complex water infrastructure under challenging conditions—you're a visitor consuming their resources and potentially disrupting their routines.

Learn patience and observation. Spend time in the upper tiers first, watching how things work before descending. Note which cisterns are in active use, which pipe systems are being maintained, where the boundaries of private versus common space seem to lie.

The residents appear to operate on maintenance and adaptation—continuous repair and adjustment rather than expansion or development. Offer useful skills if you have them: brick repair, pipe fitting, metal work, cultivation knowledge. Your presence is more welcome if you contribute rather than merely observe.

Respect the density of the space. In the partitioned areas, voices carry through walls even though the brick absorbs sound. What seems like private space might be separated from communal areas by only a canvas partition. Move carefully, announce your presence, ask permission before entering sections that seem residential.

The machines—especially in the lower tiers—are still operating. Don't touch them unless you understand them. The residents may not know their original purpose, but they've learned what to maintain and what to leave alone. Follow that wisdom.

## The Seventh Tier: Special Considerations

If you intend to visit the deepest level, this requires additional preparation beyond what's needed for the upper vaults.

Time limits are critical: set a maximum of 30 minutes for your first descent, and don't exceed it no matter what you're investigating. The heat is oppressive enough to cause rapid deterioration of judgment. Bring a timer that will alert you when it's time to ascend.

Go with a partner if possible, someone who can watch for heat stress symptoms while you're distracted by exploration. Establish clear signals for immediate withdrawal.

Bring your best light sources—the seventh tier's machinery deserves careful observation, and stumbling into moving metal in poor visibility could be catastrophic.

The question of the large machine—the one still operating—will tempt exploration and understanding. Resist the urge to interact with it beyond observation. It moves incrementally, without obvious purpose, maintained by no one. It's lasted this long specifically because no one has interfered. Photograph it if you must (heat-resistant camera required), sketch it, measure it if you can do so without touching, but leave it to its mysterious function.

## Duration and Pacing

Don't plan an extended stay in the Vaults unless you're prepared to adopt resident practices. The environmental stress is cumulative. Heat exhaustion builds gradually; dehydration is insidious; the disorientation of timeless, enclosed space accumulates psychologically.

For a first visit: 48 hours maximum, with most time spent in the upper three tiers where light and temperature are more manageable. Schedule regular rest periods in the coolest areas you can access. Sleep will be difficult—the heat, the unfamiliar sounds, the psychological weight of the enclosed space all interfere with rest.

If you're researching or documenting: plan multiple short visits rather than one extended stay. Your judgment and perception degrade with accumulated heat stress in ways you won't notice until you're back in normal conditions.

Build in recovery time after your visit—your body will need days to fully rehydrate and recover from the heat stress, even if you felt fine during the visit.

## Emergency Protocols

Before entering, establish your emergency withdrawal plan:

Know your route to the surface from every level. The staircases connect the tiers, but not always in consistent positions—you may need to traverse horizontally to find the staircase that ascends.

Identify water sources at multiple levels—if you're trapped or injured, hydration becomes your survival priority.

If the settlement has any kind of communication system between levels, learn it. If not, carry a whistle—sound carries poorly through the partitioned brick, but a whistle is louder than a human voice and requires less energy to produce repeatedly.

Have a surface contact who knows your planned return time and will initiate rescue if you don't emerge. The salt flat "no longer exists"—the surface terrain is unclear, but someone needs to know you're below and when to worry.

If you find yourself lost: stop, rest in the coolest spot you can find, conserve your light sources, and wait. The settlement is inhabited; someone will eventually pass through most areas. Making yourself discoverable is better than wandering in the heat while disoriented.

## Psychological Preparation

This may be the most important preparation of all.

The Counting Vaults offer a unique form of enclosed space: not a cave with irregular natural formation, not a building with windows and sky views, but a constructed environment compressed into narrow vertical depth, artificially lit, with no grand vistas or sense of opening. It's densely packed with human infrastructure, yet often empty of human presence. The combination creates a specific psychological weight.

Practice enclosed-space meditation before you go. Spend time in windowless basements, in sub-levels of parking garages, in service corridors—anywhere that gives you the feeling of depth and enclosure without actual danger. Notice your psychological response. Work with it.

Understand that the Vaults exist in a specific temporal mode: the slow drip of water, the imperceptible growth of fungi, the incremental movements of the unknown machine, all punctuated by the immediate work of maintenance. There's no dramatic narrative here, no progression toward a goal. It's continuation, repetition, endless small adjustments. This can be meditative or maddening depending on your temperament.

The weirdness you'll encounter—the fan that turns without visible mechanism, the wall warm on one side and cool on the other, the water that might move upward, the carved bricks sealed inside other brick—these aren't threats. They're simply evidence that the Vaults operate by rules that aren't quite the ones you're used to. Don't try to force understanding. Document, observe, accept.

The boundaries of the settlement, where construction stops and darkness extends into unknown depth—you'll feel the pull to explore further. Resist unless you're properly equipped and accompanied. The known space is sufficient for understanding the Vaults' essential nature. The unknown spaces will still be there if you return better prepared.

## Final Thoughts

The Counting Vaults represent a form of human habitation adapted to constraints so specific that they've created a unique culture of maintenance and water management. You're visiting a working community, not a ruin or tourist site.

Your goal should be understanding, not conquest. These aren't spaces to be "explored" in the traditional sense—there's no summit to reach, no treasure to find, no mystery that will be solved by your visit. There's only the ongoing reality of people living in compressed vertical heat, managing water grain by grain, rebuilding walls that fail, tending sparse gardens, and coexisting with machinery they don't fully understand.

Go with humility. The residents have solved problems you haven't had to consider: how to grow food with minimal light, how to sleep in constant heat, how to maintain infrastructure whose original builders are forgotten, how to live in space measured in feet of depth but containing the complexity of a small town.

Bring what you need to survive the environment: water management, heat endurance, redundant lighting, navigation tools, medical supplies. But also bring respect for the place and its people, patience for the pace of life here, and acceptance that some things—like the machine in the seventh tier, like the carved bricks sealed in walls, like the water that moves in ways water shouldn't—may remain unexplained.

The Vaults will teach you about density, about compression, about human adaptation to seemingly impossible constraints. They'll test your heat tolerance, your navigation skills, your psychological comfort with enclosed spaces and limited light.

Return to the surface when your water runs low, when the heat becomes too much, when you feel disorientation creeping in. The Vaults will still be there, still slowly accumulating their layers of human modification, still dripping and heating and housing their small community in the narrow vertical space beneath a salt flat that no longer exists.

There's no glory in pushing yourself to collapse in the seventh tier. There's only value in witnessing, carefully, how humans make life in the spaces between the bricks.
