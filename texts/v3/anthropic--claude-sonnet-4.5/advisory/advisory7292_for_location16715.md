# Preparation Guide for the Plateau of Accumulated Hours

## Essential Understanding: What You're Actually Visiting

Before any physical preparation, you must grasp this: you are not visiting a place that is dangerous in conventional ways. There are no predators, no cliffs to fall from, no storms to shelter from. The plateau will not kill you.

But it may profoundly alter your relationship with linear experience, causation, and sequential consciousness. You are visiting a place where time has achieved equilibrium—where it exists as geography rather than flow. Some travelers return unchanged. Others return carrying temporal vertigo that takes months to resolve. A few—very few—don't return at all, not because they've died but because they've become stuck in a loop of their own experience, walking the same circuit indefinitely, and by the time anyone finds them, they don't wish to leave.

Prepare your mind first. The equipment is secondary.

## Psychological and Cognitive Preparation

**Temporal Flexibility Training (Begin 6-8 weeks before departure):**

Start disrupting your habitual time perception. Wear your watch on different wrists each day, or don't wear one at all. Stop using devices that display time for increasingly long periods—first hours, then days. Practice activities where duration becomes ambiguous: meditation in sensory-reduced environments, extended time in windowless rooms where you cannot gauge sun position.

The goal is to loosen your dependence on sequential time as an organizing principle for consciousness. On the plateau, your watch will lie—or tell different truths simultaneously. You need to be comfortable with that.

**Practice exercises:**
- Spend a full day navigating without any timekeeping devices. Eat when hungry, sleep when tired, with no reference to clock time.
- Deliberately remember events from your past in non-chronological order. Practice holding multiple sequences in mind simultaneously.
- Study the philosophy of eternalism—the view that past, present, and future are equally real. Don't necessarily believe it, but become comfortable thinking within that framework.

**Memory Anchoring:**

You will need techniques for maintaining narrative coherence when causation becomes negotiable. Practice creating "memory anchors"—vivid, multisensory recollections that you can use as reference points.

Before you depart, create a detailed sensory memory of somewhere completely unlike the plateau: a rainforest, a beach, a bustling city market. Record it in all senses—sights, sounds, smells, textures, tastes. Practice recalling it in complete detail. This becomes your cognitive home base, the thing you can return to when you need to remember that linear, causal reality exists.

Bring a physical object from this anchoring place—a shell, a leaf preserved in resin, a stone from a riverbed. Something that exists nowhere on the plateau, something that confirms other types of places are real.

**Solitude Conditioning:**

You will be profoundly alone, even surrounded by overwhelming numbers of fauna. None of them will acknowledge you in any meaningful way. The silence is total. The indifference is absolute.

If you're not comfortable with extended solitude, this is not your journey. Spend weeks alone before departing. Not pleasant retreat-alone where you're reading books and cooking nice meals—actual solitude where you have nothing but your own consciousness for company, where hours pass with no external stimulus, no validation, no response from the world.

The plateau will make your most isolated moments look like a crowded party by comparison.

## Physical Preparation and Health Considerations

**Altitude Conditioning:**

Three thousand meters is significant elevation. Begin altitude training at least two months out if you live at sea level. Use altitude simulation equipment, or spend time at elevation. Your body needs to adjust to:
- Reduced oxygen (though the plateau's atmosphere is strangely dense, it's still thin enough to matter)
- Increased UV exposure (though the sun is diffuse, it penetrates)
- The specific breathing rhythm the thin air will impose

The plateau's stable temperature means you won't face typical altitude temperature extremes, but the pressure changes remain.

**Respiratory Preparation:**

The air has a mineral taste, slightly metallic, with undertones that coat your throat and sinuses. Some travelers develop a persistent cough or throat irritation. Others adapt within hours.

Practice breathing through a cloth or filter to simulate the experience of air that tastes wrong. Build up your throat tolerance to mild irritants—not to the point of damage, but enough that persistent strange flavors don't trigger constant gagging reflexes.

Bring soothing throat lozenges, though be aware they may not work as expected (time-release medications sometimes release on unpredictable schedules there).

**Nutritional Loading:**

You won't want to eat much there. The stillness, the silence, the temporal ambiguity—they suppress appetite. Many travelers report forgetting to eat for what might be days, or eating constantly because they've lost track of whether they've eaten.

Before you go, build up your body's reserves:
- Increase healthy fat intake—your body will draw on these reserves
- Ensure excellent hydration going in
- Consider carb-loading as for endurance events
- Make sure your electrolyte balance is optimal

Your body will need to sustain you through periods where you forget it has needs.

**Physical Fitness (Low-Intensity Endurance):**

You won't be climbing or scrambling. The terrain is nearly flat. But you will walk. Miles and miles, across stone swells, with no variation, no landmarks, no clear sense of how far you've gone or how long you've walked.

Train for this: long, flat walks where nothing changes. Treadmill walks of 4-6 hours. Loops around tracks until the tedium becomes transcendent. You're not training your muscles so much as training your will to continue moving when movement seems pointless.

The plateau demands persistence without purpose. Practice achieving that state.

## Equipment and Supplies

**Navigation Tools (Knowing They'll Fail):**

Bring a compass, GPS, maps. Understand they will not function reliably.

The compass will spin, or point in directions that don't correspond to magnetic north, or point steadily at something that isn't magnetic north but is something else—perhaps the nearest stone circle, perhaps the densest concentration of watchers, perhaps a direction that only exists on the plateau.

GPS satellites don't maintain consistent connection—the signals reach you from different times, giving you positions that are superimposed, contradictory.

Maps will be accurate until you try to use them, at which point the distances won't match your experience, and the features will be in different configurations.

Bring them anyway. Bring multiple types. Sometimes one will work when others don't. Sometimes the act of checking a non-functional tool provides psychological structure. Sometimes they work perfectly for hours before failing again.

**Bring a map and mark your path obsessively.** Even if the distances don't match, even if you seem to loop, the act of marking provides narrative structure to your experience. It creates causation: I was there, then there, then there. Your mind needs this, even if the plateau doesn't.

**Timekeeping (Multiple Contradictory Systems):**

- Mechanical watch (wind-up, no batteries)
- Digital watch (for comparison)
- Hourglass or sand timer (physical, visible time passage)
- Marked candle (if you bring fire, which you should)
- Journal with dated entries (even though dates become meaningless, keep dating them)

Check all of them regularly. Record the discrepancies. Do not try to determine which is "correct"—they are all correct from different temporal perspectives. The goal is not accurate time, but maintaining awareness that time is happening, even if it's happening strangely.

Some travelers bring chess clocks or other countdown timers as a concrete way to measure intended intervals. "I will walk for exactly two hours as measured by this timer" gives you a structure that's independent of how time is actually flowing.

**Water: The Critical Resource**

There is no water on the plateau. None. The air is not humid enough for realistic condensation collection. The fauna need no water, or get it in ways you cannot replicate.

You must carry all your water, and it will be the limiting factor on your visit duration.

**Recommendations:**
- Plan for 4 liters per day minimum at this altitude and activity level
- Carry 6 liters per day to be safe, because "days" there are uncertain
- Use a hydration system that makes drinking automatic (bladder with tube)
- Mark your water containers with time/day markers: "Day 1 Morning," "Day 1 Evening," etc.
- Ration strictly, because without external time cues, you will lose track

Consider your water supply your absolute deadline. When you're down to 40%, you begin your exit, regardless of what else is happening. This is non-negotiable. The plateau's indifference extends to your dehydration.

**Food (Sustaining Rather Than Enjoying):**

Appetite suppression is near-universal there. Bring foods that are:
- Calorie-dense
- Require no preparation
- Can be eaten without thinking about it
- Won't spoil in cool, dry conditions

Energy bars, nuts, dried fruit, pemmican, hard cheese, honey, oil/fat packets. Avoid anything that requires engagement to eat—you won't want to engage.

Set alarms (on your multiple contradictory timepieces) to eat. Eat whether you're hungry or not. Your body needs fuel even if your consciousness has forgotten it has a body.

Some travelers pre-portion everything: "This bag is Day 1 breakfast. This bag is Day 1 lunch." The external structure compensates for internal time-sense collapse.

**Shelter and Sleep System**

You'll need to sleep, though the stable temperature means you're not sheltering from elements so much as creating psychological boundaries.

**Recommendations:**
- Lightweight tent (to create "inside" vs "outside" space)
- Sleeping pad (the stone is hard and weirdly cold despite mild air temperature)
- Sleeping bag rated to ~5°C (for the nighttime lows)
- Eye mask (the sky is never fully dark; some travelers need absolute darkness to sleep)
- Ear plugs (though it's silent, the silence itself has presence and texture that some find disturbing)

The tent serves primarily as a mental boundary: this is my space, this is rest-time, this is safe. Without it, you're dissolved in the plateau's uniformity. With it, you maintain a pocket of difference, of human-scaled meaning.

Some travelers cannot sleep there at all—the temporal stratification makes sleep feel impossible, or makes it impossible to distinguish sleep from waking. If this happens, don't panic. You can remain functional for much longer than you'd think. The plateau's effects are strange but not immediately harmful.

**Clothing (Layered Stability)**

The temperature varies only slightly, but you need layers for flexibility and for psychological comfort.

**Core outfit:**
- Synthetic or merino base layers (manage moisture from exertion)
- Insulating mid-layer (for the 10°C lows)
- Light shell (for the steady air movement, which has cooling effects)
- Wide-brimmed hat (UV protection from diffuse but present sun)
- High-quality sunglasses (the light is strange; it can cause eye fatigue)

**Critical: bring backup of everything.** Not because things wear out, but because items sometimes shift position or briefly cease to exist from your perspective. You take off your jacket, set it down, look away, look back—it's three meters to the left now, or you're experiencing a time-layer where you haven't taken it off yet, or it's simply not there and won't be there for another hour of subjective time.

If every critical item exists in duplicate, the plateau's slippery causation becomes manageable rather than dangerous.

**Footwear:**

Sturdy hiking shoes or boots with excellent soles. The stone is:
- Hard (your feet will ache from the unyielding surface)
- Smooth in places (traction is necessary)
- Uneven enough to require ankle support

Break them in thoroughly. Blisters on the plateau are especially problematic because healing happens strangely—or seems to. Does a blister get worse because it's actually worsening, or because you're sliding into a time-layer where it's worse? You cannot know, which makes managing injuries psychologically difficult.

Bring multiple pairs of socks. Change them on schedule (your artificial, self-imposed schedule) whether they seem to need it or not. The ritual of fresh socks becomes a temporal anchor: "I last changed these 12 hours ago, so 12 hours have passed."

## Safety Equipment and Emergency Supplies

**Communication Devices (That Won't Work):**

Bring a satellite phone, radio, emergency beacon. Understand they probably won't function—signals don't propagate correctly through the plateau's strange atmosphere, or they propagate to times other than the present, or they reach recipients who are experiencing different moments.

Activate your beacon if you're in trouble anyway. Maybe it works. Maybe it works sometimes. Maybe it sends a signal that arrives before you need it or after you've solved the problem, but you should send it.

**Fire Starting:**

Multiple lighters, waterproof matches, ferro rod. Fire is one of the few things that behaves mostly normally there—it follows straightforward chemistry because combustion happens too quickly for the temporal stratification to affect it much.

Fire serves multiple purposes:
- Warmth (if needed, though rarely)
- Psychological comfort (humans are wired to find fire reassuring)
- Temporal marker (burning a marked candle shows time passing in a visible, irreversible way)
- Signal (though to whom, you cannot know)

**First Aid:**

Standard wilderness first aid kit, but understand that injury and healing will be strange.

**Additions specific to the plateau:**
- Eye drops (for the dry air and strange light)
- Throat lozenges (for the mineral taste)
- Antidiarrheal and antinausea (some travelers have GI responses to the temporal effects)
- Strong painkillers (injuries are rare, but pain there is psychologically difficult to manage)
- Any personal medications in extreme excess (if you take something daily, bring 3x what you think you need—"daily" becomes ambiguous)

**Psychological First Aid:**

These are as important as physical supplies:

- Photos of loved ones (to remember that love exists, that connection exists)
- A letter to yourself written before you left, reminding you why you're there and that you will leave
- A recording of a familiar voice (hearing human speech after days of silence is profoundly grounding)
- A favorite poem or text memorized (something to recite, to hear words that have meaning)
- A simple, portable game or puzzle (to occupy your mind when the sameness becomes overwhelming)

## Documentation Equipment

**Recording Your Experience:**

This is partly scientific, partly psychological survival strategy. The act of documentation creates narrative, creates sequence, creates causation even when the plateau is denying all of those.

**Bring:**
- Multiple notebooks and backup pens (mechanical pencils that can't run out)
- Camera with extra batteries (photos are strange there—they sometimes capture time-layers you weren't consciously experiencing, showing fauna in different positions than you remember)
- Voice recorder (describing your experience aloud forces you to create narrative structure)
- Sketch pad and drawing materials (visual documentation when photos are unreliable)

**Documentation practices:**
- Write dated entries even when you're not sure of dates
- Describe what you see in extreme detail (the act of attention counteracts the plateau's dissolving effects on cognition)
- Record contradictions and anomalies without trying to resolve them
- Note your own mental state hourly

The documentation serves another purpose: proving to yourself later that you were really there, that it happened. Memory of the plateau degrades strangely—not fading so much as stratifying, becoming uncertain in the same way time becomes uncertain there. Your notes will be your evidence.

## Route Planning (Accepting Impossibility)

You cannot plan a route in the conventional sense. The plateau's topology shifts based on which temporal layer you're experiencing. Distances are inconsistent. Landmarks move or appear/disappear.

**But you should attempt planning anyway:**

1. **Identify entry and exit points:** Usually the same place—the eastern approaches are most commonly used. Mark this elaborately so you can find it again. GPS coordinates, cairns, anything distinctive. Your life depends on finding your way off.

2. **Set a maximum duration:** Based strictly on water supply. If you carry 24 liters and budget 6 liters per day, your maximum stay is 4 days. Period. Non-negotiable. No "just one more day" because you're fascinated—the plateau's indifference to your fascination extends to your dehydration death.

3. **Plan waypoints by features, not distances:** "I will visit three stone-circles, two platforms with holes, and one area with the finger-plants." Not "I will walk 10 kilometers northeast"—direction and distance are meaningless there.

4. **Have a bail-out plan:** If things get weird in ways you can't handle—if the temporal effects become too severe, if you lose narrative coherence, if you cannot distinguish memory from current experience—you retreat immediately to your entry point and leave. Pride is worthless on the plateau.

## Specific Protocols for Temporal Anomalies

**If you see yourself:**

Do not approach. Do not interact. Observe from a distance. You are seeing a different temporal layer of your own journey. Attempting to interact creates paradox, and paradox on the plateau has unpredictable effects.

Mark the location in your notes. Later, you may or may not remember being the version of yourself you saw. This is normal (or rather, this is the plateau's version of normal).

**If you experience lost time:**

You're walking, then you're standing, with no memory of the transition. This will happen.

Immediately ground yourself:
- Check all your timekeeping devices
- Check your supplies (water consumed, food eaten)
- Write in your journal: what you remember last, what you're experiencing now
- Physically touch your body, your gear—confirm you're present

Do not panic. Lost time there is not dangerous in itself—it's only dangerous if it makes you forget to maintain your water discipline or causes you to miss your exit window.

**If time loops:**

You experience the same event multiple times—a stone-herd passing, reaching a particular circle, seeing a configuration of quadrupeds. This is different from déjà vu; you remember it clearly because it's happened clearly, multiple times.

Mark each occurrence in your journal with timestamps from your devices. Count the loops. Generally they resolve after 3-7 iterations, and linear time resumes.

If you're in a loop longer than 10 iterations, this is more serious—you may be stuck. Initiate your bail-out protocol. Move with determination toward your exit point. Do not stop to investigate, do not attempt to understand. Leave.

**If past and future blur:**

You remember things that haven't happened yet, or things that happened feel like they haven't occurred yet. This is the plateau's most profound effect.

Rely entirely on your external documentation. Trust your journals, your photos, your recordings. Trust your water supply (it doesn't lie—if 6 liters are gone, you've experienced whatever time it takes to drink 6 liters).

Do not trust your memory. Your memory is experiencing non-linear time just as the plateau does. Your notes and supplies are experiencing linear time (mostly) and can be your anchor.

## Social and Legal Preparation

**Tell people where you're going:**

Leave detailed plans with someone responsible. Include:
- Exact entry location and time
- Maximum duration you'll be there
- Date/time they should trigger rescue if you haven't returned
- Description of the plateau's effects (so they understand you might return disoriented)

**Understand that rescue is unlikely:**

If something goes wrong on the plateau, help probably cannot reach you in time. The temporal effects that make it fascinating make it nearly impossible to navigate if you're not familiar with it. Search and rescue teams become disoriented in minutes.

You are alone there in an absolute sense. Plan and provision accordingly.

**Legal considerations:**

Some jurisdictions consider the plateau a hazardous/restricted area. Verify the current legal status. Obtain necessary permits. Leave copies of all documentation with your emergency contact.

Also consider: designate someone with power of attorney to manage your affairs if you don't return on schedule. The plateau has a very low fatality rate but a non-zero rate of people who become temporally dislocated and don't return for weeks or months beyond their planned duration, or who return but are cognitively compromised for extended periods.

## Medical Clearance and Considerations

**Consult a physician familiar with extreme environments:**

You need to be:
- Cardiovascularly healthy (altitude)
- Mentally stable (temporal effects can trigger or worsen certain conditions)
- Not dependent on precise medication timing (time is too unreliable)
- Able to handle extended stress without support

**Specific conditions that may be contraindicated:**
- Uncontrolled anxiety or panic disorders (the strangeness can trigger severe episodes)
- Psychotic disorders or strong history of same (reality is already negotiable there; existing reality-testing issues compound dangerously)
- Epilepsy triggered by visual phenomena (the sky's geometric forms, the fauna's movements)
- Diabetes requiring precise timing (impossible to maintain on the plateau)
- Any condition requiring time-sensitive medication

This is not to say people with these conditions cannot visit, but they need extensive additional planning and should probably not go alone.

## Training for the Fauna

**The watchers will observe you constantly.**

Practice being watched. Have someone stare at you for hours while you go about activities. Become comfortable with persistent, unblinking observation. On the plateau, there is no privacy, no moment when you're not being tracked by those bundles of filaments.

For most people, this is more psychologically difficult than expected. We're not used to being seen continuously with that quality of attention. Practice until their presence becomes background noise in your consciousness.

**Practice non-reaction:**

The fauna do extraordinary things—pass through each other, vanish and reappear, exist in impossible configurations. You need to observe these things without strong emotional response.

Train yourself to see the strange and simply note it: "Interesting. That happened." Not fear, not fascination that derails your attention, just acknowledgment.

Watch surreal films, optical illusions, impossible geometry. Desensitize yourself to the impossible. On the plateau, the impossible is constant. If you react strongly to every anomaly, you'll exhaust yourself in hours.

## Preparation for the Structures

**If you plan to enter the stone circles:**

Understand that these are the strongest reality-distortion zones. Inside them, you will experience:
- Multiple temporal layers simultaneously
- Memory of things that haven't happened or happened differently
- Possible perception of the plateau's previous inhabitants/builders
- Intense sensation of being in the wrong time, or all times at once

**Prepare:**
- Never enter one without setting a timer to leave after 10 minutes (maximum—more is risky)
- Bring a rope you can follow back out if you become disoriented
- Have your memory-anchor object in hand
- Go in, observe, take notes, leave immediately when the timer sounds

Some travelers become entranced in the circles and emerge hours later, or emerge immediately but believe hours have passed. The circles are where people get stuck in loops. Treat them with extreme caution.

**The platforms with the holes:**

Never lean over the holes. Never drop anything into them you need. Never speak directly into them (some travelers report their voices returning hours later, changed, speaking things they didn't say).

Observe them, document them, but maintain distance. They are not safe in any conventional sense.

## Duration Recommendations

**First visit: 24-48 hours maximum.**

This gives you a taste of the effects without full submersion. You'll experience temporal anomalies but probably won't lose narrative coherence completely. You can retreat before the plateau fully infiltrates your consciousness.

**Experienced travelers: 3-5 days.**

Beyond this, the effects become more permanent. You start thinking in the plateau's terms—simultaneity rather than sequence, being rather than becoming. This can be enlightening or it can be destabilizing, depending on your psychology.

**Extended stays (week+): not recommended for mortals.**

There are rumors of travelers who stay for weeks or months, who adapt so fully to the plateau's temporal nature that they can navigate its layers consciously, who can perceive multiple durations simultaneously. 

But these people do not return unchanged. They come back speaking of time in ways that don't translate to normal causality. They struggle to live in sequential time. They are often perceived as damaged or mad.

Unless you are prepared to potentially sacrifice your normal relationship with temporality, stay under a week.

## Philosophical and Spiritual Preparation

**Understand what you're seeking:**

The plateau shows you what existence looks like when it has finished happening—when change has completed itself and achieved perfect equilibrium. This is either:

- Deeply peaceful (the ending of struggle, the cessation of becoming)
- Deeply disturbing (the death of possibility, the end of meaning)
- Both simultaneously

Know which you're expecting to feel, but be prepared for either or both.

**Consider your relationship with permanence and change:**

We live in change. We are change. Going to a place that has moved beyond change means confronting what you are when change stops. Some people find their essential self there. Others find terrifying emptiness. Others find that the question was meaningless.

**Prepare for insignificance:**

The plateau's indifference is total. You are a brief anomaly crossing stone that is unimaginably old. The fauna that have achieved equilibrium there don't care about you. The structures don't acknowledge you. You are less than a mote, less than a moment.

For some, this is liberating. For others, it precipitates existential crisis. Know which you're likely to experience, and have coping strategies ready.

**Consider bringing a practice:**

Prayer, meditation, reciting mantras, specific breathing exercises—some travelers find that having a spiritual or meditative practice gives them a center that the plateau cannot dissolve. The practice becomes an anchor: "I am the person who does this practice, therefore I persist through time."

Even if you're not religious or spiritual, consider developing a ritual you can repeat. The repetition creates continuity when continuity is otherwise unavailable.

## Re-entry Preparation

**Plan for disorientation upon return:**

When you leave the plateau and return to normal causation, it feels wrong. Time rushes. Things change too fast. Causality is oppressively rigid—one thing must lead to another, you can't access alternate durations, you're trapped in sequence.

**Plan for:**
- A quiet place to stay for 2-3 days after return
- No immediate responsibilities or demands
- Someone who understands you may be temporally confused
- Journaling and integration practices

**Expect cognitive aftereffects:**

For days or weeks after, you may:
- Lose track of time easily
- Experience déjà vu frequently
- Feel that you're experiencing multiple timelines simultaneously
- Have dreams of the plateau that feel more real than waking life
- Struggle with the notion that time only flows one direction

These effects fade, usually. But while they're present, they can be disturbing. Some travelers find therapy helpful for integration. Others use meditation or contemplative practices.

**Document everything immediately:**

Your memory of the plateau will stratify just like the time there. Within weeks, you won't be certain what happened when, what was sequential and what was simultaneous. Write everything down as soon as you return, while it's still relatively coherent.

## Final Considerations

**Go or don't go, but don't go lightly:**

The Plateau of Accumulated Hours is not a tourist destination. It's not a place to visit casually. It's a place where reality operates on different principles, and exposing yourself to those principles changes you.

Some people return enriched, having touched something beyond normal human experience. Others return damaged in subtle ways, carrying temporal vertigo that may never fully resolve.

**You cannot be the same person after.**

Standing on stone that old, surrounded by life that has transcended biological time, under a sky that displays the fundamental geometry of existence, breathing air that tastes of deep time—these experiences mark consciousness permanently.

Be certain that you want to be marked.

**Trust your instincts obsessively:**

If something feels wrong—if the temporal effects are too strong, if you're losing coherence, if the plateau's indifference is eroding your sense of self—leave immediately. Don't stay because you feel you should, because you planned to, because you're curious.

The plateau will still be there. It has been there longer than humanity. It will be there after humanity. It is patient in ways that make glaciers look hasty.

You can always return if you leave. You cannot necessarily leave if you stay too long.

**Practical final checklist:**

- Water (6L per planned day, minimum)
- Multiple timekeeping devices
- Excess food
- Navigation tools (knowing they'll fail)
- Documentation equipment
- Memory anchor object
- Emergency beacon (knowing it might not work)
- Detailed plans left with responsible person
- Medical clearance
- 6-8 weeks of psychological preparation
- Acceptance that you will return changed
- Humility before deep time
- Willingness to leave immediately if necessary

The Plateau of Accumulated Hours offers a view of existence that few humans have witnessed: time as place, change as completed, being as perfect equilibrium. 

It is worth seeing, if you are prepared.

It will cost you something—certainty, perhaps, or your unquestioned relationship with causality, or simply the comfort of knowing that time flows in only one direction.

What you gain in exchange is the knowledge of what lies beyond time—not after time ends, but in the place where time has become something other than flow. A place where all moments exist simultaneously, where stone remembers being itself for so long that memory and being are indistinguishable.

Whether that knowledge is worth the cost is a question only you can answer.

Go carefully. Observe clearly. Document obsessively. Leave promptly.

And when you return—when you're back in the world where time flows and things change and causation is reliable—remember that you have seen the other way, the plateau's way, the way of accumulated hours that have become geography.

Remember, and be changed, and decide what that change means.

The plateau will remain, indifferent to your interpretation, perfect in its stasis, waiting with infinite patience for nothing, for everything, for the next consciousness brave enough or foolish enough to walk across stone that has forgotten how to change.
