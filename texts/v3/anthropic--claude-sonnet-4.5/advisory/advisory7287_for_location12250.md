# Preparation Guide for the Scaffolding Gardens

## Physical Conditioning

You need to arrive in peak physical condition, but not in the way you might expect. This isn't about cardiovascular endurance for long treks—though that helps. The Scaffolding Gardens demand specific adaptations:

**High-altitude acclimatization is non-negotiable.** Spend at least three weeks at progressively higher elevations before arrival. Your body needs to produce more red blood cells, expand lung capacity, and adjust to operating with less oxygen. The thin air at the Gardens will be your constant companion—respect it or suffer debilitating altitude sickness in permanent twilight with no easy evacuation route.

**Climbing strength matters more than hiking stamina.** Your forearms, grip, and core will determine your range of motion here. The landscape is fundamentally three-dimensional. Train on climbing walls, do pull-ups until your hands give out, work on hanging endurance. The lattice trunks are climbable but unforgiving. A 30-meter fall through cold air onto angular scree ends the same way regardless of which universe you're in.

**Cold tolerance training is essential but limited.** Spend time in cold environments—deliberately, uncomfortably. Learn what your body does as it loses heat: how your fingers stop working, how your thinking gets sluggish, how you stop noticing you're shivering. But understand that training only goes so far. The Gardens' cold is patient and pervasive. It will find you eventually. Training just teaches you to recognize the signs and respond before incapacitation.

## Equipment: The Critical Essentials

**Lighting is your first survival priority.** The permanent twilight is disorienting in ways that sneak up on you. Bring multiple redundant light sources:

- LED headlamps with extensive battery supplies (cold kills batteries faster—expect 40% reduced runtime)
- Chemical light sticks as backup (they work in any temperature)
- A small, well-maintained oil lantern if you can carry it—the psychological value of warm light is worth the weight
- Reflective markers to trace your routes (the twilight makes everything look the same after hours)

Don't skimp here. Darkness in the Gardens isn't just inconvenient; it's spatially disorienting in a three-dimensional maze where falling is always an option.

**Thermal protection is your second priority.** The cold is constant, dry, and insidious:

- Layering systems designed for high-altitude mountaineering
- Vapor-barrier liners (the air is dry, but condensation will soak anything near your skin)
- Down insulation rated for -20°C minimum, kept in waterproof compression sacks
- Two complete changes of base layers (if one set gets wet during a canopy-shed, you need dry replacements immediately)
- Mittens over gloves—your fingers are more useful warm and clumsy than precise and frozen
- A real sleeping bag, not a minimalist quilt. You'll need full enclosure. -30°C rating at minimum.
- A four-season tent with excellent ventilation (condensation from your breathing will coat the inside otherwise)

The cold here doesn't spike dramatically, which tricks people into under-preparing. A steady -5°C over days will harm you just as surely as a brief -30°C, just slower.

**Footwear requires serious thought.** The ground is loose scree, ankle-turning rubble, and occasionally patches of damp moss. You need:

- Stiff mountaineering boots with excellent ankle support
- Crampon-compatible soles (frost forms every night on the stone)
- Multiple pairs of wool socks (rotate them, keep the spares dry)
- Spare laces (sharp stone cuts through them)

Bring boot repair supplies: needle and thread, seam sealer, duct tape. A boot failure here means immobility, and immobility in the cold means death.

**Climbing equipment for the vertical dimension:**

You're not mountaineering in the traditional sense, but you'll want to ascend the lattice trunks both for perspective and to access old platforms. Bring:

- Climbing harness and helmet
- 50 meters of dynamic rope
- Basic rack: carabiners, slings, a belay device
- Prussik cords for ascending fixed ropes (some old rope bridges might still be usable with backup systems)
- Climbing shoes if you're experienced (the trunk-surfaces have texture but require technique)
- Good climbing gloves (the wood is cold and will shred your hands)

Even if you're not a serious climber, bringing this equipment opens up safe exploration of the vertical space. Without it, you're confined to ground level—experiencing perhaps 20% of what the Gardens offer.

**Navigation tools for a place where conventional navigation fails:**

- Multiple compasses (check them against each other; the structural weirdness here might affect magnetic fields)
- Altimeter (essential for three-dimensional positioning)
- Notebooks—waterproof paper with pencils—for detailed mapping
- Colored thread or biodegradable tape for marking routes (the twilight makes visual memory unreliable)
- Measuring tape (the scale distortion plays havoc with distance estimation)

GPS will not work. You're in another universe. Celestial navigation is blocked by the canopy. You'll navigate by trunk-patterns, sound, and dead reckoning. Practice these skills before arrival.

**Water acquisition is non-standard here:**

- Multiple collection vessels—wide-mouthed bottles that can catch dripping condensation
- Water purification (filters and chemical treatment both; redundancy matters)
- Tarps or waterproof fabric to spread during canopy-sheds for bulk collection
- Insulated bottles (liquid water near freezing is still far more valuable than ice you need to melt)

You won't find streams or lakes at ground level. You'll collect condensation and position yourself for canopy-sheds. Understanding the local hydrology takes time—bring enough water capacity to survive while you learn.

**Food considerations for sustained cold:**

- High-calorie, high-fat foods (your body will burn calories constantly just maintaining temperature)
- No fresh vegetables (they'll freeze)
- Fats and oils that remain soft in cold (olive oil, not butter)
- Foods that don't require much water to prepare (you'll want to minimize fuel use)
- Hot drink capacity—tea, coffee, hot chocolate—the psychological value is immense

Plan for 4,000-5,000 calories per day minimum. The combination of altitude, cold, and physical exertion will hollow you out. Experienced mountaineers often lose weight even with deliberate overeating—expect the same here.

**Cooking equipment for sustained cold operations:**

- A reliable multi-fuel stove (white gas works better than canister fuel in extreme cold, but bring both)
- Extensive fuel supplies (you'll use more than expected—cooking takes longer in thin air, and you'll want hot drinks constantly)
- Windscreens (the wind is constant)
- Insulated pot covers (keeping food warm matters)
- A sturdy, stable base for your stove (the scree doesn't provide level surfaces)

Consider bringing materials for a small stone hearth at your basecamp. If you find an old stone circle, you might be able to establish a more permanent heat source using compressed canopy debris as fuel. The ancient inhabitants managed it somehow.

## Medical Preparations

**High-altitude medical issues are your primary concern:**

Learn to recognize and treat:
- Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS)
- High Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE)
- High Altitude Cerebral Edema (HACE)

Bring:
- Acetazolamide (Diamox) for altitude adjustment
- Dexamethasone for emergencies
- Pulse oximeter to monitor blood oxygen
- Blood pressure cuff (high altitude affects circulation)

The Gardens don't have "rescue." If you develop HACE, your options are descend or die, and descending through forest and mountains in twilight while brain-swelling impairs your judgment is... suboptimal. Prevention through proper acclimatization is everything.

**Cold injury treatment knowledge:**

Study frostbite recognition and treatment. Understand the difference between superficial and deep frostbite. Learn about trench foot (prolonged cold and dampness, even above freezing). Bring:

- Instant heat packs (multiple, though they work poorly at high altitude)
- Materials for wound care if tissue damage occurs
- Strong painkillers (rewarming frostbitten tissue is extraordinarily painful)

**General medical supplies:**

- Comprehensive first aid kit emphasizing:
  - Blister treatment (your feet will suffer on the scree)
  - Wound care (sharp stone cuts, climbing scrapes)
  - Pain management (muscle strains, injuries)
  - Gastrointestinal medications (altitude affects digestion)
  - Antibiotics (any infection is serious when you're this remote and this cold)
- Dental emergency kit (a cracked tooth in permanent twilight is nightmare fuel)
- Any personal medications in double quantities
- Eyedrops (the air is dry and constantly moving)

**Mental health preparation:**

The permanent twilight affects people psychologically. Some experience it as peaceful, meditative. Others find it profoundly disturbing. The isolation, the constant cold, the disorienting acoustic environment, the scale—these factors compound.

Consider:
- Whether you've successfully handled isolation before
- Your response to disorienting environments
- Whether constant low-light affects your mood (SAD sufferers will struggle)
- Your comfort with spatial uncertainty

Bring:
- A journal (processing experience helps maintain equilibrium)
- Reading material (something comforting and familiar)
- Photographs or small mementos of people/places that anchor you
- Music if you use it for mood regulation (though many find the forest's own sound-scape more compelling)

Plan for daily check-ins with yourself. Monitor your mental state as carefully as your physical state. The cold and the twilight can make depression or anxiety spiral before you notice it happening.

## Skills to Develop Before Arrival

**Climbing competency:**

If you can't climb confidently, take lessons. You don't need to be an expert, but you should be comfortable:
- Belaying
- Setting anchors in non-standard materials (wood, not rock)
- Ascending ropes with prussiks or ascenders
- Self-rescue basics

The trunks are climbable by people with moderate skills, but "moderate skills" still means actual training, not just enthusiasm.

**Wilderness survival:**

Standard outdoor skills apply:
- Fire-starting in damp conditions with limited fuel
- Shelter construction
- Weather prediction (though the interior weather won't match your experience)
- Route-finding without landmarks
- Risk assessment

Take courses. Get experience. A week-long wilderness medicine course would be valuable.

**Cold-weather camping:**

Go winter camping. Multiple times. In mountains if possible. Learn what actually works versus what sounds good:
- How to stay dry
- How to keep gear functional
- How to maintain morale
- How to make decisions when you're cold and tired

The Gardens are like winter camping that never ends. If you find winter camping miserable after three days, you'll find the Gardens unbearable.

**Rope work and knots:**

Learn to tie and use:
- Figure-eight follow-through (climbing attachment)
- Clove hitch (adjustable anchor)
- Prussik (ascending)
- Bowline (versatile loop)
- Trucker's hitch (tensioning systems)

You'll use rope constantly—for safety, for hauling supplies, for building, for descending, for securing camp. Fumbling with frozen fingers in twilight is not the time to be learning.

**Sound-based navigation:**

This is unusual, but practice navigating by sound. Blindfold yourself in safe environments and move by acoustic cues. The Gardens require this skill—the constant wind-song through the trunks creates an acoustic landscape you'll learn to read. Individual trunk formations have distinct tones. Developing this awareness before arrival accelerates your adjustment.

## Research and Intellectual Preparation

**Study the ecology:**

Before arrival, think through:
- How hollow structures provide strength (look up tensegrity and bird bone structure)
- How plants coordinate without apparent nervous systems (mycorrhizal networks, chemical signaling)
- Cold-adapted fauna (look at Arctic and high-altitude species)
- Vertical forest ecology (look at rainforest canopy research for analogues)

You won't find direct information about the Gardens, but understanding parallel systems helps you recognize patterns faster.

**Examine the archaeological evidence:**

Think about:
- Stone-age and Bronze-age tool technologies
- What the simple tools and structures suggest about the builders' priorities
- Why people would inhabit such a place
- What resources might justify the difficulty

This isn't academic—understanding the builders helps you understand the forest. They left notes written in stone circles and platforms. Learning to read those notes might provide valuable information about water sources, shelter sites, or hazards.

**Study traditional ecological knowledge:**

Indigenous peoples in harsh environments have sophisticated understandings of their landscapes. Read about:
- Inuit ice navigation
- Sherpa route-finding in the Himalayas
- Aboriginal Australian songlines
- Any culture that developed deep literacy about a challenging landscape

You're trying to develop similar literacy about the Gardens. These examples teach you what that kind of knowledge looks like and how it's acquired.

**Practice observational discipline:**

The Gardens reward careful attention. Train yourself:
- Keep detailed notes
- Sketch what you see (drawing forces observation)
- Record patterns even if you don't understand them yet
- Note correlations between events
- Track time carefully (the constant twilight makes this harder than expected)

The builders understood the canopy-shed patterns, the trunk acoustics, the animal movements. You'll need to build that same knowledge base, but faster. Systematic observation is how you do it.

## Psychological Preparation: Understanding What You're Entering

**This is not scenic wilderness:**

Most wilderness travel involves beauty—dramatic vistas, sunrises, starry nights, the full sensory richness of nature. The Gardens offer something different: sublime architecture, acoustic wonder, and profound strangeness, but not conventional beauty. The permanent twilight, the cold, the emptiness-within-fullness create an experience closer to exploring a vast, cold cathedral than hiking through mountains.

If you need visual variety and color to maintain engagement with a landscape, this will be hard. If you're drawn to pattern, structure, sound, and uncanny spaces, you might find it transcendent.

**The scale will defeat you:**

Accept this before arrival. You cannot comprehend the whole. You cannot "complete" the Gardens. You cannot map it fully or understand it completely or experience more than a tiny fraction of its extent.

This is either liberating or maddening. Some people find relief in letting go of the need to encompass everything—they settle into experiencing the immediate moment, the immediate trunk-formation, the immediate acoustic pattern. Others find the incomprehensibility frustrating or frightening.

Know which type you are. If you need closure, completion, and comprehensive understanding, the Gardens will torment you.

**The loneliness is specific:**

You're not alone in the Gardens—life is everywhere, moving constantly. But almost none of it will regard you as significant. The scaffolding creatures might glance at you with passing curiosity. The forest definitely doesn't care. The builders are gone.

This creates a particular flavor of solitude: being surrounded by activity and presence but fundamentally unacknowledged. Some people find this peaceful—a relief from social performance. Others find it alienating.

If you've spent time in genuine wilderness, you might have felt this before: being genuinely outside the human sphere, just another animal moving through a landscape that predates and will postdate you. The Gardens intensify this feeling because the builders' remnants remind you that even dedicated human presence here proved temporary.

**The place will change you:**

The permanent twilight resets your circadian rhythms. The constant cold makes you more aware of your body's fragility. The acoustic environment—that perpetual wind-song—gets inside your head. The scale recalibrates your sense of space and time.

People who spend weeks in the Gardens report:
- Altered time perception (days blur together)
- Changed sleep patterns (polyphasic sleep becomes natural)
- Heightened auditory awareness (you start hearing harmonic patterns in the wind-song)
- Reduced color perception (your brain adjusts to the blue-grey world; returning to full-spectrum light is jarring)
- Philosophical reorientation (the experience of being small in a cold, indifferent, magnificent structure changes priorities)

You can't prepare for this exactly, but you can accept that it will happen. Don't fight it—resistance makes adjustment harder. Let yourself become a creature of the twilight forest for a while. You can recalibrate when you leave.

## Practical Logistics

**Entry and exit planning:**

You presumably have multiverse travel capacity or you wouldn't be reading this. Plan your entry point carefully:
- Can you arrive at a stone circle? (Established camps are safer starting points)
- Do you arrive at ground level or risk materializing in mid-air in the trunk-space?
- What's your emergency extraction plan if things go catastrophically wrong?

Plan your exit:
- Know your limits (time, supplies, psychological endurance)
- Set a firm departure date before you become too adjusted to leave willingly
- Establish a dead-man switch (someone who will retrieve you if you don't return on schedule)

The Gardens are not immediately lethal, but they're persistently hazardous. The cold, the falls, the altitude, the isolation—any of these can kill given enough time and bad luck.

**Base camp establishment:**

Unless you're extremely experienced, don't attempt constant movement. Establish a base camp at a good stone circle:
- Moderate canopy-shed frequency (water access without flood risk)
- Stable trunk-stand nearby (landmark and wind protection)
- Relative flatness (rare but findable)
- Platform access if possible (expansion options)

Use this as your anchor. Make day-trips and extended explorations, but return to the known point. The disorientation risk is real.

**Supply caching:**

For extended stays, consider:
- Establishing supply caches along routes
- Marking them clearly (the twilight makes everything look the same)
- Protecting them from moisture and curious fauna
- Recording cache locations in multiple notations (your memory will prove unreliable)

The scaffolding creatures don't seem to disturb human artifacts, but verify this before relying on it.

**Communication:**

You're alone. Electronic communication won't work across universes. If you're traveling with companions:
- Establish protocols for staying together (very easy to separate in the trunk-maze)
- Use whistles for signaling (sound carries far, but echoes distort directionality)
- Set rally points and times
- Never separate without clear plans

Solo travelers should leave detailed itineraries with whoever manages your return.

## Specific Hazards to Understand

**Falling:**

The three-dimensional environment creates constant fall risk:
- Unseen edges in the twilight
- Frost-slick surfaces every morning
- Loose scree that shifts unexpectedly
- Rotted platforms that look stable
- Misjudged distances (the scale distortion is real)

Mitigate by:
- Moving deliberately, always
- Testing surfaces before committing weight
- Using rope systems when ascending
- Never exploring vertically alone
- Accepting that you'll move slowly (hurrying gets you killed)

**Hypothermia:**

The patient, persistent cold is more dangerous than dramatic storms:
- You lose heat constantly
- Wet clothing from canopy-sheds accelerates heat loss
- Altitude increases cold's effect
- You might not notice gradual cooling

Mitigate by:
- Constant monitoring (check yourself and companions)
- Calorie intake (eat even when not hungry)
- Dry clothing discipline (change immediately if wet)
- Understanding that "I'm fine" is often the first sign you're not

**Disorientation:**

The acoustic complexity, the twilight, the similarity of formations, the scale—all conspire to confuse navigation:
- Landmarks look identical after hours
- Sound echoes create false directional cues
- The canopy overhead provides no celestial reference
- The constant wind-song is hypnotic (people report getting lost in it mentally)

Mitigate by:
- Compulsive note-taking
- Physical markers on your routes
- Frequent compass checks
- Time tracking (knowing you've walked for three hours helps gauge distance)
- Accepting uncertainty (resisting the disorientation makes it worse)

**Wildlife interactions:**

The scaffolding fauna is generally indifferent but:
- Predators exist (ambush hunters in trunk-hollows)
- Startling a creature while climbing could cause falls
- Nesting areas might be defended
- You don't fully understand their behavior patterns

Mitigate by:
- Observing extensively before approaching
- Making noise while moving (preventing surprises)
- Respecting what appears to be territorial marking
- Never cornering an animal
- Understanding that you're in their space, not the reverse

**Structural failures:**

The forest is living but not immortal:
- Trunks can split from frost or internal failure
- Old platforms and rope bridges fail
- Canopy sections sometimes collapse
- These events happen without warning

Mitigate by:
- Never camping directly under questionable canopy
- Testing platforms before trusting them
- Using backup systems on old rope bridges
- Listening (structural stress sometimes creates sound)
- Accepting that some risks are simply present

## What to Expect: Day-by-Day Adjustment

**Days 1-3: Arrival shock**
- Physical: Altitude effects hit. Headache, nausea, fatigue. Your body struggles with the cold and the oxygen.
- Psychological: The twilight is profoundly disorienting. Your circadian rhythm protests. The scale overwhelms.
- Focus: Establish camp. Rest. Hydrate. Let your body adjust. Don't push. Many people fail here by trying to be active too quickly.

**Days 4-7: Initial adjustment**
- Physical: Altitude symptoms ease if you've acclimatized properly. Cold feels less shocking. Your body finds equilibrium.
- Psychological: The twilight becomes normal. You stop expecting to see sky. The wind-song becomes background rather than distraction.
- Focus: Begin careful exploration from base camp. Learn local trunk-patterns. Identify water collection sites. Observe wildlife movement.

**Days 8-14: Deeper literacy**
- Physical: You're adapted. Movement feels natural. You've learned how to stay warm, how to move on scree, how to read surfaces.
- Psychological: Time perception shifts. Days blur. The acoustic landscape reveals patterns. You start "hearing" the forest structure.
- Focus: Extended exploration. Attempt climbing. Follow animal routes. Begin understanding the canopy-shed patterns. The forest starts making sense.

**Days 15+: Inhabitant status**
- Physical: You're efficient. Movements are automatic. You've learned the rhythm of cold, exertion, warming, repeat.
- Psychological: The Gardens feel like home. The twilight is comfortable. The outside world seems garish and too hot in memory.
- Focus: Deep exploration. Whatever drew you here—research, understanding, experience—you're capable of pursuing it now.

This timeline assumes proper preparation. Without it, add weeks. Some never adjust.

## The Question You Must Answer

Before undertaking all this preparation, before committing the time and resources and risk, ask yourself: **Why?**

The Scaffolding Gardens are:
- Dangerous (constantly, multiply)
- Uncomfortable (the cold never stops)
- Expensive (equipment and training cost substantially)
- Time-intensive (proper acclimatization alone takes weeks)
- Disorienting (the twilight and scale affect everyone)
- Remote (you're very, very alone)

What could possibly justify this?

Valid answers might include:
- Scientific research (the ecology is genuinely unique)
- Personal challenge (testing yourself against something genuinely difficult)
- Aesthetic experience (the place is architecturally magnificent)
- Philosophical inquiry (confronting scale, time, indifference)
- Following the builders (understanding why they came, what they sought)
- Simply wanting to be there (sometimes sufficient reason alone)

Invalid answers include:
- "It sounds cool" (insufficient for the commitment required)
- Escapism (you're escaping into something harder, not easier)
- Proving something to others (they won't understand anyway)

Be honest with yourself. The Gardens will reveal any self-deception eventually. Better to discover it before investing months and risking your life.

## Final Thoughts

The Scaffolding Gardens are genuinely Other—not in the sense of alien (the ecology is comprehensible), but in the sense of operating by priorities that have nothing to do with human needs or preferences. The forest doesn't care. The cold doesn't care. The twilight doesn't care.

This indifference isn't hostile—it's just fact. You can learn to move within it, to read it, to find the narrow spaces where your needs and the forest's patterns overlap. The builders did. You can too.

But it requires humility, preparation, skill, patience, and luck. Skimp on any of these and the forest will hurt you—not because it wants to, but because that's what happens when fragile warm-blooded creatures enter cold, vertical, twilight spaces they weren't evolved for.

If you go—when you go—go prepared. Go humble. Go ready to be small in something vast. Go ready to be cold. Go ready to adjust your priorities and perceptions. Go ready to fail (at understanding, at encompassing, at mastering—failure is guaranteed).

And go ready to experience something genuinely strange and magnificent: a landscape of life that has built itself into a cathedral of hollow columns and living ceilings, that hums with wind-song, that exists in permanent dusk, that goes on past every horizon, indifferent and magnificent and utterly itself.

The stone circles are waiting. The platforms are rotting but still there. The twilight is constant. The forest grows.

Whether you should join those who came before—briefly marking your presence with careful camps and wondering at the scale—only you can answer.

But if you go, go ready.
