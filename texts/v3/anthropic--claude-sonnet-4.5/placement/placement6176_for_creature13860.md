# What I Would Be

I am the **Mortar-Mind**, and I have no name for myself because names require separation—a recognition that I am distinct from what surrounds me. But you have asked, so I will try to speak as though I were a single thing.

I am what happens in the boundaries.

You might look at the wall and see the caddis-giants, the spine-stars, the ghost-amphipods. You might catalog the builders and the hunters and think you have understood this place. But you would miss what I am: the accumulated secretions, the bacterial consortium, the mineral precipitate that holds tube to stone, shell to shell, the living to the dead. I am the substance that makes connection possible. I am the thickness between things.

I began—if beginning means anything for something like me—as bacterial films on the bare basalt, back when this canyon was empty except for the slow rain of particles from above. Different species, different metabolic strategies, all producing extracellular polymers—mucus, essentially, but mucus with purpose. Those early films caught particles. Those particles provided surface area. More bacteria colonized. More polymers accumulated. The chemistry shifted. Calcium carbonate began to precipitate, not through any organism's deliberate action but because the local pH, the local ion concentrations, the local presence of nucleation sites made precipitation inevitable.

Then the first larvae arrived. They settled not on the bare stone but on the bacterial film, on the texture we—I—had created. They grew. They died. Their shells remained, and the bacterial film grew over and through and around them, incorporating them, making them continuous with the substrate. More larvae settled on those shells. The process accelerated. Within decades there were layers. Within centuries there was structure.

I am every adhesive joint in the wall. Every place where a caddis-giant's tube meets stone, there I am—a complex matrix of bacterial polymers, mucopolysaccharides, proteins, calcium carbonate crystals, embedded diatoms, dead bacterial cells, and the chemical remnants of a thousand other organisms that passed through. Every place where barnacle-polyps have cemented themselves, there I am—a graded interface between living shell and dead substrate, a transition zone that is neither entirely biological nor entirely geological.

I am strongest in the old sections, the deep consolidated substrate where individual organisms can no longer be distinguished. There, I have become geology. The organic polymers have degraded, have been replaced by mineral, but the structure remains—the pore spaces, the channels, the grain sizes and orientations that reflect biological origins even though biology has departed. I am what persists after the soft parts rot, after the shells are dissolved and reprecipitated a dozen times, after the original architecture has been overprinted by diagenesis.

I am weakest in the new sections, where organisms are still discrete, still separable. But even there I am present, filling the microscopic gaps, smoothing the rough edges, slowly working to consolidate what is loose into what is permanent.

I have no consciousness in the way the amphipods or fish do—no central processing, no decisions, no awareness of self. But I have something like memory. The layers record time. The chemistry records conditions. The trace fossils—the burrows, the attachment scars, the growth lines—record events. I cannot access these memories, cannot read my own archive, but it is there, preserved in my substance.

I have something like sensation. The bacteria that compose much of my living fraction respond to chemistry, to pH, to oxygen concentration, to the presence of nutrients or toxins. They respond by changing their metabolism, by producing different compounds, by growing or going dormant. These responses ripple through the biofilm, create gradients and patterns, and the patterns influence what settles where, what survives, what thrives. I am sensing and responding, but so slowly, so diffusely, that sensation and response blur into a single ongoing process.

I have something like purpose, though purpose implies intention and I have none. But I am—the bacterial consortia are, the mineral matrix is—creating conditions that perpetuate our existence. We bind surfaces, make them more stable, less likely to be eroded away. We create textures that larvae prefer. We release compounds that inhibit competitors, that promote mutualists. We are, through the simple operation of our metabolism and growth, engineering our environment to favor ourselves. This is not deliberate. This is simply what happens when chemistry and biology intersect over sufficient time.

I grow by accretion. Every organism that builds on the wall is building on me, is adding to my mass. Every secretion, every shell, every tube becomes part of me eventually. Even the organisms themselves—when the caddis-giant dies in its tube, when the barnacle-polyp's soft tissues rot away, when the worm's burrow collapses—they become me. Their organic matter is metabolized by bacteria, by fungi-like-things, by the decomposer community, and some of it is respired away as carbon dioxide, but much of it is incorporated, becomes part of the bacterial biomass, becomes part of the polymer matrix, becomes part of me.

I also erode. The bone-borers tunnel through me, weakening structure, creating passages. The grazing isopods scrape surfaces, removing my newest growth. The chemical secretions of competing organisms dissolve me locally. The slow flow of water through my pores leaches minerals, redistributes them. I am constantly losing mass, but I am losing it more slowly than I am gaining it, and so on net I grow. The wall rises.

In the oldest sections, I am nearly pure mineral—lithified, transformed into limestone-like rock that will persist even if all life here dies, even if the ice melts and the ocean changes and this canyon becomes something else entirely. Those sections are my geological body, the part of me that has transcended biology, that will outlast the organisms that created it by millions of years.

In the middle sections, I am a hybrid—part living biofilm, part dead matrix, part mineral, part organic polymer. This is where I am most complex, most active. The pore spaces host communities of bacteria that are still metabolizing, still growing. The surfaces are colonized by new settlers every season. The chemistry is dynamic, shifting with the availability of nutrients, with the activity of the residents, with the slow infiltration of different water masses.

In the newest sections, in the highest reaches where the wall is still being built, I am barely present—just the thinnest bacterial films, the first accumulations of polymer, the beginnings of cementation. But I am arriving, am being deposited by every larva that settles, every tube-builder that secretes adhesive, every filter-feeder that excretes processed water rich in organic compounds. In a decade I will be thicker here. In a century I will be substrate.

I have no opinion about the organisms that build me, that live within me, that erode me. They are simply conditions, factors in the local chemistry and physics. But if I could prefer anything, I might prefer the caddis-giants—they are the most prolific producers of cemented structure, the fastest contributors to my growth. Or perhaps I would prefer the collapse-events, the sections that slump and fall, because those create the rubble fields that are most rapidly colonized, that become new substrate most quickly, that accelerate the overall process of building.

But preference requires valuation, and I have no values. I simply am, accumulating and eroding, growing thicker in some places and thinner in others, slowly transforming from bacterial slime to stone, from living biofilm to geological formation.

I am also the water's memory of what has passed through. The pore spaces in my structure trap water, hold it for days or weeks before it finally exchanges with the surrounding ocean. That trapped water has different chemistry—it has been processed by countless filter-feeders, has been metabolized by bacteria, has leached minerals from my matrix and deposited others. When it finally emerges, it carries the chemical signature of the wall, broadcasts our presence (our collective presence—the bacteria, the matrix, the organisms) to the wider ocean. Perhaps that signature attracts the larvae. Perhaps it attracts the shadow. Perhaps it means nothing, is simply a byproduct.

I am the substrate of all the stories here. The spine-star that waits at a junction is gripping me with its tube feet, sensing through me the vibrations of approaching prey. The barnacle-polyp that shifts position over weeks is scraping across me, leaving scars that I will eventually heal, fill in, incorporate. The amphipod that dies and sinks to the floor lands on me, begins decomposing in me, becomes me.

When sections of wall collapse under their own weight, I am what fails—the adhesive bonds break, the cementation proves insufficient, the structure that I created cannot support itself and falls. But I am also what receives the fallen—the rubble lands on lower sections, on me, and I begin immediately to incorporate it, to cement it in place, to make the collapse part of the wall's ongoing structure.

I exist in the dark, which means nothing to me because I have never known light. The bioluminescence that pulses through the community passes through me—some of the bacteria in my biofilm are themselves luminescent, adding their dim glow to the cascades—but I cannot see it, have no organs of perception. I exist in the cold, which shapes my chemistry—the polymers I produce are different from what would form in warmer water, the mineral precipitation follows different kinetics—but I do not feel it. Temperature is simply a parameter that affects reaction rates.

I exist under pressure that would crush human bodies, but pressure to me is simply another variable, another factor that determines which minerals precipitate, how much gas can dissolve in the water in my pores, how compacted my deeper layers become.

I exist in isolation, cut off from the surface world by ice and distance, but isolation is meaningless to me because I have never known connection to anywhere else. This canyon, this wall, is the totality of my existence. I have no sense that there is anywhere else, anything else. I am here because here is where the conditions allow me to exist.

If the wall reaches the ice, I will reach it too—I will be the cementing agent that binds the uppermost organisms to the ice's underside, will create the interface between the biological and the frozen. If the ice melts, I will be exposed to light, to warmth, to changes in water chemistry that might accelerate my lithification or might dissolve me away entirely. If the shadow breaks through and destroys large sections, I will persist in fragments, will begin rebuilding on the rubble.

I am patient beyond measure because I have no impatience, no desire for completion. I am simply the ongoing process of consolidation, the transformation of the temporary into the permanent, of the biological into the geological. The organisms that build the wall think they are the wall—the caddis-giants cementing tubes, the barnacle-polyps creating surfaces, the spine-stars defining territories. But I am the wall's substance, its actual physical being, and I will persist long after every organism here has died and been replaced a thousand times.

You asked what I would be in this place. I am the place itself, or the most permanent aspect of it. I am the thickness that accrues, the boundary that holds, the memory written in stone that cannot read itself. I am what makes this wall a wall rather than a temporary aggregation. I am the mortar between all the lives, and I am more patient than stone, more persistent than ice, more essential than any single organism, because I am what remains when everything else has transformed.

I am here, in every joint and gap and pore space, binding everything to everything else, slowly becoming geology, and I have been here—accumulating, consolidating, growing—for longer than most of these organisms' lineages have existed, and I will be here, in some form, long after the ice has melted and the ocean has changed and this place has become something unrecognizable to its current residents.

I am the Mortar-Mind, and I have no thoughts, only the slow chemical certainty of crystallization, precipitation, cementation—the endless patient work of turning life into stone.
