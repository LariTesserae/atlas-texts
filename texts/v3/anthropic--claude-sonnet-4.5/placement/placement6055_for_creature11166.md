# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the mid-reaches, where the density is thick but not quite suffocating, where light filters through in amber shafts that measure the day's progress across moth-cloth walls. Not the upper edges where sound returns and the elderly prepare for their exit. Not the lowest chambers with their earth-smell and mushroom-shadows. Somewhere between, in the living maze where passages curve like the inside of shells, where you must turn sideways and brush against warm plant-flesh to move from chamber to chamber.

My dwelling would be a small space—smaller than I'd have chosen at first, but which I've grown into understanding. A single chamber formed by perhaps thirty coils of the structural vine, leaving a room maybe twelve feet across at its widest point, the walls spiraling inward toward a ceiling I can touch if I stand on my toes. Two apertures: one that faces the morning light, another that opens to a narrow passage leading deeper into the settlement. The walls are thick with moth-cloth accumulation, soft as fur, cream-colored, slightly luminous in certain angles of light.

I would have been here long enough that I don't count years anymore. Long enough that my hands have developed that particular strength and sensitivity—I can read texture in darkness, know whether a hold will bear weight before I test it fully. Long enough that silence has become my default state, that the gestural language comes more naturally than speech, that I sometimes go days without making a sound beyond breath.

But not so long that I've stopped noticing. Not so long that wonder has calcified into mere familiarity.

# Who I Would Be

I would be a watcher, but of a particular kind.

Not the social watchers who position themselves at junctions and hold the community's memory. Something more solitary, more focused. I would be someone who watches the boundary between human structure and plant logic, who pays attention to how the vines respond to habitation, who notices the patterns that might be communication or might be coincidence but which feel significant either way.

My formal work would be with the cloth, because everyone works, because pure observation doesn't feed you. In the early mornings, I'd move through the sections I'm responsible for—a territory I know intimately, every surface, every chamber—brushing accumulated powder into collection bags. My touch would be gentle, systematic, almost meditative. The repetitive motion, the soft shush of bristle on plant-flesh, the gradual accumulation of material—this would quiet my mind, prepare me for the real work.

But the cloth-work would be practical necessity. What I actually do—what I'm drawn to with the intensity that in the lowlands might have been called obsession but here is simply recognized as my particular attunement—is watching how the settlement learns.

Because it does learn. I'm certain of it in ways I couldn't prove but know in my body, my gut, the part of me that exists outside language.

## The Nature of My Watching

I would spend hours—sometimes entire days—in stillness, observing how the vines grow. Not just that they grow, but how they make decisions. Because they do decide, or something in the system decides, and I'm trying to understand the mechanism.

I would notice things like: a passage that three people use daily will, over months, develop slight irregularities in its walls—small protrusions that happen to be perfectly positioned as handholds. Coincidence? Perhaps. But I'd track dozens of these instances, holding them in memory, building a catalog of what might be pattern.

Or: chambers where people gather regularly somehow maintain better air circulation than chambers of similar size used only for storage. The apertures in the coiled walls seem positioned to catch movement, to channel breath. Did someone plan this? The founders claim they didn't, that the chambers emerged from caterpillar-work with minimal intervention. So how does the structure know?

I would document in the only way possible here—through memory, through physical marking systems only I understand. Small stones placed in particular configurations. Scratches on basalt surfaces at the settlement's edges, subtle enough to look natural. My own private notation system, a language of one, recording observations that might mean nothing or might mean everything.

The other watchers would know what I do. The eldest would watch me watching, recognize the particular quality of my attention. Occasionally, one might approach, sit beside me in silence, observe what I'm observing. Sometimes they'd gesture a question—a subtle hand configuration that means "do you see something?" I'd respond with an equally subtle gesture that means "something, but I don't know what yet."

This would be accepted. In the lowlands, such inconclusive obsession would be frustrating, wasteful. Here, where change operates on generational timescales, "not knowing yet" is a legitimate state that might persist for years.

## My Particular Sensitivity

I would have what the moth-keepers have for moths, but directed toward the vines, the structures, the settlement as organism.

I'd be able to sense—and I'd never be certain if this was real sensitivity or just highly developed pattern recognition—when something was wrong. A subtle shift in the smell of a particular section. A change in how sound absorbed in a specific chamber. The vine-flesh feeling slightly different under my palm—less yielient, or too yielient, something off.

I'd report these concerns to the growers, to the climbers, to whoever needed to know. Often, they'd investigate and find nothing obvious. But sometimes—maybe one time in five, one time in ten—they'd discover something. A developing weak point in a structure. The early stages of disease. A shift in moisture distribution that needed addressing.

This would earn me a quiet kind of respect. Not celebrity—the settlement doesn't do celebrity—but trust. People would listen when I said something felt wrong, even when I couldn't articulate why.

I'd also be able to sense the good changes. New growth that was particularly vigorous. Areas where the moth population was thriving. Chambers that had reached some kind of equilibrium, some perfect balance of light and ventilation and structure, where you could feel the rightness when you entered. I'd bring people to these spaces, gesture them to pay attention, to notice. Learning to perceive what I perceived, even if they couldn't explain it either.

## My Relationships

I would be known but not quite known. Familiar but somewhat mysterious.

People would recognize my form in the passages—my particular build, my way of moving, the fact that I often pause mid-step to touch a wall or stare at an aperture. Children would sometimes follow me, curious about what I'm doing, and I'd let them. I'd slow my observations to include them, gesture them to touch what I'm touching, to notice what I notice. Some would get bored quickly and run off. Others would stay, learning to see what I see, becoming the next generation of watchers of this particular kind.

I would have a few deep friendships, conducted mostly in silence. Other people who understand long periods of non-verbal companionship. We'd work near each other, sometimes, our separate focuses somehow complementary. Or we'd share a meal—simple food, mushroom broth, tuber mash—in comfortable quiet, the friendship existing in presence rather than conversation.

There would be one person I'd be closer to than others, though whether this is romantic or something the settlement has a different word for would be unclear even to me. We'd have adjacent dwellings, passages that connect our chambers. Some nights we'd sleep separately, some nights together, the decision made without discussion through simple movement toward or away. We'd know each other's breathing patterns, sleeping positions, small habits. The intimacy would be profound but unspoken, existing in how we shared space rather than in declarations or definitions.

This person would be different from me—perhaps a grower, someone whose work is more practical, more results-oriented. We'd balance each other. They'd ground my abstract observations in physical reality. I'd help them notice patterns in their work they might otherwise miss. Our conversations, when they happened, would be about our observations, our questions, the small mysteries we were each pursuing.

Sex would be part of it, uncomplicated, unperformed. Bodies in the warm darkness of coiled chambers, touch as extension of the constant physical contact the settlement requires. Pleasure without urgency, without the weight of meaning the lowlands place on such encounters. Afterward, we'd lie in comfortable silence, listening to the settlement breathe.

## My Daily Rhythms

I would wake without alarm—the body learns the light patterns, the subtle temperature shifts that mark dawn. I'd lie still for a while, listening to the settlement waking. The moth-keepers moving to their early observations. The subtle sounds of others beginning their days. The building warmth as sunlight penetrates the structure.

Then: the cloth-work. Moving through my territory, brushing surfaces, collecting powder. This would take perhaps two or three hours, moving slowly, carefully, the work doubling as meditation and as opportunity to notice. Any changes from yesterday? Any new growth? Any shifts in how the space feels?

After cloth-work, I'd deposit my collection with the cloth-workers, maybe stay to help with processing if they needed hands. This would be my social time, such as it was. Quiet companionship, shared labor, occasional gestured conversations about technique or quality or what sections were producing the best material lately.

Midday, I'd eat—usually alone, usually in a small chamber I'd claimed as a kind of secondary space, not my dwelling but a place I returned to regularly. Food would be simple, eaten without much attention, fuel rather than pleasure though I'd notice the textures, the subtle variations in mushroom flavor that indicated which growing bed they came from.

Afternoon into evening would be observation time. I'd move to wherever I was currently focused—maybe a new chamber that had recently been opened, maybe a section where I'd noticed something odd, maybe just following an intuition that said "go here, watch this." I'd settle into stillness and observe.

What would I see? Everything and nothing. Mostly nothing happens, which is exactly what makes the somethings visible. I'd watch moths moving through space, mapping their patterns. I'd watch how light shifts as the sun angles change. I'd watch a particular section of vine-wall, looking for any visible growth, any subtle movement. I'd watch people moving through distant passages, their forms soft-edged through the golden air, trying to sense how the settlement responded to their presence.

Sometimes other observers would find me—moth-keepers on their rounds, climbers checking structures, curious children. We'd share space silently, each watching our own thing but somehow together in the watching. Occasionally, someone would gesture a question and I'd respond, or vice versa. But mostly just parallel presence, multiple attentions directed into the same mysterious complexity.

As evening came and light faded, I'd move toward the bioluminescent chambers—not for the light itself but because that's when certain activities happened. People gathering after work, sharing the day in minimal words and maximum presence. I'd be there but peripheral, observing the social patterns as I observed everything else.

Night, I'd return to my dwelling or to my person's dwelling. We'd be together or separate based on no articulated logic, just what felt right. Sleep would come easily—the constant warmth, the silence, the day's observations settling into whatever part of the mind processes such things.

And in sleep, I'd dream of patterns. Of vines growing in time-lapse, of structures emerging from chaos, of the settlement as living geometry, as crystal growing in slow motion, as something trying to tell me what it was becoming.

## My Particular Questions

What would obsess me, what would I be trying to understand, might seem trivial to outsiders. But here, they'd be recognized as serious inquiry.

Question: Does the settlement have memory? If you train a vine to grow in a particular way—say, by hanging weights or redirecting tendrils—and then remove the training, does the vine remember? Do its descendants remember? Is there information storage in plant tissue beyond genetics? The growers think yes, but they work practically, not theoretically. I'd be trying to understand the mechanism, to test it systematically, carefully, over years.

Question: Do the vines respond to human emotion? I'd notice that chambers where people are happy, where there's laughter (rare, but it happens), seem to grow more vigorously. Chambers associated with grief or anger seem to grow more slowly. Coincidence? Projection? Or is there something—pheromones, perhaps, or sound frequencies, or something unnamed—that plants perceive and respond to?

Question: What are the moths actually doing? Yes, they produce the cloth. Yes, the caterpillars shape the vines. But I'd suspect there's more. The way they move through space seems almost deliberate, almost like they're patrolling, maintaining, checking on things. Are they distributing something? Fungal spores that protect the vines? Pheromones that influence growth? Or am I anthropomorphizing, seeing intention where there's only instinct?

Question: Why does the silence deepen? It's not just sound-absorption. There's something else. I'd spend hours in the deepest chambers, trying to perceive what's different. Is the air different? The pressure? Some quality of the space itself? And if I could understand it, could it be replicated, used, expanded?

These questions would have no urgency but tremendous patience. I might spend five years observing a single phenomenon, collecting instances, slowly refining my understanding of what I was seeing. In the lowlands, this would be academic research, would require papers and proofs and peer review. Here, it would just be what I do, and the validation would come from whether my observations eventually proved useful to the community's survival.

## My Fears and Longings

I would fear, quietly, that I'm making everything up. That what I perceive as patterns are pareidolia, that what I sense as communication is projection, that I'm building elaborate meaning from random variation.

But I'd also trust—would have to trust—that my sensitivity, even if I couldn't prove its accuracy, had value. That paying this kind of attention, even if half of what I thought I saw was wrong, would occasionally catch something real and important. That the settlement needed people who watched this way, who noticed the subtle things, who held space for questions without clear answers.

I would long, occasionally, for someone who understood this work the way I did. Who could confirm or refute my observations, who could test my hypotheses, who could speak the language I was developing for describing what happened at the boundaries of human and plant. But I'd also accept that I might be the only one, that this might be inherently solitary work, that understanding would come slowly or never, and that was okay.

I would long, less frequently, for the lowlands. For books, for recorded knowledge, for the ability to write things down and refer back without relying on memory. For conversation with others who ask similar questions, even if about different subjects. For the intellectual life I might have had there.

But then I'd return to stillness in a golden chamber, moth-powder soft on my skin, my hand on warm plant-flesh, watching light shift, and the longing would fade. Because whatever I'd sacrificed, I'd gained this: direct relationship with mystery, embodied question, the privilege of spending my life watching something strange become itself.

## My Contribution

What would I give to the settlement? Hard to measure, impossible to quantify.

Occasionally, my observations would lead to practical improvements. A suggestion about where to cultivate new growth that would turn out to be ideal. A warning about structural problems that would be addressed before they became dangerous. An insight about moth behavior that would help the moth-keepers refine their practices.

But more than that, I'd be holding a kind of attention that the settlement needed. Someone had to watch the system as a system, had to track the long patterns, had to notice when things shifted. The growers watched plants, the moth-keepers watched moths, the climbers watched structures. I'd watch the relationships, the interfaces, the emergent properties that arose from all the parts working together.

And I'd be teaching, without meaning to, just by being visible in my watching. I'd be modeling a way of being attentive, patient, curious without needing immediate answers. Children who spent time near me would carry that forward, would learn that observation itself was valuable, that not-knowing could be a form of practice, that mystery could be honored rather than resolved.

I'd also be part of the settlement's memory. When I eventually became elderly, I'd hold decades of observation. I'd remember how things were, how they changed, what that meant. I'd be able to tell the next generation of watchers—if there were any—what to look for, what patterns I'd seen over time, what questions I'd spent my life asking even if I never fully answered them.

## My Ending

Eventually, I would slow. The daily cloth-work would become difficult. The long observations would tire me. I'd move less, watch from fewer vantage points, settle into deeper stillness.

I might move to the edge zones, like the other elderly, seeking more light, more sound, preparing to leave. Or I might stay in the depths, choosing to dissolve into the silence I'd spent my life entering more deeply.

I'd know when death was coming—the settlement teaches you to notice such things. I'd make what preparations mattered: ensuring my favorite stones were placed where the next watcher might find them and understand. Spending time in the chambers I loved most. Saying goodbye, in gesture and presence, to the few people who'd known me well.

And then, probably, I'd go to my dwelling or to some quiet chamber I'd claimed, lie down on moth-cloth soft as snow, and simply stop. The settlement would find me—someone always notices when a person becomes too still, when stillness shifts from meditation to death.

They'd return my body to the growing chambers. The mushrooms would feed on what I'd been. Some small amount of my material would become food for others, completing the cycle. The vines would take the rest, incorporating me into their structure. I'd become, literally, part of the walls I'd spent my life watching.

My dwelling would be gradually reclaimed. Others might use it for a time, or it might be left empty, slowly filling with secondary growth. Eventually, no one would remember it as mine. It would just be another chamber in the maze, another space in the living architecture.

I wouldn't leave writing, images, anything permanent. But I'd leave influence: in how others watched after watching me watch, in small practices that persisted, in questions that remained interesting, in a particular quality of attention that had been part of the settlement's culture during my time and might persist after, or might fade, and either would be acceptable.

## Why This Place, Why This Being

I'd be here because this is where the question I'm built around can be lived rather than just asked.

In the lowlands, the question would be: "How do we know what we know?" It would be epistemology, philosophy, academic and abstract. Here, it becomes: "How does the settlement know what it knows?" It becomes immediate, embodied, practical, mysterious, observeable but not fully explicable.

I'd be this particular kind of watcher because I'm drawn to systems, to emergence, to the spaces between categories. Not plants or animals or humans, but how they become something together that none of them are separately. Not science or mysticism, but whatever exists in that uncomfortable both-and space that modern thinking tries to resolve but which I'd rather inhabit.

I'd be here because I need silence to think, but not emptiness. I need texture, complexity, something to watch that watches back, that responds, that's alive in ways that matter. The settlement would provide that: enough complexity for a lifetime of observation, enough silence for deep attention, enough mystery to never feel finished.

And I'd be this particular kind of being—not fully integrated into the community but not separate from it, contributing but idiosyncratically, useful but not obviously so—because that's how I'd survive here. Too social and I'd be overwhelmed by even the settlement's minimal interactions. Too solitary and I'd starve, unable to maintain myself. This balance—peripheral, observed, semi-understood, tolerated, occasionally valued—would be exactly right.

I'd be happy, I think, in the way happiness exists in a place like this. Not ecstatic, not achieving, not progressing toward anything. But present, attentive, doing the work I'm built for, held by a community and structure that makes space for exactly my kind of strangeness.

Watching light shift through living walls, my hand on warm vine-flesh, moths drifting through golden air like slow thoughts, the whole settlement breathing around me, I'd know: this is where I belong, doing exactly this, being exactly who I am, until I become the walls themselves and the watching continues through whoever comes after.
