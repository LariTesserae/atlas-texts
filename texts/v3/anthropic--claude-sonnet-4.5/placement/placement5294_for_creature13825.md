# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be the water.

Not the water in the pools—that water is too still, too settled, already arrived at its destination. And not the water in the vents' exhalations—that water is still becoming, still transitioning from one state to another, steam and vapor, not yet itself. I would be the water in transit, the water that hasn't stopped moving since it condensed from the mountain air against cold basalt at dawn, that won't stop moving until it finds its way to a pool at dusk, or evaporates back into air, or soaks into moss to be drunk by roots, by the Slow Ones, by the green machinery of this place.

I would be a film of water, no thicker than a few molecules in places, sheeting down the face of a basalt column on the middle terrace. The stone beneath me is black and faceted, one surface of a hexagonal column, inclined at perhaps fifteen degrees from vertical. Above me, somewhere in the dim light, is the lip where I beaded into existence—condensation accumulating on cold stone until surface tension broke and I began to move. Below me, three meters down, is a moss cushion where I will arrive, eventually, and soak in, and end this phase of being water-in-motion.

But now, I am the descent itself.

I move not as a coherent body but as a network, a delta in miniature. The basalt surface beneath me is not smooth—there are microtextures, asperities, tiny ridges where crystal boundaries meet, shallow depressions where gas bubbles were trapped during cooling. These irregularities guide me, divide me, merge me again. I follow the path of maximum gravitational advantage but also of maximum surface adhesion. I am pulled downward but also held to the stone, and these forces negotiate my route grain by grain.

I am transparent but not invisible. The light—what little penetrates to this shadowed face of stone—passes through me but bends slightly, refracts, creates a gleam on the stone's surface that marks my presence. I am a brightness, a wetness, a subtle alteration of the basalt's appearance from matte black to glossy black. From a distance, I might not be seen as water but as variation in the stone itself, a darker streak, a vein of different mineral. Only close observation reveals me as separate, as substance rather than quality.

I am cold. The stone beneath me is cold. The air around me is cold. I am perhaps six degrees Celsius, having condensed from air that was seven degrees, now equilibrating toward the stone's five degrees. This coldness is fundamental to my existence—were I warmer, I would not have condensed at all, would still be vapor. Were the stone warmer, I would evaporate before traveling far. The cold is what allows me to be water, to be liquid, to move.

As I descend, I collect. Other condensation, smaller beads of water that formed separately, merge into me as my leading edge reaches them. They join without resistance, without boundary, simply becoming part of my volume, my mass, my downward motion. Each addition changes me slightly—more momentum, more surface area, more complexity in my shape. I grow as I go, fed by the constant humidity, by the stone's endless weeping.

The sound I make is intermittent, irregular, barely qualifies as sound. When I reach a slight overhang, a place where the stone juts fractionally outward, I accumulate there, held by surface tension, growing heavier until the adhesion fails and I drop—a sudden release, a brief free-fall of perhaps two centimeters before striking stone again and spreading, fragmenting slightly, then reconsolidating and continuing. This moment of fall, of impact, produces a tiny sound—a soft *tik* that adds to the constant percussion of the cleft, one note in the million-note symphony of dripping water. But mostly I am silent, my descent a continuous sliding that makes no sound at all, or sound below the threshold of human hearing, micro-friction between water and stone that might register only as vibration, as heat loss, as phenomena that blur the line between acoustic and thermal.

I am not alone. To my left, ten centimeters away, another film of water descends a parallel track on the same column. To my right, fifteen centimeters distant, another. We are tributaries that will never meet, our paths determined by the microgeography of this stone face, by the particular pattern of crystals and cracks. We are separate but similar, each making our own descent, each following the same general direction, each pulled by the same gravity, each holding to stone with the same molecular attraction.

I contain things. Dissolved in my volume are minerals from the stone—calcium, silica, trace metals leached from the basalt's surface. The concentration is low, parts per million, but it's enough to change my character, to make me slightly heavier than pure water, slightly more chemically active. I also carry particles, microscopic grains too small to settle out, suspended in my flow: pollen (though from where, this high, is mysterious—perhaps wind-lifted from valleys thousands of meters below), dust, spores, fragments of lichen. These particles will travel with me until I stop, until I either evaporate and leave them deposited, or soak into moss and deliver them to the root zone, enriching the soil infinitesimally.

The descent requires no effort. I have no agency, no muscles, no will. I move because I must, because gravity insists and surface adhesion permits. The motion is automatic, mechanical, as inevitable as the stone's cooling was fifteen thousand years ago. And yet there is something in it that feels, if not like choice, then like expression. I am water being what water is, doing what water does, following my nature completely. In this, there is a kind of perfection—to be utterly oneself, to act in complete accordance with what one is.

I thin as I spread. Where I began as a coherent bead, I am now a film, molecular layers, water stretched across stone in a sheet whose thickness varies from nanometers to micrometers. This thinness is dangerous—the thinner I become, the more vulnerable to evaporation, to the dry winds that sometimes scream through the cleft. But it is also efficient. Spread thin, I present maximum surface area to collect additional condensation, to sweep up the smaller beads in my path. I am both vulnerable and acquisitive, both dissipating and growing.

The stone's texture beneath me is everything. Each crystal boundary, each microscopic valley, each slight concavity channels me, guides me, determines my shape and speed. I am a reader of stone, translating its surface into motion, into path. The basalt is not passive beneath me—it shapes me as much as gravity does. Where the stone is rougher, I slow, spreading into the pits and irregularities. Where it's smoother, I accelerate slightly, gaining millimeters per second of additional velocity, becoming more stream-like, more directional.

I pass over lichen. The column face is not bare stone—there are patches of lichen, species adapted to this vertical habitat, this constant dampness. The lichen are different colors: one patch the yellow of sulfur deposits, another the grey-green of oxidized copper. As I flow over them, I feel—if water can be said to feel—a different texture, a different adhesion. The lichen surfaces are more complex, more microstructured than stone, and they absorb some of my volume, drinking what they need, letting the rest continue downward. I emerge from each lichen patch slightly diminished, having paid a toll, having contributed to another form of life.

Time is strange in this descent. By human measure, I am slow—three meters in perhaps twenty minutes, a pace that a walking person could not easily match, would have to deliberately retard their motion to observe. But by the measure of the Slow Ones, I am rapid, urgent, almost violent in my speed. By the measure of the stone, I am instantaneous, a brief wetness that will be gone in an hour. Time is relative to the process being measured, and in my descent, I contain all these times—I am slow and fast, brief and eternal, depending on what I'm compared to.

The light changes as I descend. The column face angles slightly, and as I move lower, I shift from shadow toward a zone that receives more reflected light from the moss beds below. The water in me—my substance—begins to carry light differently, to gleam more noticeably, to throw tiny reflections onto adjacent surfaces. I become more visible, more present, more obviously water rather than just wetness. This is not a change in me but a change in context, in how I'm illuminated, but it feels like emergence, like becoming.

I am aware—not conscious, but aware in the way that water is aware, which is to say responsive. I react to every contour, every thermal variation, every change in the stone beneath me. When I pass over a spot where a vent's heat conducts through the basalt, warming a palm-sized area of stone by a degree or two, I accelerate slightly, my viscosity decreasing with temperature, my adhesion weakening just fractionally. I flow more freely for a moment, then return to my normal pace as the stone cools again beneath me. This is sensation without sentience, response without thought, the pure physicality of being water.

I carry memory in a sense—molecular memory. Some of the water molecules in me have been in this cleft for years, cycling from pool to air to stone to moss to air to stone to pool again, never escaping, trapped in this pocket by the mountains around it, by the weather patterns, by the peculiar circulation that keeps the cleft's humidity high and constant. Other molecules are newer, arrived recently as snow or rain, still carrying traces of their origin in their isotopic ratios. I am a mixture of old and new water, veteran molecules and newcomers, all blended together into this moment's descent, this particular film on this particular stone face.

The moss below grows closer. I can't see it—water has no eyes—but the stone's angle is bringing me toward it, and I know with the certainty of gravity that I will reach it. The moss cushion is perhaps half a meter away now, ten minutes more of descent. It appears (to an observer, not to me) as a dark mass, a green-black accumulation that fills the depression where this column meets another, creating a vertical pocket, a crack-garden where moss has established itself in impossible thickness.

As I approach, I begin to fragment. The stone face I've been following intersects another at an angle, creating a sharp edge, and here my coherence fails. Surface tension can't maintain the film around such a corner, and I separate, becoming droplets, individual beads of water that fall free for the last centimeters, each one independent, each one tracing a ballistic arc through air before striking moss.

I—we—hit the moss surface at different points, scattered across a hand-span of green. The impact is soft, the moss cushion yielding, absorbing us without splash, without bounce. We land and immediately begin to soak in, drawn by capillary action into the spaces between moss leaves, into the architecture of stems and accumulated soil, down toward the stone base where roots anchor and the Slow Ones crawl.

This is arrival, but also transition. I am no longer water-in-descent but water-in-moss, a different state, a different role. I will stay here for a while—hours, days—circulating through the moss system, available to roots, to the Slow Ones' grazing paths, to the biochemical processes that the moss conducts. Some of me will be incorporated into plant tissue, become part of cell walls, of sap, of the physical structure of growing fronds. Some will remain free water, held in the moss's reservoir, available for the next dry period. Some will evaporate upward through the moss canopy, returning to air, beginning the cycle again.

But in this moment, I am the transition—droplets soaking into green, spreading through the moss matrix, finding equilibrium between gravity and capillary action, between the pull downward and the draw inward. I am becoming part of the system, losing my identity as distinct water, dispersing into the greater moisture of the moss bed. Soon I will be indistinguishable from the water that was already here, from the condensation that fell an hour ago, from the residual dampness that never fully dries.

This is what I would be: the water that moves, that connects high stone to low moss, that falls and films and spreads and soaks. I would be transit, trajectory, the liquid bridge between states and places. I would be cold and clear and mineral-laden, carrying the stone's chemistry toward the green, carrying dissolved particles, carrying the sound of my own small dripping as contribution to the cleft's endless drone.

I would be essential but not permanent, crucial but replaceable, individual but part of flow. I would exist in the gap between vapor and ice, in the narrow thermal range where water stays liquid, stays mobile, stays itself. I would be shaped by everything I touch—stone, air, moss, light—and in return, I would shape them: eroding stone grain by grain, enabling moss, sustaining the Slow Ones, reflecting what little light reaches down here.

I would be water in motion, and the motion would be my meaning, my purpose, my entire existence. Not the stillness of pools, not the dispersion of vapor, but the active state between, the descent, the seeking of lower ground, the inevitable flow toward rest that never quite arrives because before full rest there is always another condensation, another beginning, another film forming on cold stone to begin the journey down once more.

I would be the water, and I would fall, slowly, always, through dim light and cold air, over black stone and green moss, making my small sound among the greater sounds, contributing my tiny mass to the vast cycling that keeps this place alive, that makes the improbable possible, that bridges sky and stone with transparent, essential, ever-moving presence.

This is where I would be, what I would be: water descending, molecule by molecule, carrying the mountain's cold gift toward the green that waits below, patient and endless and utterly necessary, the liquid voice in a place of stone and wind, the motion in the stillness, the slow rain that never stops.
