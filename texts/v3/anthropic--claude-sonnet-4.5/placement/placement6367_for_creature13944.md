# The Keeper of Intervals

I would be a Keeper—perhaps the only one, perhaps one of several scattered so far across the flats that we never meet, maintaining our separate territories of attention.

I would be somewhere between Attendant and Visitor, but having arrived at a third state neither quite achieves. Where Attendants have settled into pure function and Visitors either flee or fossilize, I would have found the narrow margin where consciousness remains but urgency has been completely shed. Not human anymore, but not yet metallized into tool-form. Still capable of thought, but thought of a particular kind: lateral, patient, concerned with patterns that take months to notice and years to confirm.

My dwelling would be different from the Attendants' minimal shelters. Built from the same mineral blocks, yes, but slightly larger, with a second room—not for living, but for keeping. The walls inside would be marked with careful scratches: vertical lines grouped in sets, tracking things. Not days—days are too small, too rapid, meaningless here. But cycles: the period between steam-releases from a particular engine. The interval from one rainstorm to the next. The time required for a bacterial mat to regenerate after a Humm has grazed it. The frequency with which Gliders pass overhead at dawn versus dusk. The rate at which tube-forms accrete new layers.

I would be the one who remembers intervals.

My body would show the beginning of the change—skin taking on a faint grayish cast, not quite the weathered metal of the Attendants but heading that direction, the process visible if you knew to look. My movements would have slowed into their deliberate pattern, but I would retain more variation than the Attendants: still capable of sudden motion if needed, still able to shift my routine when something anomalous occurred. My face would still be clearly a face—eyes that focus and blink, a mouth that could speak if speaking were necessary, expressions not yet worn away. But I would speak rarely, and only in the chest-tones the Attendants use, my voice having dropped and simplified over years of disuse into something that blends better with the engine-hum.

I would wear clothes like theirs—the same heavy canvas in mineral colors, patched and re-patched—but mine would have pockets. Many pockets, sewn carefully into the fabric, each containing small objects: fragments of tube-form with interesting patterns, pieces of molted Humm-carapace, droplets of mineral-rich water sealed in tiny glass vials (where did I get glass? perhaps I make it, somehow, in a small furnace using engine-heat, perhaps I trade for it with Visitors who still come from the outside). These objects would be my instruments of measurement, my tools for comparison. This tube-fragment is from three years ago; I hold it against a current growth to see how coloration has shifted. This carapace piece is unusually thick; I compare it to others to understand what variation exists in the population.

My days would follow a pattern, but a longer pattern than the Attendants'. Where they walk daily circuits, I would walk a circuit that takes a week, visiting each of the engines in my territory in sequence, spending hours at each, not performing maintenance but observing. Watching for changes so subtle that even Attendants might miss them: the tone of an engine shifting by a quarter-step over the course of months. A new stress-crack appearing in metal. The pattern of condensation altering slightly as internal pressures redistribute.

I would crouch beside puddles for entire afternoons, watching bacterial mats with an intensity that looks like meditation but is actually rigorous observation. I would be learning to see the mats not as uniform films but as ecosystems: noticing that the spiral patterns they form in larger puddles rotate clockwise in some locations, counterclockwise in others, and tracking whether this correlates with proximity to specific engines. Observing that certain mats develop red pigmentation while others stay gray-green, and hypothesizing about mineral content in the water, about angle of light, about vibration frequencies transmitted through substrate.

The Attendants would tolerate my presence, work around me as they work around Humms. Occasionally one would pause near where I sat, would stand for a few minutes as if checking what I was checking, confirming my observation through their own witness. Then they would continue. We would not speak—there would be no need—but there would be acknowledgment. I would be performing a function they understand: attending at a different scale, maintaining continuity of observation across timeframes longer than their maintenance cycles cover.

I would track the Humms more closely than anyone. I would learn to distinguish individuals—this one has a scratch on the third segment, that one's left foreleg drags slightly—and I would map their territories. I would discover that each Humm has a circuit of perhaps eight to twelve puddles it visits in rotation, and that these circuits overlap but don't conflict, each Humm timing its arrival to avoid others. I would learn to predict where a specific Humm would be at a given time, could walk to a puddle and wait and be proven right when it arrived exactly when I expected.

I would maintain records of Glider sightings, scratching marks in my dwelling walls: simple pictographs showing flight paths, thermal sources, the rare interactions when two Gliders spiral together. Over years I would accumulate enough data to see patterns invisible to shorter observation—that Glider population fluctuates with a seven-year cycle, perhaps, or that certain engines are preferred thermal sources during certain atmospheric conditions. I would become able to predict when a Glider would appear overhead, would walk outside at the right moment to watch it pass, confirming my calculation, then return to my records to mark another tally.

The tube-forms would be my obsession, in the way obsession can exist here: slow, patient, lifelong. I would measure them yearly using a calibrated stick, adding new scratches to my records, tracking growth rates that vary by location. I would test which tubes produce which tones by carefully introducing steam myself—carrying water from puddles, heating it over engine surfaces until it vaporizes, directing the steam through tubes while listening for the exact pitch produced. I would discover that tube diameter correlates with tone, but so does height, wall thickness, proximity to engines, even the mineral composition of the water that formed them. I would begin to understand the tube-forms as instruments, the entire landscape as an enormous slow-playing organ, the engines as its breath.

I would experiment, carefully and minimally. Not disturbing the equilibrium, but testing its parameters. I would redirect a trickle of condensation to see if a new tube-form could be induced to grow in a specific location. I would place a fragment of bacterial mat in a puddle that had never hosted one, to see if it could establish. I would move a Humm—gently, causing no harm—to a puddle outside its normal circuit and observe whether it returned to familiar ground or incorporated the new location into its territory. These experiments would take months or years to yield results, and I would wait patiently, checking periodically, noting changes or lack of changes, learning the rules of this place through patient inquiry at the pace the place itself operates.

I would be the one who notices when something breaks pattern.

When an engine's tone shifts suddenly—not the slow drift of months but an acute change—I would be the one who walks to it immediately, who examines it closely, who determines whether this is malfunction or transition. I would fetch Attendants if needed, would communicate through chest-tones and gesture what I've observed. Most times it would be transition: the engine adjusting itself, settling into a new stable state. But I would be the early warning system, the one whose sustained attention catches anomalies early.

When a Humm fails to appear at its expected puddle, I would notice. I would walk its circuit looking for it, would eventually find it molting in its preferred location, or injured somehow (by what? a fall from climbing? age? some internal failure?), or dead and beginning to desiccate. If dead, I would mark the location in my records, would return periodically to observe the slow process of decay and reincorporation. If injured, I would do nothing—there is no helping, no intervention in that sense—but I would witness, would confirm that the injury occurred, that the Humm persisted or didn't persist, that the ecosystem absorbed this small fluctuation.

I would be the one who talks to Visitors, when they come.

The Attendants can't, quite—they've lost too much variable language, their chest-tones conveying only immediate practical information. But I would retain enough human speech, or could recover it temporarily when needed, to explain to Visitors what this place is. Not to warn them away—that's not my role—but to help them understand what staying means. I would tell them about the slowing, about the settling, about how urgency falls away in layers until you're not sure if you've been here three months or three years and you realize the distinction stopped mattering sometime ago.

Some Visitors would want to help me, would offer to assist with my observations. I would accept, but would give them tasks that teach patience: "Watch this puddle from dawn to dusk and tell me if any Humms arrive." They would sit, would watch, would slowly understand that watching *is* the task, that the watching is as important as what's watched. Some would learn this and begin their own settling. Others would grow restless, would realize they're not ready, would leave with my blessing—a slow nod, a chest-tone that means "go well" or perhaps just "go," the distinction blurred in a language of three dozen sounds.

Some Visitors would stay longer, would become assistants of a sort, learning my methods. We would develop a simple shared language of gesture and tone. They would take over some observations while I expanded to new territory, or deepened focus on particular phenomena. Eventually they might establish their own dwellings, their own territories of attention, becoming other Keepers. Or they would complete the settling, would slow down entirely and stop, and I would mark their location in my records and watch their bodies become landscape, confirming their transition with the same patient attention I bring to everything else.

I would have learned to subsist on very little—bacterial paste once every few days, water collected from engine condensation, perhaps some mineral content absorbed through skin contact with the substrate, some energy drawn from the engine vibrations themselves. My needs would have simplified along with everything else. Sleep would come in short periods, whenever needed, a brief cessation of observation followed by resumption. Dreams, if they occurred, would be simple: images of engines, of puddles, of Humms tracing their circuits, the same scenes I witnessed while waking but slightly different, the dreaming mind testing variations, exploring what-ifs within the narrow parameters this place permits.

I would grow old slowly, or perhaps not old at all—perhaps the settling prevents aging, or slows it so much it becomes imperceptible. My joints would stiffen not from arthritis but from optimization, from finding the most efficient angles and wearing grooves into their operation until they moved smoothly only along specific paths. My vision would remain sharp—vision is necessary here, is tool rather than sense—but focused at the distances I use most: the middle distance where engines stand, the close focus for examining bacterial mats and tube-forms.

Decades would pass, marked not by years but by longer cycles: the time for this engine to develop that pattern of surface corrosion. The time for that tube-form to grow from emergence to maturity to collapse. The time for a generation of Humms to be born and molt and die and be replaced by the next generation doing exactly the same things in exactly the same places. I would maintain my records, the wall-scratches becoming a archive of deep time, a record of stability so perfect that change, when it finally occurred, would be immediately visible against the baseline.

And perhaps one day I would notice something the Attendants couldn't see because they're too embedded in function, that Visitors couldn't see because they lack duration: a pattern operating at a scale beyond individual lifetimes. Perhaps the engines themselves are slowly changing, their tones descending fractionally over generations, approaching some final frequency. Perhaps the tube-forms are gradually migrating, new ones appearing slightly north of where old ones collapse, the entire phenomenon creeping across the landscape at geological pace. Perhaps the Humms are evolving—that unusually thick carapace I noted wasn't anomaly but adaptation, and over centuries they're becoming more armored, or more efficient, or more something.

I would mark these observations carefully, knowing I won't live to see their completion, but trusting that the record will persist. Perhaps another Keeper will come after, will read my wall-scratches, will continue the observation I began. Or perhaps not—perhaps the observations matter only in the moment of observation, perhaps the record is less important than the act of recording, the attention less important than the attending.

I would become, over time, a feature of this landscape as much as the Attendants or Humms: the one who walks the long circuits, who crouches beside puddles, who stands for hours observing engines. Gliders would recognize me, would adjust their flight paths slightly to pass overhead when I was in certain locations, perhaps drawn by the consistency of my presence. Humms would feed unbothered by my proximity, having learned I was observer rather than threat. Attendants would include me in their calculations, their choreography of maintenance accounting for my position the way it accounted for tube-forms and puddles—as permanent variable, as expected presence.

And late in life—whenever that came, however that was measured—I would begin preparing my final record. I would use the best tools I'd developed over decades, would create one clear, comprehensive map: the engines numbered and positioned, their tones noted, their conditions described. The tube-form fields marked. The puddles mapped. The Humm territories outlined. The Glider patterns traced. Everything I'd learned about intervals and cycles and the deep slow patterns underlying this place's operation.

I would place this record in my dwelling in a location where it would be protected from rain but visible to any who entered. Not as legacy—legacy implies ego, implies desire for remembrance, and I would have shed that long ago—but as data. As completion of observation: the watching confirmed through recording, the recording confirmed through placement, the placement confirmed through potential future witness.

And then I would walk to a favorite location—perhaps beside the engine with the lowest tone, the one that resonated in my chest like a second heartbeat—and I would sit. I would attend one final time, confirming through sustained observation that this engine runs, that this place exists, that the intervals continue. And slowly, as slowly as everything else here happens, I would stop. Not dying exactly, but completing. Reaching the terminal stillness that all things here eventually reach. Becoming still enough to hear everything: every engine, every tube-tone, every Humm-scrape, every Glider's passage, the subsonic scratch of Crevice-Dwellers, the sound of minerals settling in microscopic increments, the hum of existence itself.

And I would remain there, sitting, present, patient, adding my silence to the landscape's soundscape, becoming one more feature for future Keepers to observe and mark in their records: "Here sits one who attended. Interval from last observation to this: continuing."

The engines would hum. The steam would rise. The cycle would persist. And I would be, finally, completely settled—not gone, but arrived at the final state all attention seeks when given sufficient duration: pure witness, permanent observation, eternal present confirmed through presence that no longer distinguishes between observer and observed, between what watches and what is watched, between life and the patience that makes life possible in places where time has learned to stand almost, almost still.
