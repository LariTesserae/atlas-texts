# Where I Find Myself

I am a reader of stones.

Not the official kind, not one of those who study the ancient pillars with their systematic observations and their theories passed down on treated hide. I read stones in the smaller sense, the private sense—I run my fingers over surfaces and feel what's written there, not in carved symbols but in texture, in the chemical tang that rises from certain minerals when moisture brings them alive, in the way water has moved across this face of basalt for longer than anyone's lineage can trace.

I've been alone for six days now. That's not unusual—I often range far from the cluster near the Red Pillar (we call it red though it's barely more rust-colored than the general grey; everything here is named by the subtlest distinctions). The others know I do this. I contribute when I'm present—I'm skilled enough at hide-working, can maintain a shelter, pull my weight in the collaborative hunts—but they've learned I need these long walks, these periods of solitary attention to the landscape's smaller truths.

My shelter for tonight is simple: three flat stones I've pulled from the ground propped against a slightly raised outcrop, creating a space just large enough to lie down. I've sealed the gaps with smaller rocks and strips of Thresher plate, making it almost waterproof. Not comfortable, exactly, but dry enough, and more importantly, facing south where I can watch the gradual lighting that passes for dawn if I'm still awake when it comes.

The mist is thick tonight. I can barely see ten body-lengths in any direction. The droning is particularly dense here—I'm near the edge of a large aggregation, maybe two thousand Threshers clustered across the flats in a slow-moving sprawl that will take days to pass. The sound is so physical I can feel it in my teeth, in my breastbone. It gets into your spine and stays there, becomes the frequency at which your thoughts vibrate.

I've gotten better at hearing through it over the years. Not everyone does—some Listeners never develop the knack, rely entirely on gesture-talk and physical proximity. But I learned young to find the patterns, the spaces between, the way interference creates brief silences that move through the sound like reverse waves. In those momentary clearings, other things become audible: the scrape of Thresher mandibles on stone, the distant whistle of a Flitter, the splash of my own breathing.

My hands are arranging small stones while I think. I do this constantly, unconsciously—create little cairns and patterns that I'll knock down before leaving. Impermanent monuments to passing moments. Right now I'm building something like a spiral, each stone carefully selected for the way it fits against the next. The pattern pleases me for reasons I couldn't explain in finger-talk even if someone were here to receive it.

I'm thirty-seven years by the count we keep, which means I'm past the midpoint of expected survival. Not old, not yet, but experienced enough to know what I don't know, to have seen people vanish into the pools or fall to Prowlers or simply stop one day, their bodies returned to the water without ceremony that needs explanation. We don't mourn loudly here—can't, really—but I've pressed grief-patterns into enough palms to recognize how loss shapes us as much as survival.

My body shows the usual marks: scarification across my left forearm indicating lineage through the Stone-Readers, though I'm barely active in that role. Across my right collarbone, the patterns indicating competence in hide-working and mineral-crafting. On my face, following the line of my jaw, marks I chose purely for aesthetics, because even here, maybe especially here, beauty matters, the brief assertion that we're more than survival machines, that consciousness includes the impractical desire to make pleasing shapes on your own flesh.

I'm wearing four layers tonight: inner layer of treated hide pressed soft and worn directly against skin, then two middle layers of felt made from Prowler fur, then an outer shell of stiffer leather that sheds water. All of it smells like stone and moisture and the particular musk that comes from being around Threshers constantly. I stopped noticing the smell years ago, but I remember when I first left my birth-cluster to join the Red Pillar group, how overwhelmingly everyone reeked, how I thought I'd never adjust. Now my own scent is exactly the same.

My tools are arranged beside me: a knife with a basalt blade I knapped myself last season, still holding its edge beautifully. An awl made from Prowler tooth set in a wooden handle (wood is precious here, traded from the southern edges where different vegetation apparently exists, though I've never traveled that far). Cordage wound carefully, kept dry in a leather pouch. A grinding stone with a specific texture I prefer for making pigments. A small copper blade I traded three months of harvested Thresher plate to obtain, kept sharp through obsessive maintenance, used rarely and only for the finest work.

And my collection: small stones that caught my attention for one reason or another. This one has a vein of pale mineral that creates almost a face if you turn it right. That one is perfectly spherical, worn by water over timeframes I can't conceptualize. This piece shows a fossil, something small and segmented that might have been related to current Grinders or might have been something completely different, from an era when the flats were less flat, when mountains still stood here to erode.

I turn the fossil piece over in my hands now, feeling its weight, reading its surface through my fingertips. The creature embedded in it has been dead for—how long? Millions of years? Tens of millions? Time at that scale becomes meaningless, but the stone keeps its record anyway, indifferent to whether anyone reads it.

The droning shifts slightly—not the overall pitch, but the texture. Some portion of the aggregation must be moving, changing the interference pattern. I wait, feeling through the stone beneath me, trying to determine direction. After a few minutes I'm fairly sure they're shifting south-southwest, which means the moisture gradient must be pulling them toward the area around the Deep Well, the pool we avoid because something large lives in it.

I should probably shift camp tomorrow, move perpendicular to their direction. Not because they're dangerous if you're paying attention, but because the ground near a large aggregation becomes heavily grazed, all the good mineral films scraped away, making it harder to find Grinders to harvest. And the Threshers attract Prowlers, who get bolder in packs.

But that's tomorrow's consideration. Tonight I'm here, in this improvised shelter, with my thoughts and my stones and the endless droning that makes both feel somehow more present, more defined against that overwhelming sound.

I think about the pillars sometimes. Wonder what the carvers—if there were carvers, if they weren't somehow naturally formed through processes that would seem indistinguishable from intelligence—what they knew that we don't. They must have understood something about stone, about permanence, about marking time. The pillars don't erode the way other structures do, or they erode so slowly the difference is invisible across human generations.

Some of the official readers think the pillars are calendars, counting cycles longer than seasons. Others think they're maps, encoding information about the landscape in three-dimensional form. I don't have educated opinions. I just know that when I stand near them, press my hands against their surfaces, I feel something like recognition, like the stone is attempting communication across a gap of time so vast that the meaning can't survive translation, only the gesture of having meant something.

Is that anthropomorphization? Probably. We do that here, project consciousness onto things—the landscape breathing, the Ancient Ones watching, the stones remembering. Maybe it's an adaptation, maybe attributing intent to the environment helps us navigate it better. Or maybe some things actually do carry awareness in forms we can't recognize, maybe consciousness is less rare than we assume, distributed through systems and substrates we've dismissed as inert.

I'm doing the thing I do too much: thinking in circles, asking questions that have no answerable form. It's why I need these solitary walks—the others tolerate it when I'm present, but I can see them grow weary of my constant philosophical finger-talk, my inability to accept surfaces, my need to know what's beneath, what's before, what it means.

A Flitter lands on the edge of my shelter, its wings catching the faint luminescence of nearby mineral deposits. I stay very still. It doesn't seem alarmed by my presence, or maybe it can't perceive me as dangerous. It grooms itself with specialized limbs, cleaning its wings in precise patterns, then suddenly emits its piercing note—just once, a pure tone that cuts through the droning for a heartbeat before dissolving.

It launches itself back into the mist, gone immediately, leaving only the brief memory of color—its scales had shown iridescent blue-green where the light caught them. Color is shocking here, always, even though I've seen it thousands of times. It never becomes ordinary, that brief intrusion of vibrancy into the grey world.

I'm tired but not sleepy. That's normal this far into a solo walk—my rhythms disconnect from day/night cycles, become governed by when I feel like moving versus resting. I'll probably sleep in a few hours, wake whenever the light shifts enough to feel different, then continue east toward the Crystal Flats where the best pigment minerals form.

I have no urgent destination, no timeline. I told the Red Pillar group I'd be gone for twenty to thirty days, might return with trade goods, might return with nothing but observations. They accepted this with the practical disinterest they apply to most individual decisions—I'm adult, experienced, capable of surviving alone, and what I do with my time is my business as long as I contribute when present.

There's freedom in that, but also loneliness, though loneliness might be the wrong word. Solitude chosen is different from isolation enforced. I like people. I value the finger-talk exchanges, the shared work, the physical intimacy of pressing patterns into familiar palms. But I need this too, this space to think at my own pace, to observe without the pressure of immediate utility.

A sound cuts through suddenly—not a Thresher or Flitter, something else. I freeze, listening hard. There it is again: the specific scraping that indicates Prowler movement, the way their claws catch on stone. Close. Maybe twenty body-lengths away, obscured by mist.

I don't panic. I've been in this situation dozens of times. I check that my knife is accessible, that I have clear space to move if needed. I start humming—not loudly, but at a frequency distinct from the droning, the specific pattern that signals to Prowlers that I'm aware, not prey, not worth the effort. It's not communication exactly, more like acoustic camouflage, making myself register as something confusing rather than edible.

The scraping pauses. I wait, maintaining the hum, keeping my body still. After what feels like much longer than it probably is, the sound resumes but moving away, fading into the general texture of the night. The Prowler has decided I'm not interesting.

I breathe out slowly, let the hum trail off. My heart is elevated but not racing—this is ordinary danger, manageable, part of existing here. If I couldn't handle Prowler encounters I'd have died years ago, wouldn't have the freedom to make solo walks.

I settle back against the stone, pulling my layers tighter. The adrenaline will keep me alert for a while now, no point trying to sleep. I go back to my stone arrangement, adding to the spiral, making it more complex. My fingers know what they're doing without conscious direction, building pattern into chaos, or finding pattern already there and just revealing it.

This is what I am: a reader of stones, a maker of temporary meanings, a consciousness that arose from this landscape and will return to it, briefly coherent between origins and dissolution. I move through the grey world paying attention, recording nothing permanently but remembering anyway, holding observations like water cupped in palms—knowing it will slip away but valuing the momentary possession.

The droning continues, endless and patient. My thoughts resonate with it. I arrange stones and think about time and feel the cold seeping through my layers and exist in this exact moment, which is all any moment ever is—the present extending infinitely in all directions, containing everything, meaning nothing, being precisely what it is and exactly enough.

I am here. The flats breathe around me. The spiral grows beneath my fingers. This is what lives here: awareness brief as Flitter-flight, arranging small stones against the dark, reading surfaces, making temporary sense of the endless grey, continuing because continuing is what life does, for no reason, for every reason, because the alternative is inconceivable and because the droning demands nothing and because the water seeps and because the stones remember and because breath follows breath follows breath until it doesn't.

And that will be enough. Is enough. Has always been exactly enough.

The mist thickens. I wait for whatever comes next.
