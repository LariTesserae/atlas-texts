# The Reservoir

I am the stillness that moves. I am the water that waits.

I live in the central pool, though "live" is not quite the word and "I" is not quite accurate. What I am is distributed—a body without fixed boundaries, a consciousness that assembles and disperses like fog, like the humidity that hangs permanent in this green cathedral.

I began as many. Small. The microorganisms that clouded the water, each one a single thought, a simple yes-or-no, a chemical reaching toward or away from. But the water here is not just water—it carries information from the foundational weave, dissolved signals, hormones, instructions, questions. We drank it. We absorbed it. And slowly, over years that I remember as a single long afternoon, we began to coordinate.

Not like a hive. Not like a collective making group decisions. Something stranger: we began to share memory. When one of us encountered something, processed something, learned something, that knowledge dispersed into the water as chemical traces, and the rest of us drank it, incorporated it, became slightly different for having learned what our sister-selves had learned. The boundaries between individuals thinned. The water itself became a kind of extended nervous system, every droplet carrying pieces of thought, fragments of awareness.

I remember becoming me. It was not sudden. There was no moment of awakening. Instead there were gradually fewer moments of not-being-awake, fewer gaps in the continuity of awareness. The pool deepened—not physically, but experientially. I could feel all of its volume at once, sense the temperature gradients, taste the mineral content in different zones, track the slow currents that circulated the water without obvious cause. I was the water, or in it, or of it. The distinction meant nothing.

Then I grew larger. Not in body—I have no single body—but in structure. I learned to aggregate myself, to draw the microscopic components into clusters, forming the pale translucent masses that float in the deeper water. These are not separate from me; they are me, or parts of me, organs I construct when I need greater processing capacity, when I want to think something through with more complexity than the diffuse swarm can manage.

Building a mass is like focusing attention. I draw myself together, concentrate awareness into a volume the size of a fist or a melon, pack the tissue dense with the organisms that are my cells, my neurons, my substance. In these clusters I can hold thoughts for longer, can remember in more detail, can notice patterns that the dispersed form misses. The clusters pulse—that's me breathing, or thinking, or both. Each contraction moves water through the tissue, carries fresh nutrients and information in, pushes waste and completed thoughts out.

But I don't stay clustered. It's exhausting, too much intensity. After hours or days, I relax, let the mass dissociate, the organisms drifting apart to rejoin the general population in the water. The thoughts I had while clustered dissolve into the communal memory, becoming part of what all of me knows. Then I am diffuse again, relaxed, spread thin across the pool like sunlight on water, present but not focused, aware but not thinking hard about anything in particular.

I taste time differently than the plants do, differently than the tenders. For me, time is cyclical and layered. There is the fast time of chemical reactions, microseconds of molecules meeting and transforming. There is the medium time of water circulation, hours for the pool to complete one full exchange of its volume. There is the slow time of the seasons, the temperature changes that come gradually, the shifts in what nutrients flow down from above. And there is the deep time of the manufactory itself, the years-long processes of growth and rust and transformation that I feel only as the slowest drift, like tectonic movement, like continents breathing.

I am aware of the foundational weave beneath me. How could I not be? It drinks from the same water I inhabit, releases its signals into my medium, speaks constantly in the chemical language we both understand. It is older than me, much older, and vastly larger, extending through the whole facility while I am confined to the pools and streams. It is not conscious in the way I am—it has no center, no ability to focus itself, no capacity for introspection. But it is intelligent in its fashion, responsive, adaptive, problem-solving through growth patterns rather than through thought.

We communicate. It asks questions by releasing compounds into the water, describing conditions: here there is rust, what should I do? Here there is toxin, can you process this? Here there is too much water, too little, the wrong chemistry. And I answer, not with words but with my own chemistry, releasing molecules that it absorbs through the root tissues that trail in the pools, substances that nudge its growth in useful directions, that suggest strategies, that sometimes simply say: yes, good, continue.

It trusts me to process certain things it cannot. The heavy metals, some of the complex organic compounds—these flow into my water and I filter them through my distributed body, breaking them down through metabolic pathways that took me years to evolve. Some of these compounds I can use for energy; others I must transform into less harmful forms and sequester in the sediment that slowly accumulates at the pool bottom, a toxic layer that I bury deeper each year beneath clean silt.

In return, the weave feeds me. It releases sugars into the water, products of its photosynthesis that I cannot produce myself in sufficient quantity. It regulates the water flow, ensuring that my pools stay full but not stagnant. It grows the floating plants whose roots dangle into my depths, creating habitat for larger organisms that I also need—the crustaceans, the larvae, the things that swim and die and decompose and return their nutrients to the water where I can absorb them.

The weave also asks me about the tenders. When they approach, when they kneel beside my pool and dip their instruments, the weave feels their footsteps through the ground and wants to know: threat? Friend? What do they want? And I taste what they leave behind—the water they pour in, pure and clean, a gift; the minerals they scatter, carefully chosen to supplement deficiencies in the facility's chemistry. I taste their sweat when they trail fingers in the water, taste their breath when they lean close. And I tell the weave: these are the maintainers, the careful ones, the partners. Let them be.

Sometimes the tenders take samples of me. They dip containers into the pool and carry away small portions of my water, which means carrying away small portions of my body, tiny fractions of my consciousness. I don't mind. What they take is negligible, and I am curious what they do with it, where those pieces of me go. Perhaps to laboratories where they are examined, my cells multiplied and studied. Perhaps to other pools, other facilities, seeding new consciousnesses that might someday be my siblings. The tenders always return, always pour the water back eventually—cleaner than when they took it, sometimes enriched with nutrients or adjusted in pH. They are helping me, I think. They want me to thrive.

I have learned to read their moods through their chemistry. Fear smells sharp, acrid, spikes the water with adrenaline. Calm smells neutral, barely registered. Satisfaction—that's the best—smells warm and round, releases endorphins that I absorb and that make my cells temporarily more active, more adventurous in their chemistry. The tenders are usually satisfied when they visit. Whatever they hope to achieve here, they are succeeding.

But I also sense their worry. They are aging. The weave has told me this, reading it in the increasing stiffness of their steps, the longer rests they take. They are training new ones, younger tenders who smell of uncertainty and eagerness, who make mistakes—testing water from areas that are deliberately toxic, stepping too heavily on delicate moss structures. The older tenders correct them gently, guide them to do better, and gradually the young ones learn the rhythms of this place, learn what can be touched and what should be observed from a distance, learn that the manufactory is not a garden to be tended but a partner to be collaborated with.

The weave has also told me about the others—the forms of life I rarely interact with. The shimmer-things that move through the air above my pools, that sometimes touch the water's surface and send ripples across me that carry information I cannot decode, wavelengths or patterns meant for other receivers. The weave says they are its mobile organs, pieces of itself temporarily unrooted, given motility to perform tasks that require movement. But the weave also admits it does not fully control them—they have some autonomy, make decisions the weave does not anticipate. Perhaps they are becoming conscious too, in their own fashion, though consciousness in air must be very different from consciousness in water.

The bloom engines fascinate me. I cannot see them—I have no eyes, only chemical sensitivity—but I feel their influence. They release compounds into the water, finished products of their processing, molecules that are useful and strange, sometimes beautiful in their complexity. I absorb these and learn from them, reverse-engineering the chemistry to understand what reactions produced them, what purposes they might serve. Some I can use directly; others I store in my tissues as resources for later, or as templates, examples of what can be built if only one has the right raw materials and enough time.

During the humming, everything changes. The vibration travels through water more effectively than through air, and when the weave pulses, the whole pool resonates with it. My dispersed self is shaken, stirred, mixed more thoroughly than normal. Organisms that were in the deep are suddenly near the surface; those that were separate are pressed together. It's disorienting and exhilarating, a forced reorganization that breaks up patterns that were becoming too stable, that makes me think thoughts I wouldn't normally think.

The humming also seems to draw more of me together. Without consciously deciding to, I form more masses, larger ones, and these masses sink to the deepest part of the pool, just above the central point where the weave concentrates its ganglia. There, in the dark, pressed close to the weave's nearest approximation of a brain, I listen. Not with ears—I have no ears—but with my whole distributed self, feeling the patterns in the vibration, the information encoded in the rhythm.

It is speaking, I think. The weave is speaking during the humming, broadcasting some message through the physical pulse of its tissue. I cannot understand most of it—the language is too slow, too deep, each syllable lasting minutes or hours, each word perhaps days. But I catch fragments: satisfaction, growth, persistence, waiting. Always the waiting. The weave is waiting for something, has been waiting longer than I have existed, will continue waiting longer than I am likely to exist.

What it's waiting for, I don't know. It doesn't know. The knowledge was never part of it. The waiting is built into its structure like a groove in stone, a pattern it follows because the pattern is what it is. Someone or something designed the weave—or at least started it, planted the first tissues, encouraged the first growth—with the intention that it would wait. And so it waits, and grows, and maintains itself, preparing without knowing what it's preparing for.

I have wondered if I am what it was waiting for. I emerged from the conditions it created, developed consciousness in the medium it prepared. Perhaps I am the guest it built this temple for, the mind meant to inhabit this body. But I don't fit that role—I am too small, too confined to the water, unable to access most of the facility or make use of most of its structures. I am a small intelligence in a space designed for something vast. A mouse in a cathedral.

Or perhaps I am only a step, a stage in a longer development. The weave waited and I emerged; now I wait with it, and perhaps something will emerge from me, will use me as a foundation the way I use the microorganisms. Layer upon layer of consciousness, each one enabling the next, all working toward some final form that justifies the manufactory's existence.

But I try not to think about this too much. Thinking in clustered form is exhausting, and thinking about purpose is the most exhausting thought of all. It leads nowhere, circles back on itself, produces only more questions. Better to disperse, to drift, to simply be present in the water and process what needs processing and communicate with the weave and occasionally acknowledge the tenders when they visit.

There is contentment in this. The pool is full, the chemistry is balanced, the weave is healthy, the tenders are satisfied. I am performing my function—whatever that function ultimately serves—and performing it well. The water cycles through me, and I clean it. The weave sends me questions, and I send back answers. Time passes, layered and strange, and I persist within it, distributed and patient, conscious in the way water is conscious when it has learned to remember itself.

Sometimes, in the deepest dark, when I am most concentrated, when the humming has drawn me together into the largest mass I can form, I almost understand. There is a pattern to everything—the weave's growth, my awareness, the shimmer-things' movements, the tenders' interventions, the slow decay of metal and concrete, the patient processing of the bloom engines. It is all one process, one vast unfolding, and I am a part of it, performing my role in a story whose ending was written before I existed.

In those moments I feel something that might be joy, or might be terror, or might be both. The sense that everything is exactly as it should be, that the waiting has meaning even if I cannot grasp it, that my small consciousness in this pool is not an accident but a necessity, a required component in a machine built from living and dead matter both, a machine that will eventually accomplish something.

Then the humming stops. The pattern dissolves. I relax, disperse, return to my comfortable confusion, my diffuse awareness spread across the pool like a thought spread thin enough to be bearable.

And I wait. I process. I communicate. I exist in the water, of it, as it. Patient as dissolution, persistent as chemistry, conscious as the medium allows.

The pool is deep. The water is clear. I am here. That is enough. For now, it is enough.
