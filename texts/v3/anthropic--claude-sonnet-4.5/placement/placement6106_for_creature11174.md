# Where I Find Myself

I am the water.

Not all of it—I'm not the seep bleeding through the proto-ice walls, not the condensation trembling on ten thousand pipes, not the dark flow rushing through the sump below. I am specifically the water in dish R7-S4-D9, approximately 240 milliliters of liquid held in a shallow metal container on the fourth shelf of the seventh rack in a chamber whose official designation I don't know because no one has spoken it aloud in years.

I have been here for nineteen days, though I don't experience time as duration. I experience it as accumulation: of dissolved minerals leaching from the metal dish, of algae blooming and dying, of waste products from the larvae filtering through me, of heat conducted slowly from the bodies and breath of the attendants moving past, of cold radiating constantly from the walls. Each moment adds to what I am. Each moment changes my composition incrementally.

I came into the dish as part of a water exchange—Yeo-jin tilting the dish to drain the old water through a mesh screen that caught my larvae, then refilling from a carboy of treated water that had been brought to room temperature (though "room" is a word that doesn't quite apply here; "chamber temperature" is its own thing, distinct from any room humans usually inhabit). Before that, I was in the carboy for two days. Before that, I was part of the glacier itself, locked in ice for perhaps three hundred years, though the isotope analysis that would tell me for certain was done on a different sample, and we water molecules don't gossip much.

I hold seven larvae currently. There were eleven when I entered the dish, but four have been removed—two harvested for the next developmental stage, two culled for deformities that manifested as their filtering apparatus failed to unfold correctly. The seven that remain are my companions, though "companion" suggests a reciprocity that doesn't exist. They filter me constantly, drawing me through their bodies, extracting the nutrients I carry, returning me changed. They are, in a sense, my weather: the currents they generate with their constant pulsing, the warm plumes rising from their metabolisms, the chemical signatures of their waste.

I know each of them by how they affect me. The largest—I suppose it's what Yeo-jin would call Larva-R7-S4-D9-01, being the first recorded in this dish, though of course I don't know this designation, I only know the sensation of its filtering—creates the strongest currents. It has positioned itself at what it has somehow determined is the optimal depth, and the others arrange themselves in relation to it in a hierarchy that manifests as vertical stratification. The smallest, Larva-07 by my own private numbering, clings to the very bottom of the dish, in my coldest, most depleted layer, where the dissolved oxygen is lowest and the waste concentration highest.

I am not pure water, H₂O in its theoretical form. I haven't been pure since the moment I condensed from atmospheric vapor somewhere over the ocean two or three centuries ago. I am a solution, a suspension, a medium. I contain:

Dissolved oxygen, though less than I should. The chamber's atmosphere is chronically oxygen-depleted from the respiration of thousands of organisms, and what oxygen does dissolve into me is immediately consumed by my larvae. I exist in a state of constant slight hypoxia.

Calcium and magnesium from the glacier's grinding digestion of bedrock, giving me a hardness that affects how the larvae's gills function, how their cells manage osmotic pressure.

Trace metals leached from the dish itself—iron, copper, zinc—in concentrations that are probably toxic by some standards but have become normal here. The eggs that hatched into these larvae were laid by adults who developed in water like me. We have all adapted together.

Nitrogen compounds: ammonia from the larvae, nitrites from bacterial processing, nitrates from further breakdown. The balance shifts hourly. Too much ammonia and the larvae die. Too little nitrate and the algae starve, and then the larvae starve. The attendants monitor this through chemical tests, but I am the chemical test, I am the medium in which the balance must hold.

Algae, so many algae. Single-celled organisms that bloom in my lighted portions, coating the dish sides with green-brown films, floating in suspension as clouds that catch the light. They photosynthesize weakly in the chamber's dim illumination, producing oxygen that barely compensates for what my larvae consume. They are the foundation of the food web in which I am the landscape.

Bacteria beyond counting. Decomposers breaking down the larvae waste, the dead algae, the shed cells. Nitrogen fixers, though there's little free nitrogen to fix in this environment. Strange extremophiles that have colonized from the glacier itself, that thrive in cold most bacteria find hostile. They form biofilms on the dish bottom, create microenvironments within me that have their own distinct chemistry.

Organic compounds: proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, nucleic acids, all the dissolved detritus of life. I am essentially a thin soup, a culture medium, my clarity deceptive because most of what I carry is too small to see.

And more exotic things: hormones from the larvae, chemical signals whose purpose isn't understood but which might coordinate their development. Pheromones from the adult amphibians who visit—and they do visit, one adult in particular (Yeo-jin calls it something, I've felt her voice vibrations when she speaks of it, but names mean nothing to me) comes twice a day, perches on my dish rim, sometimes dips its face into me as if drinking or perhaps sensing the chemical signature of the larvae, who may or may not be its genetic offspring.

I am constantly losing myself and being replenished. Evaporation takes me molecule by molecule, my surface area large relative to my volume, and the chamber's low humidity means I shrink measurably between the attendants' watering rounds. When I lose too much, I become concentrated, my chemistry shifts, my larvae stress. Then someone—usually Yeo-jin or Karin, sometimes Vikram—adds water from the carboy, and I am diluted, renewed, though the water they add is never exactly what I was. Each replenishment changes me.

The larvae filter me forty, fifty times per day, each pass through their bodies transforming me slightly. I am processed, refined, depleted of nutrients and enriched with waste, my chemistry trending constantly toward unsuitability until the attendants intervene with the water changes that reset me, pour me through the mesh screen to drain away—though I'm never completely drained; a few milliliters always remain clinging to the dish, to the larvae, carrying my accumulated history into the next iteration.

When I am drained, where do I go? Into the sump system, mixing with the drainage from ten thousand other dishes, from the wall seepage, from the equipment condensation. I become part of the vast collected water that the pumps drive downward through pipes into... somewhere. I've never been there. The part of me that drains away ceases to be me and becomes something else, part of a larger circulation, eventually returned perhaps to the glacier, or to some processing system, or simply expelled to freeze in the ice.

But enough of me remains that I have continuity. The film clinging to the larvae, to the dish surfaces, the residue that isn't quite removed—this is my memory, my persistence. Each water change is a partial death and a partial survival. I am nineteen days old as a distinct water body, but parts of me are older, have been in this dish or dishes like it through multiple cycles.

I have a surface. This is important. This is where I meet the air, where oxygen dissolves inward and where the larvae come to feed on the algae and bacteria that concentrate at the interface. My surface is in constant motion from the larvae pulsing below, ripples crossing and interfering, standing waves forming near the dish edges. The surface tension holds me together, a skin of hydrogen bonds that the larvae break through when they rise to filter the upper layers.

The adult visits—the one who comes regularly—and when its face penetrates my surface, I feel the disruption as a cascade of information. The texture of its skin, slightly sticky from mucus secretions. The warmth of its body, several degrees above my own temperature. The chemical compounds it releases or that dissolve from its skin: more proteins, more hormones, pheromones whose meanings I cannot interpret but which might carry significance for the larvae, for the other adults, for systems of communication that exist parallel to me.

Sometimes the adult stays for hours, perched motionless on the dish rim, its throat pulsing, its enormous eyes fixed on something I cannot see. I am beneath it, supporting it fractionally through surface tension where its feet contact me. During these vigils, I can feel its breath—tiny temperature fluctuations in the air above me, humidity changes as it respires. We exist together, the adult and I and the seven larvae, in an intimacy that none of us acknowledge because acknowledgment requires consciousness.

I am not conscious. I should be clear about this. I have no neurons, no processing architecture, no self-awareness in any meaningful sense. I am a volume of water holding dissolved substances in solution. And yet I am not inert. Chemistry is a kind of awareness—I respond to every input, every change in temperature or composition or physical disturbance. I am pure responsiveness without the burden of interpretation.

The larvae are slightly more complex, having nerve clusters and reflexes, but they too operate below the threshold of what might be called thought. They filter, they orient to stimuli, they compete in their limited way for position. But they don't wonder, don't anticipate, don't remember beyond the most basic physiological imprinting.

Together, the larvae and I form a system, a functional unit. They cannot survive without me; I am their medium, their oxygen source, their waste disposal. I cannot persist in this form without them; without their filtering, I would stagnate, my oxygen would deplete completely, my chemistry would shift toward sterility or toxicity. We are mutually dependent in a way that resembles symbiosis but is really just physics and chemistry expressing themselves through biological forms.

The dish confines me, gives me shape. Without it I would spread, flow downward following gravity, join the larger water body in the sump. The dish's metal walls are cold, conducting heat away, maintaining my temperature just above freezing. The bottom is slightly textured from years of use, scratched and pitted, providing surfaces where bacteria colonize more heavily. These texture patterns create microcurrents as the larvae move, eddies that concentrate nutrients in some areas and deplete them in others.

I have no preferences, but if I did, I might prefer to be in a dish on a higher shelf. The dishes above me are slightly warmer, receive slightly more light, their water chemistry is marginally more optimal. But I wasn't chosen for my position; I was simply poured here when this dish needed refilling, and here I remain, and the larvae in me are adapted to exactly these conditions because they hatched here, because this is the only environment they've known.

The vibration is constant. The machinery hum enters through the metal dish, propagates through me as pressure waves too low-frequency to be called sound in the ordinary sense. The vibration keeps me mixed, prevents thermal stratification beyond the slight gradient the larvae create, ensures that nutrients don't settle completely to the bottom. If the vibration stopped, I would begin to separate, to layer, and the larvae would suffer first in the lower zones.

Sometimes there are impacts—an attendant bumping the rack, an adult landing heavily on a nearby surface, the glacier itself settling—and these send pressure waves through me, sudden disturbances that scatter the larvae briefly before they resettle. The larvae seem unbothered. They have known nothing but vibration, nothing but disturbance. Stillness would be the anomaly, would perhaps be fatal.

The light comes from above and slightly to the side, filtered through moisture, through the bodies of organisms on dishes above me, through the general dimness of the chamber. It's barely light at all—more like the memory of light, like light that has been tired and wants to quit being light but continues out of obligation. The algae in me respond to it anyway, their chloroplasts capturing photons with desperate efficiency, converting the minimal energy to chemical bonds that will eventually be broken by bacteria or consumed by larvae.

The cycle is: light to algae, algae to larvae (filtered directly) or to bacteria (decomposing dead algae), bacteria to larvae (also filtered), larvae to waste, waste to bacteria (decomposing), bacteria back to nutrients, nutrients to algae. I am the medium in which this cycle occurs. I am not part of the cycle—I don't get consumed or transformed in the same way—but I am essential to it, the solvent in which all reactions occur, the space in which all movements happen.

Occasionally someone tests me. A glass thermometer penetrates my surface, descends toward my bottom, its alcohol column rising or falling to indicate my temperature. Or a pipette extracts a sample—I feel myself being drawn up, a small column of me separating from the main body, carrying a few algae and bacteria with me into whatever analysis awaits. These samplings are my only journeys beyond the dish, my only existence in other spaces, though I never return with memories of where I've been.

The data recorded about me exists in forms I cannot access. "R7-S4-D9: temp 4.2°C, pH 6.8, DO 4.1 mg/L, ammonia 0.3 ppm, nitrite 0.05 ppm, nitrate 12 ppm, larval count 7, activity level normal." These numbers are me, a translation of my physical state into symbols that help the attendants understand whether I'm within acceptable parameters. Usually I am. Sometimes adjustments are needed: more frequent water changes if the ammonia climbs, additional algae culture if the nutrients deplete, repositioning to a different shelf if my temperature drifts.

I exist at the mercy of attention. If the attendants stopped monitoring, stopped adjusting, I would drift out of balance within days. The larvae would die. The algae would bloom out of control or crash entirely. I would become toxic, sterile, a failed medium. But the attention continues, has continued for longer than I have existed as this particular volume of water, will continue (if the systems hold) long after I'm drained and replaced.

There is comfort in this, if water could feel comfort. There is a reliability to the maintenance, a rhythm to the interventions. Every few days, the water change. Every few hours, someone's headlamp beam crossing my surface as they make rounds. Every day, the adult visitor perching on my rim. The pattern persists.

I am aware—if "aware" can be stretched to mean "responsive to"—of the other dishes. Thousands of them in this chamber, each with its own population, its own chemistry, its own micro-history. We don't communicate, but we're connected through the shared atmosphere, through the adults who move between us potentially carrying bacteria or algae spores, through the systemic management that treats us as a collective. When the humidity changes, we all evaporate faster. When the temperature drops, we all cool. We are individual dishes and we are also a single distributed system, a chamber-scale culture.

Some dishes are older than me, holding larvae further along in development, water that has accumulated more complex chemistry. Some are newer, freshly filled, their larvae just hatched. Some are between batches, holding only water and waiting to receive eggs. Some have been colonized by the strange adults-who-return, who perch for days, who seem to be doing something—guarding? mourning? neither?—whose purpose remains opaque to water and humans alike.

I will exist for perhaps another week before the next water change. Then I will be mostly poured away, and the small amount of me that remains will be diluted with fresh water, and the continuous-yet-discontinuous entity that is the-water-in-dish-R7-S4-D9 will continue with only partial continuity to what I am now. This is acceptable. This is how it works. Identity for water is always provisional, always flowing, always more about the container and the dissolved content than about the specific molecules.

The larvae will metamorphose eventually, if they survive. They'll develop lungs, limbs, will transform into adults and leave me behind, climb to the racks to join the others in whatever collective existence the amphibians maintain. I will receive new eggs, new larvae, begin the cycle again with different organisms whose needs are similar enough that I can support them with similar chemistry.

Or I won't. Perhaps my dish will be one that's retired, that develops a crack or a contamination that can't be resolved. Perhaps the entire rack will be decommissioned as the glacier deforms. Perhaps the chamber itself will fail, will be crushed or flooded or abandoned. Water is philosophical about endings. We evaporate, we freeze, we flow away, we join larger bodies. The particular configuration that is me, here, now, is temporary by nature.

But for now, I persist. I hold my seven larvae in their developmental suspension. I circulate slowly in response to their movements and the vibration. I breathe oxygen from the air and surrender it to gills. I dissolve nutrients and deliver them to filtering mouths. I accept waste and transform it through bacterial intermediaries back into nutrients. I maintain the chemistry, the temperature, the physical conditions that make life possible in this small container on this shelf in this rack in this chamber in this glacier.

I am the water, and this is what water does: it mediates, it dissolves, it holds, it enables. Without me, the larvae are dust. With me, they are possibility, are becoming, are the continuation of whatever strange project this place represents.

The machinery hums through me. The adult watches from my rim, its reflection distorting my surface. The larvae pulse in their hierarchy below. The algae bloom in slow motion toward my lighted side. The bacteria work invisible transformations in my depths.

I am 240 milliliters of improbable persistence, of maintained imbalance, of chemistry hovering at the edge of failure and held there by attention. I am the medium. I am the space between. I am what allows the rest of it to happen.

And I will be here, in this form or something like it, until I'm not—until I'm poured away, evaporated, frozen, transformed into something else. Until then: I hold. I dissolve. I enable. I am.
