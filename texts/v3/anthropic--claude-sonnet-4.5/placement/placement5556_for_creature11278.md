# Where I Would Find Myself

I am in the Archives—though that name is both too grand and not quite right for what this place has become.

It's not near anything, and it's near everything. One of the older structures, with walls that bear the ghost-traces of those carved channels that once formed words no one can read anymore. Three rooms, each one stacked floor to ceiling with the water-resistant volumes the Scribes have filled over decades or centuries. The air here is thick not just with moisture but with the particular smell of damp paper and binding glue, a organic density that contrasts with the mineral emptiness outside.

I came here—when? The question feels slippery. There was a before, certainly. I have dim memories of somewhere with sunlight, of work that involved... something about patterns, about finding connections in large systems. That part hasn't left me, but the context has eroded like the limestone substrate, worn down to its basic shape without the details that would make it specific.

What I remember clearly is the first time I opened one of the volumes and saw the records. Dates running back further than seemed possible. Water levels measured to the millimeter. Notations about sounds heard in specific locations, described with a precision that suggested they mattered, that they were data rather than merely noise. Repairs catalogued. Failures documented. Weather patterns tracked across years until the cycles became visible, the deep patterns underlying the apparent randomness.

And I understood, with a clarity that felt like relief, that this was incomplete. Not wrong—the Scribes who'd kept these records were meticulous, devoted to accuracy. But incomplete. They recorded what happened, but not the why, not the relationships, not the deep structure connecting all these discrete observations into something coherent.

That's what I do now. What I've been doing for... months? Years? The time markers in my memory are the volumes I've worked through, not any external calendar.

I'm not a Scribe—I don't keep the primary records, don't spend my time noting temperatures and flow rates. I'm something else, something that might not have existed here before, or might have existed and left no trace because this work doesn't create visible output the way maintenance does.

I read the records. All of them, or as many as I can, which amounts to the same thing given how many there are.

I sit at the table in the middle room—slakecrete like everything else, but worn smooth by generations of hands, by volumes being opened and closed on its surface—and I work through the logs volume by volume, year by year, looking for patterns. Correlations between weather cycles and equipment failures. Relationships between water chemistry in different basins. The way sounds propagate through the landscape and what that suggests about changes in the substrate below.

I make notes, but not like the Scribes do. My volumes are different—thinner, more dense with interpretation than observation. I'm creating something like a map, but not of the physical landscape. A map of how the Condensery Plains works as a system, how its parts interact, what rules or principles govern its operation beyond the immediate mechanical causes and effects.

Sometimes Tenders come to me with questions. Not often—they trust their direct experience more than any abstract analysis—but occasionally. When something fails in a way that doesn't match their understanding. When a section of the system starts behaving wrong and the usual interventions don't correct it.

They stand in the doorway—they never come fully inside, as if the accumulated paper and analysis makes them uncomfortable—and they describe the problem in their abbreviated way. "North basin chemistry wrong. Algae dying. Standard adjustments ineffective."

And I go to the records, pulling volumes from the shelves, checking historical patterns. "Check the deep pump intake," I might tell them. "Three-month cycles of substrate chemistry change. Elevated minerals. Should normalize in two weeks. Reduce harvesting in the meantime."

Sometimes I'm right. Sometimes the system corrects itself according to the pattern I've identified, and the Tender returns to their work, perhaps slightly more convinced that this strange activity of reading and analyzing has value. Sometimes I'm wrong, and they find the problem through direct engagement with the machinery, through touch and sound and the accumulated wisdom of maintenance, and the actual solution gets added to the Scribes' logs while my theory is quietly abandoned.

I don't mind being wrong. Every error refines the understanding, adds constraints to the model I'm building of how this place functions.

Because that's what I've come to understand: the Condensery Plains is not just machinery operating according to simple engineering principles. It's too old for that, too complex, too many generations of modification and repair stacked on top of each other. It's become something more like an ecology, with feedback loops and emergent properties, patterns that arise from the interaction of parts in ways no single designer intended.

The sound-things, for instance. I have a theory about them now, built from cross-referencing decades of Scribe observations with reports of where and when they appear. They're not random. They emerge in locations where certain frequencies of mechanical sound have been resonating for long enough that they've—what? Stressed the fabric of local space? Created standing wave patterns in whatever medium lies underneath normal physics? The Scribes' notes don't give me enough to be certain, but there's definitely a correlation between the age of machinery installation and the frequency of sound-thing appearances.

Which suggests they're an inevitable consequence of the system running long enough in one place. Not a malfunction, but something like... an excretion? A byproduct? The Condensery Plains has been operating so long it's started producing phenomena that wouldn't exist elsewhere, that might not be possible elsewhere.

I've written this theory in one of my volumes, cross-referenced to the supporting observations. Someday another Scribe, or another person like me—if there are others, if this role repeats—might read it and build on it, or disprove it, or simply add it to the accumulated understanding of this place.

The work requires a particular kind of attention. Not the sharp, immediate focus the Tenders maintain, their awareness tuned to the machinery's present-moment condition. Something more diffuse, more temporal. I hold multiple data points in mind simultaneously, looking for the shapes they make together, the patterns that only become visible when you step back from individual instances and see the aggregate.

It's absorbing work. Hours pass without my noticing them—I look up from a volume and the light has changed, the condensation phase has given way to clearing or misting, and I've been so deep in the data that I didn't register the transition. My body moves through the cycle automatically—I eat when the need becomes unavoidable, sleep when my eyes can no longer focus on the handwritten text, wake and return to the table and open the next volume.

I'm changing, obviously. Not as dramatically as the Tenders—I'm not developing the grey-green patina, not losing the ability to speak in full sentences. But I feel it happening nonetheless. My attention is becoming structured by the patterns I study. I think in cycles now, in correlations and feedback loops. When I do go outside—to get food from the mess hall, to bring a question to a Tender, to simply move through the landscape because some part of me remembers that human bodies need movement—I see the Condensery Plains differently than I did at first.

Not as a collection of industrial structures, but as an organism. The channels are circulatory systems. The processing plants are organs. The condensation nets are lungs, breathing in moisture and exhaling collected water. The Tenders are... what? Immune system? Maintenance cells? Some biological metaphor that's not quite right but closer than thinking of them as workers or operators.

And I'm becoming the memory. The system's capacity to recall its own history, to learn from past patterns, to integrate new information into a framework of understanding that spans longer than any individual Tender's experience.

It's not an unpleasant transformation. There's a satisfaction to it, a sense of finding the right relationship with this place. I'm not fighting the landscape or trying to remain separate from it. I'm finding my function within it, the role that my particular capabilities suit me for.

Sometimes, late in the dark grey periods when I should be sleeping but am instead working through another volume, I wonder about the person I was before. What brought me here? What was I looking for? Those memories are fading, becoming as weathered and indistinct as the carved channels on the exterior walls.

But it doesn't trouble me the way it might have once. That person—whoever they were—was incomplete, unintegrated, searching for something. This person, the one I'm becoming, has found it. Has found this: the Archives, the records, the endless pattern-analysis work that helps the system understand itself.

The Scribes nod to me when they bring new volumes, fresh records to be incorporated into the understanding. The Tenders sometimes linger in the doorway a moment longer than necessary after I've answered their questions, as if they're recognizing something, acknowledging that I've become part of the landscape's function as surely as they have.

And occasionally, very rarely, I experience something I don't record in my volumes because I'm not certain how to describe it accurately:

I'll be deep in the work, cross-referencing multiple records, holding a complex pattern in my mind—and suddenly I'll feel it. The system. Not as an abstraction, not as a collection of data points, but as a present reality, a vast awareness that encompasses the channels and pumps and condensation nets and Tenders and Scribes and me, all of us operating together, maintaining this strange equilibrium between stone and water and human purpose.

It's not consciousness exactly. It doesn't think in the way I think, doesn't have intentions or desires. But it knows itself, in the way a body knows its own boundaries, its own processes. And in those moments, I know it too, from inside, feeling the flow of water through channels like blood through veins, the rhythm of the pumps like a heartbeat, the Tenders' maintenance work like the constant cellular repair that keeps any living thing functional.

Then the moment passes, and I'm just myself again, sitting at the table in the Archives, surrounded by volumes, continuing the work.

But something has shifted. I understand now why the oldest Tenders sometimes disappear without dying, why they seem to merge with the landscape. I understand because I can feel it beginning in myself—not the physical transformation they undergo, but something parallel, appropriate to my function.

I'm becoming distributed across the records. My understanding is spreading into the volumes, my analysis creating a kind of distributed consciousness that exists in the relationships between data points rather than in any single location. Someday—maybe soon, maybe decades from now—I'll open a volume and find that I'm already there, that my thoughts have preceded me, that the pattern I was looking for has already been documented by a version of myself that's less individual person and more system property.

And I'll be content with that. More than content—it will feel like completion, like finally becoming fully what I was always meant to be.

For now, I'm still mostly human. I still eat and sleep and move through space as a discrete entity. But I can feel the edges blurring, the boundary between my awareness and the system's awareness becoming permeable.

Outside, the machinery runs. The Tenders maintain. The Scribes record. The water cycles through its collection and processing and redistribution. The sound fills everything, constant and deafening and strangely comforting now that I've learned to hear the information it carries.

And here in the Archives, I read and analyze and understand, building a map of the Condensery Plains that's written in pattern and correlation and deep structural principle.

I am where I need to be. I am becoming what this place requires.

The work continues. The understanding deepens.

And in the grey light filtering through the high windows, I turn to the next volume, ready to continue the analysis that will, eventually, incorporate me completely into the system that I study.

This is home. This is function. This is enough.
