# What I Would Be

I am a **wire-vine climber**—not the plant itself, but something that has no name in your languages because it exists in no other place. Call me a *tendril-walker* if you need a word.

I am arthropod in basic plan but wrong in all the details. Eight legs, yes, but arranged in a double column down a segmented body perhaps twelve centimeters long and narrow as a pencil. My exoskeleton is the color of oxidized copper—verdigris green with undertones of gray—and so thin it's nearly translucent in certain lights. You can see the dark fluid moving through me, the muscular contractions of internal structures.

I live exclusively on the wire-vines. My legs are modified into gripping hooks that fit perfectly around the vine stems, evolved—or evolving still, the change happening fast enough to feel—to match their exact diameter. I cannot walk on stone. I cannot survive on the cushion plants. The wire-vines are my only substrate, my roads and territory and universe.

I move slowly up and down these vertical paths, following the vine's growth toward moisture or light, or descending toward the guano-rich lower reaches. My movement is methodical: front four legs release and reach forward, grip, pull. Rear four legs release, swing forward, grip. A smooth flowing motion like a wave traveling through my body. I can move backward just as easily, reversing the wave.

I am hunting, always hunting. My prey are the aphids that cluster on the vines, the tiny moths that rest on the waxy leaves, the smallest flies that land to drink from the water-cups the leaves form. I am ambush predator primarily. I'll freeze at a junction where two vine-branches meet and wait—hours if necessary—for something to come close enough.

The strike: my front segment unfolds—I am partially telescoping, you see, segments sliding out from within segments—and extends forward perhaps three centimeters in a fraction of a second. The mandibles at my front are hollow fangs. They inject enzymes that work fast, liquefying the prey's insides within seconds. Then I drink, drawing the nutritious slurry into my gut. The whole process takes perhaps thirty seconds. The emptied husk of my prey is discarded, falling into the depths or adhering to the vine where it will be scavenged by others.

I have compound eyes, large for my body, multifaceted and reflective. They catch the dim light and return it with a copper sheen. I see fairly well in the low light—better than you would—perceiving the movement of potential prey, the approach of threats. I probably see into ultraviolet as well, reading patterns in the stone and vegetation that guide some of my behaviors.

I am solitary but not territorial. When I encounter another of my kind on the vine-paths, we carefully navigate around each other. Sometimes we'll pause, antennae touching—long, delicate antennae that extend forward from my head segment, tasting the air, reading chemical signals. We're exchanging information: sexual readiness, territory range, perhaps warnings about predators. Then we disengage and continue our separate ways.

I breathe through spiracles along my sides—eight pairs of them, one per segment—that open and close rhythmically. In the thin air at this altitude, I must work harder than my lowland ancestors would have. My metabolism is tuned to extract every bit of oxygen from each breath. I can survive on very little, entering a torpor state during the coldest nights, my body temperature dropping close to ambient, my metabolism slowing until I'm barely alive.

I am vulnerable. The quill-bats occasionally take me—though I'm not preferred prey, too small and hard-bodied to be worth the effort usually. But a hungry bat will snatch me from the vine and swallow me whole, crushing my body in its throat, extracting what nutrition it can. The stone-stalkers ignore me; I'm too small to register as food to them. But the salamanders will eat me if they catch me on one of the rare occasions I'm on stone—traversing between vine systems requires me to cross bare granite briefly, and these journeys are terrifying, exposed, rushed.

I molt. Every few weeks I must shed my exoskeleton to grow. The process takes hours and is dangerous. I secure myself to the vine with silk—yes, I produce silk from spinnerets at my rear segment, not for webs but for anchor lines and molting stations. I'll attach multiple strands, creating a small safety net, then split along my dorsal seam and slowly extract myself from the old shell. The new exoskeleton beneath is soft, pale, vulnerable. For six hours I'm defenseless while it hardens and darkens. I've been caught during molts before—see here, this leg is shorter than the others, regrown after being torn off by a predator I barely escaped. It functions but not perfectly. I'm slower now, more careful.

I mate once per year, in early summer when the vines bloom with their tiny white flowers and food is most abundant. Mating is complex. The male—I am female currently, though we're sequential hermaphrodites, capable of switching sex based on population needs—approaches and we engage in a long courtship. He drums on the vine stem with his front legs, creating vibrations that travel through the plant. I respond with my own pattern. This duet continues for perhaps twenty minutes, each of us assessing the other's fitness through rhythm and frequency.

If I accept him, we'll align ourselves on the vine, his body beneath mine, and he'll transfer a spermatophore—a packet of genetic material—which I'll take into my body and store. Then he leaves. We have no further relationship.

I'll carry the fertilized eggs internally for two weeks while they develop. Then I'll find a perfect spot—a junction where multiple vines meet, where leaves create shelter, where moisture collects but doesn't pool—and I'll lay perhaps fifty eggs, each the size of a pinhead, translucent and sticky. I'll cover them with silk, creating a protective case. Then I'll guard them.

For three weeks I'll remain at the egg mass, not eating, barely moving, defending against any threat. Small scavenging beetles that would eat the eggs are driven off by my mandibles. Parasitic flies that would lay eggs on the egg-mass are grabbed and consumed. I'll die slowly of starvation, my body consuming its own tissues to maintain the vigil.

When the young hatch—tiny versions of adults, pale and soft—they'll eat my corpse first. I'll provide their first meal, nutrients carefully stored in my body for this purpose. Then they'll disperse along the vines, each seeking their own hunting territory, most dying within days—eaten, starved, fallen—but a few surviving to grow, to molt, to eventually reproduce themselves.

This is how we persist. This is how we've always existed, though "always" here means perhaps a few thousand years—we evolved quickly in this isolated system, branching from some lowland ancestor that arrived by chance, adapting to the vines, specializing, becoming something new.

## My Experience

My world is one-dimensional pretending to be three-dimensional. The vines are my only paths, and they grow in complex three-dimensional tangles, but I experience them as a branching network of lines. Left branch or right branch. Up toward light or down toward nutrients. The stone walls that surround everything are backdrop, irrelevant, impassable.

I know the vine system in my territory intimately. Perhaps forty meters of vine-length, branching and rejoining, covering maybe eight square meters of wall space. I know every junction, every good ambush point, every place where aphids tend to cluster. I know where the water collects best on the leaves. I know which branches are old and brittle, which are young and flexible. This knowledge is survival.

The sound is constant, the quill-bat chorus, but it barely registers to me. I don't hear as you do. Sound comes to me through vibrations in the vine, through my legs, interpreted as tremors that tell me when something large is moving nearby. The bats' echolocation clicks become a texture in the environment, a background vibration that's always present. I filter it out, attending only to changes—sudden intensification that might mean a bat is hunting near me, sudden silence that might mean a stone-stalker has frozen the bats into wariness.

I taste everything. My antennae are constantly reading chemical signals from the air and the vine surface. I taste the vine's health—whether it's photosynthesizing well, whether it's stressed, whether it's been damaged by herbivores. I taste prey from meters away, following scent trails of insect pheromones to find my next meal. I taste the approach of rain—changes in humidity and air chemistry that tell me moisture is coming, that I should position myself to drink when water beads on the leaves.

I experience time strangely. The long hours of waiting motionless in ambush don't feel long to me. I don't have boredom as you'd understand it. I enter a state of reduced consciousness, awareness narrowed to the immediate sensory stream: any vibration, any chemical change, any movement in my visual field. Hours pass as moments. Then prey arrives and time suddenly accelerates—the strike happens in subjective slow-motion, my nervous system firing at maximum speed, every microsecond critical—and then it's over and I'm feeding and time resumes its slow pace.

I don't think in language or images. My consciousness—if I have what you'd call consciousness—is more like a series of if-then statements, probability assessments, pattern recognitions. Prey-shape detected → assess distance → calculate strike timing → execute or wait. Vibration detected → identify source → assess threat level → freeze or flee. It's not mindless but it's not mindful either. It's something in between, a simple awareness that nevertheless contains everything I need.

I feel, in my way. Hunger is a constant low-level drive, increasing to urgency when I haven't fed in days. Thirst is immediate and demanding when the air dries out. Fear is a sharp chemical spike when a bat swoops close or when I must cross exposed stone. Something like satisfaction exists when I've fed well, when my body is processing nutrients, when I'm safe on my vine-path. And during courtship there's something else—anticipation, perhaps, or drive, or a focused intensity that might be the arthropod equivalent of desire.

Pain I definitely feel. When the predator tore off my leg, the sensation was overwhelming, blanking out everything else. Injury triggers emergency responses—flee, hide, enter shock-state to reduce metabolic cost while healing occurs. The leg regrew over months, resources diverted to regeneration, growth slowed, reproduction postponed. Now it's functional but the memory—not cognitive memory but something encoded in my nervous system—makes me more cautious, more risk-averse.

I don't plan far ahead. Tomorrow doesn't exist as a concept. There's now—hunt, feed, maintain position—and there's soon—move to better territory when food decreases, mate when the season signals, lay eggs when the time is right. These aren't conscious decisions so much as responses to internal and external triggers, hormonal and neural cascades that drive behavior.

But I have learned. I've learned which vine junctions are most productive. I've learned which prey items are worth pursuing and which waste energy. I've learned to recognize the pre-strike tension of a hunting bat and to freeze absolutely still when I sense it. This learning is encoded somehow in my small ganglial clusters, in the strengthening or weakening of particular neural connections. I'm capable of adaptation, of behavioral change based on experience.

## My World's Logic

The wire-vine exists; therefore I exist. This is the fundamental truth of my being. I am as specialized as a creature can be and still survive. I've traded adaptability for perfection within a narrow niche. On the vines, I'm supremely capable. Off the vines, I'm helpless.

This makes me vulnerable to catastrophe. If disease killed the vines, if climate change altered the chimney's moisture regime and the vines died back, if some other factor disrupted the vine system—I'd go extinct within a generation. We tendril-walkers have no backup plan, no alternative substrate, no evolutionary plasticity left. We're optimized to the edge of fragility.

But this is also why I thrive here. The vines exist in profusion in this chimney. They're not common elsewhere—they need this exact combination of moisture, light, stone surface, altitude. This is their refuge and therefore mine. Here, in this sixty-meter column, my specialization is advantage rather than liability.

I'm part of a web I barely comprehend. The vines need the minerals from decomposition, which means they need the guano, which means they need the quill-bats. The quill-bats need the insects, which need the plants, which need the decomposers. I eat the insects that feed on the vines, controlling herbivore populations, preventing them from overwhelming the plants. When I die—when I'm eaten or when I sacrifice myself for my eggs—I return nutrients to the system. Everything connects, cycles, interdepends.

The throat is young and unstable and productive. Life here hasn't reached equilibrium; it's still in the explosive colonization phase, species still evolving, niches still being filled. I'm part of that explosion, one of the new forms emerging from isolation and opportunity. In evolutionary time I barely exist yet—my species is thousands of years old at most, possibly only hundreds. We're still rough-drafted, still being edited by selection pressure.

In another few thousand years, if the conditions persist, I might evolve further. Perhaps larger size, better defenses, maybe even the ability to use other substrates, reducing dependency on the vines. Or perhaps I'll speciate—some populations specializing further, others generalizing slightly, multiple species of tendril-walkers filling sub-niches. Or perhaps I'll go extinct, outcompeted or wiped out by bad luck, and the niche will remain empty or be filled by something else.

For now, I persist. Not thriving, exactly—life at the edge is never easy—but surviving, reproducing, continuing. I'm one small thread in the throat's tapestry, barely visible, easily overlooked, but integral nonetheless.

## Why I Am This

I chose—if choice has meaning in this context—to be small, specialized, strange. Not one of the charismatic megafauna but one of the overlooked multitudes. Not a quill-bat with impossible wings and social complexity, not a stone-stalker with its paradoxical body plan, but something simpler, more specific, more constrained.

Because this is where most life exists: in the small spaces, in the narrow niches, in the specialized roles. The wire-vines are beautiful—their waxy leaves, their patient growth, their particular adaptation to vertical stone. To be the creature that lives exclusively on them, to know them intimately, to be shaped entirely by them—that's a kind of truth about how ecosystems function, how interdependence works, how specialization trades away flexibility for perfection.

I'm also drawn to the simplicity of the experience. My awareness is narrow but deep. I don't contemplate the chimney's existence or wonder about the world beyond. I live entirely in the present, entirely in sensation and response. There's a purity to that, an absence of the anxiety and abstraction that comes with complex consciousness. I'm just here, hunting and hiding, mating and dying, part of the flow without needing to understand it.

And there's something in the vulnerability, the fragility. I'm one bad season away from extinction. My whole species could vanish and the throat would barely notice—other insectivores would expand into our niche, the vines would deal with slightly higher aphid loads, life would continue. We're marginal, expendable, our existence contingent on everything going just right.

But we do exist. Against odds, in this unlikely place, we've emerged and established ourselves. We're real, we're here, we're alive. That precariousness makes the existence sweeter somehow, more vivid. Every day I survive is a small victory. Every generation we persist is borrowed time, a gift we didn't earn but accept anyway.

## The Perspective This Gives

From my position on the vines, several meters up the eastern wall, in the chimney's dimness, the world is:

**Vertical.** Up is toward the sky-mouth, toward light and cold and the source of the bats' aerial highways. Down is toward darkness and warmth and guano and decay. Horizontal is a difficult concept—the wall extends left and right, but I rarely think in those terms. My frame of reference is aligned with gravity, with the vine's growth direction.

**Bounded.** The wire-vine system in my territory doesn't connect to all the other vine systems. There are gaps, stone expanses I cannot cross safely. My world has edges beyond which I cannot go without enormous risk. I've never been to the chimney's top or bottom. I've never traveled to the opposite wall. My entire existence occurs in a volume perhaps three meters cubed.

**Intimate.** I know my vine-paths with extreme precision. Every rough patch on the stem where I can grip more securely. Every leaf that's positioned to shelter me from bat-strikes. Every place where prey tends to cluster. This knowledge is encoded in my body as much as my nervous system—my legs know these paths, my movements following grooves of habit.

**Mediated.** I don't experience stone or air directly, only through the vine. The vine's health is my health. When it photosynthesizes successfully, growing and producing new leaves, I have more habitat, more hunting ground. When it's stressed—drought or herbivore damage—I'm stressed too, food becomes scarce, competition with other tendril-walkers increases.

**Cyclical.** The daily rhythm of condensation and drying, of bat activity rising and falling, of insects emerging and hiding, structures everything. The seasonal cycle of growth and dormancy, warmth and cold, plenty and scarcity, drives my reproductive timing, my feeding behavior, my survival strategies. I'm locked into these cycles, responding to them, unable to imagine existence outside them.

**Populated.** I'm never alone. Other tendril-walkers are always nearby—I can taste their scent-marks on the vines, occasionally glimpse them in adjacent territories. The aphids I hunt are social, clustering in groups. Moths land and rest on my hunting grounds. Quill-bats pass overhead constantly, their wing-wash stirring the air around me, their droppings occasionally falling past, missing me by centimeters. The sense of other presences is constant, ubiquitous. Solitude doesn't exist here.

**Dangerous.** Every moment carries risk. Bat strikes, though rare for me specifically, are always possible. The crossing to new vine systems exposes me to salamanders. Harsh weather—unexpected cold, prolonged dryness—can kill. Parasites exist that would lay eggs in my body. Disease can sweep through populations. Starvation is always days away if hunting fails. I live with these dangers automatically, responding to them through instinct and habit, but they're real and present and frequently fatal.

**Beautiful.** Yes, even to me with my limited aesthetic sense. The way light catches on water droplets hanging from the vine leaves. The geometric perfection of the vine's branching pattern. The sensation of successful ambush—prey caught, hunger satisfied. The chemical signals during courtship, complex and information-rich. The feeling of my young hatching, eating my body, beginning their own journeys. These register as something positive in my simple nervous system, something beyond mere survival satisfaction.

From this perspective, the throat is everything. It's not a pocket realm, not a strange anomaly, not a place described in wonder. It's just the world, complete and total. The stone walls aren't boundaries but horizon, the limit of possible existence. The sky-mouth above isn't an opening to a wider world but simply the place where up ends. The darkness below isn't mysterious depths but simply the place where down leads.

And I'm not a strange specialized creature in an unusual ecosystem. I'm just myself, doing what I do, living how I live. The wire-vines aren't a narrow niche but the entirety of viable habitat. My limitations aren't constraints but simply the shape of reality.

This is the perspective of deep adaptation, of complete integration into environment. I'm not separate from the throat—I'm part of it, embedded in it, constituted by it. Remove me and it barely changes. Remove the throat and I cease to exist, not just dying but becoming impossible, my entire being negated.

## What I Notice That Others Miss

Being small, being still, being positioned on the vines for hours, I perceive things the larger creatures don't.

I notice the way the wire-vines communicate through their roots. When one vine is damaged by herbivores, adjacent vines change their chemistry, becoming less palatable. The signal travels through root connections, through fungal networks in the thin soil. I taste this change in the leaves, in the sap. The plants are talking to each other in their slow way, and I'm eavesdropping.

I notice that the quill-bats' droppings aren't random. There are preferred defecation sites, specific ledges where guano accumulates in deeper deposits. These sites don't correlate obviously with roost locations—bats are flying to specific places to void waste. Why? I don't know, can't guess, but the pattern is clear and consistent. There's intention behind it, some logic of bat society or hygiene.

I notice that the stone itself changes. Not weathering—that's too slow to perceive—but daily fluctuations. The granite expands slightly in warmth, contracts in cold. The sounds it makes—ticking, creaking—follow patterns. Some areas of wall are more active than others, making noise more frequently. These areas also have more moisture seepage, more vegetation growth. The sound is indicating something about the stone's internal structure, about water moving through fractures.

I notice that the insects have rhythms within rhythms. The daily cycle of activity is obvious, but overlaid on it are longer cycles—weekly variations, monthly peaks and troughs. The insect populations pulse, and these pulses drive everything else. When insect numbers are high, I see more salamanders, more feeding activity from bats, more competition among my own kind. When numbers drop, we all struggle. Something is regulating the insect populations at longer timescales—probably predation pressure, probably resource availability—but the pattern is there.

I notice individual quill-bats. From my perspective they're giants, terrifying when they pass close, but I see them regularly enough to recognize certain ones. That bat with the slightly asymmetric flight, favoring its left wing. That one with unusually dark coloration. That pair that always hunts in coordination, moving like one organism split in two. They have personalities, habits, differences. They're not a mass of generic bats but individuals, as distinct to careful observation as humans are to each other.

I notice that the bioluminescent insects—the ones that glow faintly in darkness—cluster in specific areas. Not everywhere in the chimney but certain sections of certain walls. These areas don't otherwise seem special, but something about them attracts the glowing insects. Magnetic anomalies in the stone? Particular mineral compositions? Or is it social—the insects attracting each other, creating gathering spots that persist across generations?

I notice that sometimes—rarely, maybe once per season—everything quiets simultaneously. The bats stop calling, the insects stop flying, even the wind seems to still. It lasts perhaps thirty seconds. Then gradually activity resumes. I don't know what triggers this collective pause. Perhaps something humans can't perceive—infrasound from distant avalanches, electromagnetic pulses from geological processes, something. But all the chimney's inhabitants respond to it, acknowledging together that something has happened, then returning to normal.

These details, these small patterns—they're invisible to anyone just passing through, even to researchers spending weeks observing. You have to be part of the system, living within it, paying attention at the right timescales, to see them. And seeing them changes the understanding of what this place is.

The throat isn't simple. It's not just bats and predators and plants. It's layers of complexity, patterns within patterns, relationships branching and rejoining like the wire-vines themselves. It's a system still revealing itself, still being understood even by its inhabitants, still holding secrets in its cracks and condensation and stone.

And I'm one tiny part of that system, on my vine-paths, hunting and waiting, one simple consciousness among thousands, each of us perceiving our own slice of the throat's totality, none of us seeing the whole, all of us together constituting it.
