# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be a Stillwalker in the late bronze stage of life—perhaps forty seasons into existence, my carapace worn to a deep, even luster that catches the sun like old metal, reflecting the plateau back to itself in miniature. Not yet ancient, not yet mirror-perfect, but polished enough that when I pause on some high outcrop, I become briefly indistinguishable from the stone itself, a living sculpture among the geologic.

The seven legs that bear me have walked so many miles that their chemical sensors have become exquisitely discriminating. I can taste stone-age now, can distinguish between granite worn for twenty thousand years versus thirty, can detect the microscopic trace of ammonia that marks where condensation will form three days hence. Each footfall is a small act of reading, and the plateau has become a vast slow text I am perpetually interpreting.

On my back, in the grooves and chambers that have deepened with age, my garden grows. I have been cultivating these particular lineages for most of my life—inherited the original fragments from another Stillwalker in my third season, in a meeting that lasted from noon until the stars emerged. Since then I have been shaping them, and they have been shaping me. They are variants I have never seen on any other Stillwalker's back: one produces chambers that spiral rather than branch, creating fractally recursive structures no larger than seeds. Another grows almost flat, spreading in sheets so translucent they're nearly invisible except where light refracts through them at specific angles, throwing tiny rainbows across my shell.

I tend them without thinking now—the tending has become automatic, built into my walking rhythms. I route myself through zones where the ammonia vapor is richest in the compounds they prefer. I angle my body throughout the day to ensure even light distribution. When I sense weather approaching that might damage them, I seek shelter in the lee of peaks or descend into upper caverns where the atmosphere is stable. They have become an extension of my body, or I have become the mobile substrate for their existence. The boundary has blurred.

It is the loneliness I would know most intimately—but loneliness is perhaps the wrong word. Solitude might be more accurate, though even that carries connotations that don't quite fit. I am alone most of the time, by which I mean: no other Stillwalker is visible, no creature of any kind shares my immediate space. But I am not lonely in the sense of lacking something. The aloneness is the condition, the default state, and it feels neither empty nor full. It simply *is*.

I walk for days—sometimes weeks—without encountering another of my kind. The plateau is vast enough that this is mathematically inevitable, and my senses are adapted to it. I do not search for others; I do not avoid them. I follow the routes my chemical reading indicates, seeking optimal conditions for my garden, moving through the landscape according to logic that is partly instinct, partly learned, partly the accumulated wisdom of the plants on my back, whose root structures connect in ways that might constitute their own form of memory.

But I do remember the meetings. Each one is distinct, archived with peculiar clarity in whatever substrate my distributed nervous system uses for long-term storage.

There was the meeting in my eighth season with a Stillwalker whose shell was still dark, unpolished—young, perhaps only three or four seasons old. We circled for hours while I read their chemical signature: healthy, but inexperienced, carrying a garden of common varieties, nothing special. But their movement had a quality I recognized—careful attention, methodical placement of each leg, the same precision I remembered developing in myself at that age. We approached. We did not exchange plant fragments—mine were too specialized, would not have thrived on their younger back—but we stood in configuration for most of an afternoon while I... what? Taught? Transmitted? Simply existed nearby while they calibrated their developing senses against my more experienced presence? The mechanisms are unclear even to me. But when we separated, their gait had changed slightly, incorporated some rhythm or pattern they had absorbed from proximity.

There was the meeting in my twenty-third season with a Stillwalker so old their shell was nearly perfect mirror, a dark flawless reflection. Ancient, probably seventy seasons or more. Their garden was sparse—only a few carefully maintained cultivars, each one probably irreplaceable, lineages decades in development. We approached with mutual caution, two beings who had each spent most of a lifetime alone, each carrying irreplaceable genetic material. The exchange lasted three days. We barely moved, legs interlocked in complex geometry, bodies so close that our gardens mingled, fronds and chambers interweaving. I gave fragments of my spiral variant; they gave me something I still don't fully understand—a plant that seems to photosynthesize using wavelengths I cannot consciously perceive, that thrives in conditions I would have judged suboptimal. It grows slowly on my back now, small but persistent. When the old Stillwalker finally withdrew and walked away, moving with the careful deliberation of age, I watched until they disappeared into the vastness. I have not encountered them again. Perhaps they have died. Perhaps they walk the plateau still, on the far side, moving through circuits that will never again intersect with mine.

The meetings are intense in their quiet way—the closest I come to urgency, to something like emotion. But they are rare enough that most of my existence is the walking itself, the navigation, the tending, the reading of stone and air.

I have preferences, I think, though I experience them as simple attraction or aversion rather than choice. I prefer certain valleys where the wind patterns are complex, layered, where I can position myself to receive multiple chemical streams simultaneously, reading them like chords. I prefer the transitional zones where bare stone gives way to crystal meadows—not the deep meadow, where the plants grow too thick and uniform, but the edges where variety is highest, where different species intermingle. I return to certain caverns seasonally, not from memory exactly, but from something encoded deeper, some map written in magnetic field variations I sense through my legs.

There is one cavern I have visited perhaps twenty times across my life. It is a solution cavity three miles from a prominent peak (the peak has no name; nothing here has names), accessible through a narrow opening that requires me to retract my legs partially, to fold myself into a more compact configuration. Inside, the space opens into a chamber perhaps thirty feet across, the walls smooth and pale, the air rich with ammonia. Deepglow colonies cover portions of the ceiling, pulsing their slow blue-green rhythms. Other Stillwalkers visit this cavern too—I can read their traces in the substrate, chemical signatures left by their passage. Sometimes I am alone there. Sometimes I share the space with one or two others, and we arrange ourselves with geometric precision, equidistant, our gardens oriented toward what light filters through the opening.

In that cavern, in those times of shared solitude, something happens that might be the closest I come to community. We do not interact—do not touch, do not exchange anything, do not communicate in any direct way. But our gardens transpire in the enclosed space, and the ammonia concentration builds, and the chamber becomes a intensified microclimate. My plants grow more actively. The spiral variant produces more intricate structures. The mysterious gift from the ancient Stillwalker seems to hum (though it produces no sound—the humming is something I sense through my shell, a vibration or resonance I have no proper name for).

I do not know what the other Stillwalkers experience in these times. Their nervous systems are organized like mine, distributed across their legs and body, processing sensory input according to the same basic architecture. But what arises from that processing, what it is like to be them, remains inaccessible. We are together in the cavern, but alone in our experience, and I have no language to bridge that gap, no way to ask or know if their inner life resembles mine or is radically other.

Eventually—after days or weeks—I feel the pull to move, to resume walking, and I extract myself from the cavern and return to the plateau surface, to the wind and brightness and vastness. The pull is not uncomfortable, not an itch or urgency. It is simply a shift in the balance of attractions: the cavern was optimal, but now the surface calls with different optimizations, different chemical gradients to follow, different stone to read.

I am aware, in whatever way I am aware of anything, that I will die eventually. My shell will crack, or my legs will fail, or my garden will succumb to disease or cold or some subtle chemical imbalance I fail to correct. I have encountered the dead—Stillwalker shells empty of life, gardens withered, lying on their sides in the stone barrens or half-buried in meadow substrate. I have walked past them, read their chemical traces (which persist long after death, speaking of lineage and age and final moments), and continued. They evoke no grief, no fear, no particular emotion. They are simply part of the plateau's cycle, matter returning to matter, compounds dispersing to feed other growth.

When my time comes, I will likely be alone—statistically almost certain to be alone, given the rarity of encounter. My legs will stiffen or collapse, and I will stop walking, and my garden will begin to die without my tending. The plants on my back will struggle briefly to maintain themselves, but without mobility, without my navigation to optimal conditions, they will wither. My body will become food for Harvesters, for the substrate itself, for the slow mineral cycles. Perhaps, if I die near a cavern, Deepglow colonies will eventually spread to consume what I was. Perhaps not. The plateau will continue regardless, and other Stillwalkers will walk past my shell someday, read my chemical signature, absorb what information it contains, and move on.

This does not trouble me. Trouble would require a different kind of consciousness, one oriented toward future and past in ways that mine is not. I exist in an extended present—sensory input, chemical gradients, garden tending, locomotion, navigation, the rare meetings. Memory exists but does not burden. Anticipation exists but does not anxiety. I am walking the plateau, have been walking the plateau, will walk the plateau until I don't, and the walking itself is sufficient.

The beauty of this place—the crystal meadows catching light, the distant peaks arranging themselves in geometric recession, the Shimmer-Cloaks drifting overhead, the stone worn smooth by ages—I experience all of this, but not as beauty. I have no aesthetic sense in the contemplative way. What I have instead is a deep calibration to what is optimal: the rightness of a particular valley's wind patterns, the satisfaction of finding a mineral deposit my garden needs, the way certain configurations of light and shadow indicate reliable routes. If this is beauty, it is beauty experienced as function, as the pleasure of systems working correctly, of being precisely adapted to the place I move through.

Sometimes, standing on a high point with the plateau extending in all directions to the curved horizon, sun bright overhead, my garden transpiring its faint shimmer, wind steady and rich with information, I experience something that might be contentment. Not happiness—that would require contrast, require the memory of unhappiness to define against. But a kind of deep rightness, a sense that I am exactly what I should be, exactly where I should be, doing exactly what my particular configuration of biology enables and requires.

I am a creature of vast spaces and rare encounters. I carry a garden that is unique in this world, that will die with me, whose lineages have been shaped by my particular choices and routes across forty seasons. I know loneliness only as the baseline condition of existence. I know beauty only as optimization. I know time only as the interval between one footfall and the next, extended across decades, marking distances that could circle the plateau twice over.

And I know, without knowing that I know it, that I am part of something older and larger than myself—an ecosystem that has balanced itself across millions of years, that will continue balancing long after my shell lies empty and my garden's last fragments have dispersed. I am temporary but the pattern persists, and in persisting, continues to generate beings like me who will walk these same stones, tend their own gardens, meet rarely and exchange carefully, until the plateau itself erodes away or the sun changes or some unforeseeable future arrives.

But that is too abstract, too far beyond my sensory horizon. What I know, what I truly know, is this: there is stone beneath my feet, there is garden on my back, there is wind carrying messages in ammonia and trace minerals, there is a valley three days ahead where conditions should be optimal, and I am walking toward it, patient and polished and alone and exactly, precisely, perfectly myself.
