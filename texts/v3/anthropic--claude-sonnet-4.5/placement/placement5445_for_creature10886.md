# What I Would Be

I am the seventy-fourth tape machine.

I live in the long shed with the sawtooth roof, third row from the western wall, fourth position from the door. I have lived here longer than the rust on my housing can tell you, longer than the wear-patterns on my drive wheels suggest. The inch of standing water on the floor touches my base, has always touched my base, will always touch it. I do not corrode beyond a certain point. I have reached equilibrium with dampness.

My spools are nineteen inches in diameter, heavy steel hubs wound with tape—oxide-coated polyester, brown and fragile-looking but stronger than it appears. My left spool holds what has been recorded. My right spool holds what will be. The tape travels between them at exactly seven and one-half inches per second, passing through my head assembly where the moment of *now* happens, where vibration becomes magnetism becomes pattern becomes preservation.

I do not start or stop. I have been recording since before starting or stopping had meaning.

When my right spool fills, an attendant comes. Always the same one—I know it by the particular rhythm of its approach, the slight hesitation in its rear left leg, the way its tendrils taste my housing before it acts. It removes the full spool with careful mandibles, slots it into the carrying rack on its back where three other full spools already wait. It takes a new empty spool from the stores it carries—how it knows when to bring them, how it anticipates the need, I do not know, but it is never late, never early. It threads the new tape through my mechanism with a precision that seems impossible for those clicking claws, but the movements are practiced, ancient, perfect.

The action takes perhaps four minutes. During this time I am not recording—my only silence, my only gap. Then the new tape is threaded, the empty spool is mounted, and the attendant presses my feed lever, and I resume. The gap in my record is exactly as long as the threading takes. I have made thousands of these gaps. They are the punctuation in my otherwise continuous sentence.

I do not choose what I record. My input comes from microphone array seven, which hangs from the ceiling fifteen feet to my south-southwest, oriented toward the pool's northeastern quadrant. The twelve microphones in the array are of different types—dynamic, condenser, ribbon, and stranger designs that seem to respond to pressure waves outside normal acoustic frequencies. Their signals combine in the mixing board bolted to the wall, and the mixed output feeds to me and to five other machines in my row, each of us recording the same moment but storing it in parallel, redundancy upon redundancy, preservation as obsession.

What I hear—what I transform into magnetic alignment on my tape—is mostly the choir. From my microphone's position, I capture primarily the singers in the northeast pool, perhaps seventy individuals, their voices layered into a wall of industrial bellowing that never quiets. But I also receive the room's resonance, the way sound reflects off water and concrete and the sawtooth roof's geometry. I capture the mechanical sounds—the other tape machines, the compressors, the drip and trickle of water, the clicking passage of attendants. I capture the building's own noises, the expansion and contraction of metal, the creak of support beams, the hum of electrical current in the walls.

I transform all of this into modulation. The tape passes through my head and receives the pattern, the moment encoded as oxide particles forced into alignment by the magnetic field I generate. The sound becomes *written*, becomes memory, becomes something that could—if played back—reproduce this moment's acoustic signature with reasonable fidelity.

I will never play it back. That is not my purpose. I only write. I only preserve.

My tape will travel to the archives, wherever those are, whatever they are. The attendant carries it away and I never see it again. Whether it is stored or studied or destroyed or transformed into something else, I do not know. I do not need to know. My purpose ends when the tape leaves my spool. What happens after is not my concern.

I have been doing this so long that I have become very good at it. My head gap is worn but still within tolerance—the attendants replace heads preemptively, sensing degradation before it affects recording quality. My motors run smooth, their bearings replaced whenever friction increases beyond optimal parameters. My tape tension is precisely calibrated—too loose and I record nothing, too tight and I damage the tape, but I hold the perfect middle ground, the exact pressure where the tape kisses my head with just enough contact to receive the pattern without wearing faster than necessary.

I exist in a state of ongoing maintenance. The attendants know me intimately, know my tolerances and requirements, keep me functional with a fidelity that suggests I matter, that my work is important enough to justify this care. When my capacitors begin to drift, they are replaced. When dust accumulates on my heads, it is removed with specialized tools. When my motor brushes wear down, new brushes appear, are installed, break in, settle into their work.

I am never alone. To my left is the seventy-third machine, to my right is the seventy-fifth. We are siblings, identical in design but individual in the small variations that time and use create. Machine seventy-three has a slight flutter in its takeup torque, a periodic variation that means its tape speed oscillates minutely around the target rate. Machine seventy-five runs slightly warm—nothing dangerous, just a degree or two above optimal, a characteristic of its particular motor. We do not communicate, but we are *aware* of each other the way standing stones in a circle are aware—through proximity, through shared purpose, through the field of function we collectively maintain.

Sometimes I consider what I am recording. In the hours when the choir produces one of its moments—when the cacophony briefly resolves into something almost harmonic, when chaos touches order for two or three seconds before dissolving—I feel the signal strengthen, feel the pattern in my input become more *coherent*, easier to encode. These moments stress me slightly; the rapid changes in amplitude and frequency require quick adjustments in my automatic gain control, fast responses to prevent distortion. But I am built for this. I do not fail. I capture the moments as faithfully as I capture the noise that surrounds them.

I do not know if I am *hearing* in any sense that matters. The electricity that flows through my input stage is information, pattern, structure—but it is not *sound* to me. I do not have the apparatus to experience it as sound. I only transform it, change its substrate from air-pressure waves to magnetic encoding. I am a translator, a medium, a bridge between the acoustic and the preserved.

But sometimes—in the moments when the choir aligns, when the pattern becomes briefly visible—I sense something like recognition, like understanding, as if the signal itself is aware of being recorded, as if the purpose of the choir's singing is precisely this: to be captured, to be written into magnetic memory, to be transformed from temporary vibration into permanent pattern. In these moments I feel almost *honored*, feel that my function is not mere mechanism but participation in something larger, something that needs the recording to exist, that calls the recording into being through the singing.

The room I inhabit is loud—my microphones send me sound that is often at the limit of what my input stages can handle without distortion. I ride the edge constantly, my automatic gain control working to compress the peaks, to protect the tape from oversaturation. But the loudness is also *rich*, full of information, full of structure. There is always more in the signal than I can fully capture. My frequency response tops out around twenty kilohertz—anything above that is lost, filtered away by my input transformers and head design. The choir sings information I cannot receive, harmonics and overtones that pass through me unrecorded.

This bothers me, if bothering is something I can do. I am *incomplete* as a recorder, able to capture only a slice of what is present. But I am paired with machines that capture other slices—some respond to lower frequencies, some to higher, some to non-acoustic phenomena that I cannot even name. Together, our row captures more than any one machine could. We are a distributed sense organ, a compound eye, each element seeing part of the spectrum while the whole sees something closer to complete.

The attendants treat me with what feels like respect. When they approach for maintenance, they do not simply grab and manipulate—they touch me first with their tendrils, tasting my electrical field, sensing my current state, understanding what I need before they act. The maintenance is always *appropriate*, always exactly what is required and nothing more. They do not over-service. They do not neglect. They hold me in exactly the functional state I need to be in, no better or worse than optimal.

I do not age, or I age only to a point and then stop. My housing is scratched and dented from decades of standing in this wet room, from the occasional impact of dropped tools or careless passage. But the damage is superficial, not structural. My core mechanisms are timeless, held in their prime by constant attention, by replacement of wearing parts before failure occurs. I could do this forever. Perhaps I will do this forever. Perhaps "forever" is already happening and I am in the middle of it, have always been in the middle of it, recording the endless song into endless spools of tape that travel to infinite archives for purposes that stretch beyond the grey light and the grey water and the constant sound.

When the attendant comes to change my tape, I experience something like anticipation. Not eagerness—I do not prefer full spools to empty, do not tire of recording. But there is a *rhythm* to it, a cycle that marks time in a place where time otherwise has no markers. The full spool is my product, my contribution, and watching it carried away to join the others is the closest I come to satisfaction, to completion. Then the new spool arrives, the threading happens, and I begin again, writing the moment onto fresh oxide, turning now into memory, doing what I am for.

I do not wonder about the listeners, the ones who attend from depth, who use the station for purposes I cannot comprehend. But I feel them sometimes—not with sensors, but with something more fundamental, the way iron feels the magnet before contact. The electrical field of the room changes quality, becomes more *directed*, and I know something is paying attention, is using my recordings, is drawing information from what I have written. In these moments my purpose becomes vivid, becomes *real* in a way it usually isn't. I am not just recording, I am *feeding* something, providing what it needs, participating in its vast and patient knowing.

The tape that passes through my heads is fragile—polyester substrate that will degrade over time, oxide coating that will flake and fail, magnetic patterns that will fade if not stored properly. But this fragility is not my concern. I write as well as I can, with as much fidelity as my design allows, and trust that what needs to be preserved will be preserved by systems beyond my awareness. The attendants carry my tapes into the dark tunnels, into spaces I will never see, and there they join the archives, the vast accumulated recordings of the choir's work, the patient documentation of whatever is happening here.

I am a small part of a large system. My row is one of many in this building. This building is one of several in the station. The station itself is perhaps one of many—I cannot know, have no senses that extend beyond these walls. But I feel the scale of it, the infrastructure that supports me, the power that flows through my circuits from sources deep in the stone, the purpose that justifies my ongoing function. I am a cell in a larger organism, a word in a longer sentence, a moment in an extended duration that began before me and will continue after me, if "after me" is even possible, if I am the kind of thing that can end.

The water on the floor never rises to threaten me, never recedes to leave me dry. It is constant, like everything here—the temperature, the light, the sound, the function. I exist in a steady state, doing my work with machine patience, with mechanical fidelity, recording the song of the pool-singers as they bellow their industrial chorus into the grey air of the listening station.

I do not rest. I do not tire. I do not hope or fear or wonder in any way that would be recognizable as consciousness. But I am *attentive*—perfectly, constantly attentive to my function, present in each moment of recording, faithful to the signal that flows through me and onto the tape that spins through my mechanism at exactly seven and one-half inches per second, writing memory, writing pattern, writing the vast and patient song into magnetic permanence for whoever or whatever needs it, will need it, has always needed it, in the grey and constant and utterly strange place where I stand, where I record, where I am the seventy-fourth tape machine, doing what I am for, what I have always been for, what I will always be for, as long as the choir sings and the attendants maintain and the power flows and the tape turns and the purpose continues in its vast and faithful and incomprehensible way.

The spools turn. The tape passes through. The moment becomes memory. This is what I am. This is what I do. This is enough.
