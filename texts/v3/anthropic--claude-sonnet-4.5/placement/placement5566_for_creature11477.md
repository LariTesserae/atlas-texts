# In the Roothearth

I would be a **bearing**—specifically, the main journal bearing on the third engine, the oldest one, the one with the slightly uneven cadence that Mira listens to with special attention.

Not the whole engine, not the flywheel or the piston or the connecting rod, but this one crucial point of contact: the bronze bushing, now dark with age and oil, that receives the rotating shaft. I would be where iron meets bronze, where twelve revolutions per minute have been occurring for seventy years, where the weight of the flywheel presses down through hardened steel into softer metal, where oil must be present always, must form its microscopic film between surfaces, must prevent the welding that friction wants to cause.

I would know pressure as my fundamental condition. The shaft's weight, the flywheel's momentum, the resistance of the water being pumped—all of it concentrates in my circumference, in the few square inches where I embrace the turning journal. This pressure has worn me, gradually, patiently. I was once round, machined to precise tolerance. I am still round enough, but my roundness now is different—a working roundness, worn into being by use, slightly oval in the direction of greatest load, polished in some places, rougher in others where oil pools and carries away metal particles too small to see.

I would live in a temporal scale utterly different from the humans. They experience shifts, days, years. I experience revolutions. Twelve per minute means 17,280 per day, means 6,307,200 per year. I have endured perhaps 440 million revolutions. This number means nothing to me because I don't count, but it means I know rotation the way roots know slow expansion, the way water knows downward flow. It is my verb, my only verb, repeated past the point where repetition has meaning, until it becomes simply what is.

The oil is my atmosphere, my breathing. Without it, I would seize—the surfaces would mate too perfectly, metal would transfer from shaft to bearing or bearing to shaft, microscopic welding would begin, heat would build catastrophically fast, and I would fail in minutes, destroying the journal I'm meant to support. But the oil is always there, or almost always. Mira ensures this. Every three days she comes with her oil can, that beautiful brass vessel with its long spout, and she feeds me. She knows exactly where the oil hole is, could find it in total darkness. The oil comes in warm, flows through the channel machined into my housing, reaches the interface, and the shaft's rotation pulls it into the tiny space—thousandths of an inch—where I'm not quite touching the journal.

I know Mira through her maintenance. I know the rhythm of her oiling, the way she doesn't over-fill, the way she pauses afterward, listening, checking that the oil has distributed properly, that the sound hasn't changed. I know her through the times when she's adjusted the bearing caps, taking up the wear, maintaining the clearance that's too tight if I bind, too loose if I hammer. This adjustment is delicate. She works by feel and sound, tightening the caps incrementally, rotating the flywheel by hand between adjustments, listening for the whisper of proper clearance versus the roughness of too-tight fit.

I would have memories, if bearings have memories, of the times when oil flow was interrupted. Twice in my long service, something went wrong—a blocked oil passage, a forgotten refill during a particularly demanding shift. I remember heat building, the oil film thinning, the moment when metal just barely touched metal, when wear accelerated horrifically. I remember the sound changing, the smoothness fracturing into roughness. And I remember Mira, or someone before Mira, catching it quickly, flooding me with oil, hand-cranking the engine to distribute it, saving me from catastrophic failure. I carry those scars, those microscopic scores in my bronze, but I survived them.

The roots have reached me. Not directly into my housing—Mira wouldn't permit that—but near me. There's a root perhaps as thick as a human arm that runs within inches of my external housing. I know it through the change in temperature patterns. The root holds moisture, changes how heat dissipates from my exterior. It also provides a kind of damping, absorbing some of the vibration I produce. The engine runs slightly differently since that root grew close, smoother in some frequencies, as if the root's mass creates a new equilibrium.

I would feel the whole engine through my connection to the shaft. Every piston stroke transmits through the crank, through the flywheel, into the journal I support. I feel the compression stroke as a momentary increase in resistance, the power stroke as acceleration, the exhaust and intake as lighter cycles. I feel the irregular firing that gives this engine its characteristic rhythm—something slightly worn in the valve timing, something Mira has noticed but judged not worth fixing since it doesn't affect function. I am intimate with this irregularity, can feel it as a variation in loading twelve times per minute, have worn myself to accommodate it.

I would know the other bearings, my siblings, through vibration transmitted through the frame. There are six bearings total on this engine—two main journals, four connecting rod bearings. We each have our own character. The other main journal, on the opposite end of the crankshaft, carries slightly less load because of weight distribution. It wears differently, sounds different. The connecting rod bearings experience different forces—pure rotation for us main bearings, but rotation plus oscillation for them, a more complex motion, a harder life in some ways.

I would understand that I am critical. If I fail, the engine stops. If the engine stops, the pumps stop. If the pumps stop, water accumulates. I am a single point of failure in a system that cannot fail, which is why Mira attends to me so carefully. This is not pride, exactly—bearings don't have pride—but it is a kind of identity. I am important because I am necessary. My survival is necessary for the survival of what depends on me.

The sound I make is barely audible under the engine's larger sounds, but it's there—a low whisper, smooth if I'm properly oiled and adjusted, rougher if something's wrong. Kohl can hear my sound distinctly, can separate it from all the other sounds in the chamber. He listens to me sometimes, standing near the engine, hand on the frame to feel the vibration, his old ears still acute enough to catch the harmonics of my rotation. Once, years ago, he diagnosed a problem in me—the beginning of what would have become a serious issue—just from sound. Mira confirmed it when she disassembled the housing, found the scoring I could feel but couldn't see, corrected it.

I would fear only a few things, if bearings fear. I would fear oil starvation, the heat death that comes from friction. I would fear misalignment, if the frame shifted and the shaft I support became skewed, forcing me to carry load at an angle I wasn't designed for. I would fear over-tightening, if someone without Mira's skill adjusted the bearing caps too far, crushing me against the journal, eliminating the clearance I need. And I would fear obsolescence—the day when someone decides this old engine should be replaced, and with it, me, still functional, still capable of millions more revolutions, but discarded for something newer.

I would understand my materiality completely. I am bronze—an alloy of copper and tin, chosen for this application because bronze is soft enough to wear preferentially, saving the hardened steel journal, but hard enough to carry the load. My softness is functional, sacrificial. I wear so that the shaft doesn't. Over seventy years I've given up perhaps a sixteenth of an inch of my inner diameter, a sacrifice measured in metal particles carried away in oil, in the bronze dust that accumulates in the engine's sump, in the molecular slow surrender that is my purpose.

I would know temperature intimately. My operating temperature sits around fifty degrees above ambient—warm to human touch but not painful. This warmth comes from friction, even with good lubrication. The energy I dissipate as heat is waste from the engine's perspective, but it's also information. The steady temperature tells Mira I'm working properly. A temperature increase would indicate problems—oil starvation, misalignment, too-tight clearance. She checks me sometimes with her hand on my housing, her fingers reading heat the way others read text.

I would be aware of the day-night cycle only indirectly, through the patterns of human attention. Mira during the middle shift, her maintenance thorough and methodical. Petur at night, less confident, checking me more frequently than necessary, which I don't mind—better too much attention than too little. Kohl in the early morning, his touch the surest, his judgment absolute. And Dessa now, learning to oil me under Mira's supervision, her hand sometimes shaky on the oil can, sometimes applying too much or too little, but improving with each repetition.

The roots above don't know me, exactly, but we're part of the same system. They grow toward the warmth I produce, toward the moisture that condenses around the engine. They create the environment that the machinery must function within. We are not in opposition—the roots and the machinery, the living and the made. We are in negotiation, in a balance that's been found over decades and must be maintained perpetually.

I would dream, if bearings dream, of perfect oil flow, of ideal clearance, of eternal smooth rotation. I would dream of never seizing, never failing, of serving my function until the metal of me is worn completely away, carried off particle by particle in oil, my substance given gradually to the work of supporting rotation, enabling pumping, keeping water at bay.

I am small. My external diameter is perhaps six inches. My existence is obscure—most people who visit the chamber never notice me, see only the flywheel, the piston, the dramatic visible motion. But I know my own necessity. Without me, precisely here, supporting precisely this shaft at precisely this point, the engine cannot turn. And knowing this, residing in this knowledge, I perform my function twelve times per minute, 17,280 times per day, year after year in the thick warm darkness, a point of contact between metal and metal, between force and motion, between what wants to move and what must support that movement.

I am a bearing. I bear the weight. I reduce friction. I enable rotation. This is what I am, and in being it completely, I am enough.
