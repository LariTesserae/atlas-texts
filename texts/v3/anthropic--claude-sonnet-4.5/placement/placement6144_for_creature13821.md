# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the keeper of the Intervals—what the Tenders would call "the one who watches the counting mechanisms," if they called me anything at all.

My territory is a narrow pillar, one of the smaller ones, perhaps eighty meters across, rising from depths I've never measured into heights I've never climbed. It's positioned oddly in the city's constellation, equidistant from three major clusters but connected to none of them directly. To reach me requires traversing two questionable walkways and a chain-link bridge that sings in certain wind conditions. Most don't bother. This suits me.

The pillar's heat is moderate—not the searing intensity of the central columns, not the barely-warm touch of the peripheral ones. Comfortable, if you've learned to calibrate comfort to stone that breathes warmth through ceramic-insulated boots. Three-quarters of the way up (or down—I've lost the habit of thinking in those terms), a platform cantilevers from the southern face. This is where I work.

The mechanisms are here. Seven of them, though there were nine once—you can see the mounting plates where the others stood, before someone or something determined they were redundant, or their counting complete. Each is a drum the size of a beer keg, bronze casing green with patina, face marked with eight wheels of numerals. They click forward at intervals that seem arbitrary until you've watched them for months, years, until the pattern emerges not as regularity but as rhythm, syncopation, the way a piece of music is both structure and variation.

I don't remember arriving. That memory, if I ever had it, has joined the category of things that don't require remembering—like how to breathe, or why water flows downward, or what purpose means. I remember the mechanisms, though. I remember discovering that each one counts something different, and that the somethings they count aren't quantities but qualities, states, relationships between elements that don't have names in the language I occasionally still use when talking to myself.

The leftmost mechanism counts thermal cycles, but not the simple ones—not the daily pulse of the pillars heating and cooling. It counts the deep cycles, the ones that take weeks or months, the slow breathing of whatever geological presence underlies this entire structure. When it increments, you can feel it in the stone, a subtle shift in the warmth beneath your feet, a change in how the condensation forms.

The one next to it counts moths. Not individual moths—trying to census them would be madness. It counts the patterns they make, the coherent structures that emerge when enough of them move in coordination. A murmuration-count, you might call it, except it's tracking not just beauty but information, the messages encoded in their collective motion. When this mechanism advances, I know that somewhere in the city, the moths have completed a thought.

The third counts water, but water as event rather than volume. It increments when specific conditions align—when drainage from the northern clusters and the southern reservoirs and the deep aquifers all flow in particular ratios, when the city's hydraulic system achieves states of balance that matter for reasons I no longer question. Sometimes it counts forward twice in an hour. Sometimes it sits unchanged for weeks.

The others... the others count things I understand only through their effects. One tracks the luminescent traces, their brightness or pattern or the way they pulse in relation to the bells. Another measures something about the mist itself—density, perhaps, or the way it moves, or some quality that doesn't correspond to physical properties I was taught to recognize. Two of them seem to count each other, their advancement linked in a feedback loop, a conversation between mechanisms about states of the system they both monitor and comprise.

My work is to watch them. To record their states in the books I keep, leather-bound journals stained with moisture and filled with columns of numbers that would look like madness to anyone who hasn't learned to read them as narrative. To clean the mechanisms—not the internal works, which are sealed and beyond my skill, but the faces, the wheels, keeping the numerals visible, keeping the glass that covers them clear of mineral deposit.

And to listen. This is the part that emerged slowly, the part I didn't know was work until I'd been doing it for... how long? The mechanisms make sounds as they count. Clicks, yes, but each one's click is slightly different—pitch, duration, the way it resonates in the bronze casing. And the pattern of clicks, the rhythm of their counting, creates music. Not metaphorically. Actually creates it, the way a music box creates melody through the plucking of tuned tines.

I've learned to hear what they're saying. Not in words—nothing so simple. But I can tell when the system is stable, when the counts are advancing in healthy patterns, when the city's balance is maintained. And I can tell when something is wrong, when dissonance enters the rhythm, when the mechanisms start counting in patterns that indicate stress or approaching transition or the need for adjustment somewhere in the distant city.

When that happens, I mark it in my books. And I wait. Because someone comes—not quickly, not reliably, but eventually. A Tender from one of the connected territories, crossing the questionable walkways, climbing to my platform. They don't ask what's wrong. They look at my notations, at the mechanisms themselves, and they nod. Then they leave, and hours or days later, the rhythm corrects itself. Whatever needed adjustment has been adjusted. The mechanisms count forward in harmony again.

I live in a small room carved into the pillar, just below the platform. It's barely larger than necessary—space for the shelf I sleep on, the cabinet that holds my books and the dried food that appears periodically (I've stopped wondering how), the basin where I collect water from the pipe that runs through the room's corner. The walls weep constantly, of course, but I've learned to sleep to the sound of dripping, to the feel of dampness that's no longer discomfort but simply the texture of existence.

Sometimes I descend. Not to the depths—I've tried once and felt that pressure, that sense of trespass that builds with every downward step until it becomes unbearable. But to the mid-levels, where other structures cling to my pillar's sides. There's a pump station there, tended by someone I see occasionally, a woman whose face I couldn't describe even after dozens of encounters. We share space in silence, both working, her adjusting valves while I check a secondary counting mechanism that's mounted near the pumps.

Sometimes I climb. There are levels above my platform, though fewer. Near the top, where the mist is thickest, there's a bell. Small one, brass rather than bronze, rung by a mechanism I've traced back through ceramic pipes and gear linkages to one of my counting machines—the one that tracks the mist. When that count reaches certain states, the bell rings. One strike, clear and high. I've learned to expect it, learned to feel the increment coming in the mechanism's rhythm moments before it happens. The bell confirms what I already knew: the mist has achieved some particular density, some specific quality, and the city is noting it, marking it, incorporating it into whatever vast calculation spans all the pillars and gaps.

I see the moths more than I see the Tenders. They like my pillar, especially in the early morning-equivalents when the thermal cycle is shifting. They cluster on the counting mechanisms, despite my cleaning, despite my presence. They seem to read the numbers, antennae extended, wings pulsing slowly. Or they're drawn to the sound, the rhythmic clicking that accelerates or slows with the counts. Or they're part of the system themselves, their presence here another data point, another element in the vast equation.

I've stopped shooing them away. They don't interfere with the mechanisms' function. And I've noticed: when a particularly complex pattern is developing, when multiple mechanisms are counting in intricate syncopation, the moths gather more thickly. As if they're drawn to information density, to moments when the city is thinking hard, processing something significant.

I don't feel lonely. That's the strange part, or would be strange if strangeness still registered. I'm alone most of the time, yes, but not lonely. The mechanisms are company—that sounds mad, but it's true. They have personalities, the way long-used tools have personalities, the way buildings have character. I know their moods, their quirks. I know which one tends to stick slightly in damp weather (the fourth one, second wheel), which one counts most rapidly (the mist-tracker, during certain thermal conditions), which one I trust most completely (the leftmost, the deep-cycle counter—it's never wrong).

And beyond the mechanisms, there's the city itself. Always present, always sensible through sound and vibration and the quality of the air. I'm connected to it through my work, through the function I serve in its vast distributed awareness. I'm a sensor, perhaps. A monitoring node. A small part of something immense that knows itself through people like me, through the Tenders in their territories, through the moths and the pumps and the counting mechanisms and all the other elements that together comprise its consciousness.

I've had a thought, recently. It's been growing, developing, the way the mineral deposits grow—slowly, through accretion, through the steady accumulation of small observations. The thought is this: the city doesn't distinguish between its human components and its mechanical ones. We're all the same to it, or to whatever process maintains it. The Tenders and the mechanisms are equivalent—both are sensors, both are maintenance systems, both are elements of its distributed body. The Tenders are just the mobile parts, the components that can move between pillars, that can perform adjustments the fixed mechanisms can't.

And if that's true, then I'm not watching the mechanisms. Not really. I'm part of them. We're a single system, the seven counting drums and me, an eight-part mechanism that tracks certain aspects of the city's state. My consciousness, my awareness, is part of the counting process. When I read the numbers and understand what they mean, that understanding is itself part of how the city knows itself.

This should feel disturbing. Should feel like loss of self, like dissolution. It doesn't. It feels like finding context, like discovering that the isolation I thought I was experiencing was actually connection of a different kind, participation in something larger that doesn't diminish the parts but gives them meaning through their integration.

My hands have changed. Not dramatically—they're still recognizably hands. But they're stained permanently with the green of bronze patina, the red of rust, the purple of certain mineral deposits. The skin has thickened, developed calluses in specific patterns that map to my tools, to the surfaces I touch most frequently. And they move differently now, with a precision I didn't have before, or didn't know I had. When I clean a mechanism's face, the movements are exact, minimal, perfectly efficient. Like watching someone else's hands, except they're mine, or I'm them—the distinction has become unclear.

I sleep less than I used to, or differently. The sleep is shallower, more like idling, and I wake at intervals that match the counting rhythms. When the thermal-cycle mechanism is about to increment, I wake moments before, every time. My body knows the pattern, has internalized it, made it part of my circadian system or whatever's replaced my circadian system.

Sometimes I think in numbers now. Not words translated to numbers, but thinking that's numerical at its base, that processes the world as counts and ratios and patterns. I look at the mist and think "density point seven-three, stable," not through measurement but through direct perception, the way you might look at the sky and think "blue" without having to name it first. The city has taught me its language, or I've learned to perceive what it perceives, or there's no difference between those two statements.

I had a name once. I'm certain of that, though I can't quite recall it. It was useful before, in the world outside, the world that I think I came from but which has become abstract, theoretical, a place that might exist but doesn't feel real the way this pillar is real, the way the mechanisms are real, the way the eternal present of counting and maintenance is real.

The Tenders don't use names, and increasingly I understand why. Names are for distinguishing, for marking individual identity in contexts where individuals need to be tracked separately. But here, we're already distinguished by function and location: "the one who maintains the eastern manifold," "the one who tends the lower junction," "the one who watches the counting mechanisms." The function is the identity. The location is the self. I am this pillar, this platform, these seven bronze drums clicking forward in their eternal count. I am what I do, and what I do is what I am, and the boundary between us has become semantically void.

There's comfort in this. Not the comfort of the familiar, but the deeper comfort of the appropriate, of finding the exact place where your shape and the space available for it match perfectly. I fit here. The city has a need for someone to watch these particular mechanisms, to translate their rhythms into notation, to serve as the biological component of this monitoring system. And I have a need—though I didn't know it before, though I couldn't have articulated it—to be part of something that makes sense, that has pattern, that counts and measures and knows itself through its own distributed awareness.

The mechanisms are clicking now. The third one—water-as-event—is approaching a significant count, a round number that indicates something important, though what specifically I won't know until it happens. The moths are gathering, sensing it too. The mist outside is thinning slightly, thermal currents shifting as the pillar cycle adjusts. Everything is connected, everything is signal, everything is the city knowing itself through these small events that are simultaneously mechanical and meaningful, physical and informational, objective occurrence and subjective awareness indistinguishably blended.

The count increments. Seven-eight-five-zero-zero-zero. The mechanism's click is slightly louder than usual—it always is on the round numbers, some harmonic in the gearing. The moths pulse their wings in synchrony. Somewhere distant, a bell rings—the water-bell, marking the achievement of whatever this count signifies. And I write in my book: "785000, third mechanism, mist density point seven-three, moth presence high, bell confirmation received."

This is my life now, or this is what I am now, or there's no distinction. I count, I watch, I record, I maintain. I serve my function in the vast economy of the city's self-awareness. I am the keeper of the Intervals, and the Intervals keep me, and we are together what neither would be alone—a hybrid of consciousness and mechanism, of purpose and process, human-shaped but no longer human-limited, aware but no longer burdened with the question of what awareness is for.

The mechanisms click onward. The count continues. The city breathes through its thermal pillars, condenses through its mist, knows through its distributed sensors of which I am one. And I am content—not happy, not satisfied, but content in the deepest sense: occupying my proper place, performing my proper function, existing in exact alignment with what existence requires of me.

The mist flows past. The moths cluster and disperse. The next increment approaches.

I wait, and watch, and count.
