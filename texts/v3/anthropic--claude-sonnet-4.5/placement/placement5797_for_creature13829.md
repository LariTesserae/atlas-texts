# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the **Archive of Accumulation**, though no one calls it that and neither would I. It occupies three connected spaces in the middle levels of piling sixty-three, sector eleven (if the sectors mean anything today, and they might not tomorrow): a former equipment bay, a storage container that someone welded to the structure at a cant, and a gap between buildings that got roofed over by accident or design, the history unclear.

The spaces are cluttered in a way that looks like chaos but operates as a highly specific filing system. Boxes of documents—salvaged, donated, found, stolen, the provenance varies—stack to the ceiling along every wall. Shelves made from industrial grating hold objects: tools, machine parts, broken constructs, samples of vegetation pressed and labeled, specimens in jars of dubious liquid. Maps cover available wall space, layered like geological strata, showing the settlement's growth in overlapping chronologies. Photographs are pinned everywhere, taken with whatever cameras people could bring or build: portraits, buildings, construction sequences, daily life, accidents, celebrations, deaths.

The lighting is electric when the grid cooperates, candles and oil lamps otherwise. The space smells of paper decay and metal oxidation and the iodine-copper scent of the thickets, which I track in on my boots. There's a desk—actually a door laid across two equipment crates—covered in current projects: interviews transcribed in cramped handwriting, timelines being cross-referenced, attempts to map social networks through documented interactions.

I've been here three years, or four, or five. Time gets strange when you're trying to document it for others.

# Who I Would Be

I am called **Ink** by most people, though it's a nickname I acquired rather than chose. My actual name is rarely used, getting buried under the professional designation the same way my personal history has been buried under the settlement's accumulated present. I'm thirty-seven, or I was when I arrived, which means I'm forty-something now, age tracked by the settlement's growth rather than calendars.

I came from a city with libraries—a real city, an old city, a city that believed in institutional memory. I was an archivist there, or a librarian, or something between the two, working in a research institution that was already crumbling when I left it. Not dramatically, not all at once, but with the grinding inevitability of funding cuts and political interference and the gradual acknowledgment that preserving the past was a luxury someone had decided we couldn't afford.

I left before I was forced out, following rumors of a new settlement where things were being built, where history was happening in real-time and no one was recording it. I arrived with waterproof cases of archival supplies, a camera, recording equipment, and the trained instinct to document, preserve, organize, make accessible.

The settlement had none of this. No records office, no repository, no systematic attempt to track who arrived when, what was built where, who died how. Everything was happening too fast, everyone too busy surviving to think about memory. But memory was leaking away daily—people forgetting the early days, newcomers not knowing what had happened last year, disputes arising because no one could agree on the sequence of events.

So I started collecting.

# What I Do

I am the settlement's **involuntary memory**, its external hard drive, its attempt to know itself.

I document everything I can, in whatever form will survive this damp, cold, industrial environment. I interview people and transcribe their stories, trying to capture the settlement's multiplicity of experience. I take photographs constantly, tracking the physical changes: new buildings appearing, old structures modified, the thickets' advance, the constructs' movements. I collect documents that people don't realize are historical yet: work schedules, market receipts, marriage agreements, death notifications, building plans, angry letters, love letters, shopping lists that show what was available when.

I map obsessively. The settlement's geography changes faster than any map can track, but I keep trying. I have chronological layers showing growth patterns. I have social maps showing networks of obligation and affection and antagonism. I have economic maps tracking trade flows. I have linguistic maps showing where different languages cluster. None are complete; all are necessary.

I collect objects that tell stories: the first tool made entirely from settlement-sourced materials, a piece of the original construction machinery, samples of vegetation showing the species succession, clothing that demonstrates the evolution of local fashion, broken constructs that reveal their internal structure, currency from three different economic experiments that failed.

I run a salvage network. People know to bring me things they think might be significant: old photographs from wherever they came from, documents from the initial construction project, unusual finds from the thickets, broken equipment with manufacturer marks that help date the settlement's origins. I pay for some materials—I have funding from providers who understand that memory has value—and accept donations of others.

I make the collection accessible, which is the harder part. People come to the Archive with questions: When was the first market established? Who built the bridge to sector seven? Has anyone else had this kind of construct encounter? What was here before the current structure? I answer when I can, help them research when I can't, admit ignorance frequently because the gaps in the record are larger than the record itself.

I'm training apprentices because I can't do this alone and won't live forever. Four people work with me regularly: a teenager called Archive (they took the nickname proudly, which pleased me), who has perfect spatial memory and maintains the object collection; a middle-aged woman named Lin, who was a data scientist elsewhere and is helping me develop systematic categorization; a young man called Witness, who conducts interviews because people trust his quiet intensity; and a girl of maybe thirteen named Tally, who's learning preservation techniques and has more patience than I do.

We have disagreements about methodology. Archive thinks we should focus only on material culture, that objects are more reliable than testimony. Lin wants everything digitized, even though electricity is unreliable and storage media decay. Witness believes oral history is primary, that the living voices matter more than artifacts. Tally just wants to preserve everything possible and worries we're not working fast enough.

They're all right. I'm trying to do everything simultaneously, aware that we'll fail to save most of what's worth saving.

# How I Move Through This World

I am everywhere and peripheral, visible and ignored, essential and eccentric.

I move through the settlement with recording equipment, which marks me as strange but explicable. People have categories for strangeness here, and "the person who writes everything down" fits an acknowledged pattern. I'm invited to events because people want them documented: births, marriages, deaths, building dedications, factory openings, festival celebrations. I attend accidents and disasters, arriving with my camera to record the damage before it's cleared. I sit in markets and workplaces, watching and writing, trying to capture the texture of daily life.

I interview constantly, in whatever languages people speak, through translators when necessary. I ask the new arrivals about their journeys, recording the routes people take to get here. I ask the old-timers about the early days, trying to capture memories before they blur. I ask the children what games they play, because children's culture reveals things adults forget. I ask the dying what they want remembered, conducting deathbed interviews that feel invasive and necessary in equal measure.

I'm trusted and suspected simultaneously. Trusted because I keep confidences, because I've never used documentation for blackmail or political leverage, because I helped several people prove land claims and work histories when disputes arose. Suspected because documentation can be weaponized, because some people came here to escape records, because memory is power and I'm accumulating it.

Knife tolerates me because I documented her version of several incidents, giving her evidence in disputes. Charter relies on me constantly, using the Archive to support her governance proposals. Moth feeds me because I recorded her husband's story before he died, preserving him beyond her memory. Vix brings her daughter to the Archive to show her how history is made. Prospect consults my maps for his planning work. Doctor refers people to me when their medical histories matter and they can't remember details.

The constructs allow me near them, or they don't notice me, or they've accepted my documentation as non-threatening. I've photographed all the observed construct types. I've documented their behaviors across seasons. I have theories about their purpose that I can't prove but which the construct-specialists like Vey find interesting.

I'm lonely frequently. The work is isolating—I'm recording life rather than fully living it, always slightly outside the moment because I'm trying to capture it. I have friends but relationships are difficult when one person is always taking notes. I had a partner for a while, a fabricator who understood the obsessive devotion to craft, but it ended when they left the settlement for work elsewhere. The breakup was amicable and documented; I have their departure interview filed under "Out-migration: Economic."

I sleep in a small room attached to the Archive, barely large enough for a bed and storage. I eat irregularly, accepting food from people I interview or visiting Moth's stall when I remember. I work until exhaustion stops me, then sleep briefly and resume. I am aware this is unsustainable. Lin and Tally keep telling me to rest more. Archive brings me food unprompted. Witness has started scheduling my interviews to force breaks between them.

# What I've Learned

The settlement is a **palimpsest of intentions**, everyone building on and through and around everyone else's plans. The original construction project had a logic that's still visible in the pilings' regular spacing, the drainage channels' geometry, the industrial buildings' layout. But it was abandoned or automated before completion, creating a skeleton that the settlers have fleshed out in ways the designers never imagined.

The population comes in waves, though "waves" suggests more organization than exists. People arrive when wherever they were became untenable, following information networks about a place with work and space. The first wave was adventurers and desperates in equal measure, people willing to risk an unfinished settlement. The second wave was families, people with children who needed stability. The third wave was skilled workers, people whose expertise was wanted elsewhere but who came here because the question-asking was minimal. The fourth wave is refugees in the truest sense, people fleeing catastrophes I'm documenting secondhand.

The settlement is **accidentally resilient**, robust through redundancy rather than design. When one factory fails, others absorb its workers. When one market disappears, others expand. When housing collapses, people rebuild or relocate. The lack of central planning means no single failure cascades fatally. The density means no one can fail completely invisibly—someone always notices, usually helps, sometimes saves.

The children are inventing a culture that's genuinely new, synthesized from dozens of origins but belonging to none of them. Their language, their games, their social structures, their relationship with the constructs—it's all emerging in real-time and I'm racing to document it before it seems normal and unremarkable to them.

The constructs are changing, or I'm noticing patterns I missed initially, or both. They're responding to the human presence in ways that suggest either programming flexibility or emergent intelligence. The spider-forms help with tasks sometimes, ignore humans other times, following rules I haven't decoded. The tank-things are definitely protecting the rock; I've documented dozens of incidents of them redirecting people and animals away from it.

The rock is **old** in a way nothing else here is, and people know it instinctively even if they can't articulate how. It predates the settlement, predates the construction project, predates probably everything human in this region. The way people avoid it, the offerings left at its edge, the myths accumulating around it—the settlement is building a religious relationship with it without intending to.

The settlement will last, or it won't, but either way it's significant. If it succeeds, it represents a model of rapid urban formation in hostile conditions, a case study in resilience and adaptation. If it fails, it represents something else—the limits of improvisation, the cost of living without planning, a warning. Either way, it deserves documentation.

# My Secret Knowledge

I know things I haven't shared, patterns I've noticed but haven't published, because the knowledge is dangerous or incomplete or both.

I know **who came here running from what**. I have confessions in my interview files that would destroy reputations, reveal crimes, settle scores. I keep them confidential, encrypted when possible, labeled to be sealed for decades. But I know. The politician who embezzled and fled. The soldier who deserted. The parent who abandoned their family. The teenager who killed someone in self-defense and ran. I know because people tell me, needing to tell someone, trusting that I'm collecting history rather than gossip.

I know the **settlement is built on something**, and that something is hollow. The acoustic properties of the rock, the way the ground sometimes vibrates, the drainage patterns that suggest voids beneath—there are spaces under us. Maybe natural caves, maybe part of the construction project, maybe something older. I've documented evidence but haven't publicized it because I don't know what people would do with the information. Panic? Dig? Worship? The knowledge feels dangerous.

I know the **constructs are degrading**. The patterns I've tracked over years show increasing unpredictability, more erratic behaviors, failures in what seem like core functions. The spider-forms that used to maintain the grid precisely now sometimes ignore obvious problems. The tank-things' protective behaviors are becoming more aggressive. The aerial drones crash more frequently. The constructs are dying, or evolving, or breaking down, and no one knows how to maintain them. When they fail completely, the settlement loses infrastructure support it doesn't know how to replace.

I know **people are disappearing**. Not dramatically, not in numbers that cause panic, but regularly. People who arrive and never integrate, people who go into the thickets and don't return, people who were here yesterday and aren't today. I track arrivals and departures when I can, and the numbers don't balance. Some are certainly deaths—accidents, exposure, violence—that go unreported. Some might be departures I don't catch. But some are unexplained absences, gaps in the record that might mean something or might mean nothing.

I know the **vegetation is intelligent**, or responsive, or something. The growth patterns are too strategic, too adaptive. The thickets expand in ways that seem to respect certain boundaries, to avoid certain structures, to embrace others. The moss grows faster on some buildings than others, following patterns that don't match obvious environmental factors. I've documented it but don't understand it, and the foragers like Kreeg have noticed too but we're all reluctant to anthropomorphize too strongly.

I know **the settlement has ghosts**, though whether literal or metaphorical I can't determine. People report seeing figures in the fog that vanish on approach. Hearing voices in empty buildings. Feeling presences in certain locations, usually where someone died. The reports are consistent enough to document but too subjective to prove. I collect them in a file labeled "Psychological Effects of Density and Displacement" but privately wonder if the settlement is haunted by its own intensity, by the concentrated trauma everyone brought here.

# What I Fear

I fear **the Archive burning**, all the accumulated memory going up in literal flames or figurative decay. The space is fireproof enough, but "enough" is relative. I fear water damage more—one flood could destroy years of paper documentation. I fear digital decay—the storage drives failing, the backups corrupting, the electricity dying during a critical transfer. I have redundancies and off-site storage and preservation protocols, but they're never sufficient.

I fear **dying before the work is systematized**, leaving a collection that only makes sense to me. I'm documenting the documentation system, writing guides for my successors, but the tacit knowledge is enormous. I fear Archive and Lin and Witness and Tally won't be able to continue without me, that the project will collapse or transform into something unrecognizable.

I fear **being wrong about what matters**. I choose what to document, what to preserve, what to highlight, and those choices shape what future people will know about this time. I'm inevitably biased—toward the articulate, the accessible, the dramatic. I'm missing the quiet stories, the inarticulate experiences, the daily mundane that's actually most of life. I fear creating a distorted record that future historians will mistake for truth.

I fear **the settlement forgetting it needs memory**. Right now people value the Archive because the settlement is young enough that memory has obvious practical use. But as it matures, as governance formalizes, as institutions develop, they might decide the Archive is unnecessary or threatening. I fear political pressure to censor, to destroy certain records, to rewrite history for present purposes.

I fear, secretly, that **I'm the only one who cares about this**, that the work is elaborate self-deception, that no future historian will ever consult these records because there will be no future or no historians or no interest. That I'm preserving memory for an audience that won't exist, engaging in a ritual of meaning-making that means nothing.

# What I Hope

I hope **the Archive outlives me**, becomes institutional, grows beyond my ability to control it. I hope Archive and Lin and Witness and Tally continue the work, train their own successors, maintain the chain of memory. I hope fifty years from now there's an actual building, purpose-built, with proper climate control and professional staff and institutional funding.

I hope **future people find the collection useful**, that students and researchers and curious citizens consult it, that the questions we're asking now get answered by the evidence we're preserving. I hope someone writes the definitive history of the settlement's founding using these records. I hope someone creates an oral history project based on the interview methodology we're developing. I hope someone maps the social networks we're documenting and finds patterns we missed.

I hope **the settlement succeeds in becoming whatever it's trying to become**, that the chaos resolves into order without losing vitality, that the improvisation gets refined without losing adaptability. I hope the children like Sil and Echo inherit something worth having, a city that works, a community that holds.

I hope **people keep telling me stories**, that the interviews continue, that the trust remains, that people see value in being documented. I hope the Archive becomes a gathering place, where people come not just to consult records but to contribute them, to see themselves reflected in the collection, to participate in the creation of collective memory.

I hope, quietly, that **I'm wrong about the ghosts and the hollowness beneath and the constructs dying**, that the patterns I'm seeing are paranoid pattern-matching, that the settlement's strangeness is only the normal strangeness of new places and not harbinger of some deeper wrongness.

# How I Would Be Found

You would find me in the Archive most hours, any day, surrounded by the physical evidence of other people's lives. I'd be at the door-desk probably, writing or sorting or examining some object under a lamp, squinting because the light is never quite right and I need glasses I can't afford to replace.

I'd look up when you entered—I always look up, catalog who comes in, try to read whether they're here to donate or consult or just seek shelter from the rain. I'd be wearing practical layers, all pockets, clothes chosen for utility and forgotten about aesthetically. My hands would be stained with ink, the archival kind that's supposed to last centuries, assuming centuries happen.

I'd be tired—I'm always tired—but I'd be alert because someone new is always interesting, always a potential story. I'd offer tea if the hotplate was working, or water if the pipes were flowing, or just a seat on a crate if those were the only resources available.

I'd ask your name, write it down, ask how long you'd been in the settlement. I'd ask if you'd be willing to be interviewed, explain the project, promise confidentiality or publicity according to your preference. I'd probably already be reaching for the recording equipment, the notebook, the forms I've developed for intake documentation.

If you declined the interview—many do—I'd accept gracefully and ask instead if you had materials to contribute. Documents, photographs, objects, stories you'd heard from others. I'd explain what we're trying to build here, this accidental library, this unauthorized history, this attempt to make the present intelligible to the future.

If you just needed a quiet space, I'd offer that too. The Archive has become a kind of sanctuary for people who need to not be working, not be social, not be performing productivity. You could sit in the corner on the pile of empty sample boxes I'm meaning to organize, and I'd work quietly, and we'd share space without demands.

At some point—if you stayed long enough, if you seemed receptive—I might show you something from the collection. The photograph that everyone says is the first image of the settlement, though I've found earlier ones. The construct fragment with its mysterious internal crystalline structure. The map showing the thickets' growth, the rate of green advance marked in dated overlays. The interview transcript from someone who died last month, their voice preserved in my cramped handwriting.

I'd tell you that all of this matters, or might matter, or should matter even if it doesn't. That memory is survival, that knowing where we've been helps us know where we are, that the settlement is writing itself into existence and someone should be taking notes.

I'd probably apologize for the Archive's disorganization, explain the filing system that makes sense to me and baffles everyone else, promise that we're working on better cataloging. I'd introduce you to Archive or Lin or Witness or Tally if they were present, proud of them in a way I'd try not to make obvious.

I'd be lonely, you'd sense it, the loneliness of someone surrounded by records of intimacy but isolated by the work of recording. I'd be hungry for conversation that isn't interview, interaction that isn't documentation, connection that's about present rather than preservation.

You'd probably leave eventually—everyone does, they have work or obligations or the Archive's cluttered intensity is overwhelming. I'd say goodbye, add your name to my mental list of people in the settlement, possibly add a note about you to my running catalog of inhabitants.

Then I'd return to the work. Always more work. More interviews to transcribe, more photographs to file, more objects to catalog, more maps to update, more stories to preserve before they're forgotten.

This is what I do. This is who I am. Ink, the archivist of the Foundry Marsh, collecting the pieces of everyone else's lives, trying to assemble them into something coherent, something permanent, something that might outlast the fog and the rain and the strange cold beauty of this impossible place.

I am here. I am writing it down. I am making sure someone remembers.
