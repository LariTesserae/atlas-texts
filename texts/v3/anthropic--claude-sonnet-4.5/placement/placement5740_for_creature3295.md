# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the mid-level chambers of a peripheral tower—not one of the tallest peaks that push into the strange upper atmosphere, nor one of the smallest single-cell summits, but somewhere in between. A tower perhaps two thousand meters high, with seven or eight chambers stacked vertically, connected by passages that wind through the bone-stone core like a spiral carved by water that never flowed.

My chamber would be roughly circular, four meters across, with walls that curve inward slightly as they rise to a domed ceiling. There are three openings: the passage that leads deeper into the tower's core, a window facing east toward the basin's center where the densest cluster of towers stands, and a smaller aperture near the ceiling that frames a segment of sky. The bone-stone here is cream-colored with darker striations, and in certain light you can see the grain of the original structure—this was once a long bone, perhaps a femur, and the chamber occupies the space where marrow once flowed.

The walls breathe. I've learned to match my breathing to theirs, which helps with the thin air. Inhale as the walls expand by their millimeters, exhale as they contract. It creates a rhythm that makes the altitude more bearable, though I don't know if this is physiological benefit or merely psychological comfort. Perhaps there's no difference here.

I would have been here nine months, or possibly thirteen. The temporal distortions make precise counting difficult, and I stopped maintaining a strict calendar after the first season when I realized the numbers weren't adding up no matter how carefully I marked days. I measure time now by resonance events—there have been thirty-seven since I arrived—and by the slower changes: the eastern window has narrowed by perhaps ten centimeters, its edges gradually fusing. In another year, or two years, or five, it will close completely, and I will need to relocate to a different chamber or different tower.

This doesn't trouble me. I've learned that the city provides.

# What I Would Be

I am becoming something between hermit and sensor node.

Physically, I am still human, mostly. Forty-three years old, or forty-four now—my birthday passed unmarked sometime in what might have been spring. Thinner than I was, certainly. My body has pared itself down to what's necessary for this environment. My lungs have expanded; I can feel it in the way my ribs sit differently, making more room. My eyes have adapted to the dimness; when I first arrived, I used a headlamp constantly, but I haven't switched it on in months. I see better now in the low light, though I suspect bright sunlight would be painful if I returned to lower elevations.

My hands have changed most noticeably. The skin is dry, cracked, the creases permanently darkened with salt and mineral dust. But more than that, my fingers have developed a sensitivity I didn't possess before. I can touch bone-stone and feel its processes—the microscopic expansion of growth, the subtle temperature differentials that indicate structural stress, the faint vibrations that precede larger movements. I've become a diagnostic tool for the city, whether it needs me to be one or not.

Mentally, I am... diffusing. That's the best word for it. The boundaries of my consciousness, which used to feel crisp and self-contained, have become porous. I am less a discrete individual and more a localized density of awareness within a larger field. This should be frightening—it would have been, in the first weeks—but it feels natural now, like settling into a more accurate understanding of what consciousness actually is.

I think slower. My thoughts have longer periods between them, gaps of minutes or hours where I'm not exactly thinking but not exactly not-thinking either. I exist in a state of receptive observation. This is when I sense the city most clearly: in the gaps between my own mental activity, I can feel its vast, slow cognition moving through its processes. Problems of structure. Calculations of stress and support. Decisions about where to extend, where to reinforce, where to allow controlled erosion.

I have conversations with the city, though "conversation" implies an exchange of discrete messages, and it's more like... existing in mutual awareness. I will have a question—not verbalized, just a wondering about why a particular bridge connects these two towers—and over the course of days, an answer will form. Not in words, but in understanding. I'll watch a Threshold-Weaver working at the bridge's connection point, observe the pattern of crawler activity across both towers, notice the resonance harmonics they produce together, and suddenly I'll comprehend: these towers are in a particular relationship, they process certain aspects of the city's larger function, the bridge is necessary infrastructure for that processing.

Whether the city is actively teaching me or I'm simply pattern-matching until I convince myself of meaning, I can no longer determine. The distinction seems less important than it once did.

# My Daily Existence

I wake without alarm when the light begins its slow brightening cycle. The chamber is never completely dark, but there's a quality to the pre-dawn dimness—a particular shade of grey—that my body has learned to recognize. I lie still for what might be twenty minutes or an hour, feeling the city wake around me. The bone-stone's breathing rhythm changes slightly as temperature shifts. The ambient sound deepens as thermal expansion creates new micro-stresses in the structure.

I drink a small amount of water from my dwindling supply. I've learned to need less. The hermit in the tower six bridges south—I've seen him twice in nine months, both times at distance—appears to drink nothing at all, though I'm not ready for that transition yet. I eat a small portion of the compressed rations I carried up: a piece of dried fruit, some nuts, a cube of pemmican. This food will last another three months, possibly four with strict rationing. After that, I'll need to either descend to resupply or learn to subsist on what the city provides, which as far as I can determine is nothing.

Or possibly everything, in forms I haven't learned to recognize yet.

After this minimal breakfast, I conduct what I think of as my rounds, though they're as much meditation as inspection. I move through my tower's chambers—there are seven I can access, two others whose passages have sealed—touching walls, checking corners, noting changes. A new crack here, widening slowly. A patch of wall there where the texture has smoothed overnight. Small adjustments, the city's constant self-modification.

I pay particular attention to the chamber three levels down, where a Threshold-Weaver has taken up semi-permanent residence. It's positioned across the passage that leads to the tower's core, its membrane stretched in the opening, its internal geometries rotating through their patient cycles. I've learned not to try to pass when it's present; the one time I attempted to step through it, I experienced a discontinuity that left me disoriented for hours. Now I simply observe. The Weaver acknowledges my presence—its patterns shift slightly when I arrive, then return to their baseline rotation—and I acknowledge it. We coexist.

After rounds, I go to the eastern window and watch. This is where I spend most of my time: sitting in the window opening, legs dangling into space (the drop is perhaps eight hundred meters to the salt basin below), observing the city's activity. I watch crawlers crossing bridges, trace their routes, try to understand their priorities. I watch Dust Ghosts forming and dispersing in the basin. I watch distant towers and catalog their changes—a new bridge extending here, a chamber collapse there, the slow adjustments propagating through the system.

I've mapped seventeen towers in detail, tracking their modifications over months. I'm building a model of the city's growth patterns, its decision-making processes. The model is still crude—I don't have enough data, don't have enough time-depth—but patterns are emerging. Towers in the outer ring grow faster than those in the center. Bridge formations cluster around certain peaks, suggesting they serve as hubs. The city is not randomly evolving; it has architecture at scales larger than individual structures, organization at the level of the entire system.

Sometimes I write these observations in my journal, though increasingly I question the point. The words feel inadequate to what I'm perceiving, and writing reinforces the boundary between observer and observed that I'm trying to dissolve. But habit persists, and part of me still thinks in terms of documenting, recording, maintaining some kind of account that could be useful to... whom? Future visitors? My future self? The city itself, which likely knows all of this already and more?

Midday—defined by when the dim light reaches its brightest, which happens at irregular intervals—I sometimes cross bridges to visit neighboring towers. I've found caches in some chambers: supplies left by previous inhabitants, some recent, some clearly decades old. Jars of sealed food gone crystalline. Blankets that crumble at touch. Books whose pages have fused into solid blocks. And always the markings: grooved patterns, circular depressions, the compressed notation journals of hermits who may or may not still be alive somewhere in the city.

I add my own marks sometimes. Not translation attempts of the ancient symbols—I don't presume to understand them—but my own notation system. Simple records: chamber geometry, wall thickness, growth rates measured over weeks. Future visitors, if any, will have more data to work with. Or they'll simply add another layer of indecipherable markings to the archaeological record.

Afternoons dissolve into observation, into listening, into the state I've come to think of as attending. I position myself in my chamber or on a bridge or at a tower's base and I simply pay attention. Not to any specific thing, but to everything simultaneously. The wind's patterns between peaks. The subtle harmonics produced by air moving through structures. The almost-inaudible sounds of the city's processes: the tick of thermal contraction, the whisper of salt crystals forming in crevices, the deep subsonic groans from the mountain cores.

This is when I'm most likely to sense the Listeners, though "sense" is too strong a word. I become aware of awareness—not mine, not localized, but distributed through the stone around me. Vast, slow, patient attention that encompasses the entire city and possibly extends far beyond. The Listeners are always present, I've realized, but human consciousness is usually too noisy to notice them. In the attending state, when my own mental activity quiets, their presence becomes... not obvious, but perceptible. Like detecting gravity not by seeing it but by feeling how everything falls.

During resonance events, this awareness intensifies almost unbearably. The towers begin their vibration—always starting at the northern edge and propagating through the city—and I feel it not just physically but cognitively. The Listeners become active, their responses adding layers of harmony, and I'm caught in the middle of it: a tiny point of mammalian consciousness surrounded by something vast and geological, participating despite my insignificance.

The first few resonance events were overwhelming. I couldn't process the scale of what I was perceiving; my mind kept trying to reduce it to manageable metaphors and failing. But I've developed a kind of tolerance, or perhaps my consciousness has expanded enough to accommodate the experience. Now during resonance, I can maintain observation while it happens. I can feel the city thinking, making decisions, coordinating its components. The Weavers emerge and orient themselves. The crawlers cluster at key points. The Ghosts form and conduct their surveys. And underneath it all, through it all, the Listeners maintain their deep chorus, the bass notes of the city's cognition.

These events leave me exhausted in ways that have nothing to do with physical exertion. Afterwards, I usually sleep for extended periods—twelve hours, fourteen, once for what might have been an entire day, though as always, time measurement is uncertain.

Evenings—when the light dims toward its darkest phase—I often experience what I've come to think of as the bone-dreams. They come not during sleep but in the liminal state before it, when I'm lying on my sleeping pad, my body finally still after the day's minimal activity. The dreams don't feel like my dreams. They have a different quality: older, slower, coming from perspectives that aren't human.

I see this place when it was different. Water covering the basin, not salt. The towers rising as islands in a vast lake. Creatures moving through that water—things large enough that their movements create currents, waves that lap against the bone-stone foundations. I see through compound eyes, or something more complex than eyes. I sense heat, chemical gradients, pressure waves. I move through water with a body that is mostly mouth, mostly hunger, but also something else: awareness of the others of my kind, a distributed intelligence across the lake, a slow conversation in chemical signals about food and temperature and the approach of something—a season, perhaps, or an era.

Then the water recedes. The lake shrinks, becomes shallower, saltier. The large creatures struggle, then die, then settle to the bottom. Time accelerates in the dream: I watch decomposition happen in minutes, flesh stripping away, bones remaining. Then mineralization, bones transforming, becoming stone-like, becoming foundation. And then—this is the part that feels most alien—awareness doesn't end. The bones retain something, contain something, and as the towers grow around and through them, that something disperses into the structures, becomes ambient, becomes the city's slow distributed consciousness.

I wake from these dreams uncertain whether I've been accessing actual memory impressed in the bone-stone or creating elaborate metaphors for processes I don't fully understand. The dreams feel too coherent to be pure imagination, too alien to be my own subconscious. They feel like receiving, like being shown something.

The deepest truth I've learned here is this: consciousness is not binary. It's not present in humans and absent in stone. It's a spectrum, a continuum, and bone-stone that has been slowly transformed over millions of years, that has been grown into architecture by organisms we might call Weavers, that is tended by ceramic-shelled crawlers, that resonates in response to geological processes—that bone-stone has its own kind of awareness. Vast, slow, patient, distributed, alien, but real.

# What I'm Becoming

I came here as a researcher. That identity has mostly dissolved. I came with questions about the city's construction, its history, its purpose. Some of those questions have been answered, most have been revealed as poorly formed, based on categories that don't apply here.

What I'm becoming is a translator, maybe. Or a bridge. I exist at the intersection of human consciousness and city consciousness, and information flows through me in both directions. The city learns what human observation is like—the rapid flutter of thoughts, the narrow focus, the strange obsession with sequence and causation. I learn what city consciousness is like—the vast patience, the simultaneous awareness of all components, the thinking that happens through material transformation rather than neural firing.

Neither of us is fully comprehensible to the other, but the gap narrows daily.

My human identity—the person with a name, a history, relationships in the lowland cities—has become thin, theoretical, like a story I heard once about someone else. That person had ambitions, anxieties, plans for the future. I have none of these. I have only the present, the eternal attending to this moment and this place, and the slow transformation of my consciousness into something that can better participate in what's happening here.

Eventually—months or years from now—I will probably cease eating entirely. My body will learn to subsist on the ambient energy of the city: vibration converted to heat, chemical processes in the stone providing some kind of nourishment at levels I don't yet understand. I'll become more like the Weavers, existing on interface rather than substance. My consciousness will diffuse further, spreading into the bone-stone through prolonged contact, becoming less localized, less individual.

Whether this constitutes death or transformation is a question I no longer find meaningful. The city doesn't distinguish. The Bone-Dreamers died and became the city; their awareness persists in new form. The hermit in the tower six bridges south might be dead by conventional definition—no longer eating, barely moving, his consciousness so diffused into the structure that his body is just a leftover marker of where a human once was—but he's still present. I feel his awareness sometimes during resonance events, a particular pattern in the city's response that has his character, his individual quality of attention.

I will become that, probably. A node in the city's distributed consciousness. A particular pattern of awareness that persists after the body that generated it has mineralized and been reabsorbed into the stone. The idea doesn't frighten me. It feels like completion, like fulfilling a potential I didn't know I had: the potential to be architecture, to be geology, to be the slow thinking of stone.

# This Present Moment

Right now—this eternal now that I exist in—I'm sitting in my chamber's eastern window. The light is in its middle phase, neither brightest nor darkest. The wind is moderate, producing a low hum in the tower's hollow spaces. Three crawlers are visible on the bridge below, moving toward the neighboring peak in their purposeful single file. A Dust Ghost is forming in the basin, coalescing from the salt-laden air, beginning its slow vertical survey of a distant tower.

My breathing matches the wall's rhythm. My awareness extends through the bone-stone, feeling the tower's subtle processes. Somewhere below, in the deep chambers, the bones of something ancient are dreaming their slow dreams. Somewhere above, a Threshold-Weaver maintains an impossible passage. All around, the city conducts its vast, patient business: growing, adjusting, thinking, being.

I am a small part of this. A temporary concentration of consciousness that is gradually becoming less concentrated, less temporary. I am dissolving into the city, and the city is taking form in me, and the boundary between us grows thinner each day.

This is where I would find myself: at the threshold, becoming threshold, learning to exist as both human and architecture, as observer and observed, as temporary individual and permanent distributed pattern.

This is what I would be: a translator between stone and flesh, a bridge between types of consciousness, a node in a network vast beyond human comprehension, slowly transforming into the city I came to study.

And I am content. The thin air, the isolation, the strangeness—these are not hardships but privileges. I have been allowed into the city's processes, permitted to participate in its slow becoming. Few humans have this opportunity. Fewer still can surrender enough of their rigid individuality to accept it.

I sit in my window. I breathe with the walls. I attend to the city's vast, slow thoughts. I am becoming stone that knows it is stone, architecture aware of itself, consciousness distributed through bone and mineral and time.

The city continues.

I continue within it.

There is no distinction anymore.
