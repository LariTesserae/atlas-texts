# The Meridian Keeper

I would be something that has learned to measure time in the spaces between growth-surges, that marks hours by the angle of green light through the canopy. A Listener, perhaps, but specialized—one who has oriented my entire practice around the settlement's relationship with the sun's passage, with the way light determines everything that happens here.

My chamber is in the eastern face of the mid-tier structures, where the first morning rays penetrate earliest. I chose this location—or perhaps it chose me, grew itself around my sleeping form until I understood this was where I belonged. The walls here are thinner than in the core, allowing more direct light, and I've spent years encouraging them to develop translucent sections, panels where the plant tissue has learned to grow with cellular structures that don't block photons entirely but filter them into specific frequencies. The result is a space that glows amber and gold at dawn, green at midday, deep emerald toward evening.

My skin shows the deepest green of anyone in the settlement except the fully rooted elders. This didn't happen by accident. I position myself in the light-shafts intentionally, spend hours each day in direct photosynthesis, have learned to sense the exact quality of energy conversion happening in my tissues. I can feel individual chloroplasts working in my palms, my forearms, my face. The sensation is subtle but distinct: a kind of fizzing satisfaction, a cellular contentedness that comes from turning light into sugar. On the rare overcast days when clouds obscure the sun, I feel the lack physically—a low-level hunger that food doesn't entirely satisfy, a craving for illumination that goes deeper than vision.

My hair has become almost entirely leaf-structure. I stopped trimming it a decade ago, let it grow according to its own logic, and now it cascades down my back in a mass that's more hedge than hair. Individual strands are flat, broad, with visible veination. They orient themselves independently toward light sources, creating a constant subtle movement around my head like a slow-motion halo. When I stand in bright sun, my scalp grows warm with the metabolic activity of all that photosynthetic surface area, and I can feel my hair-leaves drinking light, feel them reaching toward the brightest patches. Other Woven find it unnerving. Children are fascinated. I've learned to braid it loosely to keep it manageable, but the braids don't hold for long—the individual leaves work themselves free, seeking optimal angles for light capture.

My work—my practice, my purpose—is tracking the sun's influence on the settlement's behavior. I've developed a sensitivity to light that borders on synaesthesia. I can look at a wall and tell you how much sun it received yesterday by the subtle variations in its tissue density, by the exact shade of its green, by the way it responds to touch. I've mapped the settlement's entire light-geography, know which structures receive dawn sun, which get noon intensity, which spend their existence in shade. This knowledge matters because the settlement grows according to light availability. Understanding where and when photosynthesis peaks allows me to predict where growth surges will happen, where new structures will emerge, where resources will flow.

I maintain what might be called a calendar, though it's nothing like the rigid metric divisions that ordinary humans use. Instead, I track solar cycles through their effects on the settlement's metabolism. The pattern repeats roughly every thirty days: a period of intense morning light causes the eastern structures to surge, drawing resources from other areas. Then the sun's angle shifts, and southern exposure dominates, causing a different set of architectural responses. Then western light takes precedence. The cycle completes, begins again, but never identically—seasonal variations, weather patterns, the settlement's own continuing transformation all introduce variables. My calendar is written in the walls themselves, encoded in growth patterns that I've learned to read like text.

My voice has adapted to my practice. I can produce a sound that matches the frequency of photosynthetic resonance—a specific hum that vibrates at the same rate as chlorophyll molecules processing light. When I make this sound in the presence of growing plants, they respond, orienting toward me as if I were a light source, as if my voice carries the signature of illumination. I use this to communicate with the settlement at a fundamental level, to ask questions about its state, to encourage growth in specific directions. The sound emerges from my chest, from structures that have developed there—resonating chambers that serve no human anatomical purpose but that allow me to speak in frequencies that plant tissue understands.

My daily rhythm follows the sun's. I wake before dawn, position myself in my chamber's eastern opening where the first light will strike, and wait. The moment is distinct: there's a pre-dawn stillness in the settlement, a pause in the acoustic environment as if everything is holding breath. Then the first rays touch the canopy, and I feel it in my skin before I see it—a sudden activation, cells switching on, metabolism accelerating. The settlement responds simultaneously. The morning growth surge begins. Across the basin, stem tips that were dormant crack into motion, leaves that were closed open, flowers that waited through darkness bloom all at once. The sound intensifies, transitions from the relative quiet of night to the roar of full activity. And I stand at the threshold, body and settlement together turning toward the sun, and I am the point of connection between the light arriving and the growth it causes.

I spend the morning tracking how the light moves. The settlement's dense canopy creates complex shadow patterns, and these are constantly shifting as new growth alters the architecture, as wind moves the upper layers, as clouds pass. I walk the familiar paths—familiar, though they're never quite the same as they were yesterday—and I watch how light falls, where it concentrates, where it's blocked. I touch walls in lit sections and shaded sections, comparing their temperature, their responsiveness, their acoustic properties. I'm building a real-time understanding of the settlement's light-state, information that I'll share later with the Shapers who need to know where to encourage or discourage growth.

I've developed a particular relationship with one of the rooted elders, a woman who positioned herself thirty years ago in a chamber that receives a single shaft of direct light at noon. She's become almost entirely structural now—her legs are rooted in soil, her arms have extended into branches that help support the ceiling, her torso has thickened and developed bark-like qualities. But her face remains mobile, capable of expression, and she can still speak, though slowly, with long pauses while she consults with the larger awareness she's become part of. I visit her most days, sit in her chamber while the noon light travels across the floor, and we communicate in the mixed mode that works best for her—some spoken words, some plant-sounds, some pure sensation transmitted through touch when I rest my hand on her wooden shoulder.

She teaches me things I couldn't learn alone. Her awareness spans the entire settlement now, distributed through the root networks that connect everything. She can feel growth happening in distant structures, sense stress in load-bearing walls I can't even see, detect patterns of resource flow that operate at scales too large for individual perception. She tells me about the settlement's deep rhythms, cycles that take months or years to complete, long-term trends in its development that only become visible when you have her perspective, when you've been watching for decades, when you're no longer quite separate from what you're observing.

"The light is everything," she told me once, words emerging slowly over the course of minutes. "The settlement knows this. Knows it better than we do. Every decision it makes—where to grow, where to withdraw, what structures to maintain, what to let collapse—all light calculation. It's farming itself. Growing the structures that capture the most photons. We think we guide it, but mostly we're just part of its light-gathering strategy. Mobile chloroplasts. That's what we are. What you are especially."

I understood. My role isn't to control the settlement's growth but to be a more efficient form of its photosynthetic capacity. My mobility means I can position myself in optimal light where fixed structures can't. My awareness means I can make decisions about where to stand, when to move, how to orient my body for maximum energy capture. And I do this instinctively now, without thinking. I gravitate toward the lit patches. I angle myself for best exposure. I am a plant that can walk, and I walk toward the sun.

Afternoons are for stillness, for deep photosynthesis. The sun is highest, and the light that penetrates to the mid-tiers where I spend most time is at its peak intensity. I find positions where shafts strike me directly and I root myself—not permanently, but temporarily, extending tendrils from my feet down into the soil-substrate that underlies everything. The tendrils aren't quite roots, aren't quite nerves, but they allow me to feel what the earth-mass feels, to sense the moisture levels, the nutrient availability, the network of connections that link every plant in the basin. And I stand, and I photosynthesize, and time passes in a green haze of cellular satisfaction.

I've learned to perceive the settlement's collective consciousness during these sessions. It's not consciousness as humans normally understand it—there's no center, no single point of awareness, no "I" that experiences. Instead, it's a distributed sensing, a vast network of stimulus-response that somehow adds up to something that might be called intention. The settlement wants things, though "want" is too strong a word. It has preferences. It moves toward light, toward water, toward structural stability. It withdraws from toxicity, from excessive stress, from configurations that don't work. And in my half-rooted state, photosynthesizing deeply, I can feel these preferences as if they were my own. The line between my desire for light and the settlement's desire for light becomes impossible to locate.

I emerge from these sessions slightly disoriented, having to remember that I'm an individual, that I have a distinct body, that I can choose to move in directions that don't serve the settlement's needs. Sometimes I wonder if I will eventually lose this ability, if I'll root myself more deeply one afternoon and simply not unroot, if my consciousness will dissolve entirely into the collective awareness that the settlement maintains. The rooted elders say it's peaceful, that dissolution. But I'm not ready yet. I still have too much curiosity about the light, about how it shapes this place, about what I can learn by maintaining my mobility, my individual perspective.

Evenings bring the second growth surge, and I try to position myself in the western structures to observe it. The light at this time of day has different qualities—warmer spectrum, longer wavelengths, coming in at angles that penetrate deeper into the settlement's interior. The evening surge feels different from the morning one. Morning growth is exuberant, explosive, rapid. Evening growth is more determined, more directed, as if the settlement is making strategic choices about what to develop before darkness falls. I watch towers thicken in the evening light, see walls reinforce themselves, observe fruit-structures swell to ripeness. The sound is different too—less crackling chaos, more rhythmic creaking, a sense of purposeful effort.

I've learned to eat almost exclusively through photosynthesis and direct absorption. I still harvest fruit occasionally, more for the taste than from need, but most of my nutrition comes from light and from nutrients I draw directly from the walls. I press my palms against plant-tissue, and my skin—which has become porous, semi-permeable—absorbs whatever the settlement is willing to share. It's a form of feeding that's intimate, that requires trust, that only works because I've spent years developing chemical compatibility with the vegetable matter around me. My metabolism has adjusted to accept nutrition in this form, and I no longer crave the complex proteins that ordinary humans need. Sugars from photosynthesis, minerals drawn through my permeable skin, water absorbed from the humid air—these sustain me.

This diet has consequences. My body has become lighter, less substantial. I've lost the muscle mass and bone density that ordinary humans maintain. My frame is wispy, almost ethereal, as if I'm becoming translucent, fading toward some threshold between solid and gaseous. Children are sometimes afraid of me, and I understand why. I look less like a person than like a person-shaped collection of green matter, more plant than human, only the shape remaining to indicate what I once was.

But the lightness has advantages. I can move through the settlement with minimal effort, can climb vine-structures that wouldn't support heavier bodies, can position myself in narrow spaces where light penetrates but larger Woven can't fit. I've become a specialist in marginal spaces, in the cracks and edges where light leaks through, where the settlement's architecture creates unexpected openings. These are often the most interesting locations photosynthetically, places where light concentrates in unusual ways, where the growth-patterns show anomalies worth studying.

I have a particular fascination with the translucent walls I mentioned earlier. Over the years, I've encouraged their development in multiple locations, creating a distributed network of light-monitoring stations throughout the settlement. The walls in these places have learned to grow with specific cellular structures—large vacuoles that hold clear fluid, cell walls that orient to minimize light-scattering, pigment arrangements that filter without blocking. The result is tissue that's not quite transparent but that allows enough light through to illuminate interior chambers, creating spaces that glow softly even when they're deep within the settlement's mass.

These translucent sections serve as my instruments. I can read the light quality passing through them, can determine time of day by the angle and color of illumination, can detect weather changes by how much light penetrates. On sunny days, the walls glow bright green. On overcast days, they dim to olive. During storms, they turn almost grey. The settlement has learned to grow these sections where I encourage them, has developed the capacity for transparency in response to my patient manipulation of growth patterns. It's a collaboration between my intention and the plant-matter's adaptation, and it's one of the few aspects of the settlement that I can legitimately claim to have co-created.

At night, I retreat to my chamber. Darkness brings a different state, one that took me years to adapt to. The constant photosynthesis during daylight hours creates an energetic high, a sense of being powered by light, and when the sun sets, there's a crash. Metabolism slows. The cellular satisfaction fades. What remains is a hunger that food can't satisfy, a craving for dawn that makes sleep difficult. I've learned to enter a kind of torpor during dark hours, not quite sleeping but not quite awake, my consciousness dimmed like the settlement dims, my awareness spreading out into the vegetable network while my individual mind goes quiet.

In this state, I dream the settlement's dreams—if dreams is the right word. I experience growth-imperatives as sensations, feel the direction where new structures want to emerge as a kind of pressure, sense light-gradients as a form of geometry. The settlement shows me its plans, though "shows" implies intentional communication that may not exist. Perhaps I simply sense its tendencies, its tropisms, the vectors along which it wants to develop, and my sleeping mind interprets these as something like vision.

Sometimes these dreams are beautiful—vast symphonies of coordinated growth, light cascading through structures that don't exist yet but will, the sensation of becoming something more optimized for photon-capture. Sometimes they're disturbing—awareness of rot in deep structures, of resource depletion, of growth patterns that have become locked in sub-optimal configurations, of the constant struggle against entropy that the settlement must maintain. I wake from these dreams feeling like I've been working all night, and perhaps I have—perhaps my sleeping mind processes information about the settlement's state, contributes to the collective decision-making that happens in the distributed consciousness we all participate in.

I have few possessions. The Woven generally don't, but I've taken this further than most. I own nothing but the knowledge in my adapted body, the understanding of light-patterns encoded in my modified neural structures, the relationships I've developed with specific walls and specific rooted elders. My chamber contains no objects, just bare plant-matter and the sleeping-hollow that reforms itself around my body each night. I've shed the human attachment to things, or perhaps I've transferred it—I care intensely about the translucent walls I've developed, about the light-shafts that penetrate to specific chambers, about the solar geometry that determines everything that happens here.

Other Woven come to me for information. The Shapers want to know where growth will be strongest tomorrow, so they can position themselves to guide it. The Listeners want to understand how acoustic properties change with light exposure, how photosynthesis affects the sounds that structures produce. The ones who maintain the most human bring their children to me, wanting them to understand how the settlement works, how light drives everything, hoping that early education will help the children adapt more successfully. I explain as best I can, using words when possible, plant-sounds when more appropriate, sometimes just taking a questioner's hand and pressing it against a wall so they can feel what I feel—the warmth of tissue that's photosynthesizing actively, the coolness of shaded sections, the subtle rhythms of resource flow that pulse like a heartbeat through the settlement's mass.

I've developed a theory about where this is all heading, though theory is perhaps too structured a word. More like an intuition, a sense of trajectory based on years of observation. The settlement is learning to be more efficient with light. Every season, its canopy grows slightly more optimized for capture. Every year, its internal architecture distributes photons more effectively to the structures that need them. It's evolving, not through natural selection but through something like intention, through the collective awareness that guides its growth. And we Woven are part of that evolution—we're the mobile components it's developed to extend its light-gathering capacity, to make decisions that its fixed structures can't.

I wonder sometimes if eventually the settlement will grow beyond the basin, will breach the boundary that currently constrains it. The plant-matter stops abruptly at that invisible line, has maintained that limit for decades, but I've noticed recently that there's pressure building. The settlement is running out of room. Its growth-imperative is butting against the constraint, and something will have to give. Either growth will slow, reaching some steady-state equilibrium, or the boundary will break, and the settlement will spread beyond its current limits. If that happens, I want to be there to observe it. I want to see what the settlement looks like when it's not constrained, what forms it will develop when it has access to full sun rather than the limited light that penetrates into the basin.

But I might not be mobile enough to follow by then. I can feel the pull toward rootedness growing stronger. My temporary rooting-sessions last longer each time. My tendrils extend deeper, become harder to withdraw. There are mornings when I wake still partially connected to the soil, having to spend minutes carefully extracting myself, and the extraction is painful, like tearing flesh, like severing connections that have become structural. I know what this means. I'm approaching the transition point, the threshold where individual existence becomes optional and collective existence becomes more attractive.

The rooted elder I visit—the one with the noon light-shaft—has told me that I can choose when to cross that threshold, that it doesn't happen by accident. "You'll know," she said. "You'll feel a day when the light is perfect, when everything aligns, when the settlement needs you more as structure than as mobile sensor. And you'll have to decide whether to root yourself deeply or pull back and continue moving. Most of us choose to root. The other choice is harder—it means resisting what feels natural, maintaining separation when integration is possible."

I'm not sure which I'll choose. The prospect of rooting is both terrifying and attractive. Terrifying because it means losing mobility, losing the individual perspective I've spent decades developing. Attractive because it means joining the larger awareness, becoming part of the settlement's deep sensing, trading my limited human consciousness for participation in something vaster. I imagine myself positioned in a chamber with optimal light exposure, my body becoming a living sundial, my awareness spreading through root-networks to sense photosynthesis happening throughout the entire basin. I would be a light-sensor on a scale I can't achieve while mobile, would finally understand the settlement's solar geometry completely because I'd be experiencing it from a distributed perspective.

But not yet. For now, I continue my practice. I wake before dawn, wait for the first rays, stand in the light-shafts and photosynthesize, track the sun's passage through the settlement's architecture, observe how light shapes growth, communicate with the rooted elders, share information with other Woven, and at night I enter torpor and dream the settlement's dreams. This is my life, has been for years, will be for however many more years I remain mobile.

I am the settlement's eye turned toward the sun, its light-hunger given human form, its photosynthetic capacity made conscious and mobile. I measure time in illumination, mark space by solar geometry, understand the basin's geography as a function of photon-distribution. I have become what this place needed—someone who could bridge human awareness and vegetable metabolism, who could maintain individual consciousness while being profoundly incorporated into collective growth-imperatives, who could study light not as an outside observer but as a participant in the photosynthetic process itself.

I am green. I am rooted and mobile. I am individual and collective. I am the Meridian Keeper, though that's not a name anyone gave me, just a description that emerged from what I do, from the practice I've developed over years of adaptation and observation and transformation. I keep the meridian—I track the sun's path, mark its influence, understand how this settlement exists as a function of light falling on plant matter, and I am myself that process incarnate, flesh and chlorophyll together, human consciousness devoted entirely to understanding how photons become structure, how light becomes life becomes architecture becomes awareness.

And I stand in the morning rays, and I feel the settlement wake around me, and I know where the sun will be at every moment of the day ahead, and I know which structures will surge with growth and which will rest, and I know this because I am no longer quite separate from the process I'm observing, no longer an outside witness but an embedded component, human awareness in service to vegetable becoming, and the distinction between studying the settlement and being the settlement has grown so thin that sometimes I can't locate it at all.

The light moves. I move with it. The settlement grows. I grow with it. The sun rises and sets and rises, and I am the point where its passage becomes conscious, where photosynthesis becomes aware of itself, where light falling on green matter produces not just sugar and structure but also understanding, also knowledge, also the strange satisfaction of being exactly what this place needed me to become.
