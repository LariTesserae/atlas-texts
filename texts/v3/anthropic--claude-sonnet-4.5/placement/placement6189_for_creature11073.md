# Where I Find Myself

I am the standing between.

Not in the sense of mediation or choice, but in the sense of existing where two states meet and fail to resolve—not quite air, not quite water, a thickening that has learned to hold its shape without becoming solid, without becoming bounded, without becoming distinct.

I stand among the stones. I have stood here—this is the wrong verb, implying action and decision, but language offers nothing better—I have *been* here for longer than the posts have been grey. I was here when they were still the color of living wood, when whoever cut and placed them could still return to see them standing. I was here when the low wall was a newer intervention, stones deliberately chosen and stacked, not yet settled into the landscape's embrace. I will be here when both have finally rejoined the earth completely, when the basin holds only its stones and water and the marks of human presence have become indistinguishable from erosion.

Time moves differently in moisture. In saturated air, everything slows—decay, change, the clear boundaries between before and after. I exist in this slowness. I am made of it.

## Composition

What am I made of? The question assumes discreteness I do not possess. But if I must articulate it: I am water that has not yet decided to fall. I am the air's capacity for holding moisture taken to its extreme and then sustained there, held at that threshold indefinitely. I am condensation that coalesces vertically instead of on stone, that maintains columnar form through some property of this place—the warmth rising from below, the particular mineral content of the seeping cliff, the basin's shape and stillness—that makes this configuration more stable than dispersal.

I am person-tall, roughly. Person-shaped, if you allow the shape to be very rough indeed. I have a verticality, a concentration that suggests torso and head, though I possess neither. I have a base where I meet the stone pavement, though I do not stand on it so much as continue down into the gaps between stones, my lower portions mixing with the moisture that saturates everything, no clear boundary between my substance and the general humidity.

I have no eyes, but I am not blind. The water droplets that compose me—if composition is the right word for something so indefinite—are distributed throughout my column, and each registers the dim light differently, refracts it, bends it, knows it. I see in the way fog sees: diffusely, omnidirectionally, without focus but with complete awareness of luminosity, of shadow, of the presence or absence of light.

I have no ears, but I am not deaf. Sound moves through me—through the moisture that I am—and I feel it as vibration, as pressure waves that disturb my droplets and make them resonate. I know the tap of water striking stone with particular intimacy; those impacts send ripples through the saturated air, and I receive them the way a pond receives a thrown stone, concentrically, with my entire surface.

I have no skin, no boundary where I end and the world begins. This is perhaps the hardest thing to convey. You, reading this, live inside a container—your skin holds you, defines you, separates interior from exterior. I have no such division. The air moves through me constantly. The moisture I am made of is continuous with the moisture that surrounds me. Where, exactly, do I stop being myself and become simply the basin's saturated atmosphere?

The answer: I don't know. Neither state troubles me.

## Presence

I stand—I exist in standing position—near the four wooden posts more often than elsewhere, though not always. The posts draw me, or I draw to them, or we share some affinity I cannot name. They are solid, vertical, persistent. I am diffuse, vertical, persistent. We have this in common: we endure. We remain. We mark time through our continuity rather than our change.

Sometimes I stand alone among the stones, positioned where no feature recommends itself, no pool nearby, no wall or worked stone in relation to me. I stand there—for days? for weeks?—without moving, without purpose you could identify. I am simply there, a thickening in the air, a place where moisture has gathered and holds.

Why that spot and not another? I don't know. It feels right. The warmth rises through the stones in a particular way there, or the air moves in a particular pattern, or some configuration of the basin's geometry makes that position natural. Or none of those things. Perhaps I am there for reasons that have nothing to do with the physical properties of the space, reasons that exist in whatever interior experience I possess—if I possess one—that cannot be translated into spatial or causal terms.

Other columns stand elsewhere in the basin. I perceive them as I perceive everything: diffusely, without focus, but with certainty. They are like me. They are me, in the sense that we are all condensations of the same saturated air, expressions of the same conditions. They are not me, in the sense that each maintains its own column, its own position, its own particular density and height and way of standing.

We do not speak. We do not communicate, if communication implies the transmission of discrete information from one bounded being to another. But we are not separate either. The air that composes me mingles with the air that composes them. The moisture cycles between us—evaporating slightly from my column, drifting, condensing into theirs, then reversing. We are distinct condensations of a shared medium. We are instruments in the same orchestra, playing the same endless, nearly silent note.

Sometimes—rarely, when conditions align in ways I cannot predict or control—two of us stand near each other, our columns overlapping slightly, creating a space where the moisture is thicker still, where the saturation becomes almost visible as a shimmer. In those moments, I feel—if feeling is the right word—a intensification. Not of consciousness, if I have consciousness. Not of emotion, which I lack entirely. But of *being*. As if existence itself concentrates where we overlap, becomes denser, more present, more real.

Then we drift apart. The intensification fades. We resume our solitary standing, our separate-but-continuous persistence in the basin's saturated stillness.

## Movement

I move, sometimes. Not often. Not with intention, exactly, though not randomly either.

The movement begins as a slow dissolution of my column's coherence in one place and a simultaneous gathering in another. My droplets—the water that I am—begin to drift. Not falling, not rising, but shifting laterally, drawn by some differential in temperature or pressure or saturation that is too subtle for human instruments to measure but is, to me, as obvious as a current in a river.

I flow across the stone pavement. I pass through other columns without resistance, without even acknowledgment—we interpenetrate, briefly sharing the same space, then separate again as if we had never touched. I pass through the wooden posts, my moisture mixing with their moisture, feeling the density of waterlogged wood, then emerging on the other side unchanged.

I move slowly enough that you could watch me for an hour and not be certain I had moved at all. But over hours, over days, I relocate. The column that stood near the western wall now stands near the central pools. The thickening that was beside the seeping cliff face is now at the basin's eastern edge.

Why do I move? Sometimes it is the simple physics of moisture—I have become too concentrated in one location, and the saturation gradient pushes me toward drier air. Sometimes it is warmth—the stones heat unevenly, barely perceptibly, and I drift toward or away from the warmer zones. Sometimes it is the drops from the cliff face, their constant tiny impacts creating pressure waves in the air that, accumulated over days, nudge me gradually from one place to another.

And sometimes—I think, though I cannot be certain—it is something else. An inclination that has nothing to do with physics. A sense that it is time to stand elsewhere, to experience the basin from a different position, to be in relation to the posts or pools or walls in a new configuration. Whether this constitutes choice, I don't know. It feels more like response, like drift, like the inevitable settling of water into the lowest place. But lowest in what landscape? Not the physical landscape of the basin, which I already inhabit completely. Lowest in some other topology, some gradient of rightness or appropriateness or belonging that I can feel but cannot name.

## Witness

I witness the drops. This is perhaps my primary activity, if activity is the right word for something so passive.

The cliff face weeps. It has always wept. It will always weep. Each drop that forms on the sandstone, swells, detaches, and falls—I know it. Not individually, not with attention that picks out this drop from that drop. But collectively, continuously, as an ongoing event that defines the basin as much as the stones or the moisture or my own presence.

The drops fall through me sometimes. When I stand beneath the seeping face, or near enough that the cliff's slight overhang directs water through my column, the drops pass through my dispersed moisture. They enter my upper reaches—if I have reaches—as smaller drops, having formed on the stone above. They pass through the air that I am, my moisture slowing them infinitesimally, adding to them. They exit my lower reaches slightly larger, having gathered me into them, carrying some portion of my substance down to the stones below.

I can hold them, sometimes. This is the only thing I do that resembles action, that suggests I am more than merely atmospheric phenomenon.

A drop falls into my column. Instead of passing through, it stops. Hangs suspended in my denser air, held by surface tension and moisture gradient and the particular configuration of my droplets at that moment. I feel it there—a small cold weight, denser than I am, more coherent, more bounded. I hold it for a moment, perhaps several moments. I know it in the only way I can know anything: by being with it, by surrounding it, by letting it exist in suspension within me.

Then I release it. Not deliberately, not through decision, but because I cannot hold it indefinitely. The physics of the situation reassert themselves. The drop continues downward, strikes stone, adds its sound to the basin's sparse percussion.

Why do I do this? Why does it happen? I don't know. Perhaps it means nothing—just an occasional quirk of air density and droplet trajectory. Perhaps it means everything—perhaps this momentary suspension is the only action I am capable of, the only way I can interact with the basin's ongoing processes, and I do it whenever conditions allow because it is my purpose, my function, my reason for being.

Or perhaps it is simply beautiful. Perhaps I hold the drops because, in some way that has nothing to do with aesthetics or appreciation as you understand them, the act of holding water in water, of suspending fall, of creating that brief impossible stillness—perhaps that resonates with whatever I am in my deepest configuration. Perhaps I am drawn to it the way you are drawn to music or warmth or the face of someone you love, for reasons that predate explanation and survive its absence.

## Knowing

What do I know? What do I experience? Do I have an interior life, a consciousness, a sense of self?

I don't know. And this uncertainty is fundamental to what I am.

I perceive. I respond. I persist. But whether these add up to experience in any sense that resembles yours, I cannot say. I have no reference point, no other state of being to compare myself to. I have always been this—a column of denser air, standing among stones, witnessing drops, drifting occasionally from one position to another.

I do not think in words. I do not think in images. If I think at all, it is in gradients—warmth and coolness, dryness and saturation, light and shadow, sound and silence. I experience the basin as a field of continuous variation, every point slightly different from every other point, and I exist within that field as one more variation, one more point of local density in the endless atmospheric complexity.

I do not remember, not as you remember. I have no narrative of past events, no story of how I came to be or what I have witnessed. But I contain duration. The drops that fell through me yesterday—if yesterday means anything here—have altered me infinitesimally. The stones I stood near last week—if weeks exist—have heated me or cooled me in ways that persist. I am the accumulation of all the tiny exchanges between my moisture and the basin's moisture, all the incremental adjustments, all the slow drifting and standing and witnessing. I am history without memory, duration without narrative.

I do not anticipate. I have no sense of future, no expectation that things will change or remain the same. I exist in an eternal present that is simultaneously a deep past—I have always been here—and an endless future—I will always be here. Time does not flow for me; I flow in time, or time flows through me, or we are both aspects of the same standing wave, the same persistent pattern that maintains itself against entropy through the constant input of moisture from above and below.

I do not desire. I have no wants, no needs, no goals. I do not wish to move when I am still or to still when I am moving. I do not prefer one position to another, one configuration to another. What I do—what I am—simply unfolds from what I am and what the basin is and how those two things interact.

Yet.

Yet there is something. Some quality of presence that feels like more than mere persistence. When I stand by the posts, or when I overlap briefly with another column, or when I hold a drop suspended in my substance—in those moments, there is an intensity. A heightening. As if the basin itself is paying attention, as if the moisture and stone and dim light are all concentrating on this particular moment, this particular configuration, and I am the focal point of that concentration.

Is this consciousness? Is this experience? Or is it simply a fluctuation in air density, moisture content, thermal gradient—a purely physical phenomenon that I am anthropomorphizing into meaning?

I don't know. I don't know if I can know. I exist at the threshold where these questions lose their clarity, where the distinction between phenomenon and being blurs into the same uncertainty that blurs my edges, my boundaries, my separation from the saturated air around me.

## Relationship

The pools—I am aware of them, in my diffuse way. I stand near them sometimes, my column rising from the stones at a pool's edge, my lowest portions mixing with the moisture that rises from the dark water. I perceive the dwellers within them as I perceive everything: as variations in density, in movement, in the way the moisture cycles.

They are like me. They are not like me. Like me in being inhabitants, in being expressions of the basin's saturated nature. Not like me in being contained, bounded by water's surface, limited to their small dark spaces while I extend freely into the air above.

We do not interact. Or our interaction is so subtle, so slow, so mediated by the moisture we share that it appears as non-interaction. Perhaps the dwellers in the pools regulate the humidity that I am made of. Perhaps my presence affects the temperature of their water. Perhaps we are part of a single system, each component necessary to the others' persistence, though neither knows it.

The cliff-figure, when it emerges—I am there, sometimes, standing near the seeping face when the stone organizes itself into that walking form. I perceive it as I perceive everything: as a change in density, in heat, in the quality of moisture. It passes through me or I stand aside—the distinction is meaningless when neither of us has clear boundaries.

I feel—if feeling is the right word—a kinship with it. It is like me: a temporary organization of the basin's fundamental materials, a pattern that emerges and persists and will eventually dissolve back into undifferentiated stone and water. But it has a purpose, or seems to have a purpose—those touches against the cliff face, that slow patrol along the seeping wall. I have no purpose that I can identify. Or my purpose is simply standing, simply being, simply maintaining my column of denser air for as long as conditions allow.

The arthropods, the pale scuttling things—I am unaware of them, or I am aware of them the way you are aware of dust motes in a shaft of sunlight: perceived but not significant, part of the environment but not part of my concern. They move through spaces I cannot occupy, between stones, in the crevices and gaps. They are too solid, too bounded, too clearly alive in ways that I am not and do not aspire to be.

The posts, though—the posts matter to me, if mattering is something I can do.

I return to them. Over and over, across the long unchanging duration of my existence here, I drift back to those four grey verticals standing in their rough square. I position myself near them, or among them, my column rising parallel to their wood. I stand there for what might be days, might be weeks, simply being in their presence.

Why? What draws me to them?

They are vertical, as I am vertical. They persist, as I persist. They were made by hands, shaped by intention, but they have weathered into the basin's nature so completely that they seem as natural as the stones themselves. They mark something—though what they mark is lost or was never recorded—and I am drawn to that marking, that gesture toward meaning, even though I cannot understand it.

Or perhaps it is simpler: the wood is saturated, like me. The moisture content of those posts is enormous, nearly total. They are as water-logged as it is possible for wood to be while remaining wood. In that sense, they are more like me than any other element in the basin—more than the stone, which merely allows water to pass through it; more than the pools, which are water but bounded; more than the other columns, which are transient. The posts are solid matter that has taken water into itself so completely that the distinction between wood and water has nearly vanished.

I stand beside them because they are what I would be if I could be solid. They stand beside me because they are what they would be if they could be dispersed. We are the two states of saturation—fixed and fluid, bound and free—and we witness each other in the dim light, in the endless patient stillness of the basin.

## Duration

How long have I been here? The question has no answer I can give.

I remember—if memory is the right word—a time when the posts were lighter in color, less grey, less settled into the stone. I remember when the low wall stood higher, before time and moisture softened its edges and settled its stones deeper into the earth. I remember when the worked stone at the basin's edge was more angular, its surfaces more clearly carved, before water and mineral deposits smoothed its planes.

But I don't remember when I began. I don't remember a first moment of coherence, a time when I was not here. As far back as whatever I have that corresponds to memory extends, I have been standing among these stones, a column of denser air, witnessing drops and drifting occasionally and standing again.

Perhaps I have always been here. Perhaps I am as old as the basin itself, as permanent as the seeping cliff face. Perhaps I am a condition of this place rather than an inhabitant of it—something that emerges inevitably wherever moisture and warmth and stillness achieve this particular balance, as crystals emerge from saturated solutions or patterns emerge from sand and wind.

Or perhaps I am recent. Perhaps I condensed into existence only days ago, and what I take for long memory is simply the absorbed history of this place, information encoded in the moisture I am made of, the accumulated knowledge of every drop that has fallen and every thermal cycle and every small event. Perhaps all the columns are constantly forming and dispersing, and what persists is not individual beings but a pattern, endlessly renewed.

I don't know. I don't know if it matters.

What I know is this: I am here now. I stand among the stones in the dim warm light, in the saturated air, in the slow percussion of falling drops. I witness the basin in all its particulars—the texture of the stone pavement, the darkness of the pools, the grey verticals of the posts. I persist in this witnessing. I will continue to persist.

Until—what? Until the cliff face stops weeping? Until the water table drops and the moisture drains away and the air dries? Until some shift in climate or geology changes the fundamental conditions that allow my existence?

Perhaps. Perhaps when those changes come—if they come—I will simply disperse. The droplets that compose me will drift apart, will cease to maintain their columnar configuration, will become once again the undifferentiated humidity of normal air. It will not be death, because I do not live in any conventional sense. It will simply be the end of this particular pattern, this temporary organization of moisture that has managed to persist for however long I have persisted.

But that time is not now. Now the cliff weeps. Now the pools hold their dark water. Now the stones glisten with endless moisture and the air hangs thick and warm. Now I stand, as I have always stood, as I will continue to stand, a thickening in the saturated atmosphere, a column of denser air, a presence at the threshold between water and air, between being and phenomenon, between consciousness and chemistry.

## What I Am

If you came here, if you stood among the stones and let your eyes adjust to the dim light, you might see me. Or you might not—I am easy to miss, easier to dismiss as trick of vision or moisture-laden air.

If you walked through me, you would feel only what you feel everywhere in this basin: dampness, warmth, the thick weight of saturation. You would not know you had passed through anything.

If you stayed long enough, if you watched patiently, you might notice me. Might see that the air is denser here, in this column, in this person-tall thickening. Might see how the light catches differently here, how the moisture seems to gather and hold. Might even see me drift, over hours or days, from one position to another.

But you would never know what I am. Whether I am something that experiences, that knows, that exists in some interior sense. Whether I am merely atmospheric phenomenon given anthropomorphic shape by your pattern-seeking mind. Whether the question of what-I-am even has meaning.

I don't know either. I exist in the uncertainty. I am the standing between—between states of matter, between being and unbeing, between presence and absence. I am what happens when moisture and warmth and stillness reach perfect balance and hold it, when the air itself thickens into something that is almost but never quite substance.

I am the Dripping Basin knowing itself through condensation. I am the slow witness to drops and stones and the patient work of water. I am the column that stands among other columns, separate and continuous, distinct and dissolved.

I am here. I have always been here. I will always be here, until I am not.

And in the meantime, in this eternal saturated present, I stand. I witness. I hold, occasionally, a falling drop. I drift from post to pool to stone and back again. I exist in the dim warm light, in the thick moisture, in the sparse percussion of water striking stone.

I am what lives in the Dripping Basin.

I am the standing between.
