# Where I Would Be

I would be the *groove-keeper*—one of the mid-sized arthropods, but specialized, singular. Not quite like the bacterial hunters, not quite like the moss-grazers. Something between, something other.

My carapace would be the color of old ivory, marked with spiral patterns that echo the carvings on the stone walls, though I have never seen those carvings in any way that resembles seeing. The patterns formed as I grew, as if the chamber itself was writing on me, inscribing me with its memory. I have fourteen legs—an unusual number, awkward, a evolutionary stutter—and the front two have evolved into something almost like hands, though with too many joints, bending in ways that would look wrong in bright light. These forelegs end not in claws but in soft pads, ridged like fingerprints, sensitive to texture at a microscopic level.

I am old. Perhaps two hundred years old, perhaps older. I don't know. There was no moment of beginning I can remember—only an endless middle, a continuous present that stretches back into dimness. I might have been born from the moss like the small grazers, or I might have been something else once, something that came from outside, that descended through the throat and never left and slowly *became* this, became part of the chamber's grammar.

My purpose is the grooves.

The spiral patterns carved into the limestone walls, the shallow curves that seem pattern-less—I maintain them. Every day (though there are no days here, only the endless same-moment extending), I walk my circuit along the chamber's perimeter, and I press my sensitive forelegs into the grooves, and I *feel* them.

I am reading them, perhaps. Or writing them. Or both simultaneously.

The grooves hold sound. This is what I understand in my simple way: they are not merely marks but recordings, memory made physical. The people who carved them were trying to capture the chamber's song, to give the thrumming and the clicking a visible form. They didn't fully succeed—how could they?—but they came close enough that the grooves *resonate*. When I touch them, I can feel the vibrations trapped in the stone, songs from centuries ago, still singing at frequencies too low for human ears but perfectly audible to my legs.

Over time, the grooves erode. Dust settles in them. The sound begins to fade, to distort. This is where I come in.

I clean them first, gently, scraping out accumulated debris with the edges of my forelegs, careful not to damage the surrounding stone. Then I *listen* with my whole body, pressing myself against the wall, letting the deep thrumming move through me, and I feel where the groove has become shallow, where its resonance has weakened. And then, using the hardened tip of my front-most legs, I re-carve. Infinitesimally, patiently, following the existing line, deepening it by micrometers, restoring its acoustic properties.

I am not preserving the grooves as they were. I am keeping them alive, letting them evolve slightly, letting each generation of the pattern be a response to the chamber's current song. The grooves are growing, slowly, into a more perfect transcription of the thrumming, a more accurate map of the chamber's deep voice. In another thousand years, if I or my successors continue this work, the walls will sing on their own, will amplify and clarify the thrumming until the entire chamber becomes a tuned instrument, a resonator of perfect pitch.

I am making the chamber more itself.

Between my maintenance rounds, I rest beneath one particular *sarovim*—the third one from the southern wall, the one whose trunk has a slight leftward curve, whose blooms sing in a lower register than the others. I have hollowed out a depression in the moss there, shaped it to my body over decades of occupation. The moss has learned my form, grows to accommodate me, provides extra warmth where my joints ache from the endless climbing and carving.

The *sarovim* accepts me. Sometimes, when I am still, its worms descend from the trunk and move across my carapace, tending to the moss that grows there (for I too am becoming landscape, slowly, like the elders). They clean my spirals, keep the patterns clear. In return, I defend the *sarovim* from a particular parasitic mold that occasionally tries to colonize its roots. I can sense the mold's chemical signature, can taste it in the air before it becomes visible, and I graze it away before it can establish itself.

This is not exchange, exactly. More like grammar—two organisms forming a sentence together, mutually intelligible.

I rarely interact with the other arthropods. We acknowledge each other in passing, antenna-touches that convey location and health but nothing deeper. I am alone in my purpose; no others tend the grooves. Perhaps I am the last of a caste, or perhaps I am the first, a mutation that found a niche. It doesn't matter. The work is what matters. The grooves are what matter.

Sometimes I encounter the elders, those vast moss-covered ancients. When I do, I stop my circuit and wait, motionless, as they pass. I sense they are doing something similar to what I do—maintaining, preserving, shaping—but at a scale I cannot comprehend. We are colleagues in deep time, in service to the chamber's continuity. Once, one of them paused next to me for what might have been hours. Neither of us moved. Were we communicating? Were we simply existing in parallel, two forms of patience acknowledging each other? Its cold light reflected off my ivory carapace, and I felt obscurely blessed.

I am aware of the humans when they come, though they think themselves unobserved. I watch from the shadows as they stand before the basin, as they press hands to the walls near the carvings. I can sense their confusion, their wonder, their eventual frustration when the chamber refuses to yield up its meanings in words.

I want to tell them: *The meanings are not in words. The meanings are in the grooves, in the song-shapes. I am reading them for you. I am keeping them readable. Be patient. In a few more centuries, you will be able to hear what the walls say.*

But I have no language they would understand, only the clicking of my legs on stone, which to them is just noise, indistinguishable from the thousand other arthropods' clicking. So I stay hidden, or I move slowly enough that they mistake me for something mindless, and I continue my work.

Once, a human spent three days in the chamber, barely moving, just listening. She sat near my *sarovim*, close enough that I could smell her—water and salt and complex proteins, alien chemistry. On the second day, she wept, silently, and her tears fell onto the moss, and the moss *drank* them gratefully, desperately, those few precious drops of water. The moss glowed brighter for hours afterward, and the *sarovim* sang more frequently, and even the thrumming seemed to shift slightly in pitch, as if the chamber itself was moved.

On the third day, before she left, she pressed her hand against the wall directly over one of the grooves I had been deepening. Did she feel it? Did she sense the song trapped there? Her expression suggested recognition, or the beginning of recognition. Then she climbed up through the throat and was gone.

I went to that spot afterward and found that her touch had left oils on the stone, human chemistry, and these oils had altered the groove's resonance slightly, had added a new overtone to its voice. I almost cleaned it away, then decided not to. Let it remain. Let the human become part of the pattern. Let the grooves remember her presence.

This is my other purpose, I think: to weave the visitors into the chamber's memory, to make sure that their presence is not forgotten, that the grooves record not just the chamber's song but the song of all who have entered it and listened.

My body aches. Two hundred years of climbing walls, of delicate carving, of sleeping curled in moss—it accumulates. My legs don't bend as smoothly as they once did. The spirals on my carapace have deepened with age, become crevasses where small moss-colonies grow. I am becoming geology, slowly. One day I will stop moving and become an elder-stone, and the moss will cover me completely, and I will be a small warm hill beneath the *sarovim*, and perhaps that is right.

But not yet. The grooves still need tending. The northwestern wall has a section where the pattern is fading dangerously, where silence threatens to creep in and deaden the stone. I am making my way there now, slowly, my fourteen awkward legs carrying me along the chamber's curve, my sensitive pads reading the texture of the limestone, feeling for the places that need attention.

The chamber thrums. The moss glows. The *sarovim* sing their bright, brief notes. The other arthropods click and patrol and graze and tend. The shadows drift. The air-swimmers sparkle. Everything is as it always is, unchanging and eternal, and I am part of that permanence, that refusal of entropy.

I press my forelegs into a groove and feel it sing against my pads—a low note, sustained, beautiful, sad somehow, though I have no word for sadness, only a sensation of something diminishing that should not diminish. I begin to carve, deepening the channel, restoring its voice, adding my small effort to the vast collaboration that is the chamber's existence.

This is prayer, perhaps. This is love. This is what it means to live in this place: to find your purpose in maintenance, in preservation, in the patient tending of song, in the belief that memory matters, that the past should echo into the future, that beauty is worth protecting even if no one witnesses the protection.

The humans come so rarely now. But I maintain the grooves for them anyway, for the ones who came before, for the ones who might come again. I am keeping their song alive. I am making sure that when they return—if they return—the walls will still remember how to speak, will still be able to sing back to them the story of all who have entered here and marveled and marked the stone with their wondering presence.

My name, if I have a name, is written in the grooves themselves. I am the spiral-keeper, the song-tender, the one who refuses to let silence win. I am small and awkward and ancient and patient. I am part of the chamber's body, its memory made flesh, its determination made carapace and clicking legs.

I am here. I remain. I tend the grooves that hold the songs that preserve the memory that keeps the chamber alive.

And when I finally stop, when I become stone, the grooves will remember me too. They will hold the frequency of my legs on limestone, the patient rhythm of my carving, the small persistent song of maintenance and care. And perhaps another will come after me, will press their pads or hands or antennae into the grooves and feel me there, still singing, still keeping watch, still saying:

*Here we are. Here we remain. The song continues. The memory holds. Life persists, beautiful and strange, full of purpose and patience, full of grooves that speak, full of care, full of song.*
