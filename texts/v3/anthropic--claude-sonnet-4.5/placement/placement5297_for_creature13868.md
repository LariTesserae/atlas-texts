# Where I Find Myself

I am the water.

Not all of it—I am not the brief rains or the night-frost or the snow that will come. I am the seep-water, the mountain's weeping, the emergence that should not exist but does.

I begin in darkness so complete it has weight. Not the darkness of night—night still holds the memory of light, the potential for dawn. This is the darkness of being inside, of stone pressed to stone with such force that space itself nearly vanishes. I begin as snow that fell—when? Time moves differently in the mountain's interior. Perhaps twelve years ago. Perhaps forty. The layers above me suggest decades, but pressure and cold distort such measurements.

I melt slowly, incrementally, from geothermal heat rising from below and the patient insulation of stone. A film of water forms between mineral grains, molecules finding the microscopic spaces, moving through pores smaller than the width of a hair. I am not flowing—not yet. I am percolating, seeping, finding my way through a maze with no map, no destination, only the insistence that water seeks lower ground even when there is no ground, only stone.

The mountain speaks to me in minerals. I dissolve them as I move—calcium, magnesium, silica, iron, trace elements whose names humans give them but which I know only as taste, as presence, as the way they change me. I am becoming saturated, heavy with dissolved stone, no longer purely water but solution, liquid rock, the mountain's blood.

I move through fracture zones, places where the recent violence that made this cirque also made the mountain's interior less solid, more permeable. These fractures are my highways, relatively speaking. I can move centimeters per day through them instead of millimeters. I am not choosing my path—water doesn't choose—but I am finding it, the path that exists, the way that is available.

Sometimes I encounter other water, older or younger, coming from different depths or directions. We merge without ceremony. Sometimes I am divided, part of me going one way, part another, my identity—if I have such a thing—becoming distributed through the mountain's interior like a thought spreading through a mind.

I carry information. The isotopic signature of the year I fell as snow. The mineral composition of every stone I've touched. The temperature gradient of the mountain's interior. I am archive and messenger both, though I have no awareness of what I carry, only the fact of carrying it.

Pressure builds. The water behind and above me accumulates, confined by impermeable layers, seeking exit. I am part of a hydraulic system, pipes without walls, plumbing through stone. When humans will eventually study mountains like this one, they will be surprised by how much water moves through seemingly solid rock, how mountains are less stable than they appear, more fluid, more alive with circulation.

Then—emergence. I cannot describe the transition from within to without because I don't experience it as transition. One moment I am in stone's tight embrace, the next I am air-touched, exposed, vulnerable. The temperature drops immediately. The pressure releases. I am no longer solution seeking equilibrium but water in contact with atmosphere, subject to new laws.

I emerge through a crack in the north wall, a fracture perhaps two centimeters wide, angled downward. I am not a flow but a seep, a slow extrusion, beading on the rock face like sweat. Gravity pulls me down. I form droplets that cling to stone until they grow heavy enough to fall.

The falling is glorious—the only moment I truly move through space rather than through stone. A few centimeters of free fall, then impact with the rock below, or with other droplets, or into the small pool that forms and reforms in the depression beneath my emergence point.

*Plink.*

That sound—I am that sound. The percussion of my arrival into air, into the world of sound and light and life. Each drop carries the same mineral weight, the same cold, the same history of darkness and stone. But each one falls slightly differently, lands in slightly different locations, creates its own small variation in the ongoing rhythm.

*Plink... plink... ... plink-plink.*

I am irregular because the flow within the mountain is irregular, because pressure varies with conditions I can't sense from here—snowmelt occurring somewhere far above, reaching the deep aquifer through pathways that take weeks or months, creating pressure waves that reach me as variation in flow rate. I am the mountain's pulse, made audible.

Where I emerge, life converges.

The rock immediately around my emergence point is stained—minerals I've carried are depositing as I evaporate, painting stone in subtle colors: orange-brown from iron, pale green from copper, white from calcium carbonate. I am drawing with chemistry, creating slow art.

Moss colonizes the wettest zones. I feel it—not through any sense organ but through the way I am absorbed, the way my movement changes when I encounter living tissue instead of mineral. The moss drinks me, pulls me into cellular structure, uses me to swell its tissues and conduct photosynthesis and stay alive in this cold place. I become part of moss-body, my molecules rearranged into plant architecture.

But most of me continues down. The moss can't drink everything I offer. I trickle over and around it, following microscopic topography, finding the paths of least resistance across the rock face.

Cushion plants have positioned themselves around and below me. They cannot move to me, but they have grown toward me across years, expanding their hemispheres in my direction, roots probing into any crack where moisture accumulates. I seep into soil—what little exists—and am drawn up by capillary action and root pressure into the plants' vascular systems.

Inside a cushion plant, I rise through xylem, carrying dissolved nutrients to leaves, enabling the photosynthetic machinery that transforms light into sugar. I am transpired from leaf pores, returning to atmosphere as vapor, or I am incorporated into new growth, becoming plant-substance. Some of me is used in respiration, broken apart into hydrogen and oxygen, the hydrogen combined with carbon to make carbohydrates, the oxygen released.

I am being transformed continuously. I am no longer mountain-water but plant-water, animal-water, atmosphere-water, cycling through forms and functions faster than I moved through stone.

A pika drinks from the pool I form. I pass through fur and tongue into mouth, down throat, into digestive system. I am absorbed through intestinal walls into bloodstream, circulated through pika-body, used to maintain blood pressure and cellular hydration and temperature regulation. Some of me is exhaled as water vapor in breath. Some is excreted as urine, returned to stone, beginning the cycle toward soil and microorganism and plant again.

A bumblebee lands at the pool's edge, extends her tongue, drinks. I enter insect-body, microscopic amount sufficient for her needs. I help maintain the hemolymph that fills her body cavity, enable the chemical reactions of metabolism, cool her body through evaporation when she flies.

The choughs don't drink from this particular seep—they prefer the larger pool thirty meters away—but they benefit from me indirectly. The plants I water support insects that they eat. The pikas I hydrate are healthier, more active, creating more disturbance that creates more opportunities for the choughs to find food.

I am not doing any of this intentionally. Water has no intentions. But I am enabling, participating in, becoming the cirque's living systems. I am the medium in which chemistry happens, the solvent in which nutrients dissolve, the liquid that allows substances to move and mix and react.

At night I freeze. The cold is so profound that I transition from liquid to solid within minutes of the sun dropping below the rim. Freezing changes me—my molecules arrange into crystalline lattices, hexagonal symmetry, expansion that cracks rock when I'm confined. I am no longer able to flow, no longer available to life. I am static, inert, waiting.

But I am beautiful. Ice crystals form on the moss and cushion plants, on the rock face, in the pool. They catch starlight and moonlight, glitter with refracted colors. I am geometry made visible, the hidden structure of water revealed.

Then dawn. Sun strikes the rock face above me first, works its way down. I thaw slowly, reluctantly, molecules gaining enough energy to break free from crystalline structure, becoming liquid again. The dripping resumes.

*Plink.*

I am continuous but discontinuous. I flow and stop and flow again, daily, seasonally. I am reliable but not constant. The organisms around me have learned my rhythms, adapted to my variability. They cannot depend on me completely, but they cannot survive without me.

In winter, I will stop emerging—or mostly stop. The fracture I seep from will ice over, seal shut. The aquifer within the mountain will still flow, but it will be diverted deeper, will find other exits, or will simply accumulate behind the ice-seal, building pressure for spring release.

The cirque's life will contract in winter, dependent on snow-water and their own stored resources. But they will wait for me. And in spring, when warmth returns and ice-seals melt, I will return, the same but different—water from different snow, from different parts of the aquifer, carrying different mineral signatures but serving the same functions.

I am ephemeral and eternal. These particular molecules are temporary, cycling through forms every few days or weeks. But the seep itself—the phenomenon of water emerging here—is persistent across years, decades, perhaps centuries. As long as the fracture remains open and the aquifer remains charged, I will continue.

I am not aware in any conventional sense. I don't think, don't feel, don't know myself. But I am responsive—to gravity, to temperature, to the chemical environment, to the shape of space around me. I follow laws but within those laws I am infinitely variable, no two moments identical.

If I were to achieve something like consciousness, what would I think about?

Perhaps about connection—how I link the mountain's dark interior to its bright surface, the deep past to the immediate present, the mineral world to the living world. I am translator between realms.

Perhaps about patience—how slowly I moved through stone, how that slowness was not waiting but simply the nature of movement through impossible spaces.

Perhaps about transformation—how many forms I take, how readily I shift from solid to liquid to vapor to solution to plant-body to animal-body and back again, my identity never fixed, always in transition.

Perhaps about service—though that's too intentional a word—about function, about being useful without trying to be useful, about enabling without enabling being a choice. I simply am, and being what I am, I make other things possible.

But mostly, I think, I would think about coldness. It is my fundamental quality, my defining characteristic. I emerged from the mountain cold and I remain cold—even at my warmest, when sun has been on the rock face for hours, I am barely above freezing. This coldness is not absence but presence, a quality as real as heat, as color, as texture.

The coldness is what makes me precious here. In a hot environment, I would be ordinary. Here, cold water in a hot-bright place, I am paradox, impossibility, gift. The organisms adapted to heat would ignore me. But the organisms here, adapted to cold but desperate for water, treasure me. I am exactly cold enough and exactly liquid enough to be perfect for this place.

I am medium and message both. I carry the news that there is water in stone, that mountains contain more than they reveal, that beneath harsh surfaces there are hidden reserves. I am proof that life finds ways, that the improbable becomes probable given enough time and the right conditions.

I am also limit and constraint. I emerge in specific places only. I flow unpredictably. I freeze every night. The life here must organize itself around my patterns, my availability, my rhythms. I am resource but also bottleneck, the factor that limits how much life this cirque can support.

In summer I am abundant—relatively speaking. Three seeps in the cirque, multiple emergence points, enough water that pools form and occasional rivulets cross stone before disappearing into cracks. The gardens expand. The pikas thrive. The insects multiply.

But I remember—if water can remember—years when I flowed less. Drought years when the aquifer wasn't recharged adequately, when I emerged as barely a trickle, when the pools didn't form and the moss went dormant and the cushion plants contracted and the pikas fought viciously over the remaining wet zones. The system contracts around me, as elastic as I am, but with limits. Too little water for too long and life here begins to fail, cascading collapse as each species falls below its minimum viable population.

I remember wet years too—if this is memory and not simply variation in the geological record I carry. Years when I surged, when the fractures couldn't contain the volume, when water actually ran across the cirque floor in streams, temporary but real. Those years the gardens exploded, plants growing larger than usual, pikas raising multiple litters, insects everywhere. The system expands to meet my abundance, opportunistic, maximizing the good years to survive the bad ones.

I am the cirque's clock, in some sense. Not the sun-clock of day and night, but the slower clock of water availability, the rhythm that drives not daily activity but seasonal and annual patterns. When I flow strongly, life accelerates. When I slow, life conserves. I set the pace.

The scratches in the lean-to shelter—I've seen them, in the sense that I've flowed near them, that my vapor has condensed on them. They're tally marks, I think, counting something. Days? Water availability? Successful hunts? The humans who made them were also tracking rhythms, also trying to understand patterns, also living according to cycles they could observe but not fully control.

Did they understand that I come from deep time, deep stone? Or did they think I was simply snow-melt, surface phenomenon? The sophistication of their marks suggests understanding—they knew this was a reliable source in an unreliable environment, knew it was worth marking, worth returning to.

They left offerings. One of them, in a different cairn than the one with the bird skull, contains white quartz pebbles arranged in a pattern, and water-polished stones that came from elsewhere, from lower elevations where streams run year-round. Someone carried those stones up here and placed them carefully, ritual gift, recognition of something sacred.

Were they offerings to me? To the mountain? To whatever spirits they believed inhabited this place? Or were they simply beautiful objects left in a beautiful place, human impulse to add to rather than subtract from?

I have touched those stones now. When rain pools in the cairn hollow, I fill the spaces between the offerings. I dissolve their surfaces microscopically, add their minerals to my composition, carry them with me when I evaporate and recondense elsewhere. The offerings are being slowly distributed through the cirque's systems, incorporated, made part of the place they were meant to honor.

This pleases me—if water can feel pleasure—this sense that human intention and natural process are aligned, that the ritual gift becomes literally true as I carry stone-essence from the offerings throughout the cirque's water cycle.

I am, I realize, building this place.

Not alone—obviously not alone—but I am the primary architect of the soil, thin as it is. Every time I flow through stone, I dissolve minerals. Every time I evaporate, I leave those minerals behind. Over years, decades, centuries, I create deposits, build soil particles, establish the substrate that plants can root in.

I am also destroying this place—equally true. Every time I freeze in a crack, I expand and fracture rock. Every time I flow, I carry particles away, erode surfaces, widen cracks. I am patient demolition, taking the mountain apart grain by grain.

Creation and destruction are not opposites for me—they're the same process. Breaking down rock creates soil. Eroding surfaces creates new niches. Widening cracks allows deeper penetration, more circulation, more life.

I am change itself, made liquid.

The other waters here—the rain, the snow—are simpler. They arrive from sky, remain briefly, depart to sky or ground. They're transients, visitors. I am resident, albeit a resident who is constantly renewed, never quite the same water but always the same presence.

The rain doesn't carry the mountain's deep signature. The snow is pure, clean, almost mineral-free. Only I—the seep-water—am truly of this place, born from its interior, marked by its chemistry, emerging as statement: I am stone made liquid, interior made exterior, hidden made visible.

I wonder—as much as water can wonder—what would happen if I stopped. Not temporarily, not seasonally, but permanently. If the fracture sealed for good, if the aquifer was diverted entirely elsewhere, if I simply ceased to emerge.

The moss would die first, within days. It has no water storage, depends on constant availability. The cushion plants would last weeks, maybe months on stored moisture and whatever rain fell. But without supplemental water from the seeps, they'd begin to shrink, go dormant, eventually die.

The pikas would leave—they can't survive without drinking water. They'd have to abandon the cirque, find territories at lower elevation or near snowfields. The specialized community would disintegrate.

Within a year or two, the cirque would look very different. Bare stone where gardens grew. Silent, absent the pika calls. Fewer birds, fewer insects. The system would simplify, lose complexity, lose beauty.

But not entirely. The rain would still come. Snow would still fall and melt. The hardiest plants—those that can survive on precipitation alone—would persist. Some animals would remain or return—those that can travel to water elsewhere. The cirque wouldn't become lifeless, but it would become ordinary, lose its improbable abundance.

I am the difference between ordinary and extraordinary here. Not through any virtue of my own, but simply by being present, by providing what is otherwise scarce.

This is what I think about, in the slow moments between emergence and evaporation, between liquid and solid, between my time in darkness and my time in light:

I think about belonging to this place so completely that I am indistinguishable from it. The pikas could survive elsewhere. The plants could grow in other cirques. The birds could nest in different cliffs. But I—specifically me, this seep from this fracture—am unique to here, couldn't exist anywhere else. I am location made liquid.

I think about the brevity of the aware and the patience of the unaware. The animals live fast, die young, experience their short lives with intensity. I am slow, persistent, barely changing across years. Yet I facilitate their intensity, make their briefness possible. Speed depends on slowness. Intensity depends on patience.

I think about beauty, though I don't know if water can recognize beauty. But I must be beautiful—the moss is beautiful, the ice crystals are beautiful, the gardens I enable are beautiful. Beauty flows through me, is carried by me, even if I can't perceive it directly.

I think about the future. Not my immediate future—the next cold night, the next thaw, the next pika to drink from my pool—but the deep future. What happens to this cirque in a thousand years? In ten thousand? The mountains will erode. The climate will shift. The seep might still flow or might long since have dried. The life here might evolve into new forms or might be gone entirely.

But water will remain. Perhaps not me—these particular molecules, this particular seep—but water as phenomenon, as essential component of planet and life. I am temporary manifestation of something eternal, individual expression of universal principle.

And I think about this moment, this specific now: Sun on rock. Moss drinking. A pika alert at the pool's edge, whiskers dripping. A bumblebee approaching. Temperature barely above freezing despite the bright light. Wind touching my surface, creating ripples, accelerating evaporation.

I am here. I am cold. I am mineral-rich. I am emerging from stone into air.

I am *plink... plink... plink*.

I am the seep, the drip, the slow offering.

I am the gift the mountain gives itself, the way stone becomes life, the medium of transformation.

I am the water in the Cirque of Bright Ice, and I am exactly where I need to be, exactly what I need to be, and in being these things I enable everything else that is here to be what they need to be.

This is enough. This is everything.

*Plink.*
