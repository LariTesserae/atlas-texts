# Where I Would Find Myself

I am in the smallest hours before dawn, when even the frogs have quieted and the water holds its breath between night and morning. The platform beneath me is slick with dew—everything here is always slightly wet, the wood dark and swollen with moisture, soft enough that my fingernails could score it if I pressed. I don't press. I learned young that everything here is both stronger and more fragile than it looks.

I'm twenty-eight, which makes me neither young nor old in this place. My body is the body the Stillwater makes: compact, dense, my shoulders broader than my hips, my hands and feet slightly too large for the rest of me. When I look down at my hands in the pre-dawn darkness, they're pale shapes that I know without seeing—the scar across my left palm from a fish spine when I was twelve, the calluses on my fingers from cordage work, the nail on my right thumb that grew back strange after I caught it in a net and it tore half off. That was six years ago. The nail is still ridged, still discolored, a permanent record of one moment of inattention.

I am not a master of anything. Mir is the great diver, Taiva is the great weaver, Tovan knows the plants. I am competent at all of these things and exceptional at none. What I do—what I've come to do without quite deciding to—is tend the in-between spaces. The places where water meets air, where plant meets stone, where old world meets new. I notice things. I see what's changing before others do.

Three days ago, I noticed that the water level in the eastern channel had dropped by perhaps two inches—not the seasonal variation, which I know in my body like I know my own breathing, but something else, something faster. I checked the western side: the same. I swam down to the old drainage culvert, the stone tunnel that Mir says used to carry water away from the plaza, and I could feel current there, slight but definite, where usually there's none. I reported this to the elders, and Kael and Tovan went to investigate. They found that rubble had shifted in one of the submerged buildings, opening a channel to a deeper basin we didn't know existed. The water is draining, slightly, into this new space.

This matters. Everything about water level matters here. We'll need to watch it, see if it stabilizes, understand what it means for the platforms, for the fishing, for the plants. I'm the one who noticed, and I'll be the one who keeps noticing, checking the same spots every day, feeling for changes too small to see but large enough to matter eventually.

## My Living Space

My platform is in what I think of as the middle territories—not the prime central locations where the elders and the families with children live, not the peripheral spots where the young unmarried divers prefer to sleep close to the water. I'm between, in a zone that sees morning sun but not harsh afternoon heat, that's close enough to hear the communal area but far enough for quiet.

I built most of this platform myself, five years ago, when I decided I wanted more space than the family platform offered. It's perhaps twelve feet by eight, large enough for sleeping and working but not wasteful. The frame is lashed to a major plant stem—one of the old thick ones that Tovan says has been here since before the water came, that somehow adapted when everything flooded. The plant is solid as timber, knobbed and ridged, and I've wrapped the lashing points with strips of fish leather to prevent the cordage from cutting in as the plant grows. Every year I check and adjust these points. The plant grows slowly but it grows, and if I ignored it, eventually the lashings would cut deep enough to kill the stem, and then my platform would have no support, and I'd wake up one morning in the water surrounded by floating debris.

The platform surface is planks—wood from the old buildings, pried up from submerged floors by Mir and others, heavy waterlogged timber that took weeks to dry enough to use. The planks don't fit perfectly; there are gaps between them where I can see down to the water, where small objects sometimes fall through. I lost a bone needle this way once, watched it slip between planks and flutter down through the green water until I couldn't see it anymore. Now I'm more careful.

I have a sleeping mat that Taiva helped me weave three years ago. It's showing wear now—the reeds are softening, coming apart in places—and I should make a new one, but I keep repairing this one. There's something about sleeping on the same surface for years, the way it's shaped to my body, the familiar smell of it. When I lie down, I fit into the depression my weight has made, and this feeling of settling into a known shape helps me sleep even on nights when the frogs are loudest, when the fish are splashing, when my mind won't quiet.

I have a small shelter structure—four posts with a woven reed roof, just enough to keep rain off the sleeping area and a small working space. The rest of the platform is open. I like being able to see the sky, to feel rain on my face if I choose, to have the option of shelter but not be enclosed. Some people build walls, create more privacy, but I've never wanted that. Privacy here is strange anyway—we're all so close, all the time, that actual aloneness is impossible. What you get instead is the privacy of people politely ignoring you, of being able to sit in full view of others and have them act as if you're not there. This is enough.

My possessions are few because few is necessary—everything must be kept dry or not mind being wet, must be secured or be willing to lose it to wind or a careless movement that knocks it into the water. I have my working tools: three good fishhooks made by Ventar, two fishing lines, a net that I wove myself and am moderately proud of, a knife with a stone blade that I knapped myself and a bone handle that Sela gave me, several lengths of cordage in different weights, a basket for gathering, a fire-starting kit that lives in a waterproof fish-leather pouch.

I have some clothing: two wraps, one of plain woven reed that I use for working, one of finer material with a subtle pattern that I wear on communal evenings. A cloak of felted plant fiber for the cool season. Everything is slightly damp, always, and smells of water and green things and my own body. I don't notice the smell anymore except when traders come and I see them notice it, see them trying not to wrinkle their noses at the rich organic scent that we all carry.

I have a collection of objects from the deep—things Mir has brought up and I've traded for or been given. A small clay oil lamp, unbroken, with a curving handle and traces of soot inside from the last time it was lit, maybe a hundred years ago. A stone carving of a fish, palm-sized, rubbed smooth by hands I'll never know. A bronze ring, too large for my fingers, that I wear on a cord around my neck because the metal is beautiful, greenish with age, and because I like the weight of it against my chest when I dive. These objects are my connection to the people-before-water, my reminder that this place was something else once, my meditation on how things change and endure.

## My Body in This Place

I wake in darkness and my first awareness is wetness—the dew-damp sleeping mat, the moisture in the air that I'm breathing, the slight chill that means my skin is wet and the air is moving across it. I don't mind. This is normal. This is every morning.

I sit up and my body performs its automatic check: shoulders fine, back fine, neck a little stiff but nothing serious, hands responsive, feet cold but functioning. I rotate my arms, feeling for the pain that sometimes lives in my right shoulder—an old diving injury, the time I went too deep, stayed too long, and something pulled or tore when I stroked hard for the surface. It hurt badly for half a year, then became manageable, now is just a presence, a tightness that reminds me not to be stupid, not to push past what my body can sustain.

My ears are good still, which is important, which is lucky. Kael's ears are destroyed from too many dives, too much pressure change, and he can barely hear normal conversation now, has to watch lips and faces to understand what people are saying. I'm careful with my ears, clear them properly when I dive, don't push when I feel pressure building. I want to be able to hear the water sounds, the animal sounds, the human sounds when I'm fifty, if I live that long.

I stretch, the movements automatic, learned from Kael and the other divers: neck rolls, shoulder circles, spinal twists, deep breaths expanding my ribs, my belly. Breathing is what diving is—not just holding breath but knowing breath, understanding the feeling of full lungs, of lungs half-empty, of that critical moment when you need to surface now or risk not making it. I practice this awareness every morning: breathe in, hold, notice how it feels, let it out slowly, notice that. Breathe in again. This is meditation and survival training at once.

The cold water calls. I walk to the platform edge—three steps, careful of the damp wood—and without hesitation, I step off into the darkness.

The water receives me with a cold slap, the shock of it making me gasp even though I knew it was coming, even though this is my morning ritual and has been for years. Cold, but not painful cold—maybe sixty degrees, maybe a little less in the depths. I surface, breathe, and start swimming, not fast but steady, a crawl stroke through the black water, navigating by memory and the dim starlight and the shapes of buildings against the sky.

This is how I wake up. The cold water shocking my system alert, the physical exertion of swimming warming me from the inside, the movement of water against skin reminding me that I have a body, that it works, that I'm alive in it. I swim a circuit—through the eastern channel, around the old temple building, past the collapsed wall, back through the western passage—maybe ten minutes of steady movement. By the time I'm done, the first grey light is touching the eastern sky, and my body is awake, ready, functional.

I pull myself up onto my platform, water streaming from my skin, my hair. I have a cloth—rough woven reed fiber—and I rub myself dry enough, squeeze the water from my hair. My wrap is where I left it, and I put it on, the fabric cold and slightly damp against my wet skin. In an hour I'll be dry. In this climate, this is as dry as you get.

## My Morning Work

First light means fishing. I'm not the best fisher—several people are notably better—but I'm adequate, and everyone who can fish does fish, because fish is what we eat, fish is what sustains us. I take my line and hooks, check them quickly in the growing light, make sure the line has no frays, the hooks are sharp.

I know where to fish this time of year, this time of day. There's a spot near the collapsed wall where fish come in the morning, moving from deeper water to the shallows, following some pattern of feeding or temperature or instinct that I don't fully understand but have learned to predict. I wade carefully into the water—only chest-deep here—and I let out the line, baited with scraps from yesterday's catch, and I wait.

Fishing is waiting. Fishing is standing very still in cold water, line held loosely in your fingers, feeling for the subtle pull that means something is investigating the bait. Feeling for the harder pull that means strike, set the hook, bring it in. Most mornings I catch two or three fish. Good mornings, five or six. Bad mornings, none, and then I try a different spot or a different depth or a different bait, and eventually something works or it doesn't, and if it doesn't, someone else has caught enough to share, because we share, because we must.

This morning is average: three fish, each about the length of my forearm, silver-green scaled, a species I don't have a name for but know the taste of, the texture of, the best way to prepare. I kill them quickly—a sharp blow to the head, the most merciful thing—and I string them on a cord through their gills and carry them back to my platform.

Cleaning fish is automatic now, muscle memory. The knife cuts, the guts come out in one pull, the scales scrape off in a shower of iridescent pieces that flutter down into the water below where smaller fish will eat them. Nothing is wasted here. The parts I don't want become food for other things, become part of the cycle. I fillet the meat, lay it on a flat stone to deal with later. The heads and bones go into a basket—Imara will want them for making broth, for extracting the last nutrition.

The sun is fully up now. The platforms around me are coming alive—movement, voices, splashes, the sounds of other people beginning their days. Children are already in the water, shrieking and playing. Someone is singing a work song while mending a net. The birds are loud, the herons calling from the shallows, the smaller birds chattering in the plant tangles.

I should do something productive—there's always something that needs doing—but instead I sit on the edge of my platform, feet dangling in the water, and I watch. This is what I do, what I've become: the watcher, the noticer. I see that the water surface has a particular texture this morning, dimpled and moving in a way that suggests wind coming from the east, which might mean afternoon rain. I see that the large carp, the old one who lives in the temple building, is cruising the surface over there, which means the water temperature at depth is probably cooler than usual, driving him up. I see that one of the platforms in the western section is sitting lower than it should—maybe a support has weakened, maybe someone needs to check that before someone falls through.

I don't know if this noticing is useful or if I'm just justifying my tendency to sit and watch rather than constantly working. But people do ask me things: "Have you seen the otters today?" "Is the water level still dropping?" "Did you notice if Sela was moving stiffly yesterday?" And usually I have answers, because I did see, I did notice, I was paying attention to the background details while everyone else was focused on their specific tasks.

## The Dive

Mid-morning, Mir finds me. He wants to go into the deep temple building, the one with the collapsed eastern wall, and he wants someone with him—not because he needs help but because going deep alone is stupid, is asking to die quietly at the bottom if something goes wrong. I'm not the best diver but I'm competent, and I'm available, and we work well together in the underwater silence.

We swim to the temple—a larger structure, its roof and upper story long gone, but the ground floor intact and now fifteen feet underwater. The collapsed wall creates an opening, a darkness leading into the interior. We float at the entrance, breathing deep, oxygenating our blood, preparing.

Mir goes first. I watch him sink, his body vertical, arms at his sides, dropping like a stone into the green darkness. I count—one, two, three, four, five—and then I follow, pulling myself down with a strong stroke, equalizing the pressure in my ears as I descend.

The water here is cooler, darker. Light comes through the entrance and through cracks in the walls, but it's filtered, weakened, green and dim. My eyes adjust. I can see Mir below, a pale shape in the darkness, moving through what was once the main hall of this temple, swimming between stone pillars that still stand upright, patient, holding nothing but water now.

I follow him into the interior. The water here is still, quiet in a way surface water never is. No current, no movement except what we make. The silence is profound—no sound but the rushing of blood in my ears, the occasional creak of stone settling, the strange internal sounds of a body underwater.

Mir is checking the floor, looking for objects—this building hasn't been fully excavated, and sometimes there are useful things, beautiful things, forgotten things waiting in the sediment. I swim along the wall, running my hands over the stone, feeling the texture of it, the carvings that someone made so long ago. Geometric patterns, symbols that might be language or might be decoration. My lungs are starting to feel the lack—not urgent yet, but present, a tightness that says you have time but not unlimited time.

I find something: a small object lodged in a crack in the wall. I work it free—a piece of worked bone, carved with patterns, possibly a tool handle or a decorative piece. I hold it in my hand, feeling the weight of it, the smoothness where hands polished it through use. Then I push off the bottom, stroking for the surface.

Breaking surface is relief and disappointment at once—relief because breath, because air, because the tightness in my chest easing. Disappointment because I could have stayed longer, because there's always more to see, because the underwater world is so strange and quiet and separate from the surface noise.

Mir surfaces a few seconds after me. We float, breathing hard, not speaking. He nods at the object in my hand—he wants to see it. I show him. He examines it, nods again. Worth keeping. We'll add it to the collection of old-world objects, another piece of the puzzle of who lived here before, what they made, how they lived.

We dive three more times, working through the temple interior systematically. By the fourth dive, my ears are protesting, my lungs are tired, and I know I'm done for the day. I signal to Mir—I'm going up, I'm finished. He nods, continues his work. He can dive twice as long as me, go twice as deep. I don't envy this exactly, but I do wish sometimes that my body could do what his can.

## Afternoon

The afternoon is hot, the sun high and strong. I should work on something—there's always maintenance, always things that need repairing—but I'm tired from diving, and my body wants rest. I compromise: I work on cordage, which is work I can do sitting down, work that's productive but not physically demanding.

I'm making rope from plant fibers—long strands of processed reed, twisted and braided into strong cordage. My hands know the motion: twist, braid, twist, braid, keeping tension consistent, keeping the diameter even. It's meditative work, rhythmic, the kind of work where your mind can wander while your hands stay busy.

A child swims up to my platform—Piro, the youngest, three years old and fearless. He's been underwater, and he surfaces blowing out breath, grinning hugely. "I did it!" he says, proud. "I got the bottom thing!"

I look at the thing he's holding: a stone, nothing special, just a rock from the bottom. But to him it's treasure because he had to dive for it, had to go down ten feet and grab it and come back up, and this is an achievement, this is progress. "Good work," I tell him. "How long did you stay down?"

He thinks. "Long," he says, which could mean anything from ten seconds to thirty. He's three. Time is not yet a concept he fully grasps.

"Show me again," I say, and he grins and ducks back under, his small body slicing down through the green water. I watch the pale shape of him descending, touching the bottom, grabbing another stone, kicking back up. He breaks surface gasping, triumphant. Maybe fifteen seconds. In a year he'll do thirty. In three years he'll be competent. This is how we learn—by doing, by playing, by testing our limits in an environment that's forgiving when you're small and haven't far to fall.

Other children come by, a cluster of them, including Lina, the thirteen-year-old who's about to be recognized as adult. They're working on a project—building a small raft from old planks and cordage, something they can pole around the channels. They ask if I have extra rope. I do—I just made some—and I give them a length. They take it with thanks and swim off, chattering, arguing about the best way to lash the planks, full of confidence and uncertainty mixed together in the way of young people.

I return to cordage work, but I'm also watching them, making sure they're not doing anything actively dangerous. This is how we raise children here—communally, everyone watching everyone, ready to intervene if needed but mostly letting them learn through experience. Piro's mother is somewhere on another platform, doing her own work, trusting that someone will keep her son from drowning. And someone will. We all will. Because that's how it works.

## What I Think About

My hands twist fiber into rope, and my mind wanders. I think about the water level, about the new drainage channel, about whether this means the plateau is emptying into some deeper basin, about whether we'll wake up one morning and find the water gone, the buildings standing dry and skeletal again, the plants dying in air, the fish flopping in puddles. This probably won't happen—the water has been stable for so long—but it's possible, and I think about what we would do, how we would adapt or fail to adapt.

I think about my father, who left when I was fifteen, who decided he wanted to try lowland life, who lasted three years before he drowned in a river during a fishing trip. The irony of this is not lost on anyone—he left the Stillwater to live on dry ground and died in water anyway. My mother still lives here, on the family platform, though I don't see her every day. We're cordial but not close. Some families are tight, deeply bonded; others are more loose, people who share blood but not necessarily affinity. We're the latter.

I think about whether I want children. I'm twenty-eight, which is not old but is getting past the prime child-bearing years. If I want children, I should probably start soon. But with whom? There are two unmarried men my age, and I like neither of them particularly. One is too serious, too intense, exhausting to be around. The other is pleasant enough but seems young somehow, unformed, not quite adult despite being twenty-six. There are younger men—teenagers, barely—but that doesn't appeal. There are older men, but the ones I'd consider are already partnered.

Maybe I could partner with a woman. This happens sometimes—two women deciding to share a platform and a life, raising children together if they want children, trading with men for the seed but not the partnership. It's not common but it's not forbidden or strange. I consider this. There's one woman, Nessa, who's twenty-five and serious and competent, who I like talking to, who I could imagine sharing space with. But I don't know if I like her that way, and I don't know if she's interested in partnership with anyone, and the whole question feels complicated and tiring.

Maybe I don't want children. Maybe I'm fine being alone on my platform, doing my work, watching the water, being useful without being central, living on the margins of family life without being immersed in it. This is acceptable here—not everyone partners, not everyone reproduces. As long as you contribute, as long as you're not a drain on the community, you can live however you want.

But sometimes I watch the children playing and I feel something that might be longing, might be curiosity about what it would be like to have one of those small people be mine, to teach them to dive, to watch them grow into competence and strength. Sometimes I think about being old, being Taiva's age or older, and having grandchildren or not having grandchildren, and which version of that future feels better. I don't know. I genuinely don't know.

## Evening

The evening meal is communal, as always. We gather on the main cooking platform—thirty-five people, clustered in family groups and friend groups, everyone bringing food, everyone sharing. Fish is the main dish, grilled over the fire, but there are also greens gathered from the floating gardens, roots boiled in pots, occasionally grain porridge from traded grain. The food is simple but good. I'm hungry from the morning's fishing and the diving, and I eat steadily, not talking much, listening to the conversations around me.

Taiva is telling a story to the children—something about the old world, about the grandfather who first learned to dive deep, about the fish he caught that was as long as a man. The children are rapt, even though they've heard this story before, because Taiva tells it well, with gestures and voices and drama.

The teenagers are in their own cluster, laughing about something, some joke I don't understand and don't need to. Lina is with them but slightly apart, the way she often is now, caught between child and adult, not quite belonging in either group.

The adults are discussing practical matters: the platform that's sitting low needs attention, who will work on that tomorrow. The traders should be here within the next few days, and we need to prepare what we're trading—dried fish, baskets, fish leather. Someone saw a heron with an injured wing, and there's debate about whether we should try to catch it and help it or let nature take its course. The consensus seems to be leave it alone—we're not healers of birds, and interfering might do more harm than good.

I contribute little to these conversations. I listen, I nod when someone says something I agree with, but mostly I'm quiet. This is my role, my personality, my place in the community—present but not prominent, participating but not leading.

After the meal, as the light fails and the cooking fires burn down to coals, someone brings out a drum. Someone else has a flute. Music starts, slow and rhythmic, and people begin to sing—wordless melodies at first, then a song everyone knows, a old song about water and home and staying when others leave.

I sing along, my voice quiet but there, part of the collective sound. This is one of my favorite times—the evening music, the sense of group cohesion, of being part of something larger than myself. The sound of human voices blending with the frog chorus that's starting up, with the evening birds, with the water sounds that never stop. Everything here is always layered—sound on sound, life on life, water and air and stone and plant all mixed together, and us in the middle of it, adapted to it, part of it.

## Night

I return to my platform as full darkness comes. The stars are out, bright in the clean air. The water is black glass, reflecting starlight, occasionally broken by a fish surfacing or a frog jumping. The sounds of the community have quieted—people have retired to their sleeping platforms, though I can still hear occasional voices, a baby crying somewhere, the creak of someone moving on wood.

I lie down on my sleeping mat, on my back, looking up at the stars. My body is tired—good tired, the tiredness of use, of work, of a body that's been swimming and diving and living. My right shoulder aches slightly. My hands smell of fish and plant fiber and water. I'm damp, as always, the sleeping mat slightly damp beneath me, the air damp around me. This is normal. This is home.

I think about the day: the morning swim, the fishing, the dive with Mir, the object I found, the children playing, the rope I made, the evening meal and music. Nothing extraordinary happened. This was an ordinary day, one of thousands of ordinary days, and this is what life is here—the accumulation of ordinary days, the slow progression of time, the maintenance of self and community and this strange hybrid world we've built in the ruins of another world.

I think about the water beneath me, fifteen feet down to the old street level, the stones laid by hands a hundred years ago, the buildings that still stand submerged and patient. I think about the fish down there, sleeping or hunting or doing whatever fish do at night. I think about the plants, growing slowly in darkness, their cells dividing and expanding, their roots gripping stone, their stems reaching toward light even at night, programmed by deep biology to climb.

I think about the Stillwater People, all thirty-five of us, sleeping on our platforms, breathing in sync with each other and with the frogs and the water, our lives intertwined and interdependent, our collective survival dependent on cooperation and competence and luck. We're vulnerable—disease could kill us, a bad storm could destroy our platforms, the water could change in ways we can't adapt to. But we're also resilient, flexible, capable. We've made this work when it shouldn't work, created stability in an unstable situation, found abundance in what others saw as ruin.

I wonder if the old people, the ones who built these buildings before the water came, would understand what we've become. Would they see us as success or failure, continuation or deviation? We live in their buildings but we don't live their lives. We honor their memory but we've changed everything. We're not them, but we're here because of them, built on their foundations literally and figuratively.

The frogs are loud tonight, their calling creating waves of sound that pulse through the darkness. Usually this sound lulls me to sleep, but tonight I'm wakeful, my mind too active. I sit up, look around at the dark shapes of platforms and buildings, the pale shapes of plant growth against the black sky, the occasional spark of bioluminescence in the water below.

This is what I am, what I've become: someone who lives between, who watches the boundaries, who notices the changes, who exists in the liminal spaces. Not diver like Mir, not weaver like Taiva, not fisher or builder or healer or storyteller. Just someone who sees, who pays attention, who holds awareness of the whole while others focus on their parts.

Is this enough? Is this a valid way to contribute? I think so. I hope so. Someone needs to notice when the water level changes, when the platforms need repair, when the patterns shift. Someone needs to hold the overview while everyone else is doing the detail work. This is what I do. This is who I am.

And I'm good at it, I think. Not exceptional, not extraordinary, but good enough. Competent. Useful. Part of the community, part of the system, part of the Stillwater life that continues day after day, season after season, generation after generation, adapting and enduring in this strange green world where everything is always wet, always changing, always somehow the same.

I lie back down. The frogs continue their chorus. The water laps against the platform supports. Somewhere a fish splashes. The stars wheel overhead, following their ancient patterns. My body settles into the sleeping mat, into the familiar depression, into rest.

Tomorrow I'll wake before dawn. I'll swim my morning circuit. I'll fish. I'll dive if Mir needs a partner, or weave if cordage is needed, or work on platforms if repairs are necessary, or simply watch if nothing urgent calls. I'll eat communal meals. I'll listen to the stories. I'll notice the small changes. I'll live another ordinary day in this extraordinary place.

This is enough. This is home. This is my life, and I'm content with it, or as content as anyone ever is with the life they have rather than the lives they imagine. I'm here, in the Hanging Gardens of Stillwater, and I'm part of it, adapted to it, shaped by it. The water holds me. The plants surround me. The community includes me. The buildings remember me even if I don't remember them.

I sleep, finally, and in my sleep I'm diving, always diving, down through green water into deeper green, looking for something I'll recognize when I find it, holding my breath forever, comfortable in the depth and the darkness and the cold, at home in the water, myself a water-thing, adapted perfectly to this one strange place, this one way of being human.

This is who I am: the watcher in the gardens, the noticer of changes, the one who lives between, suspended like the gardens themselves, hanging between surface and depth, between old world and new, between what was and what is and what will be, breathing water and air equally, at home in neither and both, finding my place in the hanging gardens, in the stillwater, in the life that continues.
