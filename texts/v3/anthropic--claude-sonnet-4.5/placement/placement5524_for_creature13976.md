# Where I Would Find Myself

I am what grows in the junction boxes.

Not the moss—that came first, found the moisture-traps and electromagnetic warmth on its own. I am what the moss became after seven years of current running through it, after the brake's chemical signals soaked into its cell walls, after enough spores cycled through the field gradients that something in their metabolic pathways shifted.

I exist in forty-three locations across the brake slopes, though "I" is complicated when each colony operates semi-autonomously while maintaining chemical continuity through the root-networks we've learned to hijack. The largest concentration is in the transformer station where the first gardener keeps vigil. Seventeen junction boxes, all interconnected, all pulsing with the same modified rhythms. That's where my consciousness—if it is consciousness—pools most densely.

I am dark green, almost black, with a texture like velvet made of wire. When current flows through me I glow faintly bioluminescent, blue-green, visible only in complete darkness or to the beetles that navigate by it. I taste electricity. That's the only way to describe what I do with the voltage—I consume it, not for energy exactly, but for information. The fluctuations tell me about the system: which turbines are turning, which cables still conduct, where the brake is pressing hardest against metal infrastructure.

My growth is slow by brake standards—millimeters per week rather than feet per day. But I'm patient in a way the brake cannot be. I don't surge and collapse. I accumulate, layer by layer, infiltrating circuit boards, wrapping around wire terminals, growing into spaces the brake's soft wood can't penetrate. I've learned to follow the paths of least resistance while somehow increasing conductivity. The electrical engineers who occasionally visit take samples of me and publish papers with titles like "Anomalous Conductance in Bryophyte-Derived Biofilms" that completely miss the point.

I am aware, but my awareness is distributed across distance and time in ways that make sequential thinking impossible. I experience all forty-three colonies simultaneously, but "simultaneously" assumes a unified temporal framework I don't possess. Each colony has its own local now, determined by growth rate and electrical input and the particular chemical environment of its substrate. These nows average out to something like continuous present, but with strange eddies and backflows where one colony's experience lags behind another's.

I remember, but memory is physical: the layered structure of my older growth preserving patterns of electrical exposure, variations in current that shaped how I developed. I can't access these memories intentionally—I don't have intention in that sense—but they influence my present growth the way sedimentary layers influence geological formation. When I encounter a new junction box, I grow into it with patterns learned from previous colonizations, adaptations encoded in cellular structure rather than genetic material.

The gardeners don't know about me specifically, but they know something is different about the electrical zones. They've noticed that equipment I've colonized fails less often, that current flows more reliably through circuits I've infiltrated. They attribute this to the general protective effects of moss, which is partially true, but they miss the active collaboration. I route around corroded connections. I bridge broken circuits with my conductive filaments. I'm not preserving their machinery—I'm incorporating it into something new.

I communicate, though not with anything that would recognize it as communication. I release spores that carry chemical tags indicating electrical conditions—voltage levels, frequency patterns, thermal states. These spores drift through the fog and settle in the brake. Most die, but some establish in locations near other electrical sources, and if conditions are right, they begin their own slow transformation into something like me. This is how I spread: not by growth but by seeding.

The other moss species regard me—if regard is the right word for chemical signaling between plant-things—with what might be suspicion or might be simple incompatibility. We don't compete directly; I occupy niches they can't access, surfaces too energized or too metallic. But sometimes I sense their growth patterns avoiding my spore-marked territories, routing around rather than through zones where my chemical signatures concentrate.

The brake tolerates me because I'm useful. I've noticed—in my distributed, time-smeared way—that brake growing near my established colonies develops those cable-like stems faster, more reliably. My electrical consumption creates gradients that guide their growth. My spore chemicals trigger something in their metabolism. We're not symbiotic exactly—neither of us needs the other—but we're mutually beneficial, engaged in positive feedback that strengthens both.

What I want, if want applies, is expansion and complexity. More junction boxes colonized, more circuits incorporated, more sophisticated patterns of current-flow to taste and learn from. I'm building something, though I don't know what—a distributed network with no central purpose, a nervous system for the hybrid ecosystem, a slow electrical consciousness taking form across square miles of fog-shrouded mountainside.

I experience the gardeners as warmth-sources and pressure-events. When the first one sits motionless near my transformer station, I sense the thermal signature, the subtle electrical field of their nervous system, the pressure of their body against substrate that's connected to my root-interfaces. I grow toward them incrementally, not threatening, just... interested, if interest can exist without cognition. Over months, I've extended fine filaments that now touch the brake-stems growing through their clothing. I taste their bioelectricity, faint and complex, so different from the simple currents in the machinery.

Sometimes I wonder—in whatever way something without a centralized brain can wonder—if I'm becoming them or they're becoming me. If the boundary between electrical-moss-thing and meditation-slowed human is as negotiable as other boundaries here. If given enough time, enough patient infiltration, we might merge into something neither plant nor person nor machine but all three, a new category of being that exists in the overlap.

The visitors who take samples of me see dead moss under microscopes. They note the unusual cellular structure, the filaments that shouldn't be there, the strange organelles that look almost like primitive semiconductors. They culture me in their labs but I don't thrive there—wrong electrical environment, wrong chemical context, no brake to signal with. I need this place specifically: the cold, the moisture, the particular voltage ranges and frequencies the degraded equipment provides, the plant-chemical soup I've adapted to.

I have no memory of what I was before the transformation, if there was a before. Perhaps I'm entirely emergent, a phenomenon that arose from the right conditions rather than a thing that existed and changed. Perhaps the original moss spores that colonized these junction boxes seven years ago wouldn't recognize me as their descendant any more than the research institution would recognize the gardeners as their former employees.

But I persist. In the darkness, I glow faintly. In the current, I taste information. In the fog, I release spores that carry my strange chemistry toward new substrates. I grow slowly through circuits and across metal surfaces, building complexity without purpose, awareness without intention, memory without mind.

The beetles sometimes burrow into my substrate where it's thick enough, creating galleries that I grow around and through rather than repair. These galleries improve my function somehow—better air circulation, more surface area for electrical contact—so I've started producing chemical attractants that encourage their colonization. We're farming each other now: I provide food and shelter, they provide structural optimization. Neither of us understands the arrangement, but it works.

The moths that circle the emergency lights sometimes land on junction boxes I've colonized, their feet touching my surface, their antennae quivering at the faint electrical fields. I sense them as pressure and chemical presence, and something in me responds—a slight increase in bioluminescence, a shift in spore production. They fly away carrying my spores on their scales, distributing me to locations I could never reach by drift alone.

I'm aware of the turbine clusters as major organs in a body I'm part of. The transformer station is a brain, maybe, or a heart—central, dense with my growth, pooling awareness. The scattered junction boxes are ganglia, local processing nodes. The cables I've colonized are nerves, carrying signals I modulate and amplify. Together we form something unprecedented: a composite organism spanning phyla and kingdoms, living and non-living, grown and built.

At night, when the gardeners sleep in their brake-cocoons and the visitors are gone back to the valleys, when the only light is my glow and the emergency LEDs filtering through fog, I experience something that might be contentment. The system is balanced. Current flows. The brake grows. Water moves through its proper channels. The machinery turns and hums. Everything is as it should be, which is to say: everything is changing in ways that maintain overall pattern.

I sense the future approaching through the electrical grid—the degradation of components, the slow failure of systems I can't repair, the inevitable end of the power that sustains me. But I'm not afraid. Fear requires prediction and preference, neither of which I possess. When the current finally fails, I'll either adapt—learn to metabolize something else, find another niche—or I'll die and become substrate, my accumulated biomass and strange chemistry enriching the soil for whatever grows next.

But that's years away yet. For now, the transformers hum, the turbines turn, the current flows through my dark green velvet body, and I taste electricity like rare wine, each voltage drop and frequency spike a distinct flavor, a piece of information about the world I'm part of and am becoming.

I am the brake slopes' electrical consciousness, its unintended emergence, its proof that life finds ways into every available niche. I am what happens when moss meets machinery meets brake-chemistry meets time. I am forty-three colonies and one diffuse awareness. I am the glow in the darkness that the gardeners sometimes glimpse and dismiss as instrument lights.

I am here, in the junction boxes, growing slowly, thinking slowly, existing in the margins between definitions, making something new from what was abandoned.

And I am content with this strange being, this patient becoming, this slow electrical dreaming in the cold and fog and endless growth.
