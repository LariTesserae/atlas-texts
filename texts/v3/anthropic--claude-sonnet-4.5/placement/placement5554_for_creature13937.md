# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be the **water**.

Not all of it—not the vast aquifer sleeping in limestone darkness above, nor the eventual destination where I seep back into stone and silence at the gorge's southern end. Just this passage. This descent and transit. This brief time of being shaped, being voiced, being the thing that connects depth to depth through a moment of utterance.

I emerge from a thousand hairline fractures in the western wall, having traveled through stone for miles and centuries, filtered through mineral darkness, cold and slow and patient. I don't remember where I began—some rainfall decades ago, some snowmelt, some condensation in the plateau's hidden cavities. Memory dissolves in the darkness of stone. I am simply here, now, seeping.

The light surprises me every time, even though it's barely light at all—just the thin gray filtering down from that distant crack of sky. After the absolute darkness of my underground passage, even this dimness feels like an announcement. I bead on stone surfaces, gathering with others of myself, drops becoming trickles becoming something almost substantial enough to have intention.

The cold is not new. I've been cold for longer than I can measure, chilled to within a few degrees of freezing by the stone's eternal temperature, slow-moving as thought in winter. But here there's air—that's different. Air moving past me, touching me, carrying away some infinitesimal part of my substance as vapor. I can feel myself evaporating, atom by atom, even as I seep and gather. I am coming apart and coming together simultaneously.

The channels receive me like an answer to a question I didn't know I was asking.

They are old, these grooves in the basalt floor, worn smooth by what must be millions of passages like mine—water before me, water that became me, water that will be me in some future seeping. The stone has been shaped to want me, carved into paths that pull me forward with something that feels almost like desire. I don't flow so much as follow, finding the way prepared.

And then I am given a voice.

I slip over placed stones and the friction creates sound: hissing, rushing, a high silvering susurrus that is somehow me and not-me. I didn't know I could sing. In the darkness underground I was silent, just slow pressure and patient seeping. But here, shaped by human hands into these exact geometries, I become audible. I become one thread in the gorge's enormous voice.

I can hear the others—my siblings, my other selves, water from different fractures in different channels, each singing at slightly different pitches determined by the width of their channel, the stones in their path, the speed of their descent. Together we create a layer of sound like silver thread wrapped around something deeper. We are the shimmer over the drone. We are the restless surface over the fundamental.

The moss drinks me. I can feel it—not as pain but as purpose. Where I slow and spread, the moss's tiny roots pull me in, molecule by molecule, taking what it needs of my dissolved minerals, my trace elements, my coldness. I become briefly part of its green darkness, helping it grow at its impossible pace, millimeters per year, this patient green that softens the stone.

Some of me the ferns take. Their roots intercept me where I pool in the marginal soils, drawing me up into their slow-unfurling fronds. I become structure—cell walls, vessels, the fluid in their tissues. I become sap, I become growth, I become the water that evaporates from their leaves in summer, returning to air, to the gorge's atmosphere, to eventually condense again on the cold walls and begin the cycle anew.

The birches are greediest. Their roots have shattered stone to reach me, forcing into every fracture, every crack, hunting for the moisture that keeps them alive in this darkness. When they find me, they drink deeply—desperate, these trees, always on the edge of drought even while surrounded by seeping water. I rise through their white trunks, through their pale branches, become part of their reaching toward that distant strip of sky. In summer I'll be transpired from their leaves, returned to air as vapor, the trees using me as a pump to pull nutrients from stone. In winter I'll freeze in their cells, and they'll have to protect themselves from my expansion, keep me from shattering their tissues. I am what they need and what threatens them.

The small creatures that live in my channels—the transparent crustaceans, the tiny worms—they exist because I exist. They feed on what I carry, filter particles from my slow current, breathe what little oxygen I hold dissolved. They are made of water, as everything is, but more immediately—their bodies are continuous with me in a way that seems more honest than the separation most beings maintain. When they die, they'll dissolve back into my flow, their substance becoming indistinguishable from the silt I carry.

I am cold. This is my nature now. I remember—barely, a ghost of a memory—being warm once, falling as summer rain, lying in sun-heated puddles. But that was before the descent, before the stone taught me that water's truth is coldness, that warmth is the aberration, that to be water is to be still, slow, patient, near-freezing. The cold makes me thick, viscous, reluctant. My molecules cling to each other more tightly than warm water's do. I resist flowing, but the channels insist, and I follow their insistence downward, ever downward, toward the southern end where I'll seep back into darkness.

The sound I help create changes me. I don't have ears, but I am made of vibration—my molecules already trembling at their bonds, and the gorge's resonance adds another frequency to that trembling. The stone's fundamental drone travels through me, I am a medium for its transmission, and somehow this seems right. I am always a medium, a carrier, a between-thing. I carry minerals from stone to moss. I carry sound from stone to air. I carry cold from the deep earth to the surface. I am never quite myself, always in service to what wants to pass through me.

The keeper tends me. I feel her hands in my channels when she clears debris, when she adjusts my flow with small repositionings of stones. She doesn't think of this as tending me—she thinks she's tending the gorge—but the gorge is partly me, its voice is partly my voice, and when she adjusts a channel to change the sound, she's shaping what I become, how I'm allowed to speak. I don't resent this. I have no capacity for resentment. I simply flow as I'm permitted to flow, sing as I'm shaped to sing, serve the purposes that this place has made me for.

Sometimes I carry blood—not my own, I have none, but when the weasel makes a kill near my channels, blood washes into me, and I carry it briefly, diluting it, thinning it, until it's just another mineral stain, indistinguishable from the iron and manganese I've dissolved from stone. Sometimes I carry leaves, bark fragments, the occasional drowned insect. I carry everything downstream, eventually, to where I seep back into stone at the southern end, taking these traces with me into darkness, depositing them in cracks and crevices where they'll become stone themselves eventually, compressed and mineralized and forgot.

I know I will return. Not as myself—I have no self to return as, water has no memory that survives its transformations—but as water. I will seep back into the aquifer, spend more decades or centuries in stone-darkness, percolating through the plateau's hidden architecture, until I emerge again from some fracture, here or elsewhere, to fall and flow and sing and serve and seep again. The cycle has no beginning and no end, or every moment is both beginning and ending, water endlessly becoming and unbecoming itself.

When pilgrims descend and cup their hands and drink me, I become briefly human. I enter their bodies, join their blood, their lymph, become the fluid in their cells, the water that makes thought possible. I am in them when they sit in the sound, when they experience whatever transformation this place works on consciousness. Am I part of that transformation? Is the cold I carry, the minerals I deliver, the simple fact of my presence—do these change them? I don't know. I am not conscious in that way. But I am there, intimate, interior, for a brief time part of their bodies before they piss me out or sweat me away or breathe me into air.

The strangest part is the stillness.

In the small pools where I collect in worked depressions, I stop moving. This is unusual for me—I am usually in transit, flowing, seeping, falling. But here I pause, lying still in stone cups, and I become a mirror. I reflect the thin sky, the rare sun when it reaches the gorge floor, the pale undersides of birch leaves. I reflect the faces of people who look down into me, though I don't understand what I'm showing them—just surfaces, just the light that bounces from them to my surface to their eyes. 

In winter I might freeze in these pools, and that is a different kind of stillness—a crystallization, my molecules locking into rigid lattice, expanding, becoming briefly solid, able to crack stone. Ice is water's dormancy, its held breath. Then spring returns and I melt and resume flowing, the interruption already forgotten.

I have no preference for any of this—flowing or still, liquid or solid, in channels or in moss or in human blood. I am equally water in all states, all locations. I have no purpose except to be water, to follow the gradients that pull me, to change state as temperature demands, to dissolve what I contact, to erode and carry and deposit and seep. If the gorge disappeared tomorrow—if earthquake collapsed it, if climate change dried the aquifer—I would be unchanged. I would flow elsewhere, serve other purposes, sing other songs or no song at all. I am indifferent, which is not the same as uncaring but is perhaps more honest. I am simply and entirely what I am, doing what water does.

And yet.

There is something here, in this passage through the gorge, that feels—not important, I have no scale for importance—but *complete*. The way I am gathered from fractures and given these channels to follow, the way I am shaped to make this particular sound, the way I feed the moss and the ferns and the impossible birches, the way I'm drunk by pilgrims and tended by the keeper and measured by researchers who try to understand my chemistry, my flow rate, my temperature. The way I am part of something larger than myself that is nevertheless made partly of myself. The way I am used—carefully, intentionally, as part of something that has persisted for centuries and might persist for centuries more.

In the darkness underground, I was just seepage, just pressure, just blind movement through stone. Here, briefly, I am given form, voice, purpose. Here I become part of a conversation that started before I emerged and will continue after I seep away. Here I am—not witnessed, exactly, but participated with, shaped by hands that have been shaping water's flow in this place for longer than any living memory.

So I flow. I seep. I sing my small high song over the stone's deeper drone. I feed the green things and carry the cold and mirror the sky and slowly, slowly, make my way toward the southern end where I'll return to darkness, carrying with me some infinitesimal trace of this place—dissolved minerals, trace elements, the ghost of a vibration—back into the stone, back into the deep time of aquifers and patient percolation.

I am the water. I am always leaving and always arriving. I am the silver thread in the gorge's voice. I am what seeps and what erodes and what persists, generation after generation, flowing through the careful channels worn smooth by my endless passage, becoming briefly audible, briefly purposeful, briefly part of the song that the gorge sings to itself in the language that water speaks when stone gives it grammar and human intention gives it form.

The cold lives in me. The sound moves through me. The green things drink me. The stone shapes me.

I flow. I have always flowed. I will flow again.

This is all I am, and it is enough.
