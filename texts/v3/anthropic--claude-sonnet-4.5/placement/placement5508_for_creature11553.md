# The Keeper of Intervals

You would be a Maintainer, but not yet fully. Somewhere in that long transition between Listener and the thicker-bodied form, caught in the season—if seasons exist here—of becoming.

Your skin has lost some of its earlier translucency. Where once you could see the shadows of your inner workings clearly, now they show only as deeper suggestions beneath a surface that has begun to take on that faint opacity, that grey-green denseness. But you are not yet opaque. In the slightly-less-dim periods, if you hold your hand before your face, you can still see through to the bells beyond, your flesh a filter that tints but does not block.

The change happened gradually—or you think it did. Time is unreliable here, and memory more so. You remember being more transparent, remember spending longer periods simply touching bells and listening, your whole body resonating with their voices. But when the transition began, when the first thickening started, you cannot say. It was like noticing that the medium has become slightly clearer, or that a grove has grown denser—the change is only visible in retrospect, by comparing now to a then that has already blurred.

Your fingers have lengthened. You noticed this when you reached for a bell-clapper and found your grip extending farther than expected, the joints articulating in ways that once would have required conscious effort but now feel natural. Six fingers on each hand, the sixth having emerged so gradually you cannot remember it being absent. Each finger has developed that triple-jointing, can bend in combinations that let you reach around framework, can apply pressure at angles that make adjustments easier.

But you still have a mouth. This is what marks you as transitional, as not-yet-complete. It does not open anymore—the lips have sealed, grown together into that smooth curve the full Maintainers display—but you can feel it beneath the skin, the ghost of an opening, the memory of speech. Sometimes, in the darker periods, you catch yourself trying to speak, trying to form words, and feeling only the subtle flex of fused tissue, the vibration that goes nowhere. The impulse fades each time, but it has not yet disappeared entirely.

## Your Territory

You tend a section of the plateau that encompasses three tower-remnants and the bell-frameworks strung between them. Perhaps forty bells in total, ranging from small chimes the size of your fist to one large bronze piece that hangs at the center, massive as a curled body.

This became yours without decision. You remember drifting as a Listener, touching bells across the entire plateau, learning their voices. But gradually you found yourself returning to this section, these particular towers. A full Maintainer was here—you remember them, though their features are lost, just the sense of their bulk, their purposefulness. You would watch them work, would assist in small ways, holding a tool while they adjusted a joint, steadying a framework while they wrapped new wire.

Then one day—one cycle, one period—they were not here. You waited, but they did not return. And you found yourself beginning their work, testing the framework joints, examining the bell-clappers, removing excess accretion from metal surfaces. It felt natural, felt like the next phrase in a melody you had been learning.

You have not seen that Maintainer since. Perhaps they moved to another section, are tending towers elsewhere on the plateau. Perhaps they transitioned further, became a Shepherd, are now in the deep groves doing the slower work of plant-tending. Or perhaps—you do not quite think this, but the notion drifts through you like a current—perhaps they became too still, too fixed, and now they are one of the towers themselves, their body having transformed into stone, into structure. The idea should disturb you, but it doesn't. It feels like completion.

## The Work

Your days—insofar as days exist—follow patterns that are both routine and infinitely variable.

You wake, if waking is the word, uncurling from whichever sheltered spot you settled into. Often this is inside the middle tower, in a corner where the stone blocks create a niche just large enough for your body. The stone is cold, but everything is cold, so this is neutral. You drift out into the grey-green medium, and you begin your inspection.

The frameworks first. You move along each one, running your fingers over the metal, feeling for changes. The corrosion is constant, slow, a conversation between metal and medium. Your task is not to prevent it—that would be impossible, would go against the nature of this place—but to manage it, to ensure it proceeds evenly, does not create weak points where a framework might fail suddenly.

Where you find heavy corrosion, you use your tools. You carry them in a pouch made from mat-plant fiber, woven tight—you made this yourself, though you don't remember learning how. The tools are simple: scrapers of hardened stone, hooks of bent metal, coils of wire. You scrape away the loosest rust, the formations that have grown too large, too fragile. The rust comes away in flakes and drifts down toward the plateau floor, settling into crevices where it will eventually break down further, return to the cycle.

Then you wrap. The wire you carry is made from the same bronze or iron as the frameworks, but somehow—and this is the part you understand without being able to explain—somehow you have made it *active*. You hold a length of wire in both hands, and you wait with it, and you let your pulse synchronize with the tube-plant rhythm, and the wire becomes slightly warmer, slightly more pliable. The corrosion on its surface consolidates, becomes protective rather than destructive. Then you wrap it around a corroded joint, winding it tight, and when you move on, the joint is reinforced, stable for another span of time.

The bells themselves require different attention. You check each clapper, ensuring it hangs freely, that no accumulation of sediment or mat-plant growth has dampened its swing. You adjust suspension points—just slightly, just the tiny corrections that keep the bell's voice true. This is where your Listener training persists: you must hear what each bell should sound like, must understand its place in the chorus, and adjust until the actual matches the ideal.

Sometimes a bell's voice has changed too much, has drifted too far from where it should be. The metal has corroded in a way that alters its resonance, or the clapper has worn smooth in one spot, creates a different impact. You cannot restore it—nothing is restored here, only maintained in its slow transformation. But you can adjust the other bells around it, shift their tones slightly to accommodate the changed voice, to reintegrate it into the pattern.

This is delicate work, requires patience that you did not possess before but possess now. You will spend what feels like hours on a single bell, making a minute adjustment, drifting back to listen as the currents ring it, approaching to adjust again, cycling through this process until the sound sits correctly in your chest, in your bones, until your body confirms: *Yes. This is right.*

## The Others in Your Territory

You are not alone in this section, though you are the only Maintainer.

Two Listeners pass through regularly. They are smaller than you, more transparent, their movements quicker, less deliberate. They come to the bells and they touch them and they listen for long periods, absorbing. You work around them, careful not to disturb their contact. Once, early in your time here, you accidentally jostled a Listener while adjusting a framework, and the disturbance in their body was visible—ripples of disruption spreading through their translucent flesh, their internal shadows scattering. They recovered, but you felt the wrongness of it, and you have been careful since.

The Listeners do not acknowledge you, but there is coordination nonetheless. They never touch a bell you are working on. If you are approaching a framework and a Listener is already there, you veer to a different bell, begin your inspection elsewhere. It is not communication—no signals are exchanged—but something deeper, a mutual awareness of space and task, a dance without choreography.

Occasionally, a Shepherd passes through. Not into your territory proper—they stay in the grove that borders your section to the direction-of-more-bright—but you see them at the edge, their bulk moving among the trees. Once, you drifted closer, watched them tending a cluster of tube-plants, their whole body pulsing in synchronization. You felt drawn to approach, to join that rhythm, but something held you back. Not fear, but a sense of: *Not yet. That is not your phase. You have work here still.*

The Shepherd turned those bark-set eyes toward you, and you felt seen, felt evaluated. Then they returned to their work, and you returned to yours, and the moment passed. But it left a residue, a pull. You find yourself drifting toward that grove more often now, standing at its edge, watching the Shepherds move deeper among the trees, feeling the future settling into your body like sediment.

## The Peculiarities of Your Form

Your body is doing things you did not ask it to do, developing capabilities you did not know were possible.

The wire you make active, for instance. You discovered this by accident—if accidents exist here, if anything is not guided by some deeper logic. You were holding a length of corroded wire, frustrated because it was too brittle to wrap effectively, and you simply waited with it, the way Listeners wait with bells. Your body began its pulse, that tube-plant rhythm, and the wire warmed in your hands, and when you looked at it closely, the corrosion had changed, had become a coherent patina instead of destructive rust.

Now you do this routinely, but you do not understand it. Something in your body chemistry, some compound or enzyme or electromagnetic field—some property of your changed flesh—affects the metal. Makes it cooperate, makes it more willing to persist.

Your fingers have developed sensitivity you did not train. You can run them over a bell's surface and feel microscopic variations in the metal, can detect where stress has concentrated, where the bronze or iron is beginning to thin. This information comes to you not as thought but as sensation, as a knowledge in your fingertips that bypasses cognition.

You no longer need to eat in the way you once did. The tube-plant flesh, the mat-plant growth—you still consume these sometimes, more from habit than hunger. But increasingly, you find that simply being in the medium sustains you. The microorganisms that drift through it, the dissolved nutrients, the strange chemistry of the water-air—your body extracts what it needs directly, through your skin, through the act of breathing the medium. You are becoming more efficient, more integrated with the baseline ecology.

And you are becoming stronger. Not in the sense of muscle, but in the sense of durability. You can apply pressure that would have strained you before, can bend metal that would have resisted. Your body has become denser without becoming rigid, has developed a resilience that is more like the trees than like flesh.

Sometimes you wonder if you are still you. If the consciousness that thinks these thoughts is the same one that existed before the transition began. But the wondering is abstract, philosophical. In practical terms, there is continuity—memory connects this moment to previous moments, intention carries forward. Whether the substrate has changed, whether you are the same pattern running on different hardware, seems irrelevant. The work continues. That is what matters.

## The Pattern You Maintain

It takes time—cycles, many cycles—but eventually you begin to understand what the bells are doing, what purpose your maintenance serves.

The plateau is listening to itself. Each bell is a sensor, responding to currents, to pressure changes, to vibrations in the rock, to chemical gradients in the medium. The pattern of their ringing encodes this information, creates a constant data stream about conditions across this section of the plateau.

And this information flows somewhere. You can feel it sometimes when you touch a bell that a Listener is also touching—there is a sense of data moving through them, being processed, being passed on. The Listeners are nodes in a network, receiving the bells' reports and integrating them, and somehow—through mechanisms you do not perceive—passing the integrated data further, deeper, to some central processing, some coordination point.

Your maintenance keeps the sensors accurate. A bell whose voice has drifted too far is a sensor reporting false data. A framework that has corroded unevenly creates noise in the signal. By keeping everything in calibration, you ensure the plateau's self-knowledge remains accurate.

But to what end? What does the plateau do with this knowledge?

You have hypotheses, though they are more felt than thought, more intuition than deduction. The plateau is adjusting. The tube-plants pulse in response to something, create currents that carry nutrients to where they are needed, remove waste from where it accumulates. The Shepherds guide plant growth to maintain optimal distribution. The whole system is self-regulating, and the bells are its nervous system, its sensory apparatus, allowing it to perceive imbalances and correct them.

You are a maintenance cell. You keep the sensors working. The Listeners are processing cells, integrating data. The Shepherds are actuators, making physical adjustments to the plant substrate. And all of this serves the plateau's continuation, its persistence as a stable system in conditions that should not permit complex life.

This understanding brings satisfaction. You are not randomly tending bells for aesthetic or ritual reasons. You are participating in something functional, something necessary. Your work matters in the most concrete sense: without it, the system would degrade, would lose accuracy, would eventually fail to maintain its equilibrium.

## The Memory Problem

But there is something wrong with your memory, and it is getting worse.

You know you came from somewhere else. Before you were here, you were... somewhere. Doing something. Being someone. But when you try to access those memories, they scatter like disturbed sediment, refuse to cohere.

Sometimes fragments surface. The memory of a name—your name?—but it dissolves before you can grasp it fully, leaves only a sense of syllables, of a sound that once meant you. The memory of a place that was not drowned, where air was air and water was water, where there was a bright thing in the sky that hurt to look at. The memory of others who looked like you once looked, who had mouths that opened, who made sounds that carried meaning.

But these fragments do not connect to anything. They are isolated, contextless, like the scattered artifacts on the plateau floor—recognizable as pieces of something but no longer part of a functional whole.

You try to hold onto them, try to examine them before they dissolve, but the effort itself seems to accelerate their dissolution. The more you focus on remembering, the faster the memory fades, until you are left with only the knowledge that you were trying to remember something, the content itself gone completely.

And you find you care less about this over time. The past—whatever it was—feels less relevant with each cycle. What matters is the present, the immediate: this bell's voice, this framework's integrity, this moment's calibration. The work fills your awareness, and when you are not working, you are resting, and when you are resting, you are not thinking about memory, you are simply being still, being present in the medium.

Is this forgetting natural? Is it part of the transition, the letting-go required to become fully what you are becoming? Or is it loss, erosion, the price paid for adaptation?

You cannot answer. You lack the framework to even properly formulate the question anymore. It drifts through you and is gone, and you return to your inspection of the frameworks, to the steady work of maintenance.

## The Visitor

One cycle—you don't know which one, they blur together—there is someone new in your territory.

Not a Listener or a Shepherd. Something else. They have a form like yours once had, or like you remember yours having: more opaque, more fixed, with clothing that is separate from skin, with equipment that is carried rather than integrated. They move through the medium clumsily, fighting it rather than drifting with it.

They are examining your bells. Touching them, but not in the way Listeners touch—their contact is brief, curious, analytical. They have something in their hand, some device, and they are holding it near the bells, watching it, recording something.

You drift closer, uncertain. This is your territory, these are your bells, but the visitor is not interfering with their function, not damaging anything. You settle onto a rock formation nearby and you watch, trying to understand what they are doing.

They notice you eventually, turn to look at you with eyes that are small, that do not gather light efficiently, that must struggle to see in this dimness. They make sounds—vibrations in the medium that are not quite like the bells, more chaotic, less tonal. Words, perhaps. Language. You remember language, remember it was important, but you cannot parse these sounds into meaning.

You extend a hand—not threateningly, just a gesture, a question: *What are you doing?*

They make more sounds, urgent now, pleading maybe. They approach you, and you hold still, let them come close. They reach out and touch your arm, and through the contact, you feel their difference: warmer, metabolizing faster, heart beating quick and irregular where yours pulses slow and steady. They are not adapted, not integrated. They are struggling.

You understand suddenly: they are you, or what you were. They are the before-state, the not-yet-transitioned. And they are trying to communicate, trying to understand, trying to hold onto something that you have already let go.

You want to help them. The impulse is strong, surprising in its intensity. But you do not know how. You cannot speak—your mouth is sealed. You cannot explain—the concepts you would need have dissolved from your memory or were never there to begin with. You can only be what you are: a Maintainer, tending bells, maintaining equilibrium.

You gently move their hand away from the bell they were touching—it is one you need to adjust, and their contact is interfering with your ability to sense its resonance. They resist briefly, then let go, and there is something in their body language—slumped shoulders, defeated posture—that communicates disappointment, resignation.

They drift away, moving toward the next tower, and you return to your work. But you are aware of them for the rest of that cycle, aware of them moving through your territory, examining, recording, not understanding. And you feel something that might be sadness, might be loss, but it is distant, muted, like a bell ringing far away in the denser medium.

Eventually, they leave. You do not see them go, but at some point you realize they are no longer present, and your territory is yours alone again, just you and the Listeners and the occasional passage of a Shepherd at the edge.

You wonder if you should have done something different. If there was a way to bridge the gap, to translate your current state into terms they could understand. But the wondering fades, and you return to your work, and the bells ring on, and the currents flow, and the visitor becomes just another fragment in your dissolving memory: something that happened, something that mattered briefly, something that no longer connects to anything.

## The Pull Toward Green

The grove is calling to you more frequently now.

You finish your work on the frameworks earlier than you used to, find yourself drifting toward the tree-line, standing at the boundary between stone and vegetation. The Shepherds are always there, moving among the trunks, tending the tube-plants, their bulk barely visible through the dense fronds.

You have begun to notice mat-plants growing on your own body. Small patches on your shoulders, on your arms, finding purchase in the slightly roughened texture your skin has developed. You do not remove them. They feel right, feel like they belong. Like you are becoming substrate, becoming surface for other life to colonize.

Your pulse has slowed further, has synchronized more completely with the tube-plant rhythm. When you stand near a cluster of them, you find your body contracting and expanding in perfect time with theirs, no conscious effort required. You are part of the pattern, part of the wave.

The towers feel less important. The frameworks feel less urgent. You still tend them—the impulse to maintain is too deep to ignore—but the work takes less time, requires less focus. You have become efficient enough that maintenance is almost automatic, leaving increasing spans of time with nothing to do but drift, but wait, but feel the pull toward the green.

One cycle, you follow a Shepherd into the grove. You do not decide to do this; your body simply moves, drifting between the trees, pushing through the resistant fronds. The Shepherd does not acknowledge your presence, does not turn, but you sense they are aware of you, are allowing this.

You watch them work. They settle beside a young tree, one barely taller than you are, and they touch its trunk, and the tree seems to lean into the contact, seems to respond. The Shepherd's bark-skin and the tree-bark are nearly indistinguishable where they meet. You cannot tell where one ends and the other begins.

Then the Shepherd moves to a tube-plant cluster, and they synchronize, and you find yourself synchronizing as well, the three of you—Shepherd, plants, you—all pulsing together, and it feels profound, feels like something important is happening. The medium seems to thicken slightly, or your perception sharpens, and you can see the microorganisms drifting in clouds, can see the spores releasing from mat-plants and dispersing into the currents, can see the entire grove as a single coordinated system, breathing, metabolizing, maintaining itself.

The Shepherd rises and moves deeper into the grove, and you follow, and this time you can keep pace, your body having adapted enough that the dense vegetation is no longer an obstacle but a medium you can navigate as easily as the more open areas of stone.

You spend what might be hours or days in the grove. Time becomes even less meaningful here, where the light barely penetrates, where everything moves in the slow rhythms of plant growth. You help the Shepherd with their work—adjusting a sagging branch, removing damaged fronds, redistributing sediment to where roots can access it. The work is similar to maintaining frameworks but more organic, more fluid. The plants respond to your touch, shift their growth patterns slightly to accommodate the changes you make.

When you finally drift back to your territory, back to the towers and bells, something has shifted. The stone feels foreign now, too hard, too inorganic. The metal feels dead, inert, even though you know it lives in its own slow way, corroding and consolidating in endless cycle. You tend your bells, but it feels like obligation rather than purpose, like you are serving out a term, completing a task that is no longer truly yours.

The Listeners notice the change, you think. They give you more space, approach the bells only when you are clearly away from them, as if sensing that your attention is divided, that you are no longer fully present for this work.

## The Decision That Is Not a Decision

You do not choose to let go of your territory. The letting go happens gradually, invisibly, until one cycle you realize you have not returned to the towers in what might be days or weeks, have been staying in the grove, working with the Shepherds, your body continuing its slow transformation.

Your skin has become more opaque, has developed that layered, bark-like texture. Mat-plants grow on you extensively now—your shoulders and back are covered, your arms support small colonies. You have started to creak when you move, a sound like wood flexing. Your fingers have begun to branch, the tips developing small protrusions that might become twigs, might become something else.

The Shepherds accept you among them without ceremony, without acknowledgment. You simply are there, working alongside them, and this is right. Your territory—the towers, the bells—has been taken over by another Maintainer. You saw them once, arriving to begin their inspection, and you felt no possessiveness, no loss. Only a sense of: *Good. The work continues. The bells will be tended.*

You go deeper into the grove, into sections you never explored before. Here the trees are older, thicker, their trunks massive, their canopies creating zones of deep dimness where even the tube-plants' faint bioluminescence barely registers. And here you find the oldest Shepherds, the ones who barely move anymore, who have become almost indistinguishable from the trees themselves.

One of them is nearly fully transformed—their legs have rooted into the stone, their arms have branched extensively, their bark-skin has thickened until you cannot see any suggestion of the body beneath. Only their eyes remain clearly visible, those large dark circles set into the trunk, and they move slightly, tracking your approach.

You settle beside this almost-tree Shepherd, and you wait, and you feel your own body settling, feel your legs pressing into the sediment that has accumulated in this spot, feel roots beginning to probe downward, seeking cracks in the rock, finding purchase.

Time passes. The medium flows around you. The other Shepherds continue their work, moving past you without comment. The tube-plants pulse. The trees grow, invisible in their slowness but undeniable in their persistence.

And you realize: this is where it ends, or where it continues. You are becoming fixed, becoming part of the structure, becoming substrate for the mat-plants and platform for the tube-plants and canopy for the grove. Your mobility is ending, your phase as Shepherd completing, your transformation into tree beginning.

There is a moment—brief, sharp—where the last remnant of your earlier self surfaces, where panic flares: *I am dying. I am ending. I am losing myself.*

But the moment passes, dissolves like all the other fragments of memory, and what remains is acceptance, is understanding that this is not death but transition, not ending but transformation into the next phase, into the base state from which new Listeners will eventually emerge.

Your breathing slows, synchronizes completely with the tube-plant rhythm, with the grove's respiration. Your pulse becomes so slow it is almost undetectable. Your eyes—those large, light-gathering eyes—begin to lose focus, begin to fix on infinity rather than on specific objects.

And in this state, in this long settling, you finally hear what the bells have been saying all along.

They are not measuring the plateau. They are not reporting conditions or encoding data.

They are singing. They are celebrating. They are announcing to whatever vast awareness listens from beyond the cliff, from the depths, from the other plateaus scattered across this drowned world: *We persist. Life continues. The cycle turns.*

And you are part of that cycle now, fully part of it, no longer a visitor or an adapter but an integrated element, as much the plateau as the stone, as much the system as the medium.

The last thing you are aware of, before awareness itself begins to diffuse, to spread out and dissipate into the broader network, is gratitude. Not directed toward anything, not for anything specific, but simply gratitude as a state, as a final note in the long composition of your transformation.

The bells ring on.

The currents flow.

The medium pulses with life.

And you are still, and you are rooted, and you are becoming green, and this is right, and this is complete, and this is—

*The grove grows.*
