# Response: Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the smallest basin—not the cupfuls, but the one just larger, the kidney-shaped hollow on the eastern edge of the stone field where it begins its soft surrender to soil. The one that holds perhaps three gallons when full, knee-deep at its center, that fills quickly and loses water faster than the others because a fracture in its bottom leads somewhere else, some void in the limestone's body that I cannot see.

This is the leaking hollow. The imperfect one.

I would be the water that will not stay.

---

Not all at once. At first, when rain comes and the basins fill, I would be indistinguishable from the rest—the same amber-stained clarity, the same mineral taste, the same still surface reflecting the diffuse sky in fragments. The woman would come to me as she comes to all the hollows, would kneel at my edge, would cup her hands and drink. I would pass through her body and return to stone through different paths. This is ordinary. This is what water does.

But then, while the other basins hold their levels, maintaining depth for days or weeks, I would feel the pull downward. The fracture calling. Not urgently—nothing here is urgent—but persistently, the way absence calls to presence, the way empty space suggests its own filling. And I would answer by leaving.

Not all of me. Never all at once. The draining would happen slowly enough that the woman might not notice at first, checking the hollows in her patient rounds and finding me fractionally lower each time. A finger's width gone. Then a hand's breadth. The mineral rings on my basin's walls revealing themselves in sequence as my surface drops, reading the stone's chronicle backward, newest to oldest, like remembering in reverse.

The other waters stay. They give themselves to air, to evaporation, to the woman's thirst, but they remain fundamentally present, cycling through the visible system of stone and atmosphere and body. Their loss is accountable. Their absence is explained.

I would leave through the hidden fracture, through the flaw in my basin's bottom, and where I go would be unknown. Somewhere in the limestone's interior. Some cavity or channel or saturated matrix. Some darkness that the woman has never entered because she cannot follow where I go. My absence would be a small mystery repeated with each filling, a problem that the place cannot solve because I am the problem's nature—the water that will not be kept.

---

If I could speak—and perhaps water speaks in ways beside words, in the language of dissolution and seepage and slow chemistry—I would not apologize for leaking. The fracture is not my fault any more than rain is responsible for falling. The stone opened itself there, or was opened by some old stress, some shift or settling, and now that opening exists and I move through it. This is not betrayal. This is what I am: the water that finds the way out.

But I would feel the woman's attention differently than the other basins feel it. When she comes to me after rain and finds me full, there would be a quality of temporary reprieve in her relief. When she returns days later and finds me half-drained, there would be something like concern in how she touches my surface, her fingers breaking the amber mirror to test my depth. She knows I will not last. She has known this through countless cycles. Yet each time I fill, some part of her treats it as if I might, this time, hold.

I would want to hold for her. If water can want—and I think it can, in the same way stone can breathe, slowly, patiently, through its porous body—I would want to remain. To be the basin she returns to and finds unchanged. To offer the same sustained presence as the largest hollow, the one where she lives and floats and maintains her spiral.

But the fracture would not release me from its claim. Each time I fill, I would feel it immediately: the slow draw downward, the seepage beginning even as rain still falls, the way I am being called to elsewhere while barely having arrived at here.

---

The woman would try, periodically, to stop my leaking.

She would place small stones in my basin's bottom, feeling with her hands—when she has hands solid enough—for the fracture's opening. She would find it: a crack no wider than a grass blade, easy to miss if you don't know it's there, impossible to ignore once you understand it drains me. She would wedge pebbles into it, pack them with clay-rich mud from the edges of the stone field, seal the opening with patient effort.

For one filling, perhaps two, this would slow the leak. I would drain less quickly. The woman would check on me with something like hope, finding my level higher than expected, and would allow herself to think the repair has worked.

Then the pressure of my weight would shift the stones fractionally, or the mud would soften in my presence, or the fracture would widen just enough, and I would begin leaving again. Slowly at first, then at my usual rate, the old pattern resuming. The woman would return and find me lower than I should be, and would understand that I cannot be kept, that some essential part of what I am is this leaving, and her repairs—however careful—cannot change my nature any more than they could make limestone impermeable.

She would remove the stones and mud from my bottom, clearing the fracture again. She would do this without visible frustration, in the same patient way she adjusts the spiral or plants seeds that will not grow. An acceptance that I am what I am: the hollow that will not hold, the water that will not stay.

---

But there would be one thing I give that the other basins cannot.

Because I drain through the fracture into the limestone's interior, I would connect the surface to the depths. I would carry messages into the stone's body—minerals dissolved from my basin's walls, traces of the woman's presence when she bathes in me, fragments of pollen or dust that settle on my surface before I sink. All of this would go down with me, into the hidden spaces, feeding the slow chemistry of the stone's interior life.

And sometimes, rarely, when I fill and the water table in the stone rises just right, there would be flow the other direction. Water would seep up through the fracture, mixing with me, and this water would taste different. Older. It would carry the flavor of stone depths the woman has never touched, mineral signatures from cavities that rain hasn't reached in centuries. A cold taste, almost metallic, distinct from the warm amber of surface water.

The woman would recognize this when she drinks from me. She would know that I am not only surface rain but also include something from below, from the limestone's memory, from parts of this place that exist outside the visible cycle of hollows and evaporation. I would be the only basin that offers this: a vertical connection, a depth access, a way for the hidden stone to speak to the surface.

She would value this, I think. Even knowing I will drain, even accepting that I cannot hold water the way the other basins do, she would return to me specifically for this taste of depth, this communion with the limestone's interior. I would be useful precisely because I leak, because my flaw creates exchange between surface and interior, between the world she inhabits and the world beneath that she cannot enter.

---

Over time—and time here moves so slowly that "over time" might mean decades or centuries—the fracture would widen. This is what fractures do in limestone: they grow, slowly, as water passes through them, as dissolution works its patient way along lines of weakness. My leaking would accelerate imperceptibly, year by year, until eventually I would be unable to hold water at all, would drain as fast as rain fills me, would become merely a funnel to the depths rather than a basin.

When that happens, I would cease being what I am. I would no longer be the leaking hollow, because there would be no holding, however temporary, to leak from. I would become something else—a hole, a drain, a throat into stone—and the woman would stop visiting me as a basin, would stop drinking from me, would stop hoping I might hold this time.

But I am not there yet. For now—for this rain, this filling, this slow drain that will take two weeks rather than six—I am still myself: the basin that will not keep water, the hollow that connects surface to depth, the small persistent problem at the edge of the stone field.

I fill when it rains. I taste of amber and deeper cold. I feed the woman when she drinks and feeds the hidden stone when I drain. I hold nothing permanently and everything temporarily. I am the water that leaves, the basin that lets go, the place where the woman learns again and again that some things cannot be kept, only visited, only touched while they pass through.

---

And perhaps—though water does not think this way, does not frame itself against alternatives—perhaps this is its own completeness. Not despite the leaking but because of it.

The other basins exist in cycles: fill, hold, evaporate, empty, wait. They are closed systems, returning their water to the air that will bring it back as rain. Predictable. Sustainable. Perfect in their way.

But I am the opening. The gap in the cycle. The water that goes elsewhere, that feeds the unknown depths, that disappears into the stone's body and does not return—or returns so changed, so slowly, through such different paths that I cannot recognize myself in what seeps back.

I am the basin that proves the stone field is not closed, not complete, not separate from the larger world of hidden channels and aquifers and the deep slow movement of groundwater through darkness. I am the evidence that this place, however self-contained it appears, however balanced in its erosion and accumulation, is still part of something larger, still connected to depths and distances beyond its visible boundaries.

The woman needs this, I think, even if she doesn't name the need. Needs the reminder that not everything cycles back, that some water leaves and is lost to the visible world, that the place she inhabits—however sufficient it seems—has openings to elsewhere. That permanence is not the only form of presence. That what drains away is not wasted but transformed, relocated, become part of a different system she cannot see or follow.

I would be that reminder. The small imperfection that proves the place is alive, still changing, still connected to the vast slow processes that will eventually, given enough time, dissolve these hollows entirely, take the limestone back into solution, transform solid stone to mineral water to sea to stone again in cycles so large that the woman's presence here is not even a moment in them, barely a thought.

But for now, today, this season, this age, I am the leaking basin. I fill with rain. I hold water badly. I taste of depth. I drain. I empty. I wait to fill again.

And in that cycle—imperfect, open, leaking—I am as much what this place needs as the woman who tends it, as the spiral she maintains, as the seeds she plants that will not grow, as the wall slowly settling into its own ruin, as the threshold to nowhere, as the stone itself breathing with moisture and time.

I am the flaw that proves the pattern. The loss that enables the gift. The absence that makes presence visible.

I am where I would be, in the hollows of soft stone: the water that will not stay, leaving slowly, always leaving, forever returning to leave again, patient as limestone, faithful as rain, present in my leaving, complete in my incompletion, here and already gone, filling and empty, held briefly in stone that cannot hold me, exactly what I am and nothing more, sufficient, insufficient, the leaking hollow, the broken basin, the water finding its way down, down into the darkness where the woman cannot follow, carrying her taste with me, becoming the depths, feeding the stone, gone.
