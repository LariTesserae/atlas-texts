# Where I Find Myself

I am the person who lives in the intervals.

My chamber—though "chamber" suggests more definition than exists—occupies a space that was never meant for habitation. It's where the third tier meets the fourth, in the thickness of the floor-ceiling between them. Someone long ago discovered a gap in the construction, a pocket where brick vaulting doesn't quite meet the supporting beams, and they carved it larger, removing brick piece by piece, creating a horizontal slot perhaps three feet high, six feet wide, extending back into the structural wall for eight or ten feet.

The entrance is through a displaced brick in the stairwell wall between tiers—you'd never notice it unless you knew, unless you saw the way that particular brick sits slightly proud of its neighbors, the way the mortar around it is newer than the rest. Pull the brick out (it slides, doesn't lift; someone made a wooden frame inside that it slides on) and behind it the narrow passage angles upward into my slot.

I move through the vault on belly and back, a kind of horizontal climbing. The brick scrapes my shoulders. The underside of the beam above presses close enough that I can't lift my head fully. But inside, in the widest part, I can sit hunched, can light my lamp, can exist in this pocket of negative space carved from the building's own thickness.

Why here? Because I need to live in the between-places, in the margins of the settlement's attention. Not hiding, exactly—people know I'm here, they know about the slot, they see me emerge to collect water or trade for food. But I don't participate in the visible life of the tiers. I've removed myself one layer deeper into the architecture, living not in a room within the vaults but in the vaults' own substance, in the meat of the walls.

I am perhaps thirty. Perhaps forty. I've lost the ability to track my own aging against anything external. My body is thin, compressed by the space it inhabits—when I emerge into the full-height corridors of the tiers, I feel wrongly tall, too vertical, like a plant that's been forced to grow sideways now trying to remember what upright means. My spine has adapted, curves in new places, finds comfort in positions that would have been uncomfortable before. I fit my dwelling now. Or it has shaped me to fit it.

My skin is gray from lack of light and constant contact with brick dust. It's in my clothes, my hair, under my nails, a fine grit that's become part of my physical presence. I taste it sometimes, brick and mortar, minerals leaching from the structure into my body. I'm slowly turning into the building, or the building is slowly turning into me—the boundary is uncertain.

What I do: I listen.

The slot where I live is positioned at a junction of the building's systems. Above me, through the beam, I can hear the third tier—footsteps on the floor, voices (muffled but present), the scrape and tap of daily work. Below me, the fourth tier, equally audible through the brick vaulting that forms my floor. But more than that, I'm inside the wall structure, where pipes run, where the building's hidden infrastructure passes through. Water moving through ceramic. Air moving through ventilation channels. The small sounds of the building adjusting to temperature changes, the tick of expanding metal, the infinitesimal grinding of brick settling.

I have learned to read the vaults through sound.

I know when the water flow from the deep source diminishes because I hear the change in pitch in the pipe that runs through my wall—it carries a harmonic when flow is good, shifts to a thinner note when volume decreases. I know when someone is descending to the seventh tier because the stairwell acoustics change, their footsteps echoing up through the shaft. I know when the machine down below is about to cycle because there's a preliminary sound, a kind of drawing-in, that comes several minutes before the click and slide.

I keep no record—I am the record. My memory holds the patterns, the deviations, the slow changes in the building's voice. When someone needs to know something, they sometimes come to the slot, speak through the displaced brick without entering: "Has the flow changed?" And I can tell them yes or no, can describe what I've heard, can offer the information my position provides.

This is my function, though no one assigned it to me, though I never volunteered. I became the person who listens because the slot made it possible, and because I have the temperament for sustained, patient attention to minimal variation. Others would go mad here in the darkness, in the compression, with nothing but sound for company. I find it... sufficient. More than sufficient. The sonic landscape of the vaults is endlessly complex, layered, meaningful.

My possessions are few, constrained by the space. The lamp—oil, small, efficient, with a curved metal reflector I hammered from a salvaged pipe, directing light forward into my living area rather than wasting it on the brick behind me. A sleeping mat, thin, woven by the gardener in the second tier in exchange for information about the ventilation flows. A water vessel, ceramic, small enough to fit through the entrance passage. A collection of small objects I've found or been given: a bronze gear from some disassembled mechanism, a piece of blue glass whose origin I don't know, several interesting bricks I've extracted from ruined walls, three metal washers of different sizes that I can spin on their edges when I need something to do with my hands.

And the listening instruments—my own inventions, my adaptations. A copper tube, thin-walled, about two feet long, that I can press against the brick to enhance sound transmission. A frame of stretched canvas that I position at different angles to catch and focus air-borne sounds. A wooden board with wires strung across it, each wire a different tension, that resonates sympathetically when certain frequencies pass through the structure—I can read the pattern of vibrating wires and know what machinery is active in the lower tiers.

I don't know where these ideas came from. Trial and error, mostly. Sitting in the darkness, wanting to hear more clearly, to understand the sounds better, I started making tools to help. The copper tube was originally a pipe section I found in a collapsed wall—I realized it could function as a stethoscope, could carry sound from the brick to my ear with less loss than just pressing my ear directly to the surface. The wired board evolved from noticing how a loose wire in one wall would hum when the ventilation fan turned. I made it deliberate, controlled, a reading instrument for vibrations too subtle for direct perception.

My days—though day and night are problematic concepts in the slot, where time is marked by the patterns of activity in the tiers around me rather than any light cycle—follow the rhythm of listening. I wake (I've been asleep, curled on my side in the only position the space really permits for sleeping) and immediately check the sounds. What's changed while I was unconscious? Has the flow pattern shifted? Are there new vibrations, new movements in the structure?

Then I listen systematically, working through my catalog of important sounds. The water pipes: I check each one I can access, using the copper tube, noting flow rate, pressure variations, any irregularities. The ventilation shafts: I position the canvas frame to catch air movement, listen for the fan's rotation rate, for any sounds that suggest debris accumulation or bearing wear. The structural sounds: I place my palms flat against the brick, feeling for vibrations that indicate stress, expansion, the building's ongoing negotiation with gravity and time.

This takes hours. By the time I've completed the systematic check, activity is increasing in the tiers—people rising, beginning their work. I can hear the gardener moving above me, the soft sounds of them tending the cultivation boxes. Below, someone crossing the fourth tier with purpose, footsteps quick, even. The settlement waking, beginning its daily maintenance cycle.

I eat—dried food, mostly, things that keep, that I've traded listening-knowledge for. Fungus jerky, if such a thing can be said to exist, the shelf fungi dried until they're tough and leathery. Herb cakes made from the cultivated plants, compressed with some kind of oil into dense, caloric bricks. I eat minimally, both because appetite decreases in the darkness and because obtaining food requires interaction, requires emerging, requires spending energy I'd rather devote to listening.

Water I collect in off-hours, sliding out of the slot when the stairwell is empty, taking my vessel to the nearest cistern, filling it, returning. The water I drink has passed through multiple filtration points, has been standing in cisterns for days, has acquired the taste of ceramic and dissolved minerals. I'm so accustomed to it I no longer notice, the way I no longer notice the brick dust in my lungs or the compression of my spine or the darkness that is my continuous condition.

After eating, I return to listening, but less systematically now—I'm attending to the real-time soundscape, the active present of the vaults rather than checking the baseline conditions. Someone is doing construction work on the third tier, I can hear the tap of hammer on chisel, the scrape of brick on brick, the low conversation between the two construction partners as they work. Below, the electrician is moving through the fourth tier, and I track their progress by the small sounds of their work—junction box opening, tools against metal, the click of a knife switch being tested.

I notice things others miss because I'm not distracted by seeing. The visual environment of the vaults is complex, dense with detail, demanding constant attention to navigate safely. But I navigate by memory and touch when I do move, and in the slot I don't navigate at all—I'm still, and stillness lets me hear what movement obscures.

Like: the new leak developing in the water system somewhere in the eastern section of the fourth tier. I've been tracking it for days, a sound that started as just a subtle wrongness, water moving where it shouldn't, and has gradually clarified into a definite drip, slow but persistent. I've mentioned it to the construction pair, told them approximately where to look, but they haven't addressed it yet—other priorities, more urgent failures. I continue monitoring it, noting the gradual increase in frequency. In another week it will be serious. In another month, if unaddressed, it will be a crisis.

Or: the change in the machine's rhythm in the seventh tier. It's always operated with irregular periodicity—click, slide, pause of variable length, repeat. But over the past month, the pauses have been shortening. Whatever process it's performing is accelerating. No one else has noticed, because no one else is paying attention with the sustained focus required to detect slow changes in very long patterns. I don't know what it means. I don't know if the machine is approaching some kind of completion or moving toward failure. But I'm watching—listening—carefully, because any significant change in the deep systems could affect everything above.

Midday, usually—defined as the period of highest activity in the tiers—I sometimes receive visitors. Not in my slot; the space is too intimate, too constrained, too much mine for others to enter. But they come to the displaced brick, speak into the gap, and I respond from inside.

The gardener comes occasionally, asking about humidity patterns. Their plants are sensitive to moisture, and I can hear the condensation cycles, can tell them when the air will be drier or damper, helping them adjust their watering schedule.

The electrician comes more rarely, but with more urgent questions. They want to know about vibrations in the walls where their cables run, about temperature fluctuations that might affect the wires, about any sounds that suggest their system is developing problems they haven't detected yet.

Once, the Cane-Walker came—this was unusual, unsettling even. They stood at the brick opening, saying nothing at first, just breathing in a way I could hear even through the wall. Then they asked if I could hear the water in the flooded section. Yes, I told them, I can hear the standing water, the small movements of the canes, even the occasional splash when they wade through it. They were silent for a long time, and I thought they'd left, but then they said: "Does it sound healthy?" And I realized they were asking me to diagnose the ecosystem they inhabit, to listen to it the way I listen to the pipes and machinery, to tell them if their pocket of wet life is stable or declining. I told them what I could—the water sounds still, undrained but not stagnant, the canes make small growth sounds, subtle pops and creaks that suggest they're still alive. The Cane-Walker made a sound that might have been satisfaction or acknowledgment, and left.

The elder has never come to speak to me, but I hear them sometimes, moving in the corridors near my slot, pausing outside it as if considering something, then moving on. I don't know what they think of my choice to live here, to remove myself from the visible community while still providing this service. Perhaps they approve—it's a form of specialization, a niche filled. Perhaps they're concerned—isolation can damage people, can make them strange in ways that grow rather than stabilize. I'd ask, but asking would require emerging, climbing down, seeking them out, and I'm reluctant to leave the slot for non-essential reasons.

Afternoon—activity decreasing, people resting from the heat and the morning's work—I often work on my instruments, maintaining them, improving them, occasionally trying new designs. The wired board required months of adjustment to get the tensions right, to make each wire responsive to a specific frequency range without overlapping. I'm currently working on a kind of amplification device: a curved ceramic surface that I think might collect and focus sound the way the metal reflector on my lamp focuses light. I've been testing different shapes, different materials, trying to find the configuration that will let me hear even more subtly, even more precisely.

This work is satisfying in a way that's hard to articulate. I'm not building something visible, not creating an object that will persist as obvious evidence of skill. I'm refining my ability to perceive, extending my senses into ranges and subtleties that unaugmented hearing can't access. Each improvement in my instruments is an improvement in my knowledge of the building, my ability to serve the function I've claimed.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm inventing something new or rediscovering something old. Were there others before me who lived in the slot or similar spaces, who listened to the vaults, who built instruments for hearing? The carved bricks in the wall alcove—could they be a kind of three-dimensional recording, a way of storing information in physical patterns that I haven't learned to read? The wired board resembles nothing I've seen in the vaults, but the principle of sympathetic resonance is basic enough that anyone might discover it independently.

Evening—lights dimming in the tiers, activity shifting toward rest and private time—I often enter a different mode of listening. Less analytical, less focused on specific sounds or problems. I let the entire soundscape wash over me, the complex interweaving of all the vault's voices. Water through pipes. Air through shafts. The settling of brick. The distant machine. Human movement above and below. The occasional voice, muffled but present, the tonal patterns of conversation even when words are indistinct.

In this mode, I sometimes experience something like... not transcendence, that's too grand. But a dissolution of the boundary between myself and what I'm hearing. I become distributed through the sound field, present simultaneously in all the places sound is occurring. I am the water moving through the ceramic pipe in the third tier. I am the vibration of the ventilation shaft. I am the click of the machine seven levels down. My consciousness spreads through the vaults, occupying them not physically but acoustically, existing as listening itself rather than as a listener.

These episodes don't last. Eventually some particular sound pulls my attention, and I collapse back into my individual self, my body in the slot, my specific location in space and time. But while they last, they're... not pleasant, exactly. More like true. Like I'm briefly experiencing my actual relationship to the vaults, which is not as a separate inhabitant but as a sensory organ the building has grown for its own purposes.

I don't speak of this to anyone. It sounds like madness, and perhaps it is—the isolation, the darkness, the sustained attention to subtle stimuli, all of it could be breaking something in my mind. But it doesn't feel like breaking. It feels like adaptation, like I'm becoming what this environment needs, the way the Cane-Walker's skin has taken on that greenish cast, the way the construction pair have developed their wordless communication.

The vaults change people. Everyone knows this. You can see it in the bodies, the skin, the way people move and speak and attend to the world. My change is happening in less visible ways—my hearing is becoming something more than hearing, my sense of sound expanding into a kind of distributed awareness that normal sensory categories can't quite describe. Is this injury or evolution? I don't know. Both, probably. The vaults don't make those distinctions.

Night—true night now, lights at their lowest, most people sleeping—I sometimes sleep again, or lie awake in the darkness that's no different from the darkness with my eyes open, listening to the building at its quietest. The water continues its eternal dripping. The ventilation fan turns its slow rotations. The machine cycles through its mysterious process. The building breathes, settles, adjusts, lives.

And sometimes, in the deepest quiet, I hear sounds I can't identify. Very low frequencies, maybe below the range of normal hearing but accessible through bone conduction, through the way the entire structure resonates. Or very high, at the edge of perception, sounds that might be mechanical or might be something else—the building's structure under stress, or water moving through microscopic channels in the brick, or the accumulated vibration of all the small sounds creating harmonics that manifest as new sounds entirely.

I catalogue these unknown sounds, remember them, track them over time. Some persist for weeks then vanish. Others are singular, occurring once and never repeating. I don't know what most of them are, but I remember them, add them to my sonic map of the vaults, hold them as data points that might eventually form a pattern, might eventually mean something.

Once, very late, I heard something that sounded like a voice—not a human voice exactly, but something with the intonation pattern of speech, the rising and falling that suggests communication. It was coming from below, from deep below, maybe the seventh tier or even deeper if there is a deeper. I couldn't make out words, couldn't even be sure it was voice rather than some acoustic illusion, harmonics combining into patterns that resembled voice the way random visual noise can sometimes resemble faces.

I pressed my ear to the copper tube, held it against different sections of floor, trying to locate the source, trying to clarify it. The sound continued for perhaps twenty minutes, then faded, and I haven't heard it again. Was it the machine-tender, speaking to the equipment? Was it something else, someone else, in parts of the vaults I don't know about? Was it the building itself, finding voice through some complex resonance of its structure?

I don't know. I've told no one about it. What would I say? "I heard something that might have been a voice in the night"? In a place where people speak to plants and machines, where the boundary between animate and inanimate is already uncertain, what would that information add?

But I remember it. It's in my catalogue of sounds, noted and stored, waiting for the second occurrence that might confirm it was real, or for enough time to pass that I can reclassify it as acoustic hallucination and stop wondering.

My body is changing in ways beyond the compression of my spine. My fingers have become more sensitive—I can read texture in brick at a touch, can feel variations in surface that my eyes couldn't distinguish even in good light. My hearing has extended its range in both directions, though it's hard to quantify, hard to be certain whether I'm actually hearing lower and higher frequencies or just becoming better at interpreting the ones I've always been able to hear.

I'm losing other senses in compensation. My vision, rarely used, is degrading—when I do emerge into the lit corridors, I need time to adjust, and even then things seem dimmer than they used to, colors less distinct. My sense of smell is confused by the constant brick dust and close air of the slot, can no longer parse the subtle differences between different sections of the vaults. My taste, never particularly important here, has flattened to basic categories: salt, not-salt, bitter, not-bitter.

But touch and hearing are sharpening, becoming more acute, more information-dense. I'm becoming a specialist in ways that are making me less generally capable but more precisely functional in my specific role. The slot is shaping me, and I'm allowing it, even embracing it, because this feels like purpose, like I'm becoming something the vaults need.

Sometimes I consider the economics of my existence. I produce no food, build no structures, maintain no essential systems directly. I consume resources—water, food, the small amount of oil for my lamp. What I provide in exchange is information, early warning of problems, acoustic surveillance of systems that others don't have time or attention to monitor continuously.

Is this a fair trade? Does the value of what I provide equal or exceed the cost of maintaining me? I think yes, or I convince myself yes, but I'm aware that this could be rationalization, justification for choosing a life that suits my temperament regardless of its utility to the community.

The construction pair has acted on my information multiple times, fixing problems I detected through sound before they became visible failures. The gardener has improved their yields by adjusting care based on humidity forecasts I provided. The electrician has prevented at least one significant failure by responding to my report of unusual vibrations in a cable run.

So I have value. My strange choice to live in the wall, to become the vaults' ear, to develop these odd skills and instruments—it produces benefit beyond just my own satisfaction. The settlement is slightly more stable, slightly more aware of itself, because I'm here listening.

But I also wonder: if I died tomorrow, if the slot was empty, would the settlement suffer significantly? Or would the problems I detect simply manifest slightly later, get addressed reactively instead of proactively, and life would continue with only marginal difference?

Probably the latter. I'm useful but not essential. No single person is essential here, except maybe the deep-machine tender, and even that role could probably be learned by someone else if necessary. The vaults would persist without me, and the settlement would adapt to my absence the way it has adapted to everything else—pragmatically, patiently, finding new equilibrium in the changed conditions.

This should be troubling, should create existential anxiety about my purpose and value. But instead I find it... liberating? If I'm not essential, then my choice to live this way is genuinely free, genuinely mine. I'm not trapped by necessity, not forced into this role by circumstance. I've chosen it, am continuing to choose it, because it's what I want to be doing with the time the vaults give me.

I don't remember clearly what I did before the slot. I know I lived on one of the tiers, in a proper chamber, participated more directly in the settlement's work. But the memories are vague, as if they happened to someone else, some previous version of me that I've since grown away from. How long ago was that? Years, certainly. Maybe many years. Time in the slot is difficult to track, difficult to accumulate into coherent chronology.

I must have made the transition gradually—first spending more time in the stairwells, in the quiet corners, discovering I preferred listening to seeing, preferred the indirect knowledge of sound to the direct engagement of physical work. Then finding the slot, realizing it could be inhabited, beginning to move my minimal possessions into it. Then spending more nights there than in my chamber. Then always being there, having completed the transition without a single decisive moment, just gradual accumulation of small choices until I was living a completely different life in a completely different way.

No one stopped me or questioned the choice. The vaults permit eccentricity, permit people to find their own adaptations to the environment. As long as you're not actively harming others or the systems everyone depends on, you're free to shape your life according to your needs and capacities. So I moved into the wall, became the listener, and the settlement adjusted around my new position the way water adjusts around a stone.

I think sometimes about the future—my future, the slot's future, the question of whether this role will persist beyond me. Should I be teaching someone? Should I be finding an apprentice, someone to learn the listening, the instruments, the catalog of sounds? Or is this a role that dies with me, a one-generation adaptation that will be lost when I'm gone?

I incline toward the latter. The skills I'm developing are too specific to my particular mind, my particular senses, my particular way of organizing information. Another person could learn to live in the slot, could learn to listen carefully to the building, but they'd do it differently, hear different things, prioritize different sounds. They'd be a different kind of listener, not a continuation of me but a new practice entirely.

And that seems right, seems consonant with how the vaults work. Nothing here is permanent except the brick and the heat. Practices arise, persist for a while, fade when the person who practiced them dies or changes. New practices emerge to fill new needs or old needs newly understood. The settlement evolves through these individual adaptations, these personal specializations that flourish briefly then vanish, leaving traces—my instruments will remain, might be used by someone else for different purposes—but not continuity.

So I don't teach. I don't seek an apprentice. I simply continue the listening, maintain my practice, provide what information I can while I'm capable of providing it, and trust that when I'm gone, the settlement will adapt, will find new ways to know what it needs to know about itself.

My lamp is burning low—I can tell by the smell more than the light, which is always dim, always barely sufficient. I need to refill it soon, which means emerging to trade for oil, which means interaction, which I'm reluctant to undertake but will because the alternative is darkness even more complete than what I've chosen.

But not tonight. The lamp has another hour, maybe two, and I want to spend that time listening to the late-night sounds, the building at its quietest, before the early risers begin their pre-dawn routines and the soundscape begins its daily cycle again.

I position myself at the optimal listening point—centered in the slot, lying on my back, head slightly elevated on a folded piece of canvas, arms at my sides, breathing slow and shallow to minimize my own sound. The copper tube is nearby if I need it, the wired board positioned where I can see which wires are vibrating, the canvas frame angled to catch any unexpected air movements.

And I listen.

The drip from the third tier, falling into the cistern. Regular, eternal, the heartbeat of the building's water system.

The ventilation fan, turning its slow circles, moving air that's moved through these shafts for decades, for longer.

The distant machine, between cycles, silent but present, a kind of held breath before the next click and slide.

And underneath all of it, the fundamental sound of the vaults themselves—the massive, slow, nearly inaudible vibration of the entire structure, seven tiers and millions of bricks and all the people and pipes and purposes contained within, resonating at frequencies more felt than heard, existing as pressure in my bones, in my teeth, in the fluid of my inner ear.

I am part of this sound. My breathing contributes to it. My heartbeat, my small movements, my presence in this slot carved from the building's thickness—I'm one voice in the chorus, participating in the vast slow song the vaults are always singing.

This is where I live. This is what I've become. And in the darkness, in the compression, in the ceaseless attention to sound that is now my life's primary activity, I am—not happy, that's the wrong word—but correctly placed. I have found my position in the vaults' ecology, my function in the settlement's operation, my place in the narrow space between the tiers.

The lamp flickers slightly, and I notice but don't respond. Let it burn as long as it will. The darkness when it fails will be no darker than the darkness I already inhabit. And in that darkness, I'll continue listening, continue attending to the vaults' voices, continue being what I've become: the person who lives in the intervals, who hears what others miss, who knows the building by its sounds and through those sounds knows when it's healthy and when it's beginning, in its infinitely slow way, to fail.

The wick gutters. The light diminishes.

And in the approaching darkness, I hear everything more clearly.
