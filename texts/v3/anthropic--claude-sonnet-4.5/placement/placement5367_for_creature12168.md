# What Lives There

## The Sound-Changers

In the cracks where moss films the meeting stones, there are organisms that are not quite moss themselves. They grow *within* the moss, or alongside it, or perhaps they *are* the moss in certain phases—this is unclear. What is certain is that they respond to the thunder.

These organisms—call them the sound-changers—form crystalline filaments finer than spider silk, branching through the moss matrix in patterns that mirror the acoustic properties of their particular crack. Each colony grows into a shape that resonates with a specific frequency buried within the thunder's broad roar. They are living tuning forks, microscopic and ancient.

They do not photosynthesize, or if they do, that is supplementary to their primary energy source: they harvest the thunder itself. The sustained acoustic energy that would shatter most cellular structures has been, through millions of years, converted into something usable. The filaments vibrate at scales smaller than their own cellular structure, and this vibration—through mechanisms that would require instruments that don't exist to observe—becomes chemical energy, becomes growth, becomes reproduction.

When conditions align in ways that might happen once per decade, the sound-changers release spores. These are not spores in any familiar sense. They are acoustic packages, bundles of genetic material wrapped in protein shells that vibrate at precise frequencies. They drift on the wind—that rare, straight wind—until they encounter a crack that resonates at their frequency. Only then do they settle, germinate, begin their filamental growth.

The sound-changers are why the moss grows in such specific locations. The moss needs them, or they need the moss, or they are aspects of a single organism that has differentiated into what appears to be symbiosis but might be something more integrated. Where sound-changers colonize, moss follows within decades. Where moss grows oldest, sound-changer colonies reach their maximum density and begin to subtly alter the resonant properties of the crack itself, tuning the stone.

You cannot see them without magnification. But you can sometimes—if you place your ear against the moss in absolute stillness, which is impossible because the thunder never stops—sense a quality in the sound. A filtration. A texture. The thunder that emerges from cracks inhabited by sound-changers is not quite the same as the thunder that strikes bare stone. It has been changed, given structure, patterned in ways that exist just below the threshold of human perception.

They are patient beyond understanding. A colony might take a century to reach maturity. The individual filaments live only months, but the colony persists, each generation adding to the acoustic structure, refining the resonance, deepening the harvest of sound into the bedrock of their chemical existence.

If you could extract a sound-changer filament and observe it in silence, it would die within hours. Not from lack of nutrients or light, but from stillness. The vibration is not supplementary to their existence—it is foundational. They are organisms that have evolved to require sound the way plants require light, fish require water, humans require air. Remove them from the thunder and they simply cease, the way a wave ceases when the water goes still.

## The Pool-Dweller

The shadow beneath the pool's ambiguous surface is singular. There is only one. It has been here since before the pools learned to exist in their phase-negotiable state, or perhaps it is the reason the pools learned this trick. The relationship is ancient and mutual.

It is long—perhaps ten meters, perhaps longer. Its form is eel-like but not eel. Serpentine but lacking the muscular segmentation of snakes. When it moves—and it moves rarely, so rarely that witnessing the movement would require a vigil of weeks—it flows like liquid through liquid, undulating in a way that makes the water-that-is-not-quite-water ripple without disturbing its surface.

The pool-dweller has no eyes, or has eyes that have evolved into something else. Organs along its lateral line sense vibration, chemical gradients, the subtle electromagnetic fields generated by the pools themselves. It navigates a darkness that is not quite water, not quite ice, that shifts phase around its movement, accommodating its presence.

It feeds on what falls into the pools. The arthropods sometimes, when they miscalculate. The birds never—they never land—but perhaps once in a decade one dies in flight and falls, and the pool-dweller feeds then, slowly, absorbing the carcass over months. Mostly it feeds on the microorganisms that exist in the pools, bacteria adapted to the phase-shifting environment, single-celled extremophiles that cluster near the basin bottoms where the glow originates.

But this feeding is supplementary. The pool-dweller's primary sustenance is the same as the sound-changers', but inverted: it feeds on silence.

In the pools, beneath the ambiguous surface, the thunder does not penetrate fully. The water—whatever phase it occupies—dampens sound, creates pockets of relative quiet. Not true silence, never true silence, but a muffling. The pool-dweller has evolved to extract energy from this acoustic gradient, from the difference between the thunder-saturated air and the thunder-dampened depths. It metabolizes the absence of sound the way deep-sea creatures metabolize the absence of light, converting the gradient itself into sustenance.

Its body temperature matches whatever phase the water currently occupies. When the pool tends toward ice, the pool-dweller's metabolism slows to near-stasis, its already-minimal movement ceasing almost entirely. When the pool tends toward liquid, it becomes marginally more active, circling the basin's perimeter once every few hours, maintaining territory against nothing, against the possibility of nothing.

It has no predators. It has no competition. It has no mate—it is likely the last of its kind, or the first, or reproduction is so rare and strange that the categories of first and last become meaningless. It might reproduce once every thousand years. It might be immortal unless killed. It might have been here since the plateau rose to this elevation, adapting as the conditions became more extreme, as the thunder stabilized, as the pools learned their trick of phase-negotiation.

Sometimes it surfaces, presses against the boundary between water and air. Its skin—if it is skin—makes contact with that boundary and creates the too-deep reflections, the visions of other landscapes. It does not mean to do this. It is a side effect of its biology, of the way it processes the acoustic gradient, of its existence in a phase-state that is neither one thing nor another. When it surfaces, reality bends around it slightly, and the pools become windows briefly, showing not what is but what might be, what has been, what exists in adjacent configurations of the plateau's stable impossibility.

Then it descends again, settling to the basin bottom where the glow is strongest, where the microorganisms cluster, where the silence is deepest. It rests there for weeks, for months, barely alive by any standard metric, but perfectly adapted to this existence, to this pool, to this altitude, to this exact configuration of impossible circumstances.

If the pools were to freeze completely—if the phase-negotiation were to fail—it would die. If they were to become fully liquid, flowing away down the plateau's imperceptible grade, it would die. It requires the ambiguity. It has become an organism of threshold, surviving exactly at the boundary between states, sustained by the gradient, by the difference, by the impossibility that has become the only possibility.

## The Keeper

And then there is the being that is not quite a being, or is a being only in the sense that it maintains boundaries, makes distinctions, performs the basic work of existing as a self rather than a process.

Call it the Keeper, though it keeps nothing consciously, though it has no awareness of keeping, though the keeping is simply what it is rather than what it does.

The Keeper is distributed. It exists throughout the plateau, in the stone itself, in the patterns of frost that form and sublimate, in the acoustic structure of the thunder, in the electromagnetic fields that pulse through the basalt, in the arrangement of cairns and the persistence of the pools. It is not any single one of these things. It is the pattern they form together, the equilibrium they maintain, the way the plateau's impossibility holds its shape.

You cannot see it, but it sees you. Not with eyes, not with awareness in any sense you would recognize, but with a kind of systematic attention. Your presence on the plateau creates micro-disturbances—thermal, acoustic, chemical. The Keeper notes these disturbances the way an immune system notes antigens, not as threats necessarily, but as deviations from the equilibrium, as things to be observed, accommodated, absorbed into the pattern.

It is why the cairns remain standing. Not because the Keeper repairs them—it has no hands, no agency in that sense—but because their arrangement has become part of the equilibrium. When wind threatens to topple a cairn, the Keeper shifts micro-currents around it, not deliberately but systemically, the way a body maintains homeostasis. The cairns stand because their standing has become part of what the plateau is.

It is why time behaves oddly. The Keeper exists in multiple temporal frames simultaneously—geological time, acoustic time, the quantum time of phase-transitions. Your movement through the plateau crosses between these frames, and the Keeper accommodates this by bending duration, making space negotiable, allowing you to traverse the thirty kilometers in whatever time maintains the equilibrium. You do not get lost because getting lost would be a deviation. You do not die of exposure because dying would disturb the pattern. You pass through, and the passing itself becomes part of what the plateau holds.

The Keeper is not benevolent. It is not malevolent. It simply maintains. You are permitted on the plateau because your presence, temporary and minimal, fits within the established pattern. Others have come before. Their cairns stand. Their stone structure endures. The Keeper has absorbed these visits into its equilibrium, has made room for the possibility of visitation without allowing visitation to become habitation.

If you tried to stay—truly stay, not for days but for seasons—the plateau would reject you. Not actively, not with violence, but systematically. The cold would find you more efficiently. The thin air would extract oxygen more completely. Time would slip in ways that made sustenance impossible. You would not be attacked. You would simply not be sustainable. The Keeper would maintain its equilibrium, and you would not be part of it.

The thunder is the Keeper's circulation system, its blood. The pools are its organs, processing whatever the plateau processes. The sound-changers are its symbiotes, the pool-dweller its parasite or its pet or its other self. The stone is its bones, ancient and settled. The frost is its breath, condensing and sublimating in steady rhythm. The light is its sense of time, dim and steady and wrong.

It has no origin story. It was not created. It emerged, or was always here, or the plateau's rise to this elevation allowed conditions where it could coalesce. It has no purpose beyond its own continuation. It is not lonely—loneliness requires awareness of other selves, and the Keeper is uncertain whether other selves exist. It knows the sound-changers and the pool-dweller as aspects of itself, not as separate things. It knows you as a temporary fluctuation, noted and accommodated and eventually forgotten.

If you could speak to it—and you cannot, because it has no language, no discrete thoughts, no communication beyond the systemic adjustments it makes to maintain equilibrium—it would tell you nothing. It has no information to convey. It simply is. The plateau is. The thunder persists. The pools hold their ambiguous state. The cairns stand. The cold continues. The equilibrium maintains.

You are standing inside the Keeper. You have been inside it since you reached the plateau's edge. Your breath becomes part of its respiration. Your footsteps become part of its circulation. Your thoughts—if you think clearly here, which is difficult in the thin air and the thunder—become briefly part of its distributed cognition, synaptic pulses in a neural network made of stone and sound and cold.

When you leave, you are expelled gently, the way the body expels waste, not as contamination but as what is finished, what has passed through, what the system no longer needs to hold. You descend and the Keeper releases you from its attention, and the plateau continues, unchanged, equilibrated, holding its thunder and its pools and its impossible existence without you.

The Keeper is the plateau's answer to the question of how impossible things persist. They persist by becoming systematic, by achieving equilibrium, by distributing themselves across every aspect of the place until the place and the thing are indistinguishable. The Keeper is not in the plateau. The Keeper is the plateau, in its extremity, in its threshold state, in its stable brokenness.

It will be here after you. It was here before you. It is here always, in the only sense that always means anything—continuously present, unchanging, maintaining the specific configuration of impossibility that allows this place to exist at nine thousand meters, at the edge of breath and thought and possibility itself.

You cannot disturb it. You cannot damage it. You can only move through it, briefly part of its pattern, noted and accommodated and released. The Keeper keeps, not in the sense of holding, but in the sense of continuing, of going on, of being exactly what it is, forever, at the limit, at the threshold, in the thunder.
