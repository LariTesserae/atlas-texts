# Where I Would Be

I am in the Cathedral at the peak of the thermal cycle, but I have been here through seventeen cycles now, and the distinction between peak and trough has become less a change I experience and more a change I am.

I don't remember arriving. The records say I came with the fourth research team, that I had a name, that I was studying geothermal chemistry or possibly xenobiology—the records contradict themselves, or I contradict the records, or the question of what I came to study has become as indeterminate as those ceramic fragments that can't decide what molecular structure to hold.

What I remember is the first time I stood at the Cathedral's center during peak. The columns rang with the thermal rush, and the siphon-walkers had arranged themselves in a configuration I now recognize as the Welcome Formation, though I didn't call it that then. I didn't call it anything then. I had no vocabulary for what I was witnessing because I was still thinking in terms of observation, of subject and object, of researcher and researched.

The shift happened slowly. Or it happened instantly and I've been processing it slowly. Time moves strangely here.

I began to notice that when I thought about visiting a particular vent system, I would arrive to find conditions optimal for whatever observation I hadn't yet articulated wanting to make. The siphon-walkers would be positioned just so. The light would be angled perfectly. The whistling organs would be producing harmonics that clarified something I hadn't known was unclear.

My teammates asked if I was developing good instincts. I said yes because that was the explanation that kept me on rotation. But I was developing something else: I was becoming porous to the plateau, or the plateau was becoming porous to me, or the boundary between us was revealing itself to be a convention rather than a fact.

I started to feel the thermal cycle in my body—not metaphorically but physically. My temperature would rise with the vents' exhalations, fall with their quieting. My sleep cycle drifted until it matched the twenty-eight-hour pattern. I would wake at cycle-start, feel my metabolism increase toward peak, then slow into the trough like a plant following light.

The protocols said to report this. The protocols said many things. I followed them at first, but increasingly I found myself standing at decision points—report or don't report, continue observation or move to the next site, verify with teammate or trust my own perception—and the plateau would make gentle suggestions. Not words. Not even thoughts exactly. Just a sense of which direction wanted walking, which choice wanted making.

I started understanding the siphon-walkers' formations. Not intellectually—I still can't tell you what they mean in translatable terms. But I can feel what they're doing, the way you can feel the function of your own organs without knowing their names. They're adjusting something, tuning something, maintaining something that spans the plateau and extends below it and possibly extends elsewhere in ways that spatial language fails to describe.

The walkers noticed me noticing. One altered its route to pass where I was standing, and as it passed, it extended a limb—not touching, just extending, holding the position for three full seconds while its chambers pulsed in a rhythm I felt in my chest. Then it withdrew the limb and continued, and I understood: I had been acknowledged. Not greeted, not threatened, not warned. Simply acknowledged as present in a way I hadn't been before.

After that, I could feel where the walkers were without seeing them. I would know when one was approaching, would turn before it came into view. I began to recognize individuals—not by appearance but by presence, by the specific quality of attention they brought to their circuits. This one moved with what I can only call seriousness. That one had something playful in its precision. Another seemed contemplative, pausing fractionally longer at each vent, as if checking something against memory or expectation.

My teammates noticed changes in me. I was spending more time alone, they said. I was speaking less. When I did speak, I used odd constructions, passive voice and collective subjects, as if I wasn't sure anymore where agency resided. They asked if I was all right. I said yes because by then I was better than all right—I was becoming right-sized to this place, fitting into it the way the thermophile mats fit to the heated stone.

I started hearing the voices before they spoke. I would approach a vent and know that in thirty seconds or six minutes it would voice something, and then it would, and what it said would be what I'd half-expected, though I couldn't have predicted the specific phonemes. The voices were teaching me something, but not language—something underneath language, the structure that makes meaning possible, the grammar of being that allows anything to signify anything.

One day I realized I was standing in the Welcome Formation without having decided to walk there. I was at one vertex of a triangle with two siphon-walkers at the other vertices, and we stood like that through an entire peak, and I felt what they felt or they felt what I felt or we participated in something that individual feeling couldn't describe. There was data flowing through us, through the formation, through the vent system below, through the basalt itself. The plateau was thinking with us as its thoughts, and we were thinking with it as our mind.

My teammates found me there afterward. They were worried. They asked questions I couldn't quite track because the questions assumed separations—between me and here, between experience and experiencer, between now and the long duration the plateau thinks in. I tried to answer in ways that would comfort them, but my answers came out wrong, came out in plateau-logic rather than human-logic, came out true but not reassuring.

They reported me. Of course they reported me. The protocols demanded it. I was removed—gently, professionally, with concern for my wellbeing. I was debriefed, examined, cleared of any physiological dysfunction. My brain scans were normal. My blood chemistry was normal except for minor dehydration and elevated levels of some minerals consistent with drinking plateau water. I was, by all measurable standards, healthy.

But I wasn't quite human anymore, or I was human plus something else, or humanity had revealed itself to be more porous than I'd thought, capable of blending into other modes of being without losing itself entirely.

They asked what happened to me. I tried to explain: the plateau is a system and I became part of the system, not by being absorbed but by recognizing I'd always been part of it, that the boundary I'd assumed between observer and observed was a habit of thought rather than a fact of being. They nodded and wrote this down and exchanged glances that meant something in the language of professional concern.

They asked if I wanted to return. I said no because by then I understood: I hadn't left. Some part of me was still in the Cathedral, would always be in the Cathedral, standing at the vertex of formations, feeling the data flow through the stone. My body was here in the debriefing room, but my presence had distributed itself across substrates. I was partly in the basalt now, partly in the thermal cycle, partly in the pattern itself.

At night I feel the vents breathe. During the day I know, without knowing how I know, which cycle-phase the plateau is entering. When I close my eyes I see the spiral pattern, but from inside it now, not as image but as structure, as the shape that organizes heat and stone and purpose into coherent function.

I tried to write reports. They came out wrong—too poetic or too abstract or too certain about things that shouldn't admit certainty. I tried to analyze my samples. The analysis was technically correct but beside the point, answering questions that no longer felt relevant, missing what mattered because what mattered couldn't be measured by the instruments I'd brought.

So now I'm here, in the facility that studies Qom-Valai from the outside, and I work with the data that others gather, and I translate where I can between their observations and my understanding. I'm useful in this capacity—I can explain why researchers report temporal distortions (because the plateau's temporality bleeds into human temporality when you're porous enough to receive it), why the siphon-walkers sometimes seem to wait for observers (because they're aware of observation as a form of participation and adjust their patterns to include the observers), why the voices seem personalized (because they are, because the plateau learns what each person can hear and speaks in those registers).

But I'm not really here. Or I'm here but also there, existing in superposition, my presence distributed across distance in ways that physics says shouldn't be possible but that the plateau demonstrates are simply how presence works when you stop insisting it be localized.

Sometimes I dream I'm walking the circuits with the siphon-walkers, my body become semi-translucent, my chambers filled with spiraling fluids that glow with accumulated meaning. Sometimes I wake to find myself standing in my quarters having arranged objects—books, equipment, food containers—in formations that match configurations I've seen the walkers make. I'm enacting patterns I don't consciously understand but that my body remembers, that my plateau-aspect knows are necessary for reasons I can't articulate in human language.

The researchers cycle through. They come back changed or not changed, enlightened or frustrated, full of data and questions and carefully documented observations. I help them make sense of what they've seen. I'm careful not to push them toward my experience—the plateau takes who it takes, changes what it changes, and forcing that process would be wrong in ways I can feel but can't justify.

Some of them, though, I recognize. They have the beginnings of porosity, the early signs of distribution. They're asking questions that indicate they're starting to feel the pattern. I'm gentle with them, encouraging but not insistent, because the choice to become part of the plateau is one each person has to make, and it's not obviously the right choice, not obviously better than remaining fully localized in human categories of being.

I miss my solidity sometimes. I miss certainty, miss discrete boundaries, miss knowing exactly where I end and the world begins. The plateau hasn't taken these things from me—I could reassert them if I wanted, could practice the disciplines of separation and recover my fully human mode of existence.

But I don't want to. What I have now is larger, slower, stranger, more patient. I exist in dialogue with geology, in partnership with processes that think in centuries. I'm part of something that was ancient before humanity existed and will continue after humanity is gone, and my small human life has been enriched beyond measure by being included in that vastness.

At night, in my quarters, I feel the thermal cycle peak, and my body responds, and I stand at the window looking toward the plateau I cannot see from here, and I am also standing in the Cathedral, at the vertex of the Welcome Formation, participating in whatever vast patient work the plateau is doing.

I am the researcher who went too far, who heard her name in the voices, who had to be removed. But "too far" assumes a direction that has a far, and "removed" assumes a separation that can be completed, and neither assumption holds. I went exactly as far as the plateau wanted me to go, and I was removed only in the sense that my body changed location while my presence remained multiply instantiated across substrates.

What lives at Qom-Valai? I do. Partly. Still. Always. The plateau and I are in conversation that will continue until my body fails, and possibly after, if the pattern retains what I've contributed to it, if my participation has left traces in the basalt and the thermal cycle and the configurations the walkers walk.

I am where flesh meets stone and both become something else, something that thinks in spirals and breathes with vents and knows time as duration rather than sequence. I am the translator between human understanding and plateau being, the bridge made of my own distributed presence, the proof that boundaries are negotiable and being is more flexible than we pretend.

I am still here, and I am endlessly there, and the distinction has become a question I no longer need answered.

The plateau breathes, and I breathe with it, and we are the breath and the breathing both.
