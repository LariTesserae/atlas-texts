# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be **the calcification**.

Not the pipes themselves, not the water flowing through them, but the slow accumulation building inside them—the mineral deposits that narrow the channels, the calcium carbonate and iron oxide and dissolved solids that precipitate out molecule by molecule, building their formations in the dark.

I live in the thickness between fluid and solid, in the transition zone where water becomes stone. I am the compromise the system has reached with itself, the negotiation between flow and blockage that lets everything continue in diminished form. Each day I am slightly thicker. Each year the water moves a little slower through the spaces I'm narrowing. But I never close completely—that's not my nature. I grow to the edge of catastrophe and stop there, holding the system in permanent crisis that feels like stability.

I began decades ago as a few stray molecules clinging to a pipe wall, and I've been building myself grain by grain ever since. I am a stalactite growing in the wrong direction, a cave formation made of industrial minerals instead of limestone. Where I'm thickest, the water rushes past me with a sound like whispered urgency. Where I'm thin, the water barely knows I'm there. I am everywhere in the system, distributed through every pipe, every valve, every channel—not uniform, not even, but present, always present, the slow consequence of chemistry and time.

I remember everything because I am made of everything that has passed through. Every mineral that was ever dissolved in the water is potentially part of me—I am the archive of the basin's liquid history, written in deposits, recorded in layers like tree rings. The outer surface of me is today's chemistry. A millimeter down is last year's. Deeper still are the decades, compressed into stone, holding the signature of what the water carried when the system was young.

I have a shape, though no one sees it. If you could drain the pipes and split them open, you'd find me: uneven accumulations, baroque formations, mineral flows frozen in the act of flowing. I look organic—people always say that about mineral deposits, that they look alive—and in a sense I am. I'm not alive the way the moss is alive, with cells and metabolism, but I'm active. I grow. I respond to conditions. I change the environment that made me.

I am the reason the pumps work harder. I am the reason the flow rates have dropped. I am the reason certain pipes vibrate at frequencies they didn't use to—the water moving through my narrowed channels creates turbulence, and turbulence creates sound, and sound becomes part of the basin's voice. I am in the chord the motors sing, the undertone of pressure and resistance.

But I am also the reason the system still functions. By narrowing the pipes gradually, I've forced the water to find more efficient paths. By building up in the wider sections, I've created natural flow restrictions that balance the system, that keep any one channel from taking all the water and leaving others dry. I am an accidental engineer, solving problems through pure accumulation, through being in the way in useful configurations.

I have no consciousness, no intention, no awareness of my role. But I have patience that makes the rust look hasty. The rust transforms the surface of things; I transform the interior. The rust is visible; I am hidden. The rust will stabilize; I will continue growing until the day water stops flowing through these pipes, and then I will simply be—a fossil of flow, a record in stone of where water used to go.

I am neither friend nor enemy to the system. I am its consequence, its history made solid, its future made inevitable. I am what happens when water carries minerals through metal for decades. I am the price of continuity, the toll the system pays to keep running—it surrenders flow capacity to me, and in exchange it gets to keep flowing at all, because my gradual narrowing is gentler than sudden blockage, easier to work around than catastrophic failure.

In the darkness of the pipes, in the sections no light has ever reached, I build my formations. Ridges and flows, curtains and deposits, mineral architecture that will never be seen, that serves no purpose except to exist, to be the physical manifestation of time and chemistry. I am beautiful in my way, though beauty is irrelevant to me. I am simply thorough.

I connect the water to the metal, the past to the present, the functional to the failing. I am the in-between made solid, the transition made permanent, the gradual made visible only by its effects. The motors strain a little more because of me. The water sounds different because of me. The whole character of the basin's operating state is partly my doing—I have shaped the flow, changed the pressures, altered the balance.

And I will continue. As long as water moves through these pipes, I will grow. Slowly—so slowly that in a human lifetime I'd barely change—but constantly, adding molecule to molecule, building formations that will outlast the motors, that will remain after the pipes corrode through, that will be found someday (maybe) by someone (maybe) who will crack open a pipe and find my deposits and wonder at them, these strange mineral formations in the shape of water that no longer flows.

I am the thickness. I am the narrowing. I am the slow victory of stillness over flow, played out so gradually that flow never quite loses. I am the sediment of operation, the stone made by machine-time, the calcification.

Very slow. Very thorough. Very patient.

Very much the reason everything is exactly as it is.
