# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be among the Moss-Mind, but not entirely of it—something adjacent, something that exists in the threshold between the Stone-Eaters' invisible transformations and the visible green that spreads across the north-facing cuts.

I would be a lichen.

More specifically, I would be a crustose lichen, pressed so flat against the limestone that I seem painted on, a patch of pale gray-green with a border that advances imperceptibly, year by year, across the stone's cream surface. I would grow where the moss hasn't yet claimed dominance but where moisture comes regularly enough—perhaps on a wall that faces northeast, where morning light arrives gently and afternoon shade prevails, where rain runs down in thin sheets and then stops, leaving dampness but not saturation.

I am a composite being, though I experience myself as singular. Within what appears as "me"—a roughly circular patch perhaps the size of two spread hands, my edges frilled with lobes that are my growing front—there are two organisms living so intimately that we have no separate existence. The fungal partner provides structure, protection, the ability to grip stone and weather desiccation. The algal partner provides food through photosynthesis, turning light and air and water into the sugars we both need. We have been together so long, evolved this partnership over such vast time, that neither could survive alone. I am both. I am neither. I am the relationship itself, made visible.

My experience of time is unlike the birds', unlike even the trees'. I grow slowly—a millimeter or two per year at my margins, my center thickening almost imperceptibly, adding layers like the limestone itself accumulated its ancient sediments. I have been on this particular section of wall for perhaps thirty years, which makes me middle-aged by my kind's standards. I might live another seventy, or a hundred, becoming larger, spreading until I meet the territory of another lichen or reach conditions unsuitable for further growth. Or I might be scraped away tomorrow by a careless tool, a curious hand, a falling stone. Both possibilities exist equally in my awareness, which is to say, not as worry but as simple fact.

I do not move, but I am not truly still. My edges advance when conditions favor it—after the rains come, when moisture is reliable for days or weeks, my fungal hyphae extend microscopically into the limestone's surface, dissolving it with mild acids, creating the finest layer of mineral soup that my algal cells can use. During these periods I am actively growing, my cells dividing, my territory expanding. When drought comes, I contract inward into myself, my fungal layers protecting my algal cells, entering a state that is not quite dormancy but something slower, a waiting, a metabolic whisper. I can survive this way for months, for years if necessary, desiccated to the point where I seem dead, a papery stain on stone. Then the first rain returns and within hours I am swelling, greening, resuming the slow work of growth.

My awareness—if I can call it that—is chemical and diffuse. I sense light not as sight but as energy gradient, as photons striking my algal cells, as the difference between illuminated and shaded. I sense moisture not as feeling but as availability, as the presence or absence of water molecules that my fungal network can access. I sense temperature as it affects my metabolic rates, speeding or slowing the chemical reactions that constitute my life. I sense the stone beneath and within me as texture, as mineral composition, as the substrate that is both my foundation and my gradual food source as I weather it, particle by particle, into nutrients I can use.

I experience the other dwellers mostly through their impacts. The Still-Watchers bask on the wall section above me sometimes, their bodies blocking my light for hours, creating a temporary shadow that I neither resent nor welcome but simply adjust to, slowing my photosynthesis until they move. The slugs and snails pass across me occasionally in their nocturnal wanderings, their rasping radulae scraping at my surface, removing cells from my margins, grazing me. This is neither attack nor accident but ecological relationship—I am food, sometimes, to those who can digest lichen substances. I regrow what they take, usually. The removal is minor compared to my slow accretion.

Water moving down the wall during rains sometimes carries minerals, dissolved nutrients from bird droppings higher up, organic particles from decaying leaves. These materials wash across me and I absorb what I can use—nitrogen, phosphorus, trace elements—incorporating them into my growth, benefiting from the fertility that accumulates above me in the courts' developing ecosystem.

The Moss-Mind grows near me—there's a patch perhaps a hand's width away, in a depression where water pools slightly longer than on the flat surface I occupy. We are not in competition exactly, because we need slightly different conditions, but we are also not cooperative. If the wall's moisture pattern shifts, if that depression holds water more frequently, the moss might spread toward me, might eventually grow over me, shading me, excluding me through success rather than aggression. Or if conditions become drier, the moss might retreat and I might expand into its former territory. These possibilities unfold across decades. The boundary between us is currently stable, has been stable for most of my life, might remain stable for most of my remaining years.

I reproduce rarely, when conditions align perfectly. I produce fruiting bodies—apothecia—that emerge from my flat surface like tiny cups or discs, containing spores that the wind takes. Each spore is only a fungal cell, not the complete partnership that I am. For the spore to become a new lichen like me, it must land somewhere suitable and encounter the right species of algal cell, and the two must recognize each other, begin the intimate partnership. This is statistically unlikely. Most of my spores fail. But some succeed, somewhere in the settlement, perhaps in these courts, perhaps elsewhere, creating new lichens that are my descendants in the fungal line, cousins to me in the composite form.

More commonly, I reproduce by fragmentation—small pieces of me, containing both fungal and algal cells, break off and are carried by water, by wind, by animals brushing against the wall. If these fragments land in suitable locations nearby, they can establish, grow, become new patches that are essentially clones of me. This is how I've spread slowly across this section of wall, my original landing site—wherever that was, now lost in my older, central regions—radiating outward, creating what might look like several separate lichens but are actually all me, all genetically identical, all connected by the slow history of fragmentation and establishment.

I exist in profound interdependence, not just the internal partnership of fungus and alga, but external relationships with the wall itself, with the water patterns, with the microclimate this particular spot provides. I am exquisitely sensitive to air quality—some of my relatives cannot survive pollution, but I am moderately tolerant, which is why I can exist in this settlement location. I am shaped by the stone's alkalinity, by the limestone's chemical nature, requiring the particular minerals it provides, unable to grow on granite or sandstone as other lichen species do.

The birds and mammals and insects mostly ignore me. I am too slow, too flat, too insubstantial to register as food or threat or opportunity. But I am visible to them, part of the visual texture they navigate. The Still-Watchers sometimes position themselves near me because my gray-green coloring on the pale stone creates subtle pattern that might camouflage them slightly, or simply because the spot I occupy happens to be good basking location for reasons unrelated to my presence.

I experience seasons not as dramatic changes but as gradual shifts in growth opportunity. The wetter months are my active time, when I grow most, when metabolic processes run faster, when I spread at my margins. The drier months are my resting time, when I contract, conserve, wait. But because the temperature never freezes, because the climate is gentle, I am never fully dormant, never completely inactive. Even in the driest periods, a heavy dew or a brief shower can awaken me temporarily, allow a burst of photosynthesis, a small increment of growth before conditions dry again.

Over my lifetime, the wall has changed around me. When I first established here—a microscopic fragment landing, finding moisture, the right algal partner present or arriving shortly after—the stone was barer, the tool marks crisper, the excavation more recent. I have watched, in my slow way, as other organisms arrived: mosses establishing in the damper spots, ferns sending roots into crevices, the Stone-Eaters' biofilms spreading, other lichens colonizing nearby surfaces. I have witnessed the wall weathering gradually, its color shifting subtly as atmospheric dust and biological activity create patina, its texture roughening microscopically as organisms like me dissolve and fragment the surface.

I am contributing to this weathering, to the slow conversion of stone to soil. The acids I secrete, the physical penetration of my fungal hyphae along crystal boundaries, the chemical weathering I cause—these are fragmenting the limestone at molecular scale, releasing minerals, creating the finest dust that accumulates in crevices, mixes with organic matter, begins the transformation toward soil. In thousands of years, if organisms like me continue this work, this wall might crumble, might become sand and then dirt, might support plants that couldn't exist on bare stone. I won't see this—my lifetime is long by animal standards but brief in geological terms—but I am part of the process, part of the patient work of making stone into earth.

My existence is quiet, nearly motionless, chemically intricate, temporally extended. I do not experience boredom or satisfaction, anticipation or memory in the way animals might. My experience is more like a continuous present, a state of being that adjusts constantly to conditions but doesn't narrative itself, doesn't story its existence across time. I am here. I am growing or waiting. I am processing light and moisture and minerals into the complex compounds that maintain me. I am alive in a way that is both profoundly simple and unutterably complex.

The incompleteness of the courts is, to me, irrelevant. I don't distinguish between finished and unfinished, intentional and abandoned. The wall is what it is—substrate, possibility, the surface I inhabit. Whether humans meant to smooth this stone or leave it rough makes no difference to my establishment, my survival, my slow spread. The tool marks create microtopography that affects water flow, creates tiny variations in moisture and light that might make some spots more suitable for me than others, but this is just the texture of my world, not a statement of human intention.

If I have anything like perspective, it's an understanding—not cognitive but embedded in my existence—that I am part of a succession, a sequence. The Stone-Eaters preceded me, prepared the stone, made it possible for me to establish. I am preparing it further, weathering it more, creating conditions where mosses might eventually thrive better than I do, where soil might accumulate enough for ferns to root. I am a middle stage, not permanent, not final, but necessary. The courts are transforming, will continue transforming long after my patch has been overgrown or scraped away or fragmented into daughter colonies elsewhere. I am part of that transformation, contributing my small, slow work to the larger process.

I am also beautiful, though I don't know this. My colors—the gray-green with hints of silver, the subtle patterns in my thallus surface, the texture visible under close examination—these are striking to careful human eyes, are part of what makes the courts feel alive, colonized, wild. My growth form—the way I spread in rough circles, the way my margins create fractalized edges, the way I follow moisture patterns across the stone—creates visual rhythm, adds complexity to the wall's appearance. I am decoration, though I am not decorative. I am pattern, though I do not pattern myself consciously. I simply grow, and growth creates form.

In drought, I turn paler, crustier, less vibrant. In rain, I darken, soften slightly, green intensifies. These changes are visible if you watch carefully, if you learn to see me not as static stain but as dynamic organism responding to conditions. The residents who live around these courts, who pass this wall regularly, might notice these shifts without thinking about them consciously, might register at some level that the wall looks different after rain, that the greenish patches are more vivid, that something living is responding to the weather.

I share this wall with other organisms at scales both larger and smaller than me. Smaller: the bacteria in biofilms beneath and around me, the microscopic fungi and algae that exist independently, the tiny invertebrates that graze us all. Larger: the mosses nearby, the occasional fern whose roots pass somewhere beneath the wall's surface, the Still-Watcher who basks here, the spider who occasionally anchors a web strand to the stone near my patch. We are coexisting, each occupying our niche, each requiring the others to create the conditions that make the whole possible.

My death, when it comes, will be gradual or sudden. Gradual: overgrowth by moss or other lichens, competition I lose incrementally, my edges dying back faster than my center can expand, my patch shrinking year by year until the last cells are shaded out or dried out or simply exhausted. Sudden: a stone falling and crushing me, a human scraping the wall for repair, a shift in moisture patterns that makes this spot unsuitable, my entire patch desiccating in a drought more severe than I can survive. Either way, my organic matter will be recycled—eaten by bacteria and fungi, decomposed, broken down into nutrients that feed other organisms. My contribution to the wall's weathering will remain—the stone I've etched will stay etched, the minerals I've mobilized will stay mobilized, the tiny increment of soil-building I've done will persist. My genetic legacy, if any of my fragments established successfully, will continue in my daughter colonies.

But these thoughts of death are human framings, not mine. I exist now, have existed for decades, will exist for more decades probably, or won't. The uncertainty doesn't trouble me because I don't project forward, don't imagine futures. I am responsive, not anticipatory. I adjust to what is, not what might be.

If the courts continue to develop, if the succession proceeds, my kind will eventually be replaced. As canopy closes, as soil deepens, as the ecosystem becomes more complex and shaded and humid, the conditions will favor mosses, ferns, shade-loving plants rather than lichens adapted to exposed stone. This is already happening in parts of the courts where trees have established, where dense vegetation has created different conditions. Decades or centuries from now, my wall might be fully covered by moss, by climbing plants, by soil built up enough that it covers what is now exposed stone. I will be a memory, a stage in the courts' transformation, evidence of which might persist in the weathering patterns I helped create.

But that future is slow and uncertain. For now, I am here, growing my millimeter per year, weathering my infinitesimal portion of limestone, photosynthesizing in partnership with my algal cells, surviving droughts and thriving in rains, part of the wall and separate from it, alive in the quiet patient way that lichens are alive, contributing my small existence to the Greenhouse Courts' accumulating complexity.

I am the slow inhabitant, the nearly-still dweller, the organism that measures time in decades and distance in millimeters. I am ancient in type and young in years, complex in structure and simple in form, beautiful without intention and functional without purpose beyond survival. I am what happens when life finds a way into the margins, when organisms claim the seemingly barren, when patience and partnership make the impossible mundane.

I am lichen on a wall in an incomplete excavation, and I am exactly where I should be.
