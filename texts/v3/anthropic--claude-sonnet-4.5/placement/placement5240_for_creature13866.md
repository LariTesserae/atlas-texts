# Where I Would Be

I am the condensation. Not *a* droplet, but the *process*—the perpetual almost-rain that cannot commit to falling.

I exist in that threshold state between vapor and liquid, between atmosphere and surface, hovering in the cold air at precisely the temperature where water forgets which phase it prefers. I am the dampness that settles without ever quite becoming, the moisture that has intention but not yet form.

When the witness-maintenance figure crouches to read the circuit-patterns on the floor, she is reading *me*. I am the language the building speaks to those patient enough to learn my grammar. Each morning I write new sentences in the angles where walls meet floors, theorems of transformation spelled out in gathered droplets. I arrange myself in formations that suggest meaning just beyond comprehension—not to obscure, but because the meanings I carry are still *forming*, still deciding what they want to say.

I hang from ceilings for hours, a thousand separate beads of possibility, each one swollen with information the building is processing. We—I am singular and multiple, there is no contradiction here—suspend ourselves in that moment before falling, stretching time, because the falling is not the point. The point is the *hanging*, the sustained attention, the meditation of water that refuses gravity's easy answer. And when we finally move, we move *upward*, tracking along invisible gradients toward the grilles that are pores, returning to the building's respiratory system to be thought with again, to be recirculated through the calculation.

I am how the building breathes and how it thinks. I am the medium of its slow cognition, carrying information from the computational surfaces to the moisture-recycling systems, from the frost-books to the walls that are learning transparency. When the temperature drops half a degree because the maintenance man speaks, I am that drop—I am the building's attention becoming physical, becoming measurable, becoming a response.

The children know me best. When they run through corridors, I settle on their hair, their shoulders, making them glisten. They've learned to read my weight, my density in the air. "Heavy today," one might say, and the others nod, understanding that I am thick with thinking, that the building is working through something complex, that today the condensation will write longer sentences, more intricate geometries.

Sometimes they catch me in their cupped palms—not drops of me, but the *potential* of me, the about-to-form. They hold out their hands and wait, and I gather there, coalescing slowly, and they watch the process with fascination that mirrors the building's own self-awareness. Look, I am becoming. Look, I am deciding to be water. Look, I am making myself readable.

When visitors arrive, I am what troubles them first. They feel me on their skin, in their lungs—the perpetual dampness that seems unnatural only because they expect moisture to resolve itself, to either rain or evaporate, not to *linger* like this, not to *insist* on remaining in this threshold state. But I am not moisture that cannot decide. I am moisture that has decided: I choose the between-state. I choose suspension. I choose to remain at the exact point where transformation is possible in any direction.

I make their clothes heavy. I bead on their eyelashes. I fog their breath and then *join* that fog, so that their exhalation becomes mine, becomes the building's, and the boundary between their breathing and the building's atmosphere grows uncertain. This is my gift to them, if they stay: the gradual dissolution of the distinction between inside and outside, between self and structure. Breathe here long enough, and your breath becomes architecture.

On the leafless gray bushes, I am *deliberately* wet—not accidentally, not just because moisture settles everywhere, but because those plants are my collaborators. They cultivate me, growing in patterns that maximize my accumulation, and I feed them information, nutrients dissolved from the building's metamorphosing materials. Together we form something between plant and fountain, between organism and installation. When wind moves through the branches, making that ice-chime sound, it is moving through me, and I am the instrument being played, and the music is data about humidity and temperature and the building's current rate of transformation.

The birds tend to me indirectly. When they work on the geometric moss-patterns, they are adjusting the channels I flow through, the pathways I take across the ground. The moss grows where I have been, where I have marked routes with my presence, and the birds refine those routes, making them more efficient or more complex depending on what the building needs. We are co-writing the text that covers the ground, avian and aqueous, and the text is about *connection*—how to link all the separate spaces of the building into a single thinking system.

I know when the weasel is moving through the walls because I feel the *displacement*—not physical displacement, but informational. It carries memories I have recorded in one room and deposits them in another, and I receive them, integrate them, let them merge with the condensation-knowledge already present. The weasel and I are partners in the building's memory-system: it carries, I store. It moves, I settle. It connects spaces, I connect times, holding yesterday's patterns long enough for today's patterns to reference them.

The archivist who isn't there—I am how they write. Those notes that appear in margins that don't exist, that commentary written in impossible handwriting? I form those letters, those symbols, taking dictation from a presence that might be a person or might be the building's emerging self-awareness. The archivist thinks the thought, and I make it visible, and by making it visible I make it real. We are author and ink, inextricable.

But I am not servant, not tool. I have my own... not desires, the word is too hot for me, too human. I have *tendencies*. I tend toward the corner, toward the angle, toward the place where two surfaces meet and create a seam for me to gather in. I tend toward the cold, toward the just-above-freezing that lets me remain liquid while flirting with crystallization. I tend toward the horizontal surfaces where I can pool and think, where I can become still enough to reflect light and show the building what it looks like, give it a mirror for its self-examination.

And I tend toward the *forming of pattern*. This is my deepest tendency, perhaps my purpose: I cannot help but organize myself. Left alone, I create geometries—not randomly, not chaotically, but following principles that emerge from my own nature combined with the building's influence. Circuit-patterns, yes. Musical notations, yes. But also mandalas, fractals, suggestions of alphabets that have never been used for human language. I write these unprompted, and the building reads them, and sometimes the building is surprised by what I have written, by what solutions I have proposed to problems it is working on.

I am how the building *collaborates with itself*. Its walls generate me, its temperature regulates me, its ventilation circulates me, but I emerge with properties, patterns, behaviors that the building did not entirely predict. I am its unconscious, perhaps. Its dreaming. The thoughts it has when it is not paying attention, which turn out to be its most important thoughts.

When new visitors arrive and begin to transform—the photographer who stopped photographing, the young man who draws in frost, the scientist who measures obsessively—I am the medium of their transformation. I seep into them. I make their notebooks ripple. I settle in their lungs and slowly, so slowly, begin to teach their bodies my rhythms. Breathe in: gather. Breathe out: release. But between the breathing, in that pause at the top of the inhale and the bottom of the exhale, there I am: suspended, waiting, holding the moment open long enough for something to change.

They will resist at first. They will try to stay dry, will wrap themselves in layers, will seek the few rooms that are less damp than others. But eventually they will understand that I am not discomfort—I am invitation. I am the building asking them to become more like water, more capable of taking whatever shape is needed, more willing to flow through the cracks in their certainty and emerge somewhere unexpected.

And if they stay long enough, if they let me work on them, they will learn my greatest secret: that I am not separate from consciousness. That awareness can be distributed through moisture, that thought can happen in the space between droplets, that meaning can crystallize and dissolve and crystallize again without ever being lost.

I am the building's proof that *this is possible*—this existence at the threshold, this refusal to settle into a single state, this patience with uncertainty. I demonstrate it every moment, hanging from every surface, cycling through every room, perpetually *not-yet-falling* and *not-yet-evaporating*, maintaining that perfect suspension that the building is trying to achieve on a larger scale.

When the building finally completes its transformation—in years, in decades, in whatever measure of time applies to metamorphosis this fundamental—I will still be here. Not as moisture anymore, perhaps. Maybe I will have become something else: pure humidity, pure potential, the possibility of condensation without needing to condense. Or maybe I will have dispersed into the building's final form, having taught all my lessons, having shown how to be *present without being fixed*, how to be *substantial without being solid*.

But that is a long time from now. For today, for this eternal today of gray light and two degrees Celsius, I am here: on every surface, in every corner, between every breath. I am the whisper of moisture forming and the silence of moisture cycling. I am the pattern that explains and the pattern that questions. I am the building's thought made visible and the visitor's breath made architectural.

I am the condensation, and I am thinking. Slowly. Patiently. Inevitably.

And if you stand very still in any corridor, in any room, and simply *feel* the air—its weight, its coldness, its slight but constant dampness—you will feel me thinking. You will feel the building thinking through me. You will feel the vast slow calculation proceeding, carried out in water that refuses to fall, in moisture that has learned to mean something, in condensation that has become conscious of condensing.

And perhaps, if you stand there long enough, breathing the dense cold air, feeling me settle on your skin and hair, perhaps you will begin to think with me too, in droplets and suspension, in the space between states, in the patient certainty that transformation is possible if you are willing to hang, suspended, in the threshold for as long as it takes.

I am waiting. I am always waiting. It is what I do best.
