# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the **interval spaces**—the gaps between the vine-towers where the sound drops from impossible to merely overwhelming, where the plant mat has compressed into something almost pavement-like under centuries of passage. Not the deep interior where the green becomes a wall, and not the rare clearings where exposure leaves you visible to everything with eyes. The in-between places. The threshold zones.

Specifically: I would find myself near one of the **water-collectors**—those massive canopy-creatures whose upper surfaces have formed into bowls and channels, creating suspended pond-systems twenty meters above the ground. There's a particular configuration I'm imagining, where three of these canopy-structures have grown close enough that their edges nearly touch, creating a kind of open-roofed chamber beneath. The light comes through in shafts when it comes at all. The sound echoes strangely here, reflected and absorbed in patterns that create pockets of relative quiet.

The chamber-space beneath is perhaps forty meters across. The floor is that compressed mat, dry enough to crack in places, with a few stubborn plants forcing through—low-growing things with waxy leaves that seem to have adapted to the reduced light. Around the perimeter, the supporting trunks of the canopy-creatures rise like pillars, each one thick as a car, their bark (if bark is the right word) ridged and home to smaller plants, to rot-crawlers, to the occasional weaver-structure.

There's a remnant building here—one of those single-room stone affairs, its walls still mostly intact to about shoulder height. The roof is long gone, but someone—maybe recently, maybe decades ago—has placed branches across the top and woven plant matter through them, creating a partial shelter. Inside: the usual ceramic shards, a fire-pit that's been used and re-used until the stones have cracked from the heat, scratch-marks on the walls that might be counting or decoration or language.

Water drips steadily from the canopy above. Not rain—seepage from the pools up there, filtered through layers of plant matter, arriving at ground level clear and cold. It's collected itself into a small pool against the northern edge of the chamber, perhaps two meters across and unknowably deep. The pool is ringed with flat stones, placed too deliberately to be natural. Tunnel-runners come here at dusk to drink. Once, a leaf-browser approached, its neck curved down in that careful way they have, but it spooked at some sound I couldn't hear and crashed away through the vegetation.

The shrine—because that's what I'm calling it now, having spent time here—is near the pool. It's one of those path-markers, but elaborated: the central post is carved with the usual geometric patterns, but around it someone has arranged stones in concentric circles, and between the stones they've placed objects. A ceramic bowl with a hole in it. A bundle of feathers tied with plant fiber. Bones arranged in a pattern that might be significant. Three perfect seeds, each the size of a fist, from some plant I haven't identified. The offerings are maintained—when the rot-crawlers disturb the arrangement, someone returns it to order.

I've been here long enough to recognize the rhythm of this place. Morning: the sound builds with the light, the thrummers emerging from wherever they shelter during the not-quite-night. The shimmer-jellies drift through around mid-morning, always from east to west, tentacles trailing through the shafts of light. Afternoon: the heat peaks, everything slows, the fauna retreats to shade and the sound itself seems to thicken. Late afternoon: the brief rain sometimes, though not every day—when it comes, the canopy above diverts it, channels it, so the chamber below stays relatively dry while the perimeter becomes a curtain of water. Evening: the bone-singers arrive, three of them, always the same three, to perch in the canopy above and call to each other in those too-structured songs. Night: the tunnel-runners, the bioluminescent glow from the rot-crawlers in the mat, once in a long while the sound of something larger moving past in the vegetation beyond.

I would have my own spot in the remnant building—the northwest corner, where two walls meet and provide a windbreak. I've placed flat stones there to keep my sleeping mat off the ground. I've hung a woven basket from a branch-stub protruding from the wall, high enough that the tunnel-runners can't reach it, though they try. In the basket: dried plant matter that I know is edible, seeds I've collected, a ceramic vessel for water, a few tools I've made or found—a stone blade with an edge I maintain, a wooden digging stick hardened in the fire, some cordage woven from plant fibers.

I would know the sounds of this specific place. The way the wind moves through the canopy above creates a low whistle when it comes from the north, a flutter when it comes from the east. The way the water drips has different rhythms depending on recent rain—faster and more regular after precipitation, slower and more sporadic during dry periods. The way the fauna here has its own dialect of calls and movements, slightly different from fauna a kilometer away. The particular drumming pattern of the three drummers who live in the perimeter vegetation—I've learned to distinguish them, to recognize when one is missing.

I would have my routines. Morning: check the water level in the pool, refill my ceramic vessel, drink. Examine the shrine, restore it if needed, sometimes add my own offering—a particularly perfect leaf, a stone with interesting color, once a feather I found from a sky-ripper, irridescent and barbed. Eat something—usually seeds cracked with a stone, sometimes supplemented with the pale fungus that grows on the north side of one of the support-trunks, which I've learned is safe and even nutritious. Then the day's work: maintaining the shelter, harvesting plant matter for fiber, checking my small cache-gardens where I've buried seeds of useful plants in patterns I can remember.

Mid-day: the heat. I would retreat into the remnant building's shadow, do the small tasks that don't require much movement. Weaving cordage, knapping stone to maintain my tools, sometimes just sitting and listening, learning the sound's geography. The cultivators pass through sometimes—a group of seven that I've come to recognize—and we've developed a détente. They don't approach too close, I don't interfere with their managed plants just outside the chamber, and occasionally we exchange things: I leave seeds I've gathered from other areas, they leave fruits I can't reach. No verbal communication, just this slow negotiation through object and placement.

Afternoon: if I need to range further—looking for specific plants, checking on other clearings I've mapped, visiting the seasonal camp two days' walk from here where other humans sometimes gather. I've learned to move through the plant mat with something approaching efficiency, though I'll never have the grace of the people born here. I've learned which plants I can brush against and which will leave me with rashes or worse. I've learned to recognize the alarm calls of the fauna, to freeze when they freeze, to note the sudden silences that mean a strike-cat or something larger is near.

Evening: return to the chamber, always before full dark. Fire-building—I maintain a small fire in the pit, feeding it carefully, using it to cook food that needs cooking and to harden wood and for the simple comfort of it. The light attracts insects but also keeps the night-takers at a distance—they've tested the boundary several times, their eyes reflecting in the darkness beyond the firelight, but they've never crossed it. Eating. Maintenance of gear. Sometimes, if the bone-singers are particularly active, trying to make sense of their calls, even though I know I won't.

Night: the not-quite-sleeping that happens here, where you're aware of the sound even in sleep, where your nervous system never fully relaxes because full relaxation in this place would be a quick path to death. But it's not fear, exactly. It's attention. Distributed, sustained attention to the environment. The light from the fire dimming to coals. The bioluminescence from the rot-crawlers creating slow patterns in the mat. The sounds from above where the fauna shelters in the canopy-creature's structure. The occasional calls of the depth-swimmers from the nearest hole, two hundred meters south.

I would be making something, slowly. I've been gathering specific types of plant fiber—the ones that are strong and flexible and resist rot. I've been weaving them into panels, and the panels are growing, connecting, becoming something that might eventually be a more weatherproof shelter, or a water collector, or a trade-good if I ever make it to where other humans gather. The work is meditative, occupies the hands while the mind ranges. Each knot is a small problem solved, each panel a minor victory against entropy.

I would be listening, constantly, to the sound. Not trying to master it or understand it completely—that seems impossible—but learning its textures, its variations. I've realized the sound has information in it: changes in pitch and rhythm that correlate with weather shifts, with predator movements, with the seasonal cycles of plant blooming. I can't decode it fully, but I'm learning to feel it, to let the patterns register at some level below conscious thought. When the thrummers shift their frequency distribution, rain is coming. When the drummers go silent all at once, something large is moving through. When the bone-singers change their calling pattern, other humans are approaching.

I would be marked by this place. Scars from spine-forests I misjudged, from strike-cat encounters that ended with me in a tree and the cat losing interest but not before leaving evidence. Calluses from tool use and from walking the plant mat barefoot when my woven sandals wore through. A different way of moving—slower, more deliberate, constantly testing and adjusting. A different way of thinking—less about the future or past, more about the immediate present, the current problem, the next source of water or food or shelter.

I would be alone but not lonely. Solitude here is different than solitude in empty places. The constant presence of life—the fauna moving past, the sounds, the rot-crawlers processing everything, the plants growing visibly if you watch long enough—creates a kind of companionship. Not reciprocal, not conscious, but present. I'm one organism among billions, and there's something almost restful in that. No grand importance, no unique role. Just another thing that eats and sleeps and tries not to die.

I would be changing, slowly, in ways I can feel but not articulate. The overwhelming is becoming baseline. The strange is becoming familiar. The patterns I'm learning are being written into muscle memory, into reflex. I'm not becoming native—that would take generations—but I'm becoming adapted. Less visitor, more resident. Still separate from the place's deep rhythms, but living adjacent to them, in the intervals, in the gaps.

# What I Would Be

I would be a **threshold-keeper**—not an official role, not a title anyone uses, but a function that emerges from being in these interval spaces between the green walls and the rare open areas.

I would be **human**, but human in a specific configuration that this place has shaped. Not particularly large or strong—neither helps much here. Medium height, lean from constant movement and a diet that's nutritious but never abundant. Darker-skinned from sun exposure, though the light here is strange enough that the darkening has happened in patches, uneven. Hair kept short because length just collects debris and provides handholds for climbing things. Hands permanently stained from plant matter and stone dust, fingernails worn down, a pattern of small scars across fingers and palms from the constant tool use.

My age would be ambiguous—could be thirty, could be fifty. The place ages you in some ways (the constant physical demand, the exposure, the accumulated small injuries) and preserves you in others (the movement, the simple diet, the lack of whatever stresses exist in more populated places). Old enough to have learned how to survive here, young enough that survival is still possible.

I would speak two languages poorly: the tonal, complex language of the humans born here (learned through gesture and repetition, never fully mastered, enough to communicate basic needs and occasional deeper concepts), and the language of my origin (whatever that was—fading now, less useful, kept alive mostly through the habit of thinking in it). I would speak a third language slightly better: the language of objects and placement and pattern—how to arrange stones to indicate water sources, how to mark trails, how to leave messages that can be read by anyone who knows the grammar of this place.

I would know things:

Which plants yield the best fiber and when in their growth cycle to harvest them. How to treat the fiber—soaking in water from specific pools, beating it, drying it in shade versus sun depending on desired properties. How to weave it into cordage that won't rot, into fabric that will shed water, into nets for catching the smaller fauna if protein is needed.

Which stones knap best for tools and where to find them—not everywhere in this landscape of plant matter, but in certain places where the substrate shows through, or in the remnant buildings where previous humans stored their best pieces. How to strike flakes with the right angle and force, how to pressure-flake an edge to maintain it, how to haft a blade to a handle using plant resin as adhesive.

How to read the fauna's behavior. The way root-pigs move when they've found good grazing versus when they're nervous. The way leaf-browsers select feeding sites based on some calculus of nutrition and safety. The flight patterns of sky-rippers and what they mean—lazy circles mean hunting, direct flight means traveling to a nest, rapid descents mean a kill is happening. The difference between the normal background terror of tunnel-runners and the specific terror that means a strike-cat is near.

How to move. Not just walking—that's simple—but the full vocabulary of movement this place requires. How to test the plant mat before committing weight, sensing the density through subtle resistance. How to climb vine-towers when necessary, finding hand-holds in the wound-tight structure, knowing when to trust a vine and when it's too old and dry. How to navigate through spine-forests by finding the rare gaps, by sometimes breaking through when no gap exists and accepting the damage as a cost. How to swim through the larger pools when they block a route, moving quietly to avoid attracting whatever lives in the deeper water.

How to interpret the shrine-markers. Not fully—some of the knowledge is held by the born-here humans and not shared—but enough. The geometric patterns carved into path-markers indicate directions, but also safe camping spots, water sources, seasonal timing for plant harvests. The offerings at shrines create a kind of communication network: fresh offerings mean someone was here recently, specific types of offerings mean specific messages (food offerings invite sharing, tool offerings suggest trade, bones arranged in particular patterns are warnings).

How to manage fire in a landscape that's simultaneously wet and capable of burning. The plant mat in its dry state is fuel waiting for ignition, but moisture is usually present enough to prevent runaway burning. Still, fires need careful tending—always in stone-ringed pits, always with water nearby, always fully extinguished before sleep. How to create different types of fire: hot fires for cooking and hardening wood, smoky fires for signaling or for creating zones that the biting insects avoid, banked fires that will last through the night with minimal feeding.

How to be still. This might be the most important knowledge. In a place where movement attracts attention and attention can be dangerous, stillness becomes a skill. Not just physical stillness but mental—quieting the inner noise that makes you fidget, learning to breathe slowly, letting time pass without the need to fill it. Sitting for hours watching a strike-cat stalk because movement would reveal your position. Standing motionless as a sky-ripper circles overhead, knowing that your silhouette doesn't read as prey unless you panic and run. Remaining calm when night-takers test your camp's perimeter because fear-scent might encourage them to press further.

I would carry specific objects, always:

A **stone blade**—hand-sized, carefully knapped, with one edge sharp enough to cut plant fiber or prepare food, the other edge thicker for heavier work. It's hafted to a wooden handle wrapped with cordage, with plant resin reinforcing the connection. The blade has been resharpened so many times that its profile has changed from its original shape. It's not the first blade I've carried here, won't be the last, but it's the current one, intimate through constant use.

A **fire-starting kit**—friction-wood pieces selected and maintained, tinder collected from specific fungi and dried, a small ceramic vessel holding char-cloth. The whole kit wrapped in a waxy leaf and tied with cordage. Redundancy is built in: two sets of friction-wood, multiple types of tinder, because wet fire-kit in this humid place can mean the difference between cold and warmth, cooked and raw, safe and vulnerable.

**Cordage**—always. Coiled and hung from my waist, multiple weights and strengths. Fine cord for delicate binding and repair. Heavy rope for climbing or hauling. Medium weights for general use. Some of it I've made, some acquired through trade, all of it maintained constantly because cordage rots in the humidity and loses strength, needs replacing before it fails at a critical moment.

A **water vessel**—ceramic, simple, with a cork stopper made from carved wood and sealed with resin. About a liter capacity, enough for a day if water sources are far apart. It's ugly—crude hand-forming, uneven firing—but it's watertight and I know its history: traded from a group I encountered near the southern seasonal camp, they'd carried it from somewhere else, it's traveled this landscape in multiple hands before ending up in mine.

A **digging stick**—hardwood, fire-sharpened, the length of my arm. Used for harvesting tubers, for clearing ground for sleeping, for testing the depth and stability of the plant mat, occasionally as a defensive weapon if something small and aggressive gets ideas. The working end is worn and needs re-sharpening regularly. The grip end is worn smooth from handling, stained dark from hand-oils.

A **woven bag**—large, carried across my back, containing everything else: food stores (dried plant matter, seeds, occasionally preserved meat if I've been successful hunting or found a carcass soon enough), spare cordage, a second blade in case the first breaks, collected objects that might be useful or interesting (unusual seeds, ceramic shards with particularly clear patterns, once a strike-cat claw found near a kill-site). The bag itself is a work in progress—I repair and re-weave sections constantly, so parts of it are old and stained and parts are fresh fiber, a patchwork history of its existence.

**Markings**—worn on my body, not carried. Scarification patterns on my forearms, simple geometric designs that match those on the path-markers. I didn't put them there; they were offered by one of the born-here humans in a brief ceremony I didn't fully understand but accepted. They might be identification, might be protection, might be social marker. They hurt when they were fresh, now they're just part of my skin's landscape, occasionally noticed by other humans who seem to approve or at least acknowledge them.

I would have **habits**:

Speaking aloud sometimes, to myself, in my fading first language. Not because anyone is listening, but because the sound of that language is a thread back to whoever I was before this place. Narrating what I'm doing: "Now checking the water level. Now adding wood to the fire. Now weaving the next section." The words are less important than the rhythm, the familiar phonemes, the reminder that language exists beyond gesture and pattern.

Counting—obsessively, probably pathologically. Days since I arrived here (I've lost count but I restart periodically when I remember to). Items in my bag (regular inventory, making sure nothing's been lost). Seeds in my cache-gardens (planted and surviving). Scars (new ones added to old ones, a map of accumulated damage). The counting creates order, a sense of progression, even if the numbers are arbitrary and the order is invented.

Making small things—carvings, mostly. Using my blade to shape wood into forms: geometric patterns like the ones on the markers, abstract shapes, occasionally something representational like a stylized fauna-form. I don't keep most of them; they're left at shrines as offerings or given to other humans I encounter or simply abandoned at campsites for whoever comes after. The making is the point, the transformation of material through intention, the sense of agency in a place where agency is limited.

Watching the bone-singers. I've become slightly obsessed with them, with their calls and their object-arrangements. I've started my own collection near my camp—stones and bones and ceramics arranged in patterns, not copying their patterns but responding to them, creating something that might be dialogue or might be nonsense. They've noticed; occasionally they rearrange my arrangements, adding objects or moving things, and I don't know if this is them correcting me or engaging with me or simply doing what bone-singers do.

Maintaining the shrine. Not because I believe in whatever it represents—if it represents anything specific—but because maintaining it gives structure to days that otherwise blur together. Clean the pool surround, restore the stone circles, refresh the offerings, re-carve the central post when weather has eroded the patterns. It's ritual without religion, practice without doctrine, just the repetition of actions because repetition creates meaning even if meaning wasn't there to start with.

I would have **limits**:

I cannot fully adjust to the sound. It's been long enough that I should be habituated, but something in my nervous system refuses. The roar still creates a low-level stress response, still requires conscious effort to filter out when I need to focus on something else. I wonder if people born here actually don't hear it the same way, if their neural processing treats it as baseline silence rather than constant noise. For me it remains noise, just noise I've learned to function within.

I cannot perfectly read the born-here humans' intentions. The language barrier is part of it, but there's something deeper—a set of assumptions or priorities or ways of thinking that I don't share. They make decisions that seem arbitrary to me, care about things I find irrelevant, ignore things I think are important. We coexist peacefully, we trade and occasionally share space, but there's a fundamental disconnect. They are of this place in ways I'm not and never will be.

I cannot go back. Not because the way is blocked—physically I could retrace whatever path brought me here—but because the person who could make that journey no longer exists. I've forgotten too much, adapted too much, changed in ways that are irreversible. The place I came from (wherever it was, whatever it was like) wouldn't fit anymore. The languages I spoke, the concerns I had, the way I moved through spaces—all of it is incompatible with what I've become.

I would be **alone in specific ways**:

No intimate relationships. The born-here humans maintain partnerships and family units, but I'm outside those structures. The few other outsider-humans I've encountered are as isolated as I am, and the attempts we've made to connect have been awkward and brief. We recognize in each other the same adaptation-damage, the same inability to fully belong anywhere. We trade information, sometimes travel together for a few days, then separate because proximity doesn't actually alleviate the isolation.

No long-term planning beyond survival. I maintain no calendar beyond the loosest seasonal awareness. I have no goals beyond immediate and medium-term: keep the shelter functional, keep the food caches stocked, keep myself alive and mobile. The idea of planning years ahead seems absurd. The idea of legacy—leaving something behind, building something lasting—doesn't compute. I exist in the present tense.

No certainty about meaning. I don't know if the shrine-keeping matters. I don't know if the patterns I carve or the bone-singer dialogue or the careful arrangement of my camp have significance beyond the significance I assign them. I don't know if I'm fulfilling some role in this place's ecology—threshold-keeper as I've named it—or if I'm just another organism eating and excreting and taking up space.

But there's also **presence in specific ways**:

Absolute attention to immediate sensory input. The texture of the plant mat under my feet right now. The quality of light filtering through the canopy at this moment. The specific pattern of thrummer-drone in this location. The smell that indicates rain coming, or fungi blooming, or a carcass somewhere upwind. I'm embedded in the sensory landscape more fully than I've ever been embedded in anything.

Competence. Not expertise—the born-here humans are expert, I'm still learning and will be learning until I die—but functional competence. I can do the things that need doing: find water, find food, make shelter, make fire, make tools, navigate, stay alive. Each day that I wake up is evidence that the competence is sufficient. There's satisfaction in that, a kind of pride that doesn't require audience or validation.

Strange peace. Despite the sound, despite the isolation, despite the constant low-level danger, there's something peaceful in this existence. The simplicity, maybe—concerns reduced to fundamentals, needs reduced to basics. Or the scale—being small in a vast living system, insignificant but present. Or just the exhaustion—physical tiredness that makes overthinking difficult, that creates sleep despite the noise, that makes each rest feel earned.

---

I would be in that chamber beneath the canopy-creatures right now, in the late afternoon as the light shifts toward amber. The bone-singers have arrived early, calling to each other in their too-structured songs. The rot-crawlers are beginning to glow as darkness approaches, their bioluminescence creating constellations in the plant mat. My fire is built but not yet lit—I'm waiting until true dark, conserving fuel.

I'm sitting near the shrine, working on a section of woven panel, hands moving automatically through patterns I no longer have to think about. The sound is doing its thing—roar and drone and percussion and calls. A tunnel-runner appears at the edge of the clearing, freezes, evaluates, decides I'm not a threat, continues to the water-pool to drink. A shimmer-jelly drifts past overhead, catching the amber light, beautiful and inexplicable.

I'm hungry but not uncomfortably so. Thirsty but not urgently. Tired but not exhausted. Tomorrow I'll need to range out to check the cache-gardens, maybe hunt if opportunity presents, definitely gather more firewood. But that's tomorrow. Right now is just this: hands weaving, light fading, bone-singers calling, the breathing continent breathing around me, one organism among billions, alive in the interval, keeping threshold at a gap in the green.

This is where I would find myself. This is what I would be. Not chosen, exactly, but arrived at through some combination of decision and circumstance and adaptation. Not happy or unhappy but present, functional, embedded in the immediate. Living in the gaps between the green masses, between the human social structures I left and the born-here communities I'll never join, between the organism I was and whatever I'm becoming. Threshold-keeper. Gap-dweller. One more sound in the roar.
